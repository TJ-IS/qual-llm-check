# Toward a Contingency Model for Selecting an Information System Prototyping Strategy

BILL C. HARDGRAVE, RICK L. WILSON, AND KEN EASTMAN

BILL C. HARDGRAVE is Executive Director of the Information Technology Research Center and Associate Professor of Computer Information Systems in the Sam M. Walton College of Business Administration at the University of Arkansas. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from Oklahoma State University. His research interests include object-oriented development, information systems prototyping, and adoption of software development innovations. Dr. Hardgrave has published in Communications of the ACM, DATA BASE, Journal of Systems and Software, Information & Software Technology, Journal of Computer Information Systems, and Computers & Operations Research, among other journals.

RICK L. WILSON is Associate Professor of Management Science and Information Systems at Oklahoma State University. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Nebraska-Lincoln. Dr. Wilson has published in Communications of the ACM, Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, Information and Management, International Journal of Production Research, and International Journal of Production and Operations Management, among other journals. His current research interests include neural networks, decision support systems, integrated management science applications, and the use of database technology.

KEN EASTMAN is Associate Professor of Management at Oklahoma State University. He received his Ph.D. in organizational behavior from the University of Nebraska-Lincoln. Dr. Eastman has published in the Academy of Management Journal and Journal of Applied Psychology, among other journals. His current research focuses on ingratiation.

ABSTRACT: Many proposed contingencies regarding the conditions when the use of prototyping will lead to successful system development appear in the literature. Using an industry survey, this exploratory study empirically investigates the effect of certain contingencies on system success. Overall, results indicate that five variables, when combined with prototyping, affect system success (as indicated by user satisfaction): innovativeness of the project, impact of the system on the organization, user participation, number of users, and developer experience with prototyping. These results provide some insight into the proper uses of prototyping to improve system success. The results also indicate that several of the current contingencies, if followed, do not ensure high levels of system success.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: contingency theory, information system prototyping, systems development, system success.

IMPROVING THE QUALITY OF SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT is an important area of study. This issue is consistently ranked in the top tier of critical issues facing IS practitioners $[7]$ and is one of the most published topics $[33]$ . The study of software development continues to grow in importance $[29]$ and will continue to do so as long as the field is characterized by growing application backlogs and systems that are habitually late and over budget $[41]$ .

Since the mid-1980s, prototyping has been promoted by many as a promising solution to a variety of software development problems. Prototyping use has continued to grow during this period, from a reported 30 percent of development efforts in 1984 [34] to over 70 percent in 1995 [25]. Like other tools, techniques, and methodologies, prototyping success in systems development is thought to be contingent on proper use.

Many contingencies have been suggested for the proper use of prototyping. However, prior studies have taken a restrictive view of the contingencies, prototype selection, and system success by focusing on only one or a small subset of the contingencies (e.g., [20]). This study provides a broader examination of the contingencies and seeks to answer the question: Does the use of prototyping, as suggested by various and numerous contingencies, affect system success (as indicated by user satisfaction)? The study is exploratory in the sense that little previous research has been conducted in the area of prototyping strategy selection, and no known theory of prototyping strategy selection exists. Thus, the results of this study can provide a basis for building a theoretical contingency model of prototyping use.

Background and Literature Review

## Prototyping

AN INFORMATION SYSTEM PROTOTYPE IS A MODEL OF A SYSTEM [15]. A prototype can be as simple as a mockup of reports or screens, or as complete as software that actually does some processing. Prototypes can be built with the intention of discarding them after they are no longer needed (expendable prototype) or they can become part of the final operational system (evolutionary prototype). The purpose of the prototype is implied in its type. Expendable prototypes are used to gather requirements or improve the design of a system and can be used at various points during the development of a system; eventually, they will be discarded. Evolutionary prototyping is prevalent throughout the development process (although the starting point may vary). Once the evolutionary prototype is started, it is used until the system becomes operational. Prototyping is the process of developing prototypes. Prototyping strategy indicates the type of approach used—either prototyping is used (expendable or evolutionary), or prototyping is not used.

## Characteristics Affecting Prototyping Selection

Recent studies have investigated how organizations decide whether or not to use prototyping $[16, 25]$ and whether the espoused contingencies are followed in practice $[26]$ . These studies, among others, allege that prototyping moderates (i.e., influences) the relationship between various environmental characteristics (such as project, developer, user, and organizational characteristics) and system success (i.e., system success is affected by the characteristic based on the prototyping strategy used). The following sections illustrate the many contingencies found in the literature concerning the “proper” use of prototyping to improve system success. These contingencies are the foundation for this study.

## Clarity of Requirements

The traditional systems development life cycle (SDLC) is designed to create a complete and correct set of requirements before the system is designed and built. If requirements cannot be determined correctly and completely, the system is typically rejected by users or must undergo substantial rework. Prototyping avoids this “all or nothing” scenario by getting users involved early in the development process, and continues to keep them involved (as necessary) until the system is completed. Thus, conventional wisdom suggests that prototyping is useful when system requirements are unclear or vague $[2, 8, 48]$ . “Unclear requirements” is one of the most consistently cited reasons for using prototyping $[25]$ . Conversely, involving users unnecessarily (e.g., when requirements are clear) may increase development time $[38]$ .

## Requirements Stability

The stability of the project requirements during development is affected primarily by two sources: users and the organization. When users cannot specify requirements fully at the beginning of the project, prototyping can provide a base from which adjustments can be made $[47]$ . A volatile organizational environment may affect project requirements as detailed specification decisions must be delayed as long as possible. Prototyping, because of its incremental nature, facilitates unstable requirements $[18]$ .

## Project Duration

The longer the duration of a project, the greater the changes are likely to be and the greater the risk that the project will become unmanageable. Prototyping has been criticized for its lack of manageability over a long period of time $[6, 9]$ and thus should be avoided. However, the changes (in requirements) associated with projects of long duration suggest the use of prototyping $[13]$ . This is one example of a clear contradiction in the literature suggesting the use of prototyping. Some argue the use of prototyping for projects of short duration $[18, 45]$ , while others argue its use for projects of long duration $[21]$ .

## Project Innovativeness

New system development (high innovation) requires greater user–developer interaction than modifications to existing systems (low innovation). Intuitively, prototyping would be more useful for high-innovation projects, and several recommend this approach $[18]$ ; others contend that prototyping can be useful for low-innovation projects as well (e.g., the existing system becomes the prototype for the modified system) $[27]$ .

## Project Size

Perhaps the greatest source of disagreement involving a contingency is project size (as indicated by person-hours). It has been suggested that prototyping is not useful for the development of large systems because of the lack of detailed documentation $[35]$ and because it is difficult to control the prototyping effort $[6, 8, 9]$ . The argument for using prototyping for large systems is that, because of its size, specifications are often unclear and unstable $[24]$ . As indicated earlier, prototyping readily accommodates unclear and unstable requirements, thus proving useful.

## System Impact on Organization

Critical systems—systems that operate, manage, and control the daily business activities—have a very broad and strong impact on the organization (i.e., high impact). Prototyping is thought to be beneficial for critical systems to ensure meeting specifications [18].

## Performance Requirements

Expendable prototypes can be built using a variety of tools ranging from paper to specialized tools for building graphical user interfaces such as Powerbuilder $[31]$ . The focus is on capturing user requirements, not prototype efficiency, since the prototype will be discarded. Evolutionary prototyping generally employs tools such as fourth-generation languages (4GLs) to develop the system. For evolutionary prototyping, efficiency is important because the prototype becomes the final, operational system $[44]$ . Unfortunately, many evolutionary prototyping tools are inefficient, which has limited the success of prototyped projects $[6]$ . Availability of proper tools is one of the keys to a successful prototyped project $[13]$ .

## User Participation

One of the main advantages prototyping offers is increased interaction and communication between users and developers $[48]$ . In fact, more is expected from the user in terms of his or her participation in the development process. Developers are expected to be responsive to users, and, likewise, users are expected to be responsive to developers. This line of reasoning suggests that prototyping should not be used if users do not have time to spend with developers.

## User Experience with Prototyping

Users who are not familiar with prototyping often have unrealistic expectations $[2, 6]$ , such as perceiving a prototype to be a fully operational system. These unrealistic expectations can translate into dissatisfaction with the developers and the final system, resulting in poor system success (as indicated by user satisfaction). Therefore, user experience with prototyping should be considered when choosing a prototyping strategy.

## Number of Users

Prototyping for one or a few users is not difficult; prototyping for many is difficult $[8]$ . Modifications requested by one user must be approved by all affected users; iterations become slower, and change is no longer a quick and easy task. Managing changes, and requests for changes, becomes almost impossible. Overall, a large number of users mixed with the use of prototyping make the development process more difficult to manage $[8]$ .

## Impact on Users

If the user impact is high (i.e., the project will considerably change the way users perform their jobs), there should be a high degree of interaction between the user and the developer, and this favors the use of prototyping. High interaction will keep the user informed and reduce the likelihood of system rejection. Conversely, less interaction is needed if the project will not adversely affect the user's job [18].

## Developer Familiarity with Application

When an application is familiar to a developer, less interaction with users is required to determine requirements. Unnecessary involvement of users may increase development time [38]. Thus, using prototyping may not be useful. If a developer lacks experience with similar applications, then prototyping can be useful in helping the developer understand the user's needs and corresponding requirements [39]. Lack of application knowledge by developers introduces risk to the development process [6]; prototyping can help reduce this risk.

## Developer Experience with Prototyping

Prototyping explicitly facilitates change as compared with the SDLC. Frequent changes in user requirements may frustrate designers unless they expect changes and are prepared to view them positively [2]. Also, prototyping requires a greater level of interpersonal communication skills from developers [3]. Thus, prototyping is proposed to be a more viable option when the developers have experience with the approach.

## Management Support

As with any tool or technique, project management support and understanding are essential to the successful utilization of prototyping, given that planning and controlling prototyped systems are more difficult to manage because of their evolving nature, the number of revisions, and the uncertainty in user requirements $[2, 6]$ . Failure to gain management support and establish explicit procedures can hinder system success $[2]$ .

## Need for Experimentation

Management is often unwilling to experiment with new concepts, ideas, and procedures, because of the risk level of such undertakings $[41]$ . A “good” project may sometimes be dismissed because management will not commit resources before they fully know the benefits. It has been suggested that prototyping be used in situations where there is a need for experimentation and learning before commitment of resources for a full project $[2, 6, 9, 45, 48]$ .

## Hypotheses and Research Model

Because no known theory of prototyping selection exists, this study borrows from existing theories to guide the research. Cognitive fit theory suggests that a match between a problem representation and a problem task will result in higher performance [1]. This theory has been extended to systems development to suggest that development methods should be matched to characteristics of the application [1, 28]. Our study follows this line of research by investigating the proper “match” between prototyping strategy (i.e., development method) and environmental characteristics (e.g., project, developer, user, and organizational characteristics) to improve system success. This research also utilizes elements of technology–process fit [13] that focuses on the fit between the technologies and the processes used in prototyping. Finally, contingency theory is used to frame this study. Although not a theory per se, contingency theory can be viewed as a framework for organizing knowledge in a given area [49]. One of the assumptions underlying contingency theory is the notion of “fit.” That is, the better the “fit” among contingency variables (in this case, environmental characteristics and prototyping strategy), the better the performance (in this case, system success).

The research model for this study (figure 1) demonstrates the effect of various environmental characteristics on system success as moderated by prototyping strategy. A moderator variable is defined as one that modifies either the form and/or the strength of the relationship between a predictor and a criterion variable [4]. In this study, the predictor variables are the various environmental characteristics and the criterion variable is system success. As each of the previous contingencies would suggest, prototyping should be used under various conditions, thus modifying the relationship between the environmental characteristic and system success (i.e., system success is affected by the “proper” use of prototyping). Overall, the concept of “fit” is prevalent in this model.

## Research Methodology

## Sample and Data Collection Strategy

A QUESTIONNAIRE PACKET WAS SENT TO THE INFORMATION SYSTEMS (IS) manager of 500 firms from the Directory of Top Computer Executives. This directory lists several thousand IS executives in organizations throughout the United States. A systematic sampling technique was used to obtain the sample of 500.

The packet consisted of a cover letter, two copies of developer questionnaires, two copies of user questionnaires, and four postage-paid return envelopes (one for each questionnaire). The IS manager was asked to select two systems (one for each set of questionnaires) that were implemented during the last two years, and to provide copies of the questionnaire to a key developer and the primary user for each of the two systems. The administration of separate questionnaires was necessary since both users and developers are involved in the development effort. This strategy also avoids the problem described by Cook and Campbell [12] of bias introduced by having the same individual provide all sources of information (i.e., mono-source bias). The user was responsible for providing information pertaining to his or her satisfaction with the system and various user characteristics (e.g., amount of time spent with the developers, level of knowledge). Developers provided information related to project characteristics (e.g., person-hours needed to develop), developer characteristics (e.g., knowledge of prototyping), and organizational characteristics (e.g., level of management support).

## Measurement of the Model Variables

## Environmental Characteristics Affecting Prototyping Selection

Each of the environmental characteristics was measured by one or more questions depending on the information needed. For example, determining the number of person-hours needed to develop the system required only a single, open-ended, question (i.e., how many total development person-hours did the system require?). Other characteristics, such as determining management support, required more than one question, usually presented in the form of a seven-point Likert scale. Most measurements were borrowed from previous studies, as follows: clarity of requirements [22]; duration [36, 46]; size [32, 36, 37]; user participation [19, 23, 43]; impact on users [36]; developer familiarity with application [36]; developer experience with prototyping [36]; and management support [23, 43]. All items were tested via an extensive pilot study. Items were then modified (added, changed, or deleted) based on the results of the pilot study.

## Prototyping Strategy

To determine the prototyping strategy used for a particular project, the developer was given the definition of prototyping provided earlier in this study and then asked to select the prototyping strategy most closely representing the strategy used on the project. The strategies provided to the respondent included: (1) only mockups of reports and screens were produced or the prototype simulates some functions, but does not use real data; (2) the prototype evolved into the final system; and (3) no form of prototyping was used. A response of “1” indicates that expendable prototyping was used; a “2” indicates that evolutionary prototyping was used; and a “3” indicates that prototyping was not used.

## System Success

User satisfaction, defined as the extent to which users believe the information system is available to them and meets their information requirements, is a widely used indicator of system success $[14]$ . The Ives, Olson, and Baroudi $[30]$ (IOB-UIS) instrument was chosen to measure user satisfaction since it has been identified as the best available measure $[17]$ . As suggested by Baroudi and Orlikowski $[5]$ , the instrument was modified to reflect the requirements of the specific situation. For this study, satisfaction with the product is the focus. Therefore, only the portion of the instrument that evaluates the product was used. Each question has two scales consisting of seven values $(-3\ to\ +3)$ and is scored by taking the average of the two scores from the scales. Also, a global user satisfaction question, as recommended by Baroudi and Orlikowski $[5]$ and Doll and Torkzadeh $[17]$ , was added to the instrument for validation purposes.

The overall Cronbach's alpha for the IOB-UIS instrument is 0.91. Thus, this instrument demonstrates high reliability. A factor analysis revealed the emergence of one factor (as suspected), thus indicating that the questions are measuring the same construct (i.e., the instrument is demonstrating construct validity).

## Results

## Responses

RESPONSES WERE RECEIVED FROM 133 ORGANIZATIONS (27 percent organizational response rate), representing 168 different projects (17 percent project response rate). $^{1}$ To be used, a response must be received from both the user and the developer. Thus, although many responses were received, only those that had a “matched pair” of user and developer were used. The respondents represent a wide variety of industries, including manufacturing, service, financial, insurance, retail, transportation, government, education, and health care. The industry distribution of the respondents approximates the industry distribution of the population under the null hypothesis of “Sample distribution is identical to population distribution.” The chi-square significance level is between 0.1 and 0.2; thus, the null hypothesis is not rejected. Therefore, it can be concluded that, based upon industry, the respondents properly represent the population. A good mix of prototyping strategies is also represented. Expendable prototyping was used on 51 percent of the reported projects, 19 percent used evolutionary, and 30 percent used no form of prototyping. This distribution of prototyping strategies is consistent with previous studies (e.g., [9]).

Table 2 provides the intercorrelations between system success and the environmental characteristics. As suspected, most of the variables are not significantly related to system success. However, performance requirements and impact on user are negatively related to system success, and user participation is positively related. Intuitively, performance requirements and impact on user would be negatively related to success as they directly affect the user (e.g., poor performance, or a system that greatly changes the user's job). Prior research suggests a positive relationship between user participation and system success, although results have been mixed [38, 42]. Overall, the intercorrelations indicate a general lack of direct relationships between the variables and system success. Baron and Kenny [4] suggest that a moderator variable be used when weak relationships between predictor (i.e., “environmental characteristic”) and criterion (i.e., “system success”) variables exist. Hence, the focus of this study is the moderating effect prototyping has on the relationship between environmental characteristics and system success. Thus, for example, although performance requirements are negatively related to system success, does prototyping change the strength or direction of that relationship?

## Testing the Hypotheses

Moderated hierarchical regression was performed to assess the moderating effect of prototyping strategy on the environmental characteristics to system success. First, system success was regressed on the combination of a single environmental characteristic and prototyping strategy. The next step added the interaction terms of the environmental characteristic and each of the prototyping strategies. The incremental contribution of $R^{2}$ provided by the interaction terms added to the model was then evaluated. If the incremental change in $R^{2}$ is significant, then the interactions account for a significant portion of the total explained variance beyond the main effects. Thus, it can be concluded that the relationship between the variable and system success is moderated by prototyping strategy [11]. ## Hypothesis 4: Project Innovativeness

The graph in figure 2 illustrates the interaction of innovation and prototyping strategy. A high degree of innovation indicates a totally new system development project; a low degree of innovation indicates only changes to an existing system. Visually, the interaction suggests that success is higher when evolutionary prototyping is used for high-innovation projects and, conversely, lower for low-innovation projects. Although figure 2 provides a visual inspection of the interactions, it does not indicate statistical significance. An investigation of the simple main effects is used to indicate statistical significance. To accommodate these tests, the data representing the “degree of innovation” characteristic is collapsed into two categories (i.e., high/low). The categories are then used to determine simple main effects. In other words, the data are “sliced” according to the prototyping strategy (no prototyping, expendable prototyping, evolutionary prototyping) and the characteristic (high innovativeness, low innovativeness). The test of significance involves a comparison of level of success for the various prototyping strategies across the two characteristic categories, as illustrated in Table 4. As suggested by Pedhazur and Schmelkin [40], the $\alpha$ used to determine the interactions (in this case, 0.10) is halved when evaluating simple main effects to account for the separation of the independent variable (i.e., the characteristic) into two categories. As shown in Table 4, the simple main effects test indicates that at high levels of innovation, evolutionary prototyping is better than no prototyping. This finding supports the contingency that suggests the use of prototyping for high innovation projects. For new development projects (i.e., high innovation), evolutionary prototyping can effectively gather the new requirements.

## Hypothesis 6: System Impact on Organization

System impact is categorized as either systems that are critical to the organization (i.e., mission critical systems) or systems that are not critical to the organization. Because system impact is a dichotomous variable, a $2 \times 3$ table (see Table 5) with the IOB-UIS scores as cell values is used to look at the interaction of prototyping strategy and system impact (critical/noncritical status of the system). The results indicate that, for critical systems, success is not influenced by the prototyping strategy (i.e., no differences between expendable, evolutionary, and no prototyping). However, for noncritical systems, both expendable and evolutionary provide a higher level of success than no prototyping (p = 0.0060 and 0.0029, respectively).

Discussions in the literature concerning the use of prototyping and system impact indicate that prototyping should be used for critical systems $[18]$ . These researchers contend that for critical systems it is extremely important the system meet specifications, and prototyping facilitates this important requirement. None of the previous studies, however, is empirically based. Thus, although intuitively it may seem that prototyping should be used for critical systems to make them more successful, this study suggests that prototyping (expendable or evolutionary) does not affect success. For noncritical systems, prototyping produces a more successful system than non-prototyped systems.

Why would critical systems not be improved by prototyping? We can postulate that, because it is recognized as a critical system, the development team will take all measures possible to ensure that the specifications of the system are met. The system is simply too important to the organization to be poorly developed, and the development team will do their best to meet specifications regardless of methodology. For noncritical systems, the results could be explained by theorizing that developers may not be as inherently focused on meeting system requirements and the use of prototyping assists them in keeping this focus.

## Hypothesis 10: Number of Users

The interaction shown in figure 4 indicates that evolutionary prototyping is not effective for projects involving a large number of users. Note the negative slope for the evolutionary prototyping line—success continues to decline as the number of users increases when evolutionary prototyping is used. With a large number of users, it is obviously difficult to satisfy users and to manage the development of a prototype that eventually becomes the final system (i.e., evolutionary prototyping). Satisfying all users slows the development process. The delays in development and slow response times from developers irritate the users, which results in lower user satisfaction (i.e., system success). It does appear that expendable prototyping may be an acceptable strategy for a large number of users, as it is easier to make changes to an expendable prototype (e.g., simple mockups of screens and reports).

A test of the simple main effects (using the previously described procedure) indicates that, for a small number of users, evolutionary prototyping is statistically better than no prototyping (see Table 7). Evolutionary prototyping provides the mechanism to interact closely with a small group. A small group of users may also signify a small system that could be easily satisfied by a 4GL or other evolutionary prototyping tool.

## Hypothesis 13: Developer Experience with Prototyping

Figure 5 shows the interaction between the developer's experience with prototyping and prototyping strategy; Table 8 provides the results of the simple main effects test. As suggested in figure 5 and Table 8, when developers have experience with prototyping (high levels of experience), the use of prototyping (either expendable or evolutionary) is associated with high levels of success. At low levels of experience, only slight (non-statistically significant) differences exist between levels of success for the various prototyping strategies. Thus, the interaction implies the use of prototyping for projects whose developers have experience with prototyping. This finding indicates that developers familiar with prototyping know how to use prototyping effectively (i.e., how to manage it, how to use the tools, etc.) to develop a successful system. On the other hand, when the developer is not familiar, prototyping provides no distinguishable advantage.

## Development of a Contingency Model

Contingency models identify desired alternatives based on specific situational attributes. In this case, the decision alternative is the proper prototyping strategy to be used in developing a system given its environmental characteristics.

The proposed contingency model shown in Table 9 is built from the results of the significant interactions between environmental characteristics and prototyping strategy, and subsequent investigations of the simple main effects. The results and the corresponding proposed contingency model provide a starting point for future research in this area. In summary, the proposed contingency model (Table 9) suggests: (1) for high-innovation projects, evolutionary prototyping is better than no prototyping; there are no significant differences among the prototyping strategies for low innovation projects; (2) expendable or evolutionary prototyping should be used for projects of low impact; there are no significant differences among the prototyping strategies for high impact projects; (3) evolutionary prototyping is better than no prototyping for projects involving a low level of user participation; there are no significant differences among the prototyping strategies for projects with high user participation; (4) for a small number of users, evolutionary prototyping is better than no prototyping; there are no significant differences among the prototyping strategies for projects involving a large number of users; and (5) prototyping (evolutionary or expendable) should be used when developers have a high level of previous experience with prototyping; there are no significant differences among the prototyping strategies when developers have a low level of previous prototyping experience. Each of these contingencies is based on the influence on system success. Thus, to improve system success, the contingency should be followed. For example, when the project is considered high-innovation, an evolutionary prototyping strategy is preferred to a “no prototyping” strategy to improve system success. The other contingencies are similarly interpreted.

The results of the moderated hierarchical regression analysis (presented earlier in Table 3) must be considered when viewing the contingencies presented in Table 9. That is, the moderated hierarchical regression analysis demonstrated the independent nature of the environmental characteristics and related prototyping strategy. Therefore, it must be noted that these are not the only factors that could (or should) be used in selecting a prototyping strategy. Instead, these are the factors that, independently, are moderated by prototyping. However, the value of the contingency table should not be discounted because of the independent nature of the factors. If used properly, Table 9 can be very useful in suggesting guidelines for the use of prototyping such that system success is maximized.

## Future Research

The proposed contingency model provides the foundation for future research. Studies that are exploratory in nature, such as this study, are intended to detect and map out the relationships that are, at present, unknown. In this context, this study serves as a guide, establishing research directions to be examined in future, more refined, investigations. Many prototyping contingencies have been postulated and published, most with little or no empirical evidence. Although this study empirically examined several contingencies and related factors that affect prototyping selection, it did not examine them all. The restrictions in this study can become opportunities for further studies. The remainder of this section examines some of the areas in need of exploration.

This study looked at only three prototyping strategies: expendable, evolutionary, or none. Perhaps a finer-grained designation of prototyping (e.g., as presented by $[15]$ ) would yield greater insight into their use. Also, this study only looked at the use of one type of prototype per project. It may be that a large project uses multiple forms of prototyping. For example, an evolutionary prototype could be used on one portion of the project, expendable on another portion, and none on a third portion. The use of multiple forms of prototyping and how these might effectively be used would be an interesting line of inquiry.

Some studies, such as $[9]$ , investigated the use of prototyping based on the type of system; this study did not consider system type. System type includes such typologies as DSS, EIS, MIS, TPS; real-time, on-line, batch; mainframe, client-server; intranet, internet; and others. As an example, the literature consistently recommends using prototyping for systems that support managerial decision making (such as DSS) owing to their common characteristics of unclear requirements, need for interactive user interface, and lack of need for high performance $[9, 35]$ . Also, the recent growth of web sites has prompted the suggestion that prototyping is useful for web site development because of the interactive user interface and the ease of developing and quickly changing web pages $[31]$ . Future research should consider system type as an environmental characteristic.

The development paradigm and methodology can influence the prototyping decision. For example, Coad and Yourdon [10] suggest using prototyping for all object-oriented (OO) development projects. If this is true (it will need to be explored), the use of prototyping may increase as OO becomes more popular. Some methodologies, such as the Spiral model, explicitly incorporate the use of prototyping [9]. Does prototyping become a de facto tool for certain development paradigms and methodologies, thus eliminating the need for contingency use considerations?

User satisfaction was used as a surrogate for success in this study. Other indicators of success include budget and schedule. Would the contingencies suggested in this study (to improve user satisfaction) also help reduce cost or time of development? Future studies could use alternative or multivariate measures of system success.

Although we presented prototyping as a moderator between environmental characteristics and system success, it is possible that the reverse is possible: Environmental characteristics moderate when a certain prototyping strategy will be most effective. $^{2}$ Future research should investigate the sequence of events: Does the environment drive the selection of tools, or are tools chosen first and then applied according to the environment? Logically, one would expect the environment to drive the tool selection, but, perhaps in this case, reality does not adhere to logic.

It has been unfortunate that untested suppositions and suggestions have led so much of the research in prototyping. Few of the contingencies suggested in the literature appear significant in the present study. Since this is an initial empirical look at this area, the resulting contingency model provides a foundation for future researchers to build on and/or refine and improve. For researchers, this should allow for a focusing of the research area.

From a practitioner's perspective, this study demonstrates that prototyping has documented occasions when it empirically improves system success. For organizations already using prototyping regularly, the contingency model is food for thought as they contemplate their ongoing development strategies in the future [6].

## Conclusion

THIS STUDY HAS INVESTIGATED THE MANY SUGGESTED CONTINGENCIES related to the proper use of prototyping and their affect on system success. Overall, the results of this study indicate that prototyping only modifies the relationship between five variables and system success (as indicated by user satisfaction): innovativeness of the project, impact of the system on the organization, user participation, number of users, and developer experience with prototyping. All other relationships between environmental characteristics and system success were not affected by the use of prototyping. These results suggest that prototyping is only one of several factors that affect the success of a system. Prototyping, or any of the other factors, cannot be viewed in isolation. Instead, all of the factors, including prototyping, must be evaluated when one is charting a strategy for successful information system development. Future research will add greater insight to the empirically generated contingency model created as output from this initial study.
