# Adoption and Utilization of Commercial Software Packages: Exploring Utilization Equilibria, Transitions, Triggers, and Tracks

KATHY S. LASSILA AND JAMES C. BRANCHEAU

ABSTRACT: Researchers and managers are beginning to realize that the full advantages of information technologies are not likely to be realized unless both the information technology and the organizational context are adapted during implementation. This highlights the importance of understanding and managing the relationship between information technology and organizational change. This study takes a new look at an old concept: information system utilization. A theoretical framework grounded in the punctuated equilibrium model contends that the mutual adaptation of commercial software packages and organizational processes follows a discontinuous change pattern where stable periods of utilization are occasionally disturbed by internal and external change triggers. Qualitative data gathered from interviews with software vendor personnel and a representative sample of users provide preliminary support for the existence of utilization equilibrium states. The evidence suggests that, when significant changes occur in the appropriation of technology, the users, or the organization context, the existing equilibrium state is disturbed. Following a temporary transition state characterized by redefinition of the technology and/or its context, the changes are incorporated into altered work processes, and a new equilibrium state develops. Various factors associated with the creation, maintenance, and disruption of equilibrium states are identified. Managers and users can enhance and prolong the useful life of software packages by paying careful attention to implementation efforts that heavily influence initial utilization equilibrium, identifying periods of equilibrium and transition, and managing the internal and external change triggers that influence transitions between equilibrium states.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information system implementation, information system utilization, innovation reinvention, punctuated equilibrium model, structuration model, technology assimilation.

UNDERUTILIZATION AND NONUSE OF INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY, ESPECIALLY SOFTWARE, frequently results in failure to meet objectives and frustration on the part of senior managers. This may be true even when the technology is functioning exactly as planned $[18]$ . Research has shown that, regardless of the features available, users mediate software impacts through avoidance, resistance, or adaptation $[17]$ . In addition, the intended and actual utilization of new software may vary widely, resulting in considerable variation in the benefits achieved $[18, 24]$ . If business managers are to meet project objectives and achieve expected returns on investment in new software, they must develop a more sophisticated understanding of how and why variation in utilization occurs.

Researchers and managers agree that the full advantages of information technologies are not likely to be realized unless both the information technology and the organizational context are adapted during implementation $[3, 5, 9]$ . This highlights the importance of understanding and managing the relationship between information technology implementation and organizational change, particularly as software packages are increasingly employed as catalysts for redesigning business processes.

This article presents a conceptual framework for the investigation of differences in information technology utilization, based on the relationship between information technology and organizational change. The framework is used to guide the analysis of data from an exploratory field study of packaged software utilization within eight firms. Following introduction of the theoretical framework, the results of the field study are presented and discussed. The article concludes with implications for practice and research.

Amount is typically measured as the cumulative (or average) number of hours of technology use. Reliance is often measured with a set of scaled items specifying how much a user depends on the technology to carry out work processes. Diversity is usually measured as the number of different software features used by one or more users. While this lends itself to quantitative measurement, it misses much of the richness present in organizational utilization contexts. We offer an additional, more holistic look at information technology use that is more in line with interpretive models of utilization [17, 22]. We do not recommend replacing earlier definitions of utilization, but that these definitions be broadened where the research objectives and context benefit accordingly.

Tyre and Orlikowski [22, 23] contend that the adoption and utilization of technology are not solely an incrementally adaptive process as described by stage models, such as those based in the innovation diffusion literature [2, 20]. Instead, their work provides evidence that the mutual adaptation between technology and organizational processes occurs in a discontinuous pattern that frequently displays periods of the routine use characteristic of stage models. The pattern shows alternations between brief episodes of intensive change activity and periods of routine operation and use.

Tyre and Orlikowski's findings are consistent with similar challenges to the evolutionary, incremental characterization of organizational change raised by the punctuated equilibrium model [7]. The punctuated equilibrium model conceptualizes organizational change as consisting of long periods of stable infrastructures that accommodate only incremental adaptations, punctuated by brief periods of frame-breaking change that signal revolutionary upheavals in the status quo. Gersick [7] identifies the three main features of the punctuated equilibrium model illustrated in figure 1 and described below:

\- Deep structure refers to “the set of fundamental ‘choices’ a system has made of (1) the basic parts into which its units will be organized and (2) the basic activity patterns that will maintain its existence” [7, p. 14]. Deep structures tend to be highly stable since the selections made by an organization preclude many alternatives, and because the activity patterns established within the organization reinforce these structures via mutual feedback loops.

\- Equilibrium periods are characterized by stability in the organization's structure and activity patterns. “The equilibrium period consists of maintaining and carrying out these choices [deep structure]” [7, p. 16]. During equilibrium periods, systems may experience incremental adaptation, but their underlying deep structures remain unchanged. In addition, equilibrium periods are sustained by inertia resulting from various factors of cognition, motivation, and obligation. In general, as long as a system’s deep structure is intact, it is difficult to change.

\- Revolutionary periods are characterized by major upheaval and reformation of deep structure. For significant change to occur, “the deep structure must first be dismantled, leaving the system temporarily disorganized, in order for any fundamental changes to be accomplished. Next, a subset of the system's old pieces, along with some new pieces, can be put back together into a new configuration, which operates according to a new set of rules" [7, p. 19]. Disruption in the existing deep structure may occur from internal changes that cause structures and activities go out of alignment, or from external changes that impair the system's ability to adjust with its environment.

To distinguish between the general punctuated equilibrium model and our specific extension to the study of information technology and organizational change, the distinctive patterns of technology utilization that emerge during equilibrium periods are referred to as equilibrium states. The periods of upheaval and potential change between equilibrium states are referred to as transition states.

In some cases, the initial implementation of a new information technology may mark the beginning of a revolutionary period or transition state, which concludes when the work processes and the information technology are mutually adapted within the organizational context. These adaptations can result in relatively stable, consistent, or “routine” utilization of the information technology, signaling the onset of an equilibrium period. The equilibrium period is characterized by a distinct pattern of information technology utilization representing the underlying deep structure within the organization.

The outcome of a transition period is a new equilibrium state. The realignment activities that occurred during the transition period and resulted in the new equilibrium may or may not have led to revisions in deep structure $[7]$ . Realignment may have resulted from making the utilization of new technologies consistent with the existing deep structure. In either case, the new equilibrium state is observable via the current processes and procedures, which, for the time being, have become “routine operations” within the organizational context. Thus, a major implication of the punctuated equilibrium model for technology implementation and organizational change is the need to identify the potential equilibrium states resulting from transition states or revolutionary periods. This leads to the formation of the first key research question:

## R1: What equilibria states may result from the revolutionary period(s) potentially caused by the implementation of a new information technology?

While Gersick's theoretical treatment provides little guidance in the identification of specific equilibrium states, the work of Greenwood and Hinings [8], Hinings and Greenwood [10], and Johnson and Rice [11, 12] provides substantial insight. Hinings and Greenwood develop the concept of an archetype, which is highly consistent with Gersick's view of deep structure. They define archetypes in terms of two general statements:

First, organizational structures and management systems are best understood by analysis of overall patterns rather than by analysis of narrowly drawn sets of organizational properties. . . . Second, patterns are a function of ideas, beliefs, and values—the components of an “interpretive scheme”—that underpin and are embodied in organizational structures and systems. An archetype is thus a set of structures and systems that reflects a single interpretive scheme. [10, p. 1052]

In terms of the adoption and ongoing utilization of an information technology, the archetype defined by Hinings and Greenwood represents a distinct pattern of information technology utilization, or equilibrium state. These patterns are not static, but dynamic in nature. Webster's Third Unabridged International Dictionary offers a definition that helps emphasize the dynamism inherent in equilibrium states: dynamism is “[a] condition in which opposing forces are so balanced that there is no tendency to change in one way or another” (p. 319). This definition captures the idea of dynamic forces whose continuous interaction results in what might be observed as relatively static structures.

Hinings and Greenwood [10] argue that the existence of archetypes is best established by comparing an empirically revealed pattern with a previously identified model pattern. Since no work on the identification of utilization equilibrium states appears in the information systems literature, patterns of information technology utilization are difficult to discern. However, a study of word processing systems in the communication literature provides some insight. Johnson and Rice [11] produced a four-level typology based on reinvention of software applications. This typology is consistent with the concept of a utilization archetype or equilibrium state, and we use it as the model pattern for comparison in our analysis.

Reinvention, as described in the innovation diffusion literature, is the extent to which the user modifies an information technology after its original development $[19, 20]$ . Reinvention may involve both the information technology as a tool, and the roles, processes, and procedures involving its use. As such, reinvention may involve changes to deep structure, represented by organizational structures and work processes. Over time the incorporation of the new information technology and changes in associated business processes become imbedded or institutionalized within the organization. Institutionalization of the “reinvented” technology is synonymous with the concept of an archetype equilibrium state.

Johnson and Rice [11] classify the four types of systems resulting from reinvention as either adoptive or adaptive. Adoptive systems are characterized by appropriation of the technology to support existing processes and enhance their efficiency. Johnson and Rice further classify adoptive systems as either low-integration or standard adoption systems. These types of appropriations support minimal changes to deep structure. Adaptive systems, on the other hand, involve the appropriation of technology to redesign existing work processes. Johnson and Rice further classify these systems as either expanding or high-integration systems. These types of appropriations support significant changes to deep structure.

The four reinvention system types introduced above represent distinct patterns of utilization, or archetype equilibrium states. These states emerge as a result of ongoing interactions among organizational members, deep structure, and information technology. The initial software implementation and subsequent change processes ultimately result in a new equilibrium state. Hinings and Greenwood [10] contend that the punctuated equilibrium model implies that archetypes represent patterns of organizational equilibrium, and that organizational change represents a transition between archetypes. In terms of the relationship between information technology and organizational context, archetypes are represented in patterns of technology utilization, which embody interpretive schemas about the role of technology in organizational structures and processes. Stable patterns of utilization thus constitute a utilization equilibrium state for the organization. Similarly, change is represented by transitions between various utilization equilibria. The movement among equilibrium states via transitions over time is referred to as a “track” and, in the case of information technology, a utilization track. The resulting research question is:

## R2: What transitions exist among utilization equilibria states over time?

The initial implementation of an information technology is an important potential trigger for change. Following the initial introduction of the information technology, a variety of both technology- and non-technology-related factors might generate change triggers that affect deep structure and signal the onset of a new revolutionary period or transition state. During the transition, organizational members attempt to reconcile the misalignments by modifying existing operating procedures, developing new procedures, discarding obsolete procedures, or modifying other organizational factors or the newly introduced information technology.

When applied to the phenomena surrounding information technology utilization, this paradigm suggests that the introduction of new information technology is a “change trigger” with the potential to have an impact on existing deep structure. In addition, other factors can serve as triggers influencing the relationship between organizational context and information technology utilization. This generates the third key research question:

R3: What “change triggers” cause breaks in and movement among equilibrium states?

The primary purpose of acquiring and implementing new information technology is the attainment of benefits for the organization. Since the equilibrium period is characterized by routine use of an information technology, it provides an opportunity to assess the impact of that information technology and associated work modifications on the organization. Different patterns of information technology use may result in either positive or negative impacts within the organization. Thus, the fourth and final research question is:

R4: Do some equilibria states and/or utilization tracks result in more positive or greater impacts than others?

The focus of the current study is on research questions 1, 2, and 3. While the fourth research question is raised, it was not rigorously investigated in this initial study. Much more needs to be defined and investigated regarding the equilibrium states and tracks before a study can be developed to accurately assess the impacts of different states or tracks on organizational processes and effectiveness.

## Research Context

FACED WITH GROWING APPLICATION BACKLOGS DUE TO RISING software development costs, the need for rapid deployment of new systems to keep pace with business, and the migration of applications to the desktop through client-server architectures, organizations increasingly turn to packaged software applications to solve a variety of business problems. Packaged software applications represent the high end of the growing preengineered software component market. These applications provide relatively complete support for standardized business processes and come either ready to use (shrink-wrapped) or with varying degrees of integration services. They range in scope from individual productivity tools (word processor, spreadsheet, etc.) to enterprise-class applications (customer care, inventory control, etc.). Regardless of scope, the benefits information systems (IS) managers expect from software packages include lower implementation costs, shorter implementation timeframes, and faster attainment of project objectives. Unfortunately, the mere availability of these applications does not guarantee that they will be used effectively by knowledge workers or that project objectives will be met [15].

The punctuated equilibrium perspective provides a useful conceptual model for exploring the utilization of commercial software packages. The introduction of a software package in an organization can result in incremental adaptation and the maintenance of the existing equilibrium, or trigger a period of revolutionary change leading to the development of a new equilibrium. New information technologies support the status quo when their appropriation is patterned after previous experience. Poole and DeSanctis $[17]$ have shown that the tendency to adapt technologies to current practices is strong.

Commercial software packages provide an interesting case, as they are more likely to result in new equilibrium states than custom-developed applications. Custom-developed applications frequently support the existing deep structure and serve to maintain the current equilibrium. Lucas et al. [13] state that users are not likely to modify custom-developed applications that closely match their needs. Software packages, on the other hand, are likely to contain features and capabilities that, when utilized, can disrupt the current equilibrium by altering relationships among current business structures and activities. Packaged applications are frequently dedicated to a particular business function trading off customized practices for “industry standard” approaches. Changes to organizational processes are likely as users adjust their work procedures to fit the standardized processes supported by the software. In some cases, custom-designed applications may be developed in an effort to make substantive changes to business organization and processes, thereby attempting to affect deep structure proactively and trigger a period of revolutionary change. Technology-enabled business process reengineering projects offer many examples of software-enabled change efforts with this objective [5]. From the punctuated equilibrium perspective, these efforts are likely to be difficult, since developers are confronted with cognitive, motivational, and obligation-based barriers to substantive change [7]. The high failure rate of reengineering projects appears to support this contention [4].

## Research Approach

THE PREVIOUSLY STATED RESEARCH QUESTIONS WERE EXPLORED in a multiple-site field study. As part of a comprehensive study of software package implementation, we obtained implementation and support documents from the vendor of an enterprise-class commercial software package and interviewed a random sample of its customer/users.

Multiple implementations of an engineering decision support system (EDSS) provided the focus for the research. This particular software package is designed for engineering professionals in the natural resources industry. It supports engineering problem solving by providing relational database management features, data and statistical analysis tools, and data modeling/visualization capabilities. The package is generally recognized as the market leader among the majority of companies in the mining industry. It is well suited to our research objectives because its enterprise-class scope offers the potential for use in ways that affect deep structure.

A sample of ten companies was randomly selected from a vendor-supplied list of approximately 120 EDSS installation sites. The 120 installation sites provided by the vendor were a comprehensive list of all EDSS installations at the time of the study. Key individuals representing the ten companies as EDSS liaisons were contacted to determine their company's willingness to participate in the study. Of the initial ten companies, two companies declined to participate: One company had recently acquired the EDSS and had not yet implemented the software; the other company was undergoing a major downsizing effort and was unwilling to take the time to discuss their experiences.

Of the eight participating companies, the earliest adopter had acquired the EDSS six years earlier, while the latest adopter had acquired it only one year earlier. On average, the companies had been using the EDSS for 3.25 years. The majority of EDSS liaisons said they “provided input into the acquisition decision but were not directly responsible for that decision.” Respondents from seven of the eight companies reported that the acquisition decision was based on the perceived “need to manage all the data in our business” and the knowledge that EDSS represented “the leading software package in the industry.” The eighth company acquired the EDSS when it merged with another mining firm. We viewed this situation positively since the liaisons’ relatively low involvement in the acquisition reduced the potential for social-desirability bias. Furthermore, since the reasons for acquisition were similar across seven of the eight companies, it is unlikely that differences in the acquisition decision process had a major impact on our findings about utilization equilibria.

Interviews were conducted with twenty-one geologists, engineers, and managers representing the eight companies in the study. All eight of the companies are involved in the natural resources industry, primarily in mineral exploration and mine development. To minimize single respondent bias, at least one manager and one geologist/engineer were interviewed wherever possible (in seven of eight companies). The primary EDSS user was interviewed in every company. Additional users were interviewed where possible (in seven of eight companies). To provide a more objective viewpoint, we made sure to interview at least one person who interacted with the EDSS but whose primary job responsibility did not require its direct use. Respondents ranged from 27 to 48 years of age, with 2 to 18 years of experience in the mining industry, 2 to 16 years of experience with their current company, and 1 to 10 years of experience with the EDSS. The companies ranged in size from 3 to 650 employees, with annual revenues from \$250K to \$149M. In addition to users and managers, interviews were conducted with four persons representing the EDSS software vendor. These vendor respondents provided technical and user support to the companies participating in the study.

User and vendor interviews ranged in length from half an hour to two and a half hours. Additional followup interviews were conducted via telephone to clarify essential points. An interview guide was developed based on a preliminary research model combined with information from our initial discussions with vendor representatives. These discussions focused on the primary features of the EDSS and the typical implementation process. Our interview guide included both unstructured and semi-structured questions designed to address the following points:

1. Extent and character of individual/company utilization of the EDSS now.

2. Extent and character of individual/company utilization of the EDSS beginning with initial implementation and unfolding over time.

3. Internal and external factors that influence current utilization of the EDSS.

4. Internal and external factors that influenced past utilization of the EDSS.

The interview guide also contained questions to identify each respondent's perceptions of the key characteristics of potential equilibrium states. A summary of the questions included in the interview guide is provided in the appendix.

The same investigator conducted all interviews at the company site. Preprinted interview guides were prepared and used by the interviewer during each interview.

The guides were structured to ensure that each interview followed the same template and minimized the amount of note-taking necessary to capture the required data. Following each interview, the notes were summarized and the required data from user responses to the key characteristics of utilization equilibrium were captured in a central data repository for later analysis. Data retrieved from respondents in the same company were compared for accuracy and consistency. Any discrepancies were reconciled in followup phone conversations.

Archival documents and data from company users and vendor representatives provided an additional source of information. These archives included customer correspondence, company memos, software manuals, and results from the vendor's customer satisfaction study. The archival data were used to verify qualitative data collected during the interviews and support user comments with specific dates and timeframes. Data related to the dates of EDSS acquisition/installation, guidelines/requirements for use of the EDSS, training topics and schedules, and user participation in implementation activities and work process design were extracted from the archival documents and used to triangulate the interview findings. Where any discrepancies were noted, followup phone calls were used to resolve them.

## Data Analysis and Findings

OUR PRIMARY ANALYSIS INVOLVED A QUALITATIVE REVIEW OF INTERVIEW field notes and archival data. The analysis is organized into several subsections related to the research questions: R1 is discussed in the section on “Current Utilization Equilibria States”; R2 is discussed in “Utilization Tracks”; R3 is reviewed in “Change Triggers and Utilization Tracks”; and R4 is discussed briefly in “Benefits of Utilization Equilibria/Tracks.”

## Current Utilization Equilibria States

Our interview questions were designed to determine the extent and character of both individual-respondent and companywide use of the EDSS. Specifically, the characteristics of the proposed utilization equilibrium states were explored for each company. Both users and vendor representatives provided information about feelings of control over the software and associated work processes, attitudes toward the usefulness and potential contribution of the software, the amount of training and experience with the software, the time allowed for experimentation with the software, and the level of communication and participation regarding work-related decisions. Key phrases from respondents about each characteristic were extracted from our interview notes and coded to generate three-level responses such as low, moderate, and high.

A high degree of consistency existed between multiple user and vendor responses for each characteristic. Inconsistencies were followed up with phone interviews for further clarification. To identify and classify the current equilibrium state of each company with respect to EDSS use, aggregate company responses were compared to the archetypes shown in Table 2. Based on matches between the interview data and archetype characteristics, the companies were assigned to an equilibrium state. To verify our assignment, respondents independently reviewed descriptions of the equilibrium states and selected the one they felt most closely described their current environment. Six of the assignments were confirmed by respondent selections. The remaining two were discussed with both user and vendor representatives, and agreement was reached on the “best” classification. Our coded interview data are summarized in Table 3.

All eight companies' utilization equilibrium states are consistent with the characteristics of the reinvention system types described by Johnson and Rice, with two notable exceptions. Both the communication and participation characteristics were constant across all eight companies (shown with dotted border in figure 3). For all eight companies, users felt that communication with peers and supervisors was maintained at high levels. Users also felt they had a high degree of participation in decisions affecting their immediate work tasks and procedures. It does not appear, based on the sample selected for the study, that these two characteristics are significant for classifying utilization equilibria.

A plausible explanation for this finding is the difference in research contexts between this study and Johnson and Rice's study. That study involved clerical personnel using a word processing system. Clerical personnel are typically more closely supervised than EDSS users. EDSS users are typically highly trained professionals responsible for incorporating the software into their work processes with a great degree of latitude. Their contacts with supervisors are usually limited to “as needed” meetings, and they work independently to accomplish their data analysis tasks. The respondents indicated that they had access to their supervisors as necessary. The latitude given these users may make communication with supervisors and participation in decision making less significant than for the clerical users of a word processing system.

Based on the remaining characteristics, companies A, B, C, and D currently exhibit low-integration equilibrium. For these companies, the degree of control, user attitudes, training, and experimentation were consistent with low-integration states. Vendor support representatives confirmed these findings. Based on their knowledge of other user organizations, they felt that the EDSS was underutilized in these organizations. In these low-integration cases, implementation of EDSS had little impact on existing deep structure.

Two companies, E and F, exhibit standard adoption equilibrium. Companies E and F are consistent with standard adoption equilibrium with respect to degree of control, user attitudes, training, and experimentation. In our interviews, respondents described using the software to support existing work procedures. Their reports also suggested that implementation of EDSS had only minimal impact on business processes or organization structures.

## Utilization Tracks

Each of the respondents was asked to describe their own experiences (and those of their company) with the EDSS over time. The reported experiences were compared and aggregated to create a composite “implementation account” for each company. These accounts made it clear that the utilization equilibrium states observed at the time of our interviews had not always been in place. Some organizations had changed more than others. Both positive and negative changes were described.

Both users and managers described periods of time when the incorporation of the EDSS into work processes was unstable. The reported periods of instability occurred first during the initial implementation and recurred each time there was a change from one equilibrium state to another. These periods of instability during initial implementation and between equilibrium changes are referred to as transition states. When changing from standard adoption to a low-integration state, the phrases used to describe the transition state were always negative, such as “uncertain,” “awkward,” “time-consuming,” or “impossible.” When changing from low-integration to a standard adoption state, or from standard adoption to an expanded or high-integration state, the phrases used to describe the transition state were positive, such as “exciting” or “great potential.”

In our observations about the EDSS utilization over time, the periods of instability represent a temporary state that was not predicted in the Johnson and Rice $[11]$ study, possibly due to its cross-sectional design. The transition state does not appear to be a separate equilibrium state per se, but rather a temporary period of instability between equilibria, as depicted in figure 2. The transition state seems to represent a turbulent period in the change process during which users attempt to define or redefine the role of the packaged application in the work environment, as well as the rules, resources, processes, and procedures associated with its use. It is the equivalent of the revolutionary period described by Gersick $[7]$ as a state of upheaval during which mutual adaptation of the software package and associated work processes occur in an attempt to realign deep structure. The transition state is also consistent with Hinings and Greenwood's $[10]$ identification of embryonic archetype coherence and schizoid incoherence states during organization changes. These states represent varying levels of discord between organization structures and processes and the underlying ideas and values of the organization's interpretive scheme. Consistent with the experiences of all eight companies, transition states were identified during the initial implementation when introduction of the EDSS disrupted the existing work environment.

Following implementation and initial equilibrium, four of the companies moved out of their initial equilibrium states and through an additional transition state prior to achieving a new equilibrium state. ## Change Triggers and Utilization Tracks

The change triggers associated with the utilization tracks we observed are summarized in Table 4. They are organized into groups representing the movements between specific equilibria via temporary transition states. The identified factors are discussed below in more detail.

Following the initial implementation of the EDSS, companies A, C, D, and F moved into low-integration equilibrium, characterized by minimal use of the software package. Several users described scenarios in which a decision was made not to incorporate the EDSS into ongoing work processes. A variety of reasons are cited for this decision. One manager indicated that although the application was indeed powerful, it clearly was not for the “weak-hearted” in terms of its complexity and difficulty to understand. Another user, whose primary responsibility was petroleum exploration, had access to the EDSS but used it in very limited ways. The user viewed the technology as a tool for mineral exploration and not at all compatible with petroleum exploration and engineering problems. Vendor personnel indicated that this user might have acquired this perception from knowing that the original developer of the package was a mineral engineer, not a petroleum engineer. This knowledge may have biased the user’s perception of the task–technology fit. Users at another company cited a poor initial experience during the implementation phase as the reason for their limited use of the package. These users did not feel they were given adequate training and support from the vendor during system startup. Consequently, they developed a negative attitude toward its usefulness and its potential contribution to their work environment. They showed no interest in increasing their minimal use of the system.

Several of these users remarked “we know this system can do some great things.” They said they would use the software more “if we could just find the time to work with it and figure out what it can do for us.” The users in low-integration equilibrium typically learned one or two functions of the system and then claimed they did not have the opportunity to learn more. A manager in one of these environments cited the gap between the knowledge level of potential users and the sophisticated tools provided by the packaged system as the primary reason for minimal use. In talking about the advanced statistical capabilities of the software and his attempts to get his users to apply these tools, the manager commented that, for his organization, “buying [EDSS] is like buying a Maserati and putting your grandmother behind the wheel.” Because of this “knowledge-technology” gap, the users incorporated the package features they knew and understood into their work, while relying on other tools for remaining tasks.

In the case of each company experiencing low-integration equilibrium, several different factors were identified. These included the overwhelming complexity of the packaged application, the user perception that the packaged application was not an appropriate tool to aid them in completion of their work tasks, lack of user support for the software and a poor relationship with the vendor, lack of time for training and experimentation, and poor knowledge–technology fit.

The above factors seemed to trap three companies into low-integration equilibrium following initial implementation. A fourth company, company F, eventually moved out of low-integration into a transition state, and from there into standard adoption equilibrium. This change was triggered by the hiring of a new employee who was charged with the task of learning to use the EDSS and apply it to data collection and analysis tasks. The new hire had been introduced to the EDSS as an engineering student. During a transition period characterized by further training and renewed experimentation with the software, this user found new ways to apply the EDSS to existing work tasks. This eventually resulted in transition to standard adoption equilibrium, as the EDSS-supported work tasks became standard operating procedures within the company. In this scenario, the hiring and assignment of a new employee with EDSS familiarity provided the “change trigger” needed to move the company out of low-integration equilibrium, through transition, and into a standard adoption equilibrium.

Companies B, E, G, and H moved into standard adoption equilibrium following their initial transition state triggered by the implementation project. Following implementation, users in these companies continued to experiment with the software and find ways to incorporate it more fully within their work environment. The standard adoption state involved use of the basic functions and features of the software as designed by its developers.

Interviews with users in standard adoption equilibrium exhibit many consistencies. Several of these companies had users who were either experienced with EDSS prior to its initial implementation, or who were familiar with many of the sophisticated tools provided by the EDSS. Their prior experience helped minimize both the initial disruptions to the work environment, and the amount of training and experimentation necessary to blend the software effectively with existing work processes. These users had high respect for the packaged system and its capabilities, and good rapport with vendor representatives. The vendor frequently consulted these users for guidance on modifications and enhancements to the software package. The important factors related to gaining standard adoption equilibrium included: users with prior EDSS experience, a minimal knowledge-technology gap, positive attitudes toward the software and its vendor, and strong user–vendor relationships.

Of the four companies that moved into standard adoption equilibrium following the initial transition state, only company E maintained that state without further change.

The EDSS was widely used in this company, and the associated rules, resources, and processes established during its introduction became part of standard operating procedures. Many of the geologists and engineers who worked at company E were already familiar with this software package. They were either exposed to the EDSS as students or had worked with it at another company. In this case, the use of the system was patterned after existing organizational practices and procedures. Based on the knowledge of the users and the features of the package, there was little impetus for moving out of standard adoption equilibrium.

The remaining three companies eventually moved out of standard adoption equilibrium. The essential factors cited as reasons for transition include changes in the business environment, employee turnover, and the identification of new and better ways to apply system features to the work environment. Company B was originally involved in gold mining and used the software package for mineral deposit estimates and mine planning. The company purchased several taconite mines and prepared to begin production. The existing work environment involving the EDSS was structured around providing support for gold mining operations, which differ significantly from taconite mining. Users needed to adjust work processes and procedures to accommodate the change. This moved company B into the transitional state as users experimented with the software in an effort to apply it to taconite estimation and mine planning. The primary EDSS “power user” left company B and experimentation with the EDSS was abandoned. Instead, a different system originally owned by the taconite mining operation was adapted to work tasks and the EDSS fell into a minimal use pattern. Both a change in the business environment and employee turnover helped to move company B into a low-integration equilibrium with respect to EDSS use.

The identification of new and better ways to apply the EDSS in the work environment led companies G and H out of standard adoption equilibrium and into transition. Company G was initially motivated to search for a solution within the EDSS by their poor record keeping of mine yields. The company moved out of standard equilibrium and into a transition state. As the EDSS users experimented with various features provided by the software package, they discovered that field geologists could use the EDSS on notebook computers at mine sites. Incorporating several previously unused features of the software, field geologists modified their work processes to provide a partial solution to the record-keeping problem. As the field geologists exploited their opportunities for mine site data collection and record keeping, these new work processes and procedures were integrated into the organization's deep structure, resulting in an adaptive, expanding equilibrium state.

After a period of initial transition and standard adoption equilibrium during which the basic EDSS functions were adopted, a technically knowledgeable user at company H learned how to capture sequences of EDSS commands in a server log, and transfer these commands for use by less experienced users. This moved company H out of standard adoption equilibrium and into a transition state. As advanced users at the main office experimented with new features and procedures, they developed their own logs and sent them to regional offices. The routine use of server logs at regional offices provided training tools and a means of introducing new work practices and procedures on an ongoing basis to the geologists and field engineers at company H. This moved the company from an adoptive, standard adoption state to an adaptive, high-integration equilibrium. New work practices were absorbed into company H and other users became adept at creating and using these logs. The EDSS vendor eventually made the log procedure a standard feature of the EDSS and incorporated it into its next software release.

Of the eight companies, only companies G and H moved into adaptive equilibrium states signaling a change in deep structure. Following the initial standard adoption equilibrium achieved through these companies' implementation projects, a period of experimentation and uncertainty regarding the appropriation of the EDSS marked the transition. The instability associated with transition occurred as users experienced both successes and failures in applying previously unused EDSS features and developed new adaptations through trial and error. When new and creative uses for the EDSS were defined, the companies moved into higher-level equilibrium states representing more complete assimilation of the software.

For company G, the movement to an expanded equilibrium state was facilitated by the high degree of control that users felt over the software package and their ability to adapt it to their work environment. For company H, the movement to a high-integration equilibrium state was facilitated by a high degree of control over both their work processes and the software package. In addition, users in both companies felt a high degree of freedom to experiment with EDSS in their organization.

The movements among equilibria states and transitions associated with the adoption and utilization of the EDSS discussed here are highly consistent with the work of Johnson and Rice $[12]$ . In low-integration and standard word processing environments, their findings showed that clerks tended to use the computer in much the same way they had used its predecessor, the typewriter. Little advantage was taken of many features provided by the word processor. This was attributed to little or no training, and training that focused on the technological “how-tos” as opposed to how the computer could be integrated with the work process.

In addition, Johnson and Rice [12] found that support for experimentation with the technology and time to learn to apply the technology in new ways were superseded by the belief that the purpose of the technology was to enhance the efficiency of word processing or typing tasks. The belief in this objective seemed to stifle the technology's adaptation within organizations and maintain the low-integration or standard equilibrium. This was also evident in companies A, C, and D where, although it was recognized that EDSS had powerful features, little time or resources were provided to allow users to explore them. The deep structure in these companies may have held the belief that the purpose of the EDSS was to enhance the efficiency of a highly paid geologist. The extra time required for learning and experimentation would thus be inconsistent with the short-term efficiency perspective.

## Benefits of Utilization Equilibria/Tracks

The focus of the study was on the identification and classification of equilibrium states, developing and understanding of transition among equilibrium states over time, and uncovering key change triggers that influenced movement among equilibrium states. Prior to verifying the utility of the theoretical constructs introduced in this initial study, we felt the examination of benefits or impacts of the equilibrium and tracks was premature. Thus, the information collected regarding the perceived benefits was gathered by self-report and not emphasized in this study.

When asked to comment on the benefits received from EDSS use, it is noteworthy that, among the eight companies, companies G and H reported that they had realized greater benefits from EDSS than the companies in low-integration and standard adoption equilibria. Respondents consistently claimed that their work tasks would be difficult, if not impossible, to complete without the use of EDSS. This is evidence of the high degree of integration achieved between the technology and the work process. This further suggests that movement to expanding and high-integration equilibrium states, aided by a high degree of user control over application of the software and redesign of workflow, may increase systemwide benefits through mutual adaptation of information technology and organization context. In other words, giving users more freedom to adjust the features of the software to suit their needs and freedom to modify organizational processes to take advantage of system features may allow organizations to maximize their return on information technology investments.

The achievement of systemwide benefits stemming from expanding or high-integration environments was also noted by Johnson and Rice $[12]$ . Their study showed that word processing centers that experienced adaptations of the innovation after adoption reported more satisfied internal customers. Word processors were able to meet the needs of professionals within the organization more closely and thus the benefits extended beyond those experienced by the immediate clerical user. The extension of the benefits of an innovation to other parts of the organization is an interesting area for potential study.

## Implications for Managing Change Triggers and Transition States

IT IS IMPORTANT TO EMPHASIZE THAT TRANSITION STATES are inherently unstable and do not inevitably result in more favorable equilibria. As observed in this study, change triggers can lead to more effective utilization states or to less effective ones. The theoretical guidance of Gersick [7], Hinings and Greenwood [10], and Johnson and Rice [11], and the results of this study suggest several successful tactics for managing the successful adoption and assimilation of software packages.

## Careful Attention to Initial Implementation Efforts and Activities

Our results underscore the importance of creating a positive initial experience for users of a new software package. While there is a tendency to cut training and associated implementation costs in an effort to reduce software expenses, these actions can result in negative user attitudes and a low-integration equilibrium. Once low-integration equilibrium is established, it can be particularly difficult to transition out of because it requires changing user attitudes. One potentially effective tactic suggested in our data is the assignment of a new employee to the affected work group(s). Another important tactic includes enhanced training on both the packaged system features and related work processes.

## Awareness and Identification of Utilization Equilibria and Transitions

Managers with responsibility for software package implementation should closely monitor utilization of the software and the resulting benefits on an ongoing basis. This study and others $[16]$ point out that utilization does not necessarily remain on a single track over time. Furthermore, periods of transition represent pivotal episodes when managerial actions can strongly influence the establishment of a new equilibrium state. If guided appropriately, transitions can potentially result in increasing benefits.

## Management of Key Events and Change Triggers

By identifying the current equilibrium state and its associated impacts, a manager can assess the need for both proactive and corrective actions. Tyre and Orlikowski [23] suggest that “windows of opportunity” may exist (or be created) during which managers can enhance their organization’s use of technology. Particular attention should be paid to the internal and external events identified in the study that have the greatest possibility of disrupting equilibrium states. Change triggers or potential areas for influence include employee turnover, vendor–user relationships, time for experimentation, and flexibility in work process design, and changes in the business environment. Organizational actions can be taken both to disrupt an existing equilibrium and to guide the change processes during the transition state in the direction desired [14].

## Attention to Utilization Tracks

Managers should be aware of the potential shifts in equilibrium states over time. It is likely that some utilization tracks may evolve that produce greater benefits than others. The experience of companies G and H suggests that the disruption of standard adoption equilibrium and a guided transition to expanded or high-integration states may produce the greatest returns on software investments over time.

## Implications for Future Research

WHILE SUBSTANTIAL EFFORT HAS BEEN EXPENDED ON DETERMINING the conditions under which organizational change occurs and the extent of those changes, little research has sought to define and establish organization archetypes [10]. This situation in management research parallels the substantial effort in IS research that has gone toward the identification of critical success factors in information systems implementation, identification of factors influencing quantitative levels of utilization, and the development of effective implementation processes. The observable outcomes in terms of patterns of information technology utilization and their corresponding benefits have been largely ignored in favor of investigating standardized system success measures such as amount of use and user information satisfaction [6].

In addition to identifying and refining the unique patterns of utilization equilibria, the change triggers that influence movement between them and the nature of the transition state warrants further attention. Although it will be difficult, the most important issue may be identifying the organizational consequences resulting from specific change triggers and utilization tracks, particularly in terms of achieved benefits and returns on information technology investment.

## Conclusion

THE INTRODUCTION OF A NEW INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY, particularly packaged software, represents a potential disruption to the equilibrium state of an existing work environment. The existing work environment is frequently characterized by standard operating procedures, both formal and informal, that guide day-to-day operations. The initial disruption and the consequent attempts to assimilate the new software package into the work environment signal the onset of a transition state during which the system may be reinterpreted within the existing deep structure of the organizational environment. It may also cause users to reinterpret the deep structure through the creation of new practices, procedures, and forms of organization for work.

Based on the experiences of the eight companies reported here, it appears that utilization equilibria emerge as a result of change processes immediately preceding or initiated during a transition state. As the use of system features is reinforced by repeated incorporation of the package in ongoing work processes, utilization patterns stabilize and can be characterized as equilibrium periods. As significant changes occur in appropriation of the software package, the users, or the organization context, the equilibrium is disturbed and a transition state eventually gives way to another equilibrium state. Although we lack conclusive evidence, it seems likely that different equilibrium states result in different performance outcomes.

This study reinforces the idea that the key to successful adoption and assimilation of packaged software lies in understanding and managing the relationship between information technology and organizational context. Specifically, we recommend additional research aimed at identifying utilization equilibrium states, discovering the triggers that give rise to changes in equilibrium states, mapping the utilization tracks that organizations follow over time, and implementing strategies to move organizations along those tracks that ultimately provide the greatest benefit. With additional research, we can develop a more accurate portrait of the dynamics of utilization equilibria, transition states, change triggers, and utilization tracks, and use this knowledge to help maximize returns on IT investment.

## II. Characteristics of Utilization Equilibria States

## Degree of Control:

• Who determines what work tasks you complete and when?

• Who determines how EDSS will be used to complete your work tasks?

\- Would you characterize the degree of control you have over determining and structuring your work tasks as: none, low, medium or high?

\- Would you characterize the degree of control you have over determining how to apply EDSS to your work tasks as: none, low, medium or high?

\- Do you feel you have more control over your work tasks than the use of EDSS?

## Attitude Toward EDSS:

\- Do you feel EDSS is an essential/valuable tool for completing your work tasks?

\- Do you feel comfortable using EDSS to complete your work tasks?

\- Would you characterize the degree of respect you have for EDSS as: none, low, medium or high?

\- Would you characterize your comfort level using EDSS as: none, low, medium or high?

## Training:

\- Describe the level and extent of training you received regarding EDSS.

• What topics were covered in your EDSS training?

\- Were instructions on using the features of EDSS included in your training?

\- Were instructions on incorporating EDSS into your work processes included in your training?

\- Do you feel you are given adequate time to learn the features and uses of EDSS in your work tasks?

## Experimentation:

\- Are you given adequate time to experiment with the different features of EDSS while performing your work tasks?

\- Are you encouraged by your supervisor to experiment with EDSS?

\- Do you feel that your supervisor considers experimentation with EDSS unnecessary or a waste of time?

\- Do you feel that you are expected to experiment with EDSS and find new ways to use it to enhance the performance of your work tasks?

## Communication:

\- Describe your required/expected/necessary communication with your supervisor and peers during the performance of your work tasks.

\- Is communication limited to regularly scheduled staff meetings?

\- Do you feel you have “walk-in/open door” access to your supervisor and coworkers?

Participation:

\- Are you actively involved in decisions about the design of work processes in your area/company?

\- Is participation in the design of work processes required in your job?

\- Is your participation limited to providing direct feedback only when asked?

\- Do you feel you are encouraged/expected to participate in the design of work processes?

## III. Benefits

• How does EDSS help you complete your work tasks?

\- Do you feel it makes you more effective at completing your work tasks?

\- Are you able to complete your tasks with greater speed/accuracy/quality?

\- How do you feel EDSS contributes to the overall performance of your company?
