# Alignment Is Not Enough: Integrating Business and Information Technology Management with the Balanced Business Scorecard

J.T.M. VAN DER ZEE AND BEREND DE JONG

J.T.M. VANDER ZEE is Director of the Nolan Norton Institute, an international research arm of Nolan, Norton and Co. Management Consultants in Utrecht, the Netherlands. He is in charge of thought leadership and innovation of consulting approaches for business strategy, organizational development, and IT strategy and management. Prior to reoining KPMG/Nolan, Norton and Co. in November 1996, he was managing consultant for Nolan, Norton and Co. in Europe and the United States from 1986 until 1991. He also worked for Arthur D. Little and the Index Group/Butler Cox in senior consulting positions. Prior to that, he worked in managerial IT positions for multinational companies. Since 1989, he lectures at the University of Tilburg in the Netherlands on effectiveness and efficiency of IT.

BEREND DE JONG is a Senior Consultant in the Nolan, Norton and Co. office in Melbourne, Australia. He also participates in the research and multiclient program activities of Nolan Norton Institute. His primary research and consulting interests focus on governance and management of information technology, information economics, and sourcing of information technology. He received an M.S. in technology and human affairs from Tilburg University in the Netherlands and Washington University, St. Louis, Missouri.

ABSTRACT: The continuously growing importance of information technology (IT) requires organizations to integrate IT decisions with their common planning and decision-making processes at all organizational levels. Trying to align distinct and separate business with IT management cycles is just not enough. Multilevel, integrated business and IT management are aimed at fully integrating the capabilities of IT with business strategies and management's expectations, and vice versa. Although the need to integrate business and IT strategy has long been advocated, until now a practical framework did not exist.

We seek to contribute to the discipline of IT value management. Our framework includes the planning of and setting goals for IT, and the evaluation of results, integrated with the business context. First approaches to IT planning and evaluation, and their limitations, are discussed. We then elaborate on the concepts of the Balanced Business Scorecard in two case studies. By investigating the benefits and limitations of the framework, and comparing them with other common frameworks, we conclude that the Balanced Business Scorecard can be a valuable contributor to implementation of an integrated business and IT planning and evaluation process.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Balanced Business Scorecard, information technology planning and control, information technology strategy, strategic alignment.

ONE OF THE MOST SIGNIFICANT DRIVERS OF STRATEGIC CHANGE in the world is technological innovation. In particular, the application of innovative information technology (IT) is radically altering the basis of business competition. Today, the benefit of exploiting IT not only relates to making business processes and tasks more efficient. Instead, IT also enables the creation of products, services, distribution channels, and links with customers, suppliers, and other stakeholders. IT is virtually interwoven with almost every aspect of modern organizations, their business network, and their environment as a whole.

A recent survey of sixty-seven senior IT executives from three different continents showed that they perceived aligning IT and corporate goals $[22]$ as their most important task. Business success increasingly depends on the organization's ability to fuse the potential power of IT into the fabric of business processes and business networks. The continuously growing importance of IT requires organizations to integrate IT decisions with their common planning and decision-making processes at all organizational levels. Trying to align distinct and separate business and IT management processes is just not enough! Multilevel integrated business and IT management is aimed at fully integrating the capabilities of IT with business strategies and management's expectations, and vice versa. Although the need to integrate business and IT strategy has long been advocated, until now, no practical framework existed $[9, 10, 15]$ .

We seek to contribute to the discipline of IT value management including the planning of and setting goals for IT, and the evaluation of results, in a business context. To stress that yesterday's approaches of trying to align business and IT decision-making processes really fall short, we first look at the traditional approaches for IT planning and evaluation, and their limitations. We then elaborate on the concepts of the Balanced Business Scorecard. By investigating the benefits and limitations of the framework, and comparing its possibilities with other common frameworks, we conclude that the Balanced Business Scorecard can be a valuable contributor to the implementation of an integrated business and IT planning and evaluation process. Two case studies—one of a bank, one of a food retailer—illustrate the application of this integrated management approach. We conclude with limitations on the use of the Balanced Business Scorecard and suggest directions for further research.

## The Evolution of Business and IT Management

MANAGEMENT APPROACHES, BOTH FOR BUSINESS AND FOR IT, have changed over time. In general, they followed (more or less) the same evolution, although a considerable time lag between new business management approaches and IT management approaches can be observed.

## Evolution of Business Management

For decades, the traditional linear approaches described by, among others, Anthony [2], Fayol [8], and Ansoff [1] dominated the tradition of business management. Traditional approaches define management as a systematic, top-down process of planning, organizing, leading, and controlling the efforts of organization members and of using all other organizational resources (finances, equipment, and information) to achieve stated organizational goals.

During the late 1980s and early 1990s it became clear that traditional planning approaches were too complex, too time-consuming, and in fact ineffective because of the rapidity with which the business environment changes. Top-down, functionally oriented planning processes were replaced by new approaches for business planning and evaluation. These new approaches underpin the notion that strategic thinking develops at any time and at any place in the organization, typically through less concise processes of informal learning, by people at various levels and from different disciplines. Mintzberg [16] stresses a more fluid and broader strategy development approach compared with the traditional planning processes. Management theories that became popular during the late 1990s, such as organizational learning (e.g., Argyris and Schön [3]) and scenario planning (e.g., van der Heijden [23]), confirm this opinion.

Drucker [7], another proponent of modern planning and evaluation approaches, asserts that a business needs a “score to play”: a set of strategic guidelines and performance targets, structured around:

\- Goals that clearly state management's performance expectations for the enterprise and for each part and specialist, and

\- Organized feedback that compares results with these performance expectations.

This idea was further developed by a research group, initiated by the Nolan Norton Institute and consisting of consultants, representatives from a select group of U.S. Fortune 500 companies, and academics. This consortium developed a new framework for business planning and the measurement of business performance, the Balanced Scorecard [18] (see figure 1). Kaplan and Norton [12] later described the model as “a set of measures that gives top managers a fast but comprehensive view of the business. The Balanced Scorecard includes financial measures that tell the results of actions already taken. It complements the financial measures with operational measures on customer satisfaction, internal processes, and the organization’s innovation and improvement activities—operational measures that are the drivers of the future financial performance.”

The Balanced Scorecard development was triggered by a general unhappiness with traditional (financial) performance measures. Traditional measures, such as quarterly sales and operating income, were only looking at the past, not the future. With the shareholder value analysis, where future cash flows are discounted to calculate a current value, management was able to start looking forward. The problem with the financial measures was that the final result of the many activities undertaken by an organization still remained. By including perspectives of the customer, internal processes, and the organization's innovation and improvement activities, management is more capable of tracking the whole series of causes and results of actions ultimately leading to the firms' (financial) success. Since its publication [18] in 1991, the scorecard has been widely adopted. A practical, simplified example of how an actual scorecard might look is shown in Table 1. The example is a scorecard for a software company.

## Evolution of IT Management

Because business success increasingly depends on IT-enabled processes, products, and services, it is important to link business goals and the potential IT contribution. The art and science of IT planning and evaluation followed more or less the same patterns and stages of evolution of business planning and evaluation $[24]$ . However, a rigid distinction has always been made between the demand side of IT (the business side focusing on the application of IT) and the supply side of IT (the technical side focusing on the delivery of the IT as requested by the demand side). This distinction is still valid, but the way each side is managed is evolving in different directions.

## IT Management—Demand Side

IT management on the demand side is concerned with the overall direction of the application of IT within an organization. The term “IT planning,” as part of the overall IT management cycle, has been used to indicate the planning activities following business initiatives, to determine IT requirements in broad terms.

Traditionally, IT requirements were defined to prepare the organization to meet its short-term (1–2 years), medium-term (2–4 years), and long-term (5–7 years) IT needs. Traditional top-down methods, like Business Systems Planning (BSP), share three basic sequential steps.

The first step is to document the business vision, mission, objectives, structure, processes and/or functions. In line with this, Rockart [21] and others [9, 4] have applied the critical success factor (CSF), planning, to derive a clear understanding of the information requirements of the chief executive officer. Rockart defined CSFs as “those few critical areas where things must go right for business to flourish.” CSFs are derived from goals of business management and thereby intended to align the business and IT domains.

The second step is to derive an information strategy: the determination of the objectives, strategy, policies, functions, and organizational aspects for the development and acquisition of information systems and technical facilities for IT. A typical example of this process is described by King [15].

The third step is to define the IT architecture—the structure of IT, consisting of high-level blueprints for key IT categories: information systems, data models, hardware, and communication networks.

The most important goal of IT planning has always been alignment of IT capabilities and activities with business objectives and business requirements, including decision making on the scope, scale, and pace of IT projects. However, alignment of IT with business objectives requires a stable, robust, and relatively homogeneous business-oriented foundation in the form of a well-accepted and committed business plan. Unfortunately, the availability and reliability of such business plans are questionable at best in most organizations. Even if they are available, business plans are often obsolete once the IT planner receives them. The time lag between business planning processes and IT planning processes is often just too long to be acceptable. Second, our experience has taught us that defining an IT strategy and IT architecture is not primarily a rational, analytical, and certainly not a technical activity. Potential success is highly dependent on the communication skills of all involved. In many organizations, business managers and IT planners have shown that they are unable to express themselves in a common language, necessary to cope with the absence of rationality and analyzability, so that successful links between business objectives, the IT strategy, and the IT architecture were insufficiently built.

The recognition of the lack of alignment between business and IT planning processes is analyzed by Henderson and Venkatraman $[10]$ , who propose to use the strategic alignment model, where two types of integration are identified: strategic integration and operational integration. Strategic integration deals with the capability of IT functionality to both shape and support business strategy. Operational integration deals with the linkage between organizational infrastructure and processes, and IT infrastructure and processes. Henderson and Venkatraman then describe four different types of relationships that can exist within their framework. Although they do recognize the need for integration, they do not provide a practical framework to implement this. Broadbent and Weill $[5]$ use a more specific alignment model, which is consistent with the model of Henderson and Venkatraman. They identify organizational practices that contribute to alignment and outline a series of questions with which managers can review the practices of the firm. One of their conclusions is that “the most effective management of I/S occurred when these resources were managed by those closest to the business need,” but unfortunately Broadbent and Weill do not provide a practical framework to implement this either.

So the traditional IT planning methods that share the underlying assumption that IT planning is aimed at merely translating an organization's business strategy and business objectives into a structure and plan for IT are obsolete. The conventional top-down IT planning process as well as alignment efforts need to move toward a more iterative, goal-oriented, evolutionary, and continuous process of organizational learning and some degree of experimentation. Such an IT planning process should be much more dynamic and interactive, allowing for the incorporation of good ideas that bubble up from the operational level and making sure that planning results are understood early on by making the deliverables visible and tangible. The exemplar process of developing an IT architecture is typically iterative. Although it should start with the business vision, the developed models are made possible, even suggested, by emerging technologies. During the process, the model is continuously tested and enhanced by reviewing whether the advantages of appropriate technological opportunities are included.

## IT Management—Supply Side

Traditionally, IT management on the supply side is concerned with efforts directed at the planning, coordination, and control of IT supply within an organization. It manages the objectives, directions, guidelines, performance targets, and organizational structures for operational supply and management of IT products and services. IT services, in this context, include the management of IT infrastructures and data centers, IT development projects, implementation projects, and maintenance of IT. A mix of different planning and evaluation approaches for effective management of different types of IT supply (development, maintenance, operations) can be observed. Most recent improvements in the planning of IT supply have been aimed at aligning business and IT issues, meeting agreed deadlines, and thus reducing the uncertainty of results delivery, cost reduction, and improved quality levels of IT supply. For example, to manage the data center, Service Level Agreements (SLAs) are put into place. SLAs include a set of performance measures such as applications availability, timeliness and accuracy, responsiveness of user support, and cost of operation. The purpose of an SLA is to provide a basis for the planning and objective assessment of operations performance and to establish clear procedures for problem resolution. Performance targets are accompanied by standards or criteria for success and specific measures.

Another example is the management of IT development projects. Conventional sequential “waterfall” application development processes have gradually been replaced by the more iterative and continuous process of evolutionary development. The planning practices for development projects include the regular setting of commonly agreed and predefined time frames (time-boxes) for the delivery and implementation of subsequent versions of the application being built. In addition, to manage the maintenance of IT applications, organizations tend to formalize and organize their work around “releases.” Releases contain the modifications and enhancements to applications, grouped together as a result of the assembly of change requests over a given period of time. Releases are implemented according to predefined time intervals that have been agreed upon by both business and IT supply representatives.

## Toward Integrated Business and IT Management

Today's planning for IT is becoming less rigid, less sequential, and less elaborate than traditional IT planning approaches. The first signs of integrated business and IT management are becoming visible. While IT might follow an organization's strategic direction, at the same time, the business strategy can also be led by IT opportunities. An integrated business and IT planning process needs to be carried out by those who understand the strategic direction of the company, who are deeply involved with the business issues at hand, who are able to think in terms of business results, and who understand the capabilities of today's technology.

In our opinion, in line with the analyses of Mintzberg and Drucker, organizations need fluid management processes, performance goals, and feedback mechanisms, covering both business goals and the potential role of IT, and need to include them in an integrated set of Balanced Business Scorecards that cover both.

However, there is one exception. In terms of the integration of IT into business management processes, we suggest that one specific aspect of IT supply be excluded: the management of IT infrastructures. In particular, the management of associated central, shared, capital-intensive, and vital resources with emphasis on efficiency and reliability, such as data centers, networks, and end-user computing services require special treatment because their role cannot be derived directly from business and functional requirements. Management of an IT infrastructure, meeting the demands for continuously accessible connectivity, as expressed by Keen and Cummins [14], calls for a more rigorous, structured, and detailed planning and control approach. Planning and control processes for IT infrastructure focus on formal standards and minimizing risk of failure. The management of IT infrastructures should be performed by IT specialists who understand the capabilities, risks, and constraints of today's technologies. Integrating this part of IT-management into business planning processes would only burden the business with undesired and unnecessary technical issues.

## How Can the Balanced Scorecard Contribute to IT Management and Solve Problems?

WE HAVE ADDRESSED TWO MAIN PROBLEMS IN BUSINESS AND IT MANAGEMENT. The first problem was the time lag between business and IT planning processes. The second was the lack of a common “language” between business and IT management. We propose to use the Balanced Business Scorecard to tackle both problems. The benefits of using the Balanced Business Scorecard approach, in contrast to traditional methods, are:

\- Business and IT management can use the same “performance measurement” language, thereby integrating IT planning and evaluation fully into the business context. The Balanced Scorecard introduces overall goals and quantified norms for the whole business including IT.

\- Integrating the business and IT management processes eliminates, or at least considerably reduces, the time lag between the two.

The benefits of successfully deploying the Balanced Business Scorecard approach over other alignment methods are described below.

## IT Can Be Managed Using an Integrated Planning and Evaluation Cycle

When building scorecards, one needs to identify business goals and the (potential) drivers of business success. When all relevant drivers contributing to business goals have been identified, measures (or norms) per driver have to be determined. The Balanced Scorecard therefore introduces overall goals and quantified norms, including those for IT. Subsequently, these goals and norms can be used to prioritize investment plans. Investment plans in this way are based on business goals in which IT is fully integrated; there are no longer separate business and IT plans. As a consequence, separate IT investment plans will no longer exist when the Balanced Scorecard is used as a management system.

Management can use exactly the same goals and norms during the development of projects and the operational use of IT systems to evaluate how well things are going. By measuring the realization of common business and IT goals, it is possible to measure the contribution of IT to the business. A beneficial side-effect of the use of the Balanced Scorecard is that, when all measures are being reported, one can calculate the strength of relations between the value drivers. For example, if the relation between high software development costs and high profit levels is weak for a long time, one might infer that the developed software does not sufficiently contribute to results as expressed by other (e.g., financial) performance measures.

Business and IT Management Can Use the Same

"Performance Measurement" Language

"The language of upper management is money," said Juran [11] long ago. Kaplan and Norton [13] state that a measurement system should make the relationships among objectives in all of the four perspectives (financial, customer, internal/business process, and learning and growth) explicit so that they can be managed and validated. With the general acceptance of a broader range of business performance measures, and especially the Balanced Business Scorecard, a new language is born. This language enables business and IT management to think together about what IT can do to support business performance in terms of cause and effect. It overcomes the myopia of people who claim that all IT investments should directly result in positive financial results; they also can contribute to other, nonfinancial goals. It also guarantees that IT specialists do not develop a language of their own because discussions and communication about IT management are not limited to people in the IT function. Integration of the management cycles of business executives and IT specialists can only become reality when a common and broadly accepted management (and for that matter, communication) approach is adopted. The application of an integrated Balanced Business Scorecard meets that requirement.

In conclusion, there are at least two major benefits of totally integrating business and IT management processes. The most important benefit is that integration will lead to shared visions, harmonized action, and therefore more effective business solutions. By teaming up with the business, IT professionals will be in a better position to spot business opportunities and to deliver solutions that really contribute to business goals. The second benefit of integration is that IT functionality can be planned more quickly, and therefore the time to market of business changes will be shorter because planning cycles do not have to wait for one another. The next section describes two case studies that demonstrate that the benefits of an integrated management approach are attainable, but only if the prerequisites, as described above, are met.

## Case Studies: Companies That Integrated Business and IT Management by Balanced Business Scorecards

WE NOW LOOK AT THE PRACTICAL USE OF THE Balanced Business Scorecard via two examples of companies that are dealing with a business in transformation through a major impact of IT. We demonstrate how these companies, which initiated and successfully used the integrated Balanced Business Scorecard, have met their strategic goals. The cases establish the importance of integrating IT planning and evaluation into the process from strategy building to strategy implementation.

## Case 1: A Small European Bank

With fewer than 1,200 employees, this bank is a small player in the European market. The bank is traditionally organized, with a long history dating back to the beginning of this century. For decades, the bank has serviced a group of very loyal customers in a small regional area.

Due to increased competition and new products offered by other banks and insurance companies, the bank is losing customers and market share. The formerly loyal customers are starting to experiment with electronic banking, which is only provided by competitors. Some of these customers have actually started to cancel their relationship with the bank. Operational costs, especially the costs of personnel, are still rising, while revenue is dropping. The board needs to make a radical decision to survive.

## Case 2: A National Food Retailer

The food retailer is a national leader in the Netherlands, with about 16,000 employees. The supermarkets are known by customers for their high-quality service and products, for which customers are willing to pay a (small) premium. Business is going well, but management concludes from an external consultant's study that they are not using all the IT opportunities their (smaller) competitors are taking advantage of.

In both cases management is about to introduce a new strategy for the company. In both cases IT plays an important, if not an essential, role.

The management of the bank was prompted by the realization that it had failed to recognize the opportunities of electronic banking. The bank was approximately two years behind the competition. While proper use of IT might be the potential strategic solution for this bank, today, the lack of it is the bank's major problem. Maintenance of back-office legacy applications takes up more than 75 percent of the system development resources, leaving insufficient room for the development of new applications. The complexity of the old applications (and thus the associated maintenance and exploitation costs), and the lack of resources for new development, are important causes for the growing financial problems.

The national retailer wanted to use Balanced Business Scorecards to guarantee an integrated business and IT management approach to select and implement new systems. For the food retailer, IT is an important item on the management agenda because it represents new business solutions in the supply and delivery chains, such as Efficient Consumer Response (ECR), Category Management, and Customer Loyalty Programs.

## The Process of Building a Scorecard

It is obvious that before one can start to build an integrated scorecard, commitment from all stakeholders and support within the organization must be established. The role of the IT organization in the process of building a scorecard is not different from that of any other important department such as marketing, sales, finance, production, distribution, logistics, and the like. The initiative to develop integrated Balanced Business Scorecards will therefore seldom be taken by the IT department. The (business) unit as a whole must recognize the need and value of developing Balanced

Business Scorecards. Project teams consisting of representatives from all disciplines involved need to be formed.

If management has decided to adopt the scorecard approach, the vision of the business needs to be translated into action. For this, three different questions need to be answered:

1. Whom do we want to be?

2. How will we get there and what goals do we have to achieve?

3. What do we have to measure?

## Step 1: Whom Do We Want to Be?

In this first step, management addresses the future identity of the business, covering issues such as markets, products, services, and distribution channels. As stated earlier, business success increasingly depends on the organization's ability to fuse the potential power of IT into the business. The role of IT should therefore be an integral part of the strategy, rather than an ex-ante IT strategy drill.

To define an organization's strategy, one can use many methods [6, 17, 19, 20]. Strategy formulation methods tend to focus on one of three perspectives:

\- Customer/market positioning as proposed by Porter [19];

\- Competitive positioning as proposed by D'Aveni [6];

\- Core competencies as proposed by Prahalad and Hamel [20].

Rather than elaborating on the pros and cons of each of these, we focus on the strategy process, rather than on the strategy method, for the remainder of this article.

## Case 1: A Small European Bank, Developing the New Strategy

After long discussions and thorough internal and external information gathering, the board decided that new directions for the bank needed to focus on two strategic areas. The first was cost leadership. To establish cost leadership, it was necessary to bring down operational costs. High costs were caused by relatively high personnel numbers and a low degree of automation of business processes. The second area of strategic focus was their distribution channel strategy; customers, including the more aged ones, were becoming familiar with telephone banking and Internet banking for handling their financial affairs, instead of going to the nearest bank office.

The board started off their strategy formulation process by organizing a series of intensive meetings in which all board members participated. During this process, the role of the CIO (chief information officer), who was not a member of the board, was to provide most of the figures about potential cost savings by process automation and best-practice case descriptions in electronic distribution channels. She was not involved in any decision making, however.

## Case 2: A National Food Retailer, Developing the New Strategy

To retain customer loyalty and to draw new customers into the supermarkets, management decided to transform the firm from a “distribute and sell” organization to a

“sense and respond” retailer. The opportunity to automatically replenish new supplies when an item has been checked out at the cash registers, and to start tracking what customers buy (per individual buyer!) was seen as a competitive necessity.

In this case, business and IT management developed the new strategy together in a two-day session. An external consultant provided benchmark figures, quantitative analysis, and facilitated the lively strategy formulation process.

As already mentioned, the strategy formulation process of the small European bank was basically executed by the management board, without extensive participation of the CIO, whose role was limited to a more or less supportive one. Nevertheless, the results were crystal clear and accurate, thanks to the fact that the board members were able to ask the right questions that the CIO could understand. She, as a result, could gather the appropriate information and provide the board with the right and, for them, understandable answers. This illustrates that a strong CIO participation in strategy formulation is not mandatory, as long as those who formulate the strategy are sufficiently aware of the potential impact of IT, and, in the case where they need more information, are able to talk to specialists using a common language.

The strategy process of the retailer was a much more integrated one. Both business and IT consequences of the new “sense and respond” strategy were discussed and taken into consideration, in real time during the workshop, by business and IT representatives. All IT implications from the strategy were clear from the start to business management, while IT management understood the importance of required IT solutions from a business perspective.

## Step 2: How Will We Get There and What Goals Do We Have to Achieve?

Once the strategy has been formulated, an even more difficult step needs to be taken: to define and describe how to migrate from the current situation to the desired future situation and how to convince all stakeholders involved that the change is necessary. This is where the Balanced Business Scorecard also can help.

## Case 1: A Small European Bank, Designing the Scorecards

The board first identified a number of change agents: managers who would be responsible for the initiation and implementation of the new strategy. These business managers were sent on a week-long intensive training course. This course provided them with examples of how some competitors had organized their processes and how contemporary IT could work for the bank in terms of introducing new distribution channels and optimizing process automation. Immediately after this course, the selected managers were asked to develop an overall scorecard, resulting in the scorecard shown in figure 2.

The strategy implementation of the bank clearly shows a participative rollout. Managers were given a lot of freedom to design what they thought were the best ways to cut costs of current operations and to develop new distribution channels on top of the existing ones.

The cause-and-effect scheme that was used at the bank was central to their discussion. By focusing on relationships that the bank could influence, and supported by new insights into examples of new distribution channels, business management was able to generate a concept in which business and IT were very much interwoven. The same concept would later be used to measure and validate the realization of the business results being sought. By the way, the CIO of the bank was still not involved in the process at this point. Despite her absence, a scheme had been developed that appeared to be satisfying to the board.

## Case 2: A National Food Retailer, Designing the Scorecards

At the food retailer, the board appointed five pilot outlets. The management teams of these outlets, each supported by two IT professionals with ample experience in logistical and retail information systems, were commissioned to identify the drivers of what would eventually lead to being a “sense and respond” retailer. After two weeks, the five teams reported back to the board, using Balanced Business Scorecards, about what they regarded as the main (potential) contributors to the realization of the retailers’ strategy. The five results were blended together during a joint workshop, resulting in the scheme as shown in figure 3.

The cases illustrate how the Balanced Business Scorecard approach can be used as a framework for discussion. When the overall strategy has been formulated, the Balanced Business Scorecard cause-and-effect relationships guarantee that cross-functional discussions are always pointing toward realization of a common business strategy. In addition, these discussions provide IT management with an opportunity to pinpoint the value of IT in business terms, while business executives are made aware that there is more to making profit than cash flow alone.

## Step 3: What Do We Have to Measure?

Goals themselves should consist of a mix of outcome measures and performance drivers. The scorecard should be the translation of the organization's strategy into a linked set of measures that define both the long-term strategic objectives, as well as the mechanisms for achieving those objectives [13]. The old adage, “if you cannot measure it, you cannot manage it,” explains why identifying the goals alone, as in step 2, is not enough. Therefore, the measures attached to the goals must be made quantifiable. The two examples below illustrate how this can be done.

## Case 1: A Small European Bank, Defining the Measures

Using the developed Balanced Business Scorecard, the board added target measures to selected (high-level) performance indicators. They decided, for example, that operational costs would have to go down by 25 percent; more than 10 percent of sales had to come through new distribution channels within three years; employees would be granted a percentage of actual cost reduction realized as an incentive, and so on. Department managers had to translate the general scorecard into specific scorecards for their departments. The managers' appraisals were directly linked to their measured performance.

## Case 2: A National Food Retailer, Defining the Measures

The targets of the high-level corporate scorecard were made more specific for headquarters' functions, distribution centers, and outlets. Two teams were added to the five pilot teams: one to deal with scorecards for the distribution centers and one to develop the headquarters' scorecards. The newly assigned teams consisted of employees from different disciplines. All teams had to set quantified targets related to their specific situation. Representatives from the IT-department took part in all teams. The whole process resulted in seven different scorecards and associated quantified measures, which were used to measure the progress of the strategy implementation. ## Evaluating the Results of the Two Cases

The application of the approach provided both the bank and the retailer with the possibility to analyze their current position and set business and IT directions in a rapid, synchronized way:

\- The business and IT strategy definition processes were better integrated with each other than before, in the opinion of the boards of the two organizations.

\- The relative speed of the strategy definition processes was definitely much faster than before at both organizations.

\- The integration of both planning definition processes led to much better awareness and application of the power of IT for business solutions compared with previous attempts.

\- At the food retailer, the integrated approach added a good deal to the motivation of both business and IT managers to work with each other during the implementation phase, mainly because of the common frames of reference and uniform language, developed during the planning process.

This last point needs some clarification. While the food retailer benefited from the chemistry of business and IT people working together, the bank did not accomplish such results to the same extent. At the bank, the scorecard approach was clearly driven by the board and business people, without much involvement of the CIO or IT professionals. Although the scorecard development process delivered good results, the absence of the CIO and IT professionals led to some unanticipated problems along the implementation road. What happened? During the strategy implementation phase, it became evident that the migration from an old transaction application toward a new one, necessary to meet stated objectives, had been estimated not to take very long. This was clearly induced by the opinion of some business executives that the replacement of a core information system could be done almost overnight. This ignorance resulted in a program delay of more than one year, so that the expected cost savings were not established according to schedule. The case of the bank clearly shows that the use of the scorecard framework alone is no guarantee for harvesting benefits from otherwise satisfying planning results.

## Limitations of the Balanced Scorecard and Further Research

ALTHOUGH THE APPLICATION OF AN INTEGRATED Balanced Business and IT Scorecard approach may seem easy, there are a few important limitations to consider. Kaplan and Norton [13] identified four specific barriers to effective Balanced Scorecard implementation that have to be overcome:

\- Visions and strategies that are not actionable;

• Strategies that are not linked to departmental, team, and individual goals;

\- Strategies that are not linked to long- and short-term resource allocation;

\- Feedback that is tactical, not strategic.

These barriers need to be recognized and addressed before any Balanced Scorecard implementation can proceed, because the Balanced Business Scorecard does not strictly enforce an integration of business and IT management processes by itself. Each barrier must be surmounted, especially when the application of an integrated Business and IT Scorecard is to be used as the cornerstone of a new strategic system for integrated business and IT management. In the case where an organization is not able to overcome these barriers, it is too immature to really integrate IT into the business. Starting the implementation of an integrated Balanced Business Scorecard can then be a waste of time and money, unfortunately.

Using the Balanced Business Scorecard approach for integrating business and IT management underlines the conditions that will need to be met, as identified by Kaplan and Norton. These conditions are:

\- Business and IT specialists must be aware of what they can do and what they want to do with IT, from a business perspective. They must be willing and able to share their frames of reference in order to attain common and actionable views on the business driven use of IT.

\- Business and IT specialists must be responsive to each other, and must be able to speak in a common language about the business-driven application of IT. They must be willing and able to translate business and IT visions and strategies into terms that can be understood and acted upon by all. They also must be willing and able to provide the feedback on how strategies are being implemented and whether they are working.

\- Responsibilities and authorities of business and IT specialists must be unambiguously defined and communicated in order to avoid misunderstanding about what can be expected from one another. Business and IT visions and strategies must be translated into goals for departments, teams, and individuals, and then acted upon by linking action programs and resource allocation to longer-term strategic priorities, rather than just short-term, operational performance.

Again, the Balanced Business Scorecard does not strictly enforce an integration of business and IT management just by itself. The integration sought will only result when the willingness and culture exist for business managers to share information about the business planning and for IT managers to share parts of their former authority. Second, the skills of the people who will define and use an integrated Balanced Business and IT Scorecard need to be appropriate. Third, formal responsibility for IT demand-side management is the last crucial point for a integrated management approach to succeed.

Therefore, further research should try to explicate clearly the prerequisites for integrated business and IT management in terms of culture, skills, and responsibilities. Other future challenges are a clear definition and management framework for the interface between the business and the IT supply side, and the identification of successful integrated management cases, where the results will be measured per industry rather than in the functional IT perspective.

## Conclusion

TRADITIONAL, TOP-DOWN METHODS OF BUSINESS AND IT planning, like BSP, generally fail because they are too rigid. Traditional approaches have proven to be long overdue in the business environment; the same is true for the IT environment. Attempts to align the worlds of IT with the world of business have not been successful for two reasons. First is because of the time lag between separate business and IT planning processes. Business planning processes, as well as IT planning processes and alignment efforts, just take too much time and can easily suffer from too great a gap in time between them. For an organization to outperform its competitors, time is critical. In today's competitive and dynamic business environment, there is no time for lengthy alignment exercises: real-time integration of IT into the business planning process should be the solution. Through integration of business and IT planning cycles, an organization can fully grasp the opportunities of IT for optimal business performance. The second reason is that business executives and IT specialists have fundamental problems understanding each other; their language is too different. Through alignment of business and IT planning processes, one only defines the interface between business and IT, but in essence “never the twain shall meet.” Only by using a common management language is real integration possible.

For the greater part of IT management activities, there is no longer a need to regard business and IT as two separate worlds. The two case studies confirm that the integrated Balanced Business Scorecard approach, if applied intelligently and with awareness of its limitations, can successfully integrate business and IT planning processes.
