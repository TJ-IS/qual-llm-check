# Exploring Mediation Between Environmental and Structural Attributes: The Penetration of Communication Technologies in Manufacturing Organizations

CHOONG C. LEE AND VARUN GROVER

ABSTRACT: The relationship between organizational structures and information technology (IT) has been the subject of much discussion in IS research. While studies have not yielded conclusive results, the importance of examining the relationship between structure and technology is increasing in an environment where organizations are using contemporary IT to redesign themselves in order to compete more effectively. This paper presents a study that examines the relationship between the use of an important class of IT, communications technologies, and organizational structural attributes within a broad contingency context. Hypotheses are proposed, based on theory from the information-processing paradigm examining the mediating role of communications technologies (CT) in the relationship between environmental characteristics and organizational structural characteristics. Data from 153 manufacturing firms are collected and analyzed. The results show that CT seems to play a direct role in reinforcing structures that emerge from environmental dictates. The expanded set of variables considered in this study and the results provide potentially strong implications for future work in this important area.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: communication technologies, environmental characteristics, mediation, organizational structure, telecommunications.

RECENTLY, THERE HAS BEEN CONSIDERABLE INTEREST IN THE REENGINEERING of business structures. Much of the discussion has involved the role of information technology (IT) in such efforts $[14, 30, 80]$ . While many prominent IS researchers have been optimistic about the promise of IT to cope with the structural changes $[7, 33, 34, 37, 44, 46, 67]$ , there has also been a great deal of debate in the IS literature over the relationship between IT and organization structure $[2, 24, 61, 72]$ . Commenting on the fragmentary and mixed evidence of the previous “IT impact” research, George and King $[24]$ argue that many of the musings are “speculative” and rely on the license of fiction or popular journalism. They go on to say that these studies “avoid the difficult question of what is actually happening as a result of computerization.... The systematic studies required to answer the question have not been undertaken, so there is little empirical evidence to go on” $[p. 64]$ .

In an attempt to distinguish this study from the relatively long accumulated research tradition in studying the relationship between IT and organizational structure two fundamental changes in assumptions are made. First, it is assumed that the contemporary IT that proliferated from 1990 is different, with a prominent role played by communication technologies (CT) as opposed to more traditional computing technologies. In 1987 there was a PC for every thirty employees in Fortune 1,000 companies, while in 1994 the ratio was 1:6 [31]. During this period, there has been tremendous growth in local area networks, groupware, and open architectures. We expect that these technologies would have a more profound and direct effect on structural attributes than the data-processing technologies that typified prior studies. While evidence of this is sparse, some recent studies have demonstrated a more direct relationship between IT metrics and metrics representing organizational structure [21, 31, 71]. Second, CT variables would intervene and mediate the contingent relationship between exogenous variables such as environment and organizational structure. In other words, the environmental changes manifest structural changes that are accomplished through deployment of CT. To our knowledge, no study has specifically studied the mediating effect of CT.

Based on these assumptions, this study proposes and tests a research model that examines the effects of communications technology on the relationship between environment and organizational structure. The objective of this line of research is to provide empirical evidence, albeit descriptive, that expands on the literature base in this area and contributes to our understanding of the complex relationships between the use of more contemporary IT and organizational structures. Specifically, the research question can be stated as:

To what extent does communications technology mediate (or reinforce) the relationship between environmental attributes and organizational structural attributes?

The following two sections summarize IT impact research and examine some of the assumptions made. This is followed by a discussion of the research model including the key variables being examined and their proposed relationships. Hypotheses are justified based on the information-processing paradigm. The methodology section describes the research approach, sample, variable measurement and validation. The final sections include presentation and discussion of results.

## IT Impact Research

TRADITIONALLY, EVEN IN THE EARLY YEARS OF DATA PROCESSING, researchers considered IT as a major organizational determinant, affecting structural design in a way similar to that of organizational size, production technology, and environment [24]. However, early empirical findings from traditional IT impact research were disappointing, possibly because of the back-room function of IT at a time when it was too early to treat IT as a major determinant of organizational process or structure [2, 24, 72]. Recently, the contingency view in IT impact research has gained greater popularity in IS research [86]. It defies the traditional “IT-centered” research trend in studying a bivariate relationship between computing technology and organizational structure. This view also examines the relationship in a broader organizational context [24, 26, 56]. These studies argue that the relationship is not a simple causal one that can be identified according to whether the technology drives the change (technological imperative) or whether it is the organization (organizational imperative) [24, 63]. Rather, “the relationship is filtered through an organization’s history, context, power structure, management intention and environment” [24, p. 70], and consideration of these variables can lead to more robust and consistent results.

Hunt and Newell [37] recognize IT's supporting role and suggest the importance of considering IT as a moderating variable between contextual variables and structure. Robey [72] also views computing technology as a moderating variable, affecting the strength of a causal relationship between environmental uncertainty and organization structure. His empirical research shows that, depending on the environmental conditions, computers can either facilitate or constrain the centralization of decision making in an organization. Gurbaxani and Whang [26] state that the impact of IT on organizational centralization is not definitive and depends on other organizational and environmental factors such as organizational culture, managerial intention, and the role of IS in the firm.

Therefore, following these perspectives, one can argue that we need to expand the study of IT impact research by considering people, structure, environment, and task within a broader organizational context.

## Conventional and Contemporary IT: C² Technologies

ONE OF THE MAJOR RESEARCH PROBLEMS IN STUDYING THE LINK BETWEEN IT and organization structure relates to the conceptual issue of how to treat IT as an organizational variable [50, 72, 73]. Robey [72] raises this problem and suggests that a categorical approach can reflect the more specific nature of IT. Huber [33] drew a distinction between two categories of IT, computing and communications technology, which he collectively referred to as "C-squared" technology. According to his definition of C-squared technology, computing technology is a combination of MIS, knowledge systems, and DSS, while communications technology entails any communication-related technology [33], such as local area networks (LAN), wide area networks (WAN), electronic mail systems, voice mail systems, radio-phones, video-text, voice mail, and electronic conferencing.

Distinguishing between two parts of a traditionally monolithic IT raises a legitimate question: Is communications technology fundamentally different in importance or impact from other aspects of computing technology? Keen [38] states three points of difference between the two: (1) communications technology is interlinked with factors of business change that in themselves are new and complex; (2) communications technology is largely an enabling technology providing an information highway system; and (3) the technology and its economics are evolving so rapidly in some areas that in a very short time, new uses and impacts on organization and work quickly emerge [38]. By introducing a “critical mass” explanation for the diffusion of communications technology, Markus [55] also defines two characteristics not shared by the diffusion of computing technologies. First, widespread usage demands almost universal access, creating a public good that individuals cannot be prevented from enjoying even if they have not contributed to it. Second, use of communications technology entails reciprocal interdependence, in which earlier users are influenced by later users as well as vice versa [55].

Many researchers now propose that advances in communications (e.g., intranets) technology $[41, 68]$ revolutionize the way people work, think, and make decisions. Benjamin, Rockart, Scott-Morton, and Wyman $[4]$ already predicted that the changing role of communications technology combined with advanced decision-making technology will be an important driving force for the increasing volume of information in the firm. Similarly, Sullivan and Smart $[78]$ indicate that, with increasing need for cohesion and integration, companies are turning to communications as a central force for the future. While Keen $[39]$ points out that communications technology already affects the core of organizations, he emphasizes the need to study how (and if) communications technology can be expected to change organization structure and processes.

Despite the increasing diffusion of communications technology [25], empirical research in this area is still in its embryonic stages. Probably the most important conceptual studies to date have been done by Huber [32, 33, 34, 35, 36] and Keen [38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. Huber [34] describes how changes in environment can cause changes in organizational capabilities such as decision making, intelligence, and organizational design, and how communications technology affects this adaptation process. His other studies focus on those technology-facilitated changes in organizational design that affect the quality and timeliness of intelligence and decision making [34, 36]. Keen [39, 41] also argues that the changes in the decision-making structure and the level of participation in decision making can result from the diffusion of communications technology. He indicates that communications technology allows organizations to gain benefits of both centralization and decentralization. Further, organizational complexity such as complex managerial layers, administrative overhead, and formal procedures can be simplified by employing communications technology [39, 40, 41]. Also, Hitt and Bryjolfsson [31] argue that recent developments in IT are novel, consistently delivered by the computer industry, and coincide with emergence of network structures, providing at least circumstantial evidence of a more proactive role for these technologies. They provide preliminary empirical evidence for a relationship between network nodes, among other IT measures, and structural decentralization.

From the foregoing overview, it becomes evident that, to better understand the interaction of IT and organizations, (1) the IT concept needs to be expanded to explicitly examine communications technologies, and (2) IT needs to be understood as an element of broader organizational contingencies. Contemporary literature provides insights into the limitations of prior conceptualizations of IT impact research, which narrowly focus on the deterministic nature of the IT-organization relationship [88]. Newer perspectives encourage investigations of the ongoing interaction between IT and organization that “seek patterns across certain contexts and certain types of IT, rather than abstract, deterministic relationships that transcend socio-historical settings, technology, and intentions” [62, p. 423]. Also, the distinctive characteristics and usage of communications technology can be distinguished from those of general computing technology. In studying the linkage between organizations and CT, we argue that it is important to recognize this distinctiveness to provide a better understanding of the unique impacts of these technologies on organizations.

## Research Model

AS INDICATED EARLIER, THIS STUDY ADOPTS A BROADER PERSPECTIVE on IT impacts by investigating (a) the impact of environmental contingencies on structural characteristics of the organization and (b) the role of communications technology in mediating these relationships. Classical causal links between perceived environmental characteristics and organizational structure are examined. The information-processing paradigm [22, 23] and Huber's [34] theory of the effects of advanced communications technology on organizational design is especially useful in deriving hypotheses. Below, the variables selected for the study are presented. Most of these are based on classical organizational theory. This is followed by presentation of the research model and hypotheses.

## Research Variables

Three categories of research variables are discussed: the environment, organizational structure, and CT. In the environmental category, two variables from Duncan's [18] seminal work, environmental dynamism and environmental complexity are included. Environmental dynamism, as defined by Duncan [17, 18] and Miller and Friesen [58], refers to the rate of change in the environment and the unpredictability of environmental changes. Environmental complexity refers to the variety of external forces with which the organization should interact. These two characteristics are most often discussed in the information-processing paradigm [35] and are widely used in investigating the process through which the environment influences an organization's structure, especially its information processing or communication [35]. Collectively, these two variables are strongly related to Perceived Environmental Uncertainty (PEU) [18].

One classic depiction of organizational structure is the organic versus mechanistic dichotomy $[6]$ . There is also some agreement, despite difficulties in operationalization, that centralization, complexity, and formalization are the major theoretical dimensions of organizational structure $[28, 29]$ . Centralization refers to the extent to which the locus of decision making is located at the top level; hence the lack of participation in decision making available at the lower level of organizational hierarchy. Formalization is defined as both the degree of job codification and the degree of rule observation $[27]$ . Formalization denotes the extent to which the roles and activities of the various organizational actors are clearly documented and reported by way of written rules and procedures. Since rules and regulations are the organizational mechanisms that are used to ensure the uniformity of the business process, formalization guides business process in the standard execution of the job assignment $[69]$ . Structural complexity refers to the degree to which the different subunits/functions are distinguished with respect to goals, task orientation, time horizon, and degree of autonomy $[29, 45]$ . From another related perspective, complexity can also refer to the number and variety of different market strategies, technologies, and products with which the organization interacts $[69]$ . The terms structural complexity and complexity are used interchangeably throughout the study and are distinct from environmental complexity. It has been shown that the organic/mechanistic dichotomy can be explained by these three variables $[27]$ . More recently, integration has been discussed as an important structural dimension $[57, 58, 59]$ . Integration reflects the degree to which the activities of separate actors in the organization can be coordinated through formal coordination mechanisms. Collectively, these four variables—centralization, complexity, formalization, and integration—will be used to capture organizational structure.

In the IT category, the level of communications technology (CT) $^{1}$ penetration in the organization was used to capture the breadth and depth of utilization: how widely an organization depends on CT, and how much the CT penetration is routinized. Zmud et al. [90] use IT penetration to describe the extent to which IT is embedded within an organization's strategic, managerial, and operational work systems. Using the Lewin-Schein model of stage descriptions from innovation and diffusion research, their study considers IT penetration as the degree of infusion, routinization, and diffusion of systems. Such measures as Ein-Dor and Segev's [19] "intensity of end-user computing," Srinivasan's [76] "technical sophistication," Li et al.'s [51] "IS sophistication," and Weill's [85] "experience with IT" all share the similar notion of measuring IT in terms of the level of IT diffusion in the organization. This study uses a similar measure for communication technology penetration.

## Models and Hypotheses

## Formulation of Preliminary Research Hypotheses

All hypotheses are stated in terms of the relationships of the four characteristics of organization structure (centralization, structural complexity, formalization, and integration) to each of the two environment dimensions (environmental dynamism and complexity). From the eight associations possible between the two constructs, two associations are excluded because of the lack of strong support from previous organizational literature. The proposed six relationships can be explained within the information-processing paradigm in which organic structures (less centralized, more complex, less formalized, more integrated) adapt to increasing environmental uncertainty (more dynamism and complexity). The specific hypotheses to be tested in this model are:

Hypothesis a: The more dynamic the environment, the less formal the organization structure [6, 9, 17, 45, 79, 81, 89].

Hypothesis b: The more dynamic the environment, the more complex the organization structure [6, 9, 18, 45, 59].

Hypothesis c: The more dynamic the environment, the more integrated the organization structure [45, 58, 59].

Hypothesis d: The more complex the environment, the more decentralized the organization structure [22, 27, 59, 66].

Hypothesis e: The more complex the environment, the more complex the organization structure [8, 59, 79].

Hypothesis f: The more complex the environment, the more integrated the organization structure [22, 54, 59, 82, 83].

Model: Mediating Role of Communications Technology

In general, IT can be expected to facilitate the ability of organizations to cope with environmental uncertainty. Pfeffer and Leblebici [67] assert that “It is when the organization faces a complex and rapidly changing environment that IT is both necessary and justified” [p. 246]. Huber [33] also postulates that the need for information-processing capacity increases during periods of increased environmental turbulence and complexity, while Lee and Leifer [49] argue that the proper alignment between organizational and information systems (IS) structure is critical for organizations to achieve flexibility and efficiency in competitive and turbulent environments. More recently, Ferioli and Migliarese [20] present a “relational” model of IT in order to cope with the organizational changes (e.g., greater coordination) required to respond to changes in the environment.

Investments in the CT component of IT are going up. A recent survey by Datamation and Cowen reports that network investments have shown a rapidly increasing trend since 1985 and are expected to experience about a 9 percent annual growth in budget through the millennium [65]. While the convergence of computing and communication technologies makes it difficult to demarcate their effects, their basic difference is that, while communications technology makes this happen by reducing the coordination cost [52], computing technology provides organizations with the ability to handle a more complex environment through its basic functions of summarization and analysis. In other words, while the CT can reduce the cost and time of transmitting larger volumes of information about the external environment, computing technology provides a better understanding of the organization's external environment.

As shown in figure 1, it is proposed that the relationships between the perceived environment and organization structure are better explained by a more direct role of CT penetration. As in the arguments forwarded by Lederer and Mendelow [48], the environmental changes create problems for the firm and it has to develop mechanisms (i.e., structures) to cope with these problems. Higher environmental dynamism and complexity are expected to create the need for larger amounts of information and greater information processing and sharing. This need leads to more information technology investments, especially in CT [25]. In terms of the relationship between the penetration of CT and organization structure, the assumption in this model reflects the technology imperative in that a direct impact of CT on organization structure is assumed. Keen [39] notes

The very idea of using telecommunications for business innovation means not automating the status quo but explicitly trying to change entire aspects of the organization or its interactions with its business environment. Communications technology is viewed as a major element in managing and creating changes in the business process. [p. 591]

In similar vein, Teng et al. [80] argue that CTs are major enablers of fundamental changes in business processes. These technologies not only facilitate cross-functional interaction, but also allow sharing of common data and knowledge repositories among process participants, thereby allowing greater collaboration and understanding of the entire process rather than fragmented parts of the process. Fiedler et al. [21] found that IS architectures that have strong communication components tended to promote integrative structures.

Based on the information-processing paradigm, the direct effects of CT on organization structure can also be rationalized. The greater information-sharing capacity made possible through CT penetration can cope with the need for additional information interpretability and the need for more information sharing, both of which are generated by a more dynamic and complex environment. By dramatically reducing the costs of coordination and by increasing information processing speed and quality, these communications technologies enable people to coordinate more effectively, by forming new coordination-intensive organizational structures $[22, 23, 52]$ . Our argument is that the newer (post-1990) applications of IT such as electronic data interchange, e-mail, videoconferencing, videotext, multiuser relational DBMS, GroupWare, client/server systems, or on-line external database searching will lead to corresponding changes in organization structure.

It is important to note that the arguments made above are for a mediating effect of CT. In other words, the environmental contingencies require firms to invest in CT which in turn manifests itself in structural changes. Here in the absence of CT it would be very difficult to create the desired structural response to the environment. This is in contrast to a moderating model in which CT is exogenous to the environment and is not a consequence of dynamism and complexity. In such a model CT does not have direct effects on structure. We find the theoretical arguments for a mediating model more plausible, given recent work that documents direct impacts of CT on structure. Further, the information-processing paradigm makes a case for investment in information systems, if not CT, in order to cope better with environmental change.

It is therefore postulated that communications technologies, which are induced by increasing environmental dynamism and complexity, will lead more directly to coordination intensive structures. In other words, the base hypotheses (a, b, and c) regarding environmental dynamism and structure will be mediated by communications technology. As dynamism increases, less formal, more complex, and more integrated structures that are required to cope with this dynamism will occur through the penetration of communication technology. These hypotheses can be stated as:

H1: The negative and direct relationship between environmental dynamism and formalization will be mediated by greater penetration of communications technology.

H2: The positive and direct relationship between environmental dynamism and structural complexity will be mediated by greater penetration of communications technology.

H3: The positive and direct relationship between environmental dynamism and integration will be mediated by greater penetration of communications technology.

Similarly, environmental complexity leads to less centralized, more complex, and more integrated structures as discussed in base hypotheses d, e, and f. It is proposed that communications technologies will be adopted and used in response to the environmental changes. These in turn will reinforce the structural outcomes of less centralization, more complexity, and greater integration. The hypotheses can be stated as:

H4: The negative and direct relationship between environmental complexity and centralization will be mediated by greater penetration of communications technology.

H5: The positive and direct relationship between environmental complexity and structural complexity will be mediated by greater penetration of communications technology.

H6: The positive and direct relationship between environmental complexity and integration will be mediated by greater penetration of communications technology.

## Methodology

IN ORDER TO ENHANCE THE DEGREE OF INTERNAL VALIDITY, a relatively homogenous sample of larger manufacturing firms was chosen as the sample frame. These firms would maintain similar applications and business processes, alleviating moderating effects of the economy and industry. A field survey was chosen as the methodology for data collection. It is assumed that the cross-sectional data used to study organization structure and other contingency variables represents numerous organizations in distinct stages of growth or change $[74]$ . Considering that each model eventually represents a certain stage of CT impact, the “snapshot” property of a cross-sectional study fits the purpose of this research to capture one moment in the evolution of CT impact on organizations. Also, given the well-grounded nature of the core constructs, previously validated measures facilitated the ability to pursue systematic inquiry.

## Measurement of Variables

Environmental dynamism was measured using five items adopted from Miller and Friesen $[58]$ . These five items reflect the changes in marketing practices, product sustainability, consumer demand and tastes, competitors, and production technology. Past research has used the number of external entities for an organization $[18]$ or the diversity of these entities $[58]$ as a measure of environmental complexity. This study adopted the six items used by Ramamurthy $[69]$ covering both size and diversity of customer, supplier, and competition.

In this study, four dimensions of organization structure are examined: centralization, formalization, structural complexity, and integration. Perceived measurements are used to capture the more emergent nature of organization structure, which may reflect the short-term structural adaptation to environment. Numerous ways to capture these structural variables appear in organization theory literature $[1]$ . Ramamurthy's $[69]$ twelve items, adapted from the measures developed by Miller and Friesen $[58]$ , were used (six items for centralization, three items for formalization, and three items for structural complexity) for this study. Ramamurthy's three additional items, based on Aiken and Hage $[1]$ , were used to capture the level of formalization. They are the extent of formal documentation of operating procedures, obedience to these rules, and tolerance for violation of these rules and procedures. In evaluating integration, the main emphasis was given to the mechanistic overlays of different functions in the organization $[57]$ . The level of utilization of coordination mechanisms—such as liaison personnel, task forces, and steering committees—was captured using Miller and Friesen's $[58]$ three items.

Communications technology entails “any communication-related technology for moving data, such as electronic data/document transfer, electronic mail systems, or local area network.” This definition was confined to the computer-supported CT. Thus, conventional communications media such as telephone or telex were excluded. CT penetration was measured by the extent to which standard business applications in the manufacturing sector are using the technology. $^{2}$ The measures capture not only how deeply the technology is embedded in the organization but also how many different applications are regularly used. For developing the list of business applications, Cron and Sobol’s [11] types of software capabilities, Raymond’s [70] computerized applications in small manufacturing firms, and Weill’s [85] fourteen business applications for computerization were consolidated and modified into a list of twenty-seven business applications. A seven-point Likert scale was used to determine the extent to which CT was utilized in each application. The scale ranged from “no use” to “widely used.” A “not relevant” category was also added. The appendix provides a complete list of questionnaire items.

## Pretesting and Administration

Prior to the large-scale survey, the questionnaire instrument was refined through a pilot study in a major university. Initially, the CT penetration part of the questionnaire was reviewed by six faculty members, including three in the production and operations area regarding the relevance of the items to the manufacturing sector. Eight doctoral students then studied the overall questionnaire design and offered comments. As a result of this review, several changes were made in the questionnaire. At the pretesting stage, fifteen face-to-face interviews were completed to gather feedback from one MIS director in state government and fourteen other senior executives in local manufacturing firms. Feedback was evaluated and for the most part minor wording changes were made in the questionnaire.

Responses from key top executives in U.S.-based manufacturing firms were sought. $^{3}$ The sample frame of the study was composed of 515 firms in the Southeast and East Coast area. Firms with a minimum of 200 and a maximum of 5,000 employees were included in the sample. Compact Disclosure, which incorporates the Disclose DataBase and the software necessary to access it, were used as a sampling frame for selection of manufacturing firms. It contains financial and management information on over 12,000 public companies. Company data are extracted from annual and periodic reports filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). In addition to the reasonably comprehensive list of U.S.-based firms in Compact Disclosure, Standard and Poor's Index to American corporations provided the supplemental sources for generating the sample.

## Preliminary Data Analysis

TWO MAILINGS OF THE QUESTIONNAIRE WERE MADE TO THE SAMPLE of 515 firms in the 1993–94 period. $^{4}$ The first round yielded 87 usable responses from 84 different companies. The second round yielded an additional 77 responses from 73 companies, raising the total response to 164 from 153 companies. This produced a final response rate of 30.5 percent. Multiple responses (mostly two responses) were received from a total of 11 firms. For the rest of this study, only the higher ranked officer's response in these 11 samples was used to represent each firm's general opinion after testing and confirming the similarity of response patterns.

To check the diversity of responding firms within a manufacturing sector, two key characteristics were analyzed: industry type and organizational size. A profile of the responding firms in terms of these criteria is shown in figure 2. The traditional dominance of the textile, commercial machinery, and computer equipment industries along the East Coast is reflected in the industry profile. Almost 90 percent of the firms had revenues ranging from \$51 million to \$500 million, and none of the firms had fewer than fifty employees. In addition, the responding firms had been using some form of computing technology for an average of 16 years and CT for an average of 7.2 years. Figure 2 indicates that most respondents were in senior executive positions, which is presumed to be the best level to respond to items regarding the firm's environment, structure, and level of CT penetration.

Testing for nonresponse bias is important and often involves additional data collection from a sample of nonrespondents. Since such additional data are not easily obtainable, such bias is often approximated by simply comparing the answer patterns of early respondents with those of late respondents $[75, 84]$ . The nonresponse bias was examined to compare key characteristics through t-tests (for number of years using computing and CT) and chi-square tests (for sales volume, number of employees, and industry type). The results show no significant differences between the “early” and “late” respondents regarding their number of years using computing and CT $t = 0.876, p > 0.30; t = 1.584, p > 0.10$ , number of employees (chi-square value = 1.02, 3 df, p > 0.70), sales volume (chi-square value = 9.09, 5 df, p > 0.10), and industry type (chi-square value = 14.11, 11 df, p > 0.20). These results suggest that the results could be generalized within the boundary of the study sample frame.

## Validity/Reliability Assessment of Research Variables

It is imperative that the reliability and validity of the research variables be rigorously tested. Even though this study adopts or adapts validated and reliable instruments used in earlier research, the demanding requirements of validity and reliability were considered under the guidelines suggested in the literature $[10, 12, 43, 60]$ .

## Construct Validity of Perceived Environment and Organization Structure Domain

The following steps of factor analysis were followed to establish convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs in these domains:

1. After initially examining the reliability of the items of predefined constructs, those items that have a significantly low item-to-total correlation were candidates for being dropped after careful review. One in the organizational structure domain was finally dropped from the study. The remaining items in each domain were subjected to factor analysis using common factor analysis to identify the common underlying nature of the measured construct [16]. Oblique rotation was chosen to rotate the factor structure because constructs in the domain were expectedly correlated [10].

2. Two commonly employed decision rules for factor identification were applied: (a) a minimum factor loading of 0.40 was used for each item; although 0.30 has been suggested as sufficient, the threshold was set at 0.40 to maintain greater confidence [10]; (b) single-item factors were dropped unless retaining them would be critically appropriate or parsimonious [60].

Using the above criteria, the results for constructs in environmental and organizational domains are displayed in Table 1. Overall, factor results for the organizational domain indicates strong support for the construct validity (convergent and discriminant validity) of the structural variables. The results for the environmental variables were also strong insofar as the items did not exhibit any cross-loading, although some had to be dropped due to weak loadings on all factors.

## Reliability

All of the multiple items adopted for the perceived environment and the organization structure constructs have established acceptable reliability and validity level from past research. However, in an effort to improve the appropriateness of the instrument items, some questions were slightly altered in wording and style. The equivalence measure of reliability, using Cronbach's alpha coefficient, was examined to validate the effect of these minor changes in the instrument. The values of Cronbach's alpha for all the extracted constructs are presented in the last column of Table 1. Nunnally [60] suggests that, when a previously validated instrument has been adopted, a higher cutoff value of 0.7 may be used. Four factors (centralization, formalization, complexity, and integration) in the organizational structure and communications penetration factor all had values higher than the 0.70 cutoff value, ranging from 0.70 to 0.96. However, the environmental dynamism and complexity factors showed relatively low Cronbach alpha scores of 0.534 and 0.612, with three and four items retained.

Since Cronbach's alpha is highly sensitive to the number of items in the construct even when the average intercorrelation between the items is quite significant, the interrater reliabilities were thus applied to determine the item reliability for these two factors. To provide confidence in the questions on each research construct, the same questions were asked of two senior executives in a single organization. The twenty responses from eleven matched firms were subjected to $t$ -tests to measure the extent to which the raters are interchangeable. The results showed no significant difference for these two environmental factors. The results provided supplementary evidence that the two respondents approach agreement in their perceptions of their environments.

## Testing the Mediating Effects of CT Penetration (COMM)

As decision criteria for the mediating effect of CT, four basic rules were applied. Initially, two separate regression models were developed, the first with only the original predictor variables in the regression model and the second with the inclusion of mediator variable to the original predictor variable set as illustrated below:

$$
\text { Regression   1: } Y = a + b x
$$

Regression 2: $Y = a + bx + cz,$

where Y = the dependent variable; x, z = independent and mediator variables; a = the slope coefficient; b = the beta coefficient for the original independent variable; and c = the beta coefficient for the mediator variable.

Four rules were applied in the analysis as follows: (1) b, the beta coefficient for the original independent variable is statistically significant in regression 1; (2) when z, the mediator variable, is included in regression 1, c is statistically significant; (3) the explanatory power of regression 2 is greater than regression 1 in terms of the adjusted R-squared; and (4) the significance of b in regression 1 is weaker than in the regression 2 [3].

The results of this procedure for all six cases revealed that COMM was a strong and significant mediator in three of the six relationships. As can be seen in Table 4, COMM mediates the relationship between DYNAM and COMPLEX, between E\_COMP and COMPLEX, and between E\_COMP and INTEG.

In summary, three posited cases of a mediating role for CT were found to be significant. Rather than indirectly and passively influencing organization structure as a moderator, CT played a more influential and direct role in selected environment–structure relationships. CT creates organizational change by leading organizations to be more complex and diversified, and by facilitating the use of more integrative mechanisms, such as formal and informal meetings and steering committees. Significant links between CT and structural complexity and integration were also confirmed. However, none of the statistical analyses supported any significant effects of CT on centralization. The results indicate that a decision authority structure was not particularly dependent on more diffusion of CT, but perhaps on other factors such as environmental dynamism.

## Discussion

RESULTS OF THE REGRESSION ANALYSES TESTING A MEDIATING ROLE OF COMM provide support for the model. A mediation model for CT penetration has the following form and supports hypotheses 2, 5, and 6:

(1) Environmental complexity $(x) \rightarrow \text{CT penetration } (m) \rightarrow \text{integration } (y);$

(2) Environmental complexity $(x) \rightarrow \text{CT penetration } (m) \rightarrow \text{structural complexity } (y)$ ;

(3) Environmental dynamism $(\mathbf{x})\to \mathrm{CT}$ penetration $(m)\rightarrow$ structural complexity $(y)$ , where $x$ is the antecedent, $m$ the mediator, and $y$ the consequence.

In general, the first part of the mediating relationships, from the perceived environment to CT, is well explained and predicted in the literature [e.g., 25, 47]. Leblebici and Salancik [47], for instance, point out that high volatility (dynamism) will result in less formal, more varied use of information, which can be achieved by greater use of CT.

The second part of the mediating relationship from COMM to organizational structure implies that a greater investment in CT, spurred by increasing environmental complexity and dynamism, leads to more use of integration mechanisms and more structurally complex organizations. This constitutes a fundamental shift from the indirect nature of the moderating role of IT to what may be a more direct influence on structure. $^{6}$

The relationship between CT and the level of integration mechanisms used has been discussed in prior work. Keen [41] predicts that telecommunications will reduce administrative overhead and the need for integrative mechanisms such as meeting and reporting systems. This IT role of job simplification has been assumed since Whisler's [87] classic prediction about IT's role. However, our findings are more consistent with the discussions of Dean et al. [15] and Huber [34], both of which suggest that CT facilitates integrative mechanisms (such as interdepartmental committees, task forces, and liaison personnel). For instance, Huber notes that "if managers and others involved in making organizational decisions believe that use of the technologies will result in more effective meetings, the availability of the technologies may encourage them to have more decision-related meetings than they would otherwise" [34, pp. 55–56]. Another explanation for the increasing integration level by COMM is related to the limitation of CT as an alternative to traditional face-to-face meetings. As long as the use of CT is limited to increasing efficiency in delivering highly routinized information, such as reimbursement requests or job applications, the need for interdepartmental communications would not diminish.

In contrast, when the types of information required become more complex and richer using today's plethora of user-friendly data-mining/graphical technologies on increasingly critical decisions and processes, CT may not fully satisfy the requirement of internal communication. In such cases, meaning is created through integration mechanisms such as social interaction and discussion in group meetings [35]. In fact, CTs can further facilitate “team-based” structures by allowing rapid capture and dissemination of information in “real time” as required by the cross-functional teams.

Another significant relationship exists between COMM and structural complexity. This finding generally coincides with previous research. Blau et al. state that, in a study of manufacturing firms, there is no evidence that automation of plant functions by on-site computers leads to the consolidation of departments or a reduction in their number $[5, p. 32]$ . Enhancing the manager's own handling capabilities and reducing internal coordination costs would permit the manager to control and coordinate more complex, differentiated organizations $[67]$ . CT is enabling a breakup, a “dis-consolidation” of organizational forms. The results of the mediating effects of COMM indicate that, as organizations manage a more complex environment, they rely on a more organic structure by adopting a more complex structure and by using more integration mechanisms. This organic structural adaptation under high environmental uncertainty is well explained by the information-processing paradigm [23].

## The Reinforcing Innovation Cycle

Overall, with industry demanding more simplified organizational forms through downsizing, business reengineering, or restructuring efforts, the findings of this study indicate that responses to environmental pressures foster CT penetration, which in turn leads to greater integrative mechanisms and structural complexity. It could be argued that this perspective presumes a technological imperative, in that CT had a direct effect on structural attributes. However, the broader model might more appropriately reflect the managerial imperative, in that deliberate CT investment decisions made by organizational actors are really responsible for the investment in CT in response to the environment.

Alternatively, a reverse causality could be recognized between CT penetration and structural attributes. Loosely referred to as the organizational imperative, this perspective is reflected in the copious innovation and implementation literature that argues that certain favorable structural conditions (i.e., organic) would favorably reflect on organizational adoption of (radical) innovations $[13]$ . Although not explicitly tested, the consistency of the direction of the results with those found in innovation literature [e.g., 13, 25] might suggest a reinforcing cycle where organic structural attributes such as complexity and integration foster further adoption of newer CT.

## Second-Order Coordination Effects

It is important to note that this study uses cross-sectional data. Therefore, our ability to draw definitive causal implications are limited at best. A more accurate statement of the results would be: Organizations that have greater perceived complexity and dynamism would tend to have greater CT penetration and would tend to have greater structural complexity and integration mechanisms (for complexity). Statistically, the relationship between environmental attributes and structural attributes would be significantly weaker were it not for CT. Therefore, it is fair to say that CT reinforces or changes structural attributes that are required to cope with the environment.

Keen [41] points out that telecommunications inherently involves organizational as well as technical change. Telecommunications forms the basis for the new enabling systems that help top managers redesign their organizations [39]. In this innovative way, CT can be a means to achieve organizational simplicity, by reducing the layers of management, the reliance on formalized rules and procedures, and the bureaucratic processes that constrain rather than help organization members [77]. Considering these unlimited capabilities of the new technology, overall directions for CT's impact on organization structure in this study show that the use of CT in the sample firms allows them to address environmental challenges in a more pathologic way by creating greater organization complexity and integration. This phenomenon can also be explained by Orlikowski [62]'s "time-space discontinuity" concept, which explains the time gap between the design and use of IT:

[M]any of the actions that constitute the technology are often separated in time and space from the actions that are constituted by the technology, with the former typically occurring in vendor organizations, and the latter occurring in customer sites. [p. 407]

The application of CT may reflect this time-space discontinuity between IT designers and actual users. That is, the more challenging utilization of CT may not have been realized by the user community. This is further discussed by Orlikowski and Robey [64] when they acknowledge that the most typical manifestation of IT use is to sustain existing institutional structures through conformation.

Malone and Rockart [52] have observed some interesting trends based on reduction in coordination costs that might further explain these results. They argue that the first-order effects of reducing coordination costs are simply the substitution of IT for human coordination, where business activities such as payroll are automated to replace clerical workers. A second-order effect occurs when organizations seek out new opportunities for IT by coordinating tasks that could not be efficiently accomplished by humans. This requires structural changes, which result in overall increased organizational coordination. A third-order effect would then be creation of new coordination-intensive jobs and creating “coordination-intensive” structures. The results of this study suggest that environmental complexity accelerates this trend by facilitating greater integration in structurally complex organizations. This greater coordination capacity of organizations, a second-order effect, is at least partially possible through greater penetration of CT. As organizations evolve toward coordination-intensive structures, a third-order effect, simpler structures might emerge. With further reduction in coordination costs through CT, disaggregation of the organizations (i.e., outsourcing) might yield simpler, mission-specific structures.

## CT and Centralization

Another interesting finding is that COMM was not associated with the centralization level of an organization. One possible explanation is that CT reflects the desires and acts of management and leads to the desired organizational structure. Since CT can occasion similar dynamics and lead to different structural outcomes because of either managerial intention or its propensity to reinforce existing (centralized or decentralized) structures, CT does not produce a consistent variation in the centralization level of firms. Another possible explanation would be the presence of a hybrid structure that is characterized by neither centralized nor decentralized forms. Gurbaxani and Whang [26] predict that a firm may use IT to centralize some decision rights while decentralizing others, leading to a hybrid structure. Similarly, Malone [53] proposes that decreased communication costs have created flexible decentralized but connected structures that can allow for both centralized and decentralized decision making.

## Limitations and Future Research

DESPITE THE BEST EFFORTS TAKEN IN THIS STUDY TO VALIDATE THE INSTRUMENT during the development stages, it was necessary to assume that all the variable measures used were reliable and valid in order to conserve the overall purpose of the study. For example, while the study intended to collect multiple respondents from each participating company, only eleven pairs of multiple responses were usable. Therefore, the study makes use of the perceptual responses of a single, senior executive in most cases. It is not uncommon to have divergent beliefs and perceptions among executives within the same organizational unit. The problems of equating individual responses with organizational-level responses and interpreting them as such thereafter have been highlighted in past research [69].

Another limitation involves the relatively low reliabilities of the environmental constructs. This might reflect a weakness in content validity and may pose a threat to the generalizability of the findings. Also, while the research model was theorized to be causal, the data-collection approach adopted in this study was only “cross-sectional.” This creates problems for causal inference since the “effect” data are captured at the same time as the “cause” data in cross-sectional research. For better causality testing, future studies need to explore causal issues by using longitudinal research designs. These studies could concentrate on following a number of firms over time and discovering the extent to which structure is explicitly shaped by technological change. And, finally, it should be noted that, as is typical of studies carried out on macrolevel environmental constructs, the explained variance is small and the mediating effects are not pronounced. However, we believe that the existence of such effects provides preliminary evidence upon which contingency studies can be built.

While any proposal of a strict causal sequence might involve a “leap of faith,” we would argue that the results suggest the following:

Complex and dynamic environments (which are characterized by high uncertainty) will spawn greater need for communication technology in basic business activities. This could be at least partially attributed to the greater need to disseminate information within the organization in order to cope with this uncertainty. The communications technology penetration could be reactive or proactive through managerial intervention. Penetration of these technologies reflects positively on interfunctional integration mechanisms that are at this stage reinforced and enhanced but not replaced by the technologies. In other words, cross-functional departments and committees tend to meet more frequently and presumably use communications technology to facilitate this integration. Also, communication technologies enhance the product line and production technology (structural complexity) required to cope with environmental uncertainty. Further, it should also be recognized that organizations characterized by structural complexity and strong integrative mechanisms might create innovation conditions to reinforce adoption (and subsequent penetration) of newer communication technologies.

The research discussed here raises a number of key issues for theory building and testing in the domain of IT impact research. Four such issues are:

1. One inference made in the discussion was that the use of CT may be a way to cope with the increasing demands for coordination mandated by the environment. Structural changes led by CT are being used to reinforce and expand existing political structures. A factor ignored by this study was the extent of managerial imperative mediating the deployment of CT and organizational structural attributes. As Huber [34] pointed out, more and more of the IT effects on organization structure will be the outcomes of top managers' intentions. In order to reflect this trend, strategic variables, such as the strategic orientation or manager's intention, need to be introduced into the contingency theory tradition of simple technology–structure relationship. It will eventually provide a comprehensive picture that reflects the structural changes as a joint product of technology and managerial choice and is consistent with more recent evidence in restructuring and reengineering [14].

2. While CT were argued to be the more contemporary technologies that have transformational impacts on organizations, it is very difficult to ignore traditional computing technologies. These technologies are increasingly intertwined with networks, and are also experiencing exponential performance/cost improvements over time. Therefore, it is very feasible that computing technologies expand the bounded rationality of organizations, allowing them to process more information in response to environmental changes. Inclusion of a more holistic conceptualization of IT might enhance our understanding of the results. Also, reassessing these results with the more open TCP/IP-based architectures present today might strengthen the findings.

3. Few firms implement advanced technology while holding constant all other aspects of their organization. Many firms implementing IT today are also pursuing such initiatives as Just in Time, downsizing, outsourcing, and Total Quality Management. The interrelationships of these and other initiatives with IT are also worthy of study [15]. To do so, the unit of analysis for a possible future study using this model should not be restricted to the level of organization. Rather, such a study should incorporate a mixed level, such as the individual and firm levels, to enable a more integrative theoretical perspective of “IT and organization” relationship. Emphasizing the need for a more integrative approach in the study of IT impact, Orlikowski and Robey [64] also state the need for including human factor constructs along with institutional properties. Based on Giddens’s theory of structuration, they provide a guide to study the interaction among institutional properties, human actors, and IT. The model represents occasions for organizational change, where the actions facilitated by different ITs may, over time, institute a new way of doing things and a new sensibility about what IT is appropriate.

4. Finally, the possibility of stages of effects has been raised. Organizations that are more advanced in their deployment of CT might experience a discontinuity of sorts, where complexity and integration are better managed across simpler structural units than within. Studies that can characterize levels of CT deployment that can be related to coordination costs (e.g., administrative overhead) might add granularity to our understanding of these results.

## Conclusion

THIS RESEARCH PROVIDES A THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK WITHIN WHICH TO ASSESS the impact of IT in general and CT in particular on organizations' structural adaptation. The study broadens earlier studies by focusing on the newer, more powerful set of CTs, rather than the arguably more “passive” information-processing technologies. These newer technologies are argued to have a more direct impact on structural attributes. The model highlights the role of CT in coping with environmental uncertainty and its impact on the organizational structure of firms. A major finding of the study is the support for a mediational role of CT between certain attributes of the environment and structure. The existence of such relationships not only brings to the forefront the more direct role of contemporary IT in influencing structure, but also emphasizes the need to look at these impacts within a broader network of contextual constructs.
