# Supervisor Support and Career Anchor Impact on the Career Satisfaction of the Entry-Level Information Systems Professional

JAMES J. JIANG AND GARY KLEIN

JAMES J. JIANG is the Max Watson Professor of Computer Information Systems at Louisiana Tech University. His Ph.D. in computer information systems was awarded by the University of Cincinnati in 1992. His current research interests include project management and information technology infrastructure of knowledge-based organizations. He has written more than sixty academic articles in journals such as, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, Communications of the ACM, Decision Support Systems, and the Project Management Journal. He is a member of IEEE, ACM, and DSI.

GARY KLEIN is the Couger Professor of Information Systems at the University of Colorado in Colorado Springs. He obtained his Ph.D. in management science at Purdue University. He served with Arthur Andersen and Company in Kansas City and was director of the Information Systems department for a regional financial institution. He was previously on the faculty at the University of Arizona, Southern Methodist University, and Louisiana Tech University and served as Dean of the School of Business at the University of Texas of the Permian Basin. His interests include project management, knowledge management, system development, and mathematical modeling, and he has over sixty academic publications in these areas.

ABSTRACT: Keeping satisfied information systems (IS) professionals at the entry level has become a difficult task for IS managers. Financial incentives have led to escalating costs without widespread improvement of the situation. To capitalize on nonfinancial rewards, organizations must understand the relationships between employees' internal desires, the environment fostered by the organization, and career satisfaction. This paper is a report on a study of 101 entry-level IS professionals. The data analysis indicates that IS professionals find more satisfaction with their career when supervisor support is prominent and an adequate range of opportunities that satisfy career desires exist within the organization.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: career anchors, career satisfaction, MIS entry-level personnel, supervisor support.

PROGRAMS TO SATISFY INFORMATION SYSTEMS (IS) EMPLOYEES' CAREER DESIRES are ever elusive in today's professional market. Management must consider effective use of IS human resources [13, 46], including aspects of rising personnel costs relative to hardware costs [55], shortages of skilled entry-level support staff [58], and a high rate of employee turnover. For example, the Information Technology Association of America estimated about 346,000 vacant jobs for IS employees in 1998 [58]. Fortune 500 firms have a 25–35 percent turnover rate in their IS department and are often forced to delay important projects [40]. Estimates of the U.S. business loss due to shortage of expertise approach \$59 billion, more than half of which are at least 80 percent over budget [53].

Today's IS managers tend to count on pay as the main recruitment and retention method. One study found that in order to recruit and retain IS employees, 42 percent of the IS managers have reviewed their basic pay structure, 13 percent have introduced bonuses, and 15 percent have made other changes relating to salary [52]. One major employer in the Atlanta region pays its IS staff salary about 15 percent higher than average market rates and has succeeded in maintaining low turnover in their information systems departments [24]. Lanier Worldwide gives staying bonuses of \$10,000 to \$30,000 to induce IS professionals to stay for a minimum two-year period. Such shortsighted compensation practices have fostered double-digit annual IS budget increases [58]. These policies also ignore other aspects of career satisfaction so critical in retention and recruitment of qualified employees [37, 47, 56]. To help IS managers better understand IS career management in lieu of pay incentives, researchers need to focus on what may differentiate levels of IS employee career satisfaction.

Early research on IS career management focused on defining career paths for IS employees. Career paths are organizationally defined career advancements and fall into a class of “external” career opportunities—such as the dual ladder and the pyramidal models $[14]$ . Other external career opportunities include intangibles associated with location, pleasing organizational culture, and professional challenge. Other researchers examined another aspect of careers—“internal careers” $[31]$ . Internal careers are the psychological attractions that serve to guide, stabilize, and integrate an individual’s career. Internal career anchors are one central component of self-concept that an employee is reluctant to relinquish $[64, 65]$ .

Researchers claimed that internal career desires must be satisfied and that organizations must attempt to provide work conditions to match underlying internal career anchors $[31]$ . This idea of a fit between the employee and the work environment has been examined in the context of stress $[11]$ , adjustment $[22]$ , and job design $[38]$ . The fit is considered important between expectations in both the job tasks and the reward structure $[8, 59]$ . The potential consequences of a lack of employee–job fit include anxiety, strain, job dissatisfaction, turnover, among others $[11, 22, 39]$ . Given the purported importance of the “external” career opportunities and “internal” career anchors on one’s career decisions, this study examines the relationships these “external” career opportunities and “internal” career desires have with IS employees’ career satisfaction.

However, the development of external opportunities to match internal anchors is likely not to be effective without good management [63]. This implies that an IS manager can also help employees develop their careers and thereby retain valued employees. Some organizations have formal development programs for all levels of employees, while others offer limited career counseling in an annual performance review $[25]$ . Regardless of the extent and kinds of career programs offered by organizations, the role of the supervisor is important to success $[66]$ . As supervisors conduct formal appraisals, they should be concerned with their subordinates' career advancement and encourage growth in that direction. Given the importance of the IS managers' supporting role in their subordinates' career development, we also examine how supervisor support can affect the IS employees' perceived career opportunities provided by the organization and the employee's career satisfaction.

Research has found diverse career desires among IS employees [18, 49, 47]. This paper expands the IS career anchors literature to include both external career opportunities and supervisor support factors. We believe that one's career satisfaction is determined not only by the internal career desires but also by the external career opportunities. In addition, we include the supervisor support critical to early career success [28]. We examine whether career satisfaction depends on this critical support as well as the “internal” and “external” factors for the entry-level IS professional.

## Research Model

IN ADDRESSING THE ABOVE RESEARCH QUESTIONS, THIS STUDY DRAWS FROM TWO related research areas. Computer personnel research has long focused on the impact of internal career anchors on career satisfaction. Human resources management research draws significant conclusions on the relationship of the external career opportunities and supervisory support to the employees' career satisfaction. The research model of this study (figure 1, discussed in more detail later) synthesizes the relevant research in these two areas and suggests that all three constructs (supervisory support, external career opportunities provided by organizations, and individual internal career anchors) significantly influence the IS professionals' career satisfaction.

## Internal Career Anchors

In the last decade, internal career aspects of IS personnel has received attention in the IS human resources management literature $[31, 46]$ . Internal career considerations focus on the individual's self-concept and career values. An important element of an employee's internal career is the career anchor or career orientation $[23, 64]$ . A career anchor refers to an individual's self-perceived needs, values, and talents that shape career decisions. It can be thought of as a central component of self-concept that an employee is unwilling to relinquish, even when forced to make a difficult choice. The career anchor is significant because it influences career choices, affects decisions to move, shapes career desires, determines an individual's view of the future, and sways employee reactions to work experiences. Measuring an individual's career anchors makes explicit the career orientations of that individual employee.

This study considered eight internal career orientations [23, 65]:

1. Managerial competence—individual IS employee pursues greater responsibility for accomplishing results through others.

2. Technical competence—individual IS employee focuses primarily on the exercise of technical expertise.

3. Organizational security—individual IS employee seeks company loyalty, tenure, and financial security.

4. Geographic security—individual IS employee links him or herself to a particular area on a long-term basis.

5. Autonomy—individual IS employee seeks situations in which he or she will be free of organizational constraints and control.

6. Identity—individual IS employee has strong desire for status and prestige from belonging to certain organizations.

7. Service—individual IS employee is dedicated to helping other people and contributing to causes.

8. Variety—individual IS employee desires a number of different tasks and challenges.

Contrary to the conventional wisdom that IS personnel have either technical or managerial internal career desires, studies in IS literature have consistently found that IS personnel exhibit a diversity of internal career desires [18, 47].

## External Career Opportunities

External career opportunities refer to the extent to which an organization provides support to the above eight internal career anchors. Researchers have argued that information on one's internal career anchors will allow an organization to restructure jobs to respond to individual needs [45]. IS employees have differing career needs, and organizations should attempt to provide incentives that are consistent with underlying career values $[31]$ . The dual ladder may not deal effectively with the various career options desired by IS employees as it usually assumes only two options: technical or managerial $[31]$ . “Motivation-maintenance” theory $[42]$ suggests that a lack of career opportunities (e.g., job security, autonomy, and achievement) will lead to a lower level of career satisfaction and a higher rate of turnover. In general, given the importance of internal career desires in employees’ career decisions, it is critical that organizations provide work conditions that meet their employees’ internal career desires. Researchers began to pursue this line of inquiry when they examined the match between internal career orientation and job type $[47]$ . A match leads to higher career satisfaction. However, the study did not consider a model that examines the extent of input of each variable and considered only two classes of job settings (managerial and technical).

## Supervisor Support

In the human resources management literature, researchers in the last two decades suggest that supervisor support in their subordinates' career development has a significant impact on their subordinates' career satisfaction and turnover [36]. Supervisor support has been defined as the relationship between subordinate and supervisor, which is viewed by the subordinate as having a positive contribution to career development [54]. A subordinate would consider a relationship as contributing positively to career development if the supervisor provided either helpful information (feedback) on career advancement opportunities in the organization or personal support or both.

There is evidence that having an appropriate level of supervisor support facilitates one's career advancement and results in a higher level of career satisfaction and a lower rate of turnover [36]. From the converse view, absence of supervisory interest in a subordinate's career aspirations and infrequent provision of performance feedback are likely to stunt an employee's professional growth on the job [35, 51]. In this study, supervisor support is defined as the degree of supervisor provision of information on employees' career opportunities and helpful feedback on performance.

## Career Satisfaction

Career outcomes can be viewed from both an external perspective, as judged by an organization, and from an internal perspective, as judged by an IS employee [65]. IS employees' advancement prospects represent an externally defined career outcome. IS employees' level of career satisfaction is an internally defined career outcome [35]. The present research examined career satisfaction, which is often identified as important in the IS literature [45]. In this case, career satisfaction refers to the extent to which an IS employee expresses a positive orientation toward his or her career. Career satisfaction is also highly related to other variables that have an impact on successful IS staff management. Studies have found a relationship such that career satisfaction is an indicator of commitment and turnover [37, 47, 57].

## Research Hypotheses

IF CAREER DEVELOPMENT IS TO SUCCEED, IT MUST RECEIVE THE COMPLETE support of the employees' supervisor [66]. Some supervisors implement career-planning activities to coach and guide employees through self-assessment and goal setting [68]. The primary objective of career planning is to increase employees' awareness of themselves and their career goals. Supervisors also show support by providing information and opportunities to employees. Supervisors who keep employees informed about different organizational career opportunities and take time to learn about their employees' career goals can reduce turnover and improve performance [69]. In addition, supervisors' feedback has been found specifically to help individuals whose career expectations or self-motivation are low [10]. These general employee guidelines should be applicable to IS professionals [27].

The basis of these, and other, techniques is in “self-fulfilling prophecy” theory, which argues that there is a positive relationship between a manager’s expectations and the resulting performance of subordinates $[12]$ . Therefore, managers cannot overlook their obligation and responsibility to subordinates $[70]$ . In general, it is believed that, by providing supervisor career support to employees, both the organization and the individual will benefit $[28]$ . These arguments suggest the following two hypotheses:

H1: The level of supervisor support is positively associated with the IS employees' perceived external career opportunities provided by their organizations.

H2: The level of supervisor support is positively associated with the IS employees' career satisfaction.

Organizational career management literature has long emphasized the importance of activities and opportunities sponsored by the organization in an attempt to ensure that career planning will meet the future human resources requirement of the organization and employees' career satisfaction $[68]$ . Individuals have various kinds of needs that must be satisfied and their career decisions and behaviors are often dominated by these needs $[66]$ . The presence of these opportunities is an important externality to the employee and is labeled the “external opportunities” for the employee, though the specific actions are internal to the organization.

The IS literature has demonstrated a diversity of internal career desires among IS professionals, such as autonomy, identity, technical achievement, managerial responsibility, and job security $[18, 45]$ . Recognizing that IS employees have a variety of career needs, organizations should attempt to provide work situations that meet with employees' underlying internal career desires $[31]$ . Furthermore, “motivation-maintenance” theory $[42]$ suggests that employees with lower satisfaction levels on identity, job security, and technical advancement often have a lower level of career satisfaction. These arguments suggest the following hypothesis:

H3: The degree of external opportunities provided by an organization is positively associated with the IS employees' career satisfaction.

Although there is no formal theory in the IS literature, many studies have shown a positive relationship between the IS professional's internal career anchors and career satisfaction. Researchers argue that an individual's career desires serve to motivate [45, 63]. It has been demonstrated that the level of “motivation” has a positive relationship with the measures of job performance and career satisfaction [41]. In short, it is believed that the extent of one's “internal career anchors” should be viewed as an individual's career motivations. Based upon the above arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4: The degree of IS employees' internal career desires (anchors) is positively associated with their career satisfaction.

Career anchors are the pattern of self-perceived talents, motives, and values that guide an individual's career decisions [47]. These career anchors can emerge over time and may be modified by work experience [20, 26, 67]. For example, career anchors have changed in the early years of a person's work career, stabilizing after about five years of employment [65]. This may be due in part to IS majors often choosing IS for the wrong reasons and expectations [71]. Job “switchers” and “nonswitchers” had equal talent; however, the difference between them was that “switchers” did not have the expected environment in their organizations [15]. Furthermore, rapidly changing work environments encourage employees to modify their career desires [44]. We therefore expect there is a significant relationship between external career opportunities and internal career desires and propose the following hypothesis:

H5: The degree of IS employees' external career opportunities is positively associated with their internal career desires (anchors).

## Research Methodology

## Sampling Strategy

THREE LARGE SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT ORGANIZATIONS LOCATED IN A MAJOR metropolitan area of the southern United States agreed to participate in the study. The vice-presidents of Human Resources assisted in securing the support of the IS management in each organization. An IS manager from each of the three participating organizations asked forty entry-level IS personnel in their own organization to complete the survey described below. A total of 101 questionnaires out of the possible 120 were returned and used in the data analysis. The high response rate is comparable to that obtained in other studies using similar sampling techniques [20, 33, 37].

Respondents include system analysts, programmers, and technical support analysts. The organizations under study considered these to be entry-level positions. The narrowing of the sample population to individuals in the first stage of their careers, the software development industry, and one particular geographic location served to control for undesirable confounding of responses $[21, 32]$ . ## Characteristics of Sample

External validity refers to the extent to which the findings can be generalized across times, persons, and settings [16]. Because of the limited number of respondents, nonresponse bias could have threatened the study's external validity. For example, nonresponding subjects could have differed systematically from the participating subjects on some important characteristics, such as career desires, which could be a critical determinant of one's career satisfaction [10, 64]. A t-test comparison of means was undertaken to determine if there was a systematic difference in career satisfaction among the respondents by dividing the sample into two groups. The finding of a significant difference would imply a nonresponse bias. No significant difference was found. We also compared our subjects' career satisfaction means with those from another study [45]. No significant difference was found (3.42 in their study versus 3.55 in this study). Nonresponse bias therefore appeared unlikely.

The external validity of the findings is threatened if the sample is systematically biased—for example, if the responses were generally from IS employees receiving appropriate levels of supervisor support. The mean for supervisor support was 3.21, the median was (3.17), the skewness was (-0.36), and the kurtosis was (-0.07). The responses had good distribution on supervisor support since the mean and median were similar, skewness was less than 2, and kurtosis was less than 5 [30]. Overall, supervisor support-related bias seemed unlikely. External validity is also threatened if there is a systematic bias with regard to career satisfaction—for example, if responses were obtained largely from relatively satisfied or unsatisfied employees. The mean of this dimension was 3.55, the median was (3.47), the skewness was (-0.97), and the kurtosis was (0.83). This indicated that career satisfaction-related bias was unlikely.

Additional treats to external validity could occur if the samples showed other systematic biases in terms of demographics, such as age, gender, and positions. An ANOVA was conducted by using career satisfaction (as the dependent variable) against each demographic category (independent variables). The results did not indicate any significant relationships (none is significantly related to career satisfaction). Likewise, no significant relations held for supervisor support as the dependent variable. These results are similar to those in previous studies, which found no differences in gender according to job satisfaction $[4, 48, 47]$ . Differences related to gender in previous studies are due to factors not as relevant to entry-level personnel, such as salary, promotion, and position $[4, 34]$ .

Demographic content similar to other studies increases the external validity and ability to compare results across studies. One IS personnel study was conducted in the same geographic region using IS majors $[56]$ . We compared our samples' demographics with this previous study since we expect entry-level qualities to closely resemble student characteristics. The numbers were quite similar: sex: 61 percent male and 39 percent female in $[56]$ , 64 percent male and 37 percent female in this study; age: 83.8 percent 30 or younger in $[56]$ , 86 percent 35 or younger in this study. Other studies involving entry-level subjects had similar numbers: One had male at 59 percent and female at 41 percent, with 72 percent in the under-25 age group $[67]$ ; in another, 83 percent were 30 or younger, with 37 percent female and 63 percent male $[50]$ . Studies of more advanced career stages had more advanced age and greater percentages of males $[5, 20]$ . In sum, although the number of participating organizations was small, the series of tests conducted above did not identify any significant threats to external validity.

Another important research task is assessment of statistical power, the probability that the statistical test will correctly reject a null hypothesis. In this study, alpha is set at 0.05 and power (P) at 0.80, according to convention. MIS research has typically had small to medium effect size [5]. Many previous MIS studies on internal career anchors exhibited a small effect size [45]. Based on a standard effect-size value (d-small = 0.20, d-medium = 0.50, d-large = 0.80), the expected effect size was set at 0.35, that is, at the mean of small and medium effect size. The required sample size for a 0.80 power level can then be calculated at N = 51. Consequently, if actual effect sizes corresponded to those found in MIS research in general, this study's single-respondent sample size (N = 101) is expected to generate adequate power (above 0.80). This sample size was therefore expected to enhance the statistical conclusion validity of the findings [16].

Although the factor structure of the career anchors is widely accepted, a factor analysis was conducted to examine the structural validity of the measure. An initial principal component analysis produced nine factors with eigenvalues greater than or equal to 1.0. A subsequent factor analysis (with varimax rotation) was conducted using nine factors, eight of which were established anchors [18, 65]. The criteria used to identify factors were that a given item should load 0.50 or higher on a specific factor and have a loading no higher than 0.40 on other factors. All but managerial and technical competence factors load exactly as expected after varimax rotation (see Table 3). The ninth factor is composed of two items from the managerial anchor (items 17 and 28) and one from the technical anchor (item 15). Items 17 and 28 also loaded heavily into their expected component.

Individual factor structures for each of the eight anchors finds that each anchor scale has only one component with an eigenvalue greater than one, with the exception of technical competence, which has two such components. The homogeneity of the items within each factor was established further by computing their internal consistency reliability scores (coefficient alpha) by applying the recommended formula $[19]$ (see Table 3). This formula is widely used as a measure of internal consistency and represents a conservative estimate of the reliability of a scale. Discriminant validity is highlighted in Table 4, which shows that the variance between two anchors are always lower than the average variance extracted by the items measuring each construct $[24]$ . A second-order factor analysis on the above eight internal career anchors produced a single-factor solution.

An eight-item scale measured external career opportunity. Individuals were asked to indicate their agreement or disagreement with each statement on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree. The specific items used in this study are shown in Table 5. An external career opportunity measure from a previous study was adopted and modified to match with the eight internal career anchors examined in this study [28]. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) of these items produced a single factor solution with an eigenvalue of 2.75 that accounted for 50 percent of the explained variance. Responses to the eight items were averaged to create an external career opportunity score. The Cronbach's alpha for this measure was 0.71.

## Analytical Techniques

THE RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESES WERE TESTED USING PATH ANALYSIS, where a single indicator measured each variable in the model. In this study, path analysis was undertaken using structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques. SEMbased path analysis assumes that the variance and covariance of the independent variables are also to be estimated, in contrast to traditional path analysis using multiple regression, which assumes they are known numbers. Consequently, SEM-based path analysis provides better estimates of the path coefficients than traditional techniques.

The specific SEM-based technique used in this study was SAS's Covariance Analysis of Linear Structural Equations (CALIS) procedure, which can be used for both path analysis and latent variable modeling. CALIS estimates parameters and tests the appropriateness of linear structural equation models using covariance structure analysis. Fit indices reported in CALIS to identify goodness of fit include: (1) the Goodness of Fit Index (GFI); (2) GFI Adjusted for Degree of Freedom (AGFI); (3) Root Mean Square Residual (RMR); (4) the comparative fit index (CFI); (5) the normed fit index (NFI) such as the Bentler-Bonett NFI; and (6) a non-normed fit index (NNFI). Values greater than 0.90 are desirable for each fit index except AGFI (greater than 0.80 is preferred) and RMR (less than 0.10 is preferred). The average absolute standardized residual (AASR) indicates the proportion of the variance not explained by the model and should have small values. If the model's conformance with the observed data is to be considered acceptable, the probability value for the model's chi-square statistics should exceed a standard cutoff of 0.05 [6].

The theorized model should be compared with a more constrained model, where previously free effects are fixed, and a less constrained model, where previously fixed effects are free to be estimated. The former comparison is undertaken in CALIS by the Wald test, while the latter is performed by the Lagrange Multiplier (LM) test. The LM test estimates the reduction in the model's chi-square that would result from freeing a fixed parameter and allowing it to be estimated. In other words, for a CFA the LM estimates the degree to which chi-square would improve if a new factor loading or covariance were added to the model. Support for the model is enhanced if both the Wald and LM tests do not suggest any modifications that would significantly improve fit. The sample size to parameter ratio for the model was approximately 25:1, which conforms to recommendations of higher than 5:1 [6].

## Control for Missing Variables

In attempts to develop a parsimonious model that describes the effects of interest, it is likely that a number of missing variables may serve as potential confounds. For example, internal career anchors could be influenced by the nature of an individual's career stage, age, gender, industrial types, geographic locations, or job category [2, 9, 21]. However, three primary considerations make the model useful to evaluate even without these variables. First, the intent of the model is not to explain organizational career opportunities or IS personnel's career satisfaction, but to establish whether supervisor support, external career opportunities, and internal career desires are significant indicators of IS personnel's career satisfaction. Second, by choosing variables that are linked by a common theoretical thread—that is, “motivation-maintenance”—a stronger causal chain between the variables may be established. Third, the effect of missing variables can be reduced somewhat by a careful choice of methodological controls.

One approach to controlling for these variables is to include them explicitly in the model. However, given the potentially large number of such variables that affect career satisfaction, the difficulty in collecting data for these variables was insurmountable. A simpler, methodological approach to partially control for such confounds used in this study was random selection of respondents, a well-established experimental research technique.

Even though this study used well-established constructs, significant effort was directed at assessing validity and reliability. In particular, construct validity, external validity, statistical conclusion validity, statistical power, and alpha reliabilities are evaluated. Model and hypotheses testing were undertaken only after a set of valid and reliable measures were established for each variable in the model as described in the construct section above. The Pearson correlations between the constructs (Table 8) suggested a priori that there were many significant relationships. However, because Pearson correlations can be subject to spurious relationships, more convincing conclusions required path analysis.

## Testing the IS Entry-Level Professionals Model

The theorized model fit the data, with a p value of chi-square 0.63 exceeding the minimum cutoff of 0.05, all the fit indices above 0.90 (NFI = 0.96, NNFI = 0.97, CFI = 0.99, GFI = 0.99, AGFI = 0.94), and a low RMR (0.02). Moreover, the LM test did not suggest any additional effects to increase model significantly fit. However, the Wald test suggested that the direct effects of external career opportunities on internal career anchors and internal career anchors on career satisfaction were not significant in this model. The standardized path coefficients were used to test the research hypotheses (see Table 9).

Supervisor support had a significant and positive effect on perceived career opportunities $p < 0.01$ which suggested support for hypothesis 1, that more supervisor support leads to a perception of increased career opportunities by entry IS professionals. The standardized score for this effect was 0.37. The direct effect of supervisor support on career satisfaction was also positive and significant $p < 0.05$ , supporting hypothesis 2, that greater supervisor support relates to new IS employees' higher career satisfaction. The standardized effect was 0.16. The direct effect of career opportunities on career satisfaction was positive and significant $p < 0.01$ , which suggested support for hypothesis 3, that the higher the perceived career opportunities by IS employees the more their career satisfaction. The standardized effect was 0.49. The direct effect of career opportunities on internal career anchors was positive; however, the p value was not significant at the 0.05 level. Similarly, the direct effect of internal career on career satisfaction was positive (0.16), again not significant at the 0.05 level. Thus, hypotheses 4 and 5 could not be supported with these data.

## Conclusions and Implications

THIS PAPER REPORTED A STUDY OF THE RELATIONSHIPS BETWEEN THE ENTRY-LEVEL information systems professional's career satisfaction and the role of the supervisor, internal career anchors, and external career opportunities. A sample of 101 programmers, system analysts, and technical support analysts completed questionnaires to indicate perceptions on scales representing the variables in figure 1. An analysis using structural equation modeling revealed positive relationships between degree of external career opportunities and career satisfaction, the level of supervisor support and career satisfaction, and the level of supervisor support and the perceived external career opportunities within the organization. Not supported was a relationship between an employee's internal career anchors and career satisfaction and a relationship between external opportunities and internal career anchors.

The lack of support for a relationship between internal career anchors and career satisfaction seems to run counter to past research described in the literature $[45, 47]$ . For example, some researchers found that four internal career anchors were significantly correlated with career satisfaction; however, they caution that “the relationships are quite small (the largest correlation was 0.21) and probably have little practical significance” $[45, p.149]$ . The results of the correlations between the eight internal career anchors and career satisfaction are shown in Table 10. As in previous studies, we found that two (i.e., variety and service) of the eight internal career anchors were significantly related to career satisfaction; however, the magnitude of the effects was 0.29 and 0.17, respectively. In addition, all the anchors had a positive relationship with career satisfaction except the autonomy anchor, as in previous results $[45]$ . Another study found no significant relationships between career anchors and career satisfaction in five out of eight correlations $[4]$ . The significant correlations that did exist were service, identity, and job security. All the significant correlations were positive and small (< 0.30).

One reason why our study found only weak support for the relationship may be the restriction to entry-level professionals who may still be developing many of their values as they approach career management tasks for the first time $[21, 57]$ . Another possible reason is the inclusion of the supervisor variable in the model. Other works on career anchors did not consider supervisor support along with external career opportunities, yet still found weak relationships. Even though there was a correlation between internal anchors and career satisfaction in the data, the effects were dominated by the more significant variables. Regardless of the reason, the results call for examining other career stages and other samples to determine the accuracy of previous results and to see if internal anchors overcome the dominance of these other two variables.

Likewise, the lack of a strong relationship between external opportunities and internal anchors bears examination. This lack may be due to the strength of the supervisor's influence. Entry-level employees are expected to accept supervision and are expected to exercise directed creativity and initiative [13]. This would tend to confirm prior results of the importance of supervisors on IS employees' productivity: "First-line supervisors of IS professionals (e.g., programmers, system analysts) are the managers who establish the immediate work environment and affect productivity" [28].

Another reason for the lack of a significant relationship between external opportunities to internal anchors may be the nature of the tasks involved with entry-level positions. A difficult adjustment for entry-level employees is learning to live with a seemingly never-ending routine. Newcomers are eager to have their own project or their own clients [21, 43]. IS people strongly desire jobs with high motivating potential to be productive and satisfied [17].

The strong result of the relationship of the supervisor in the satisfaction model cannot be stressed too highly. Many studies have found the importance of the mentor to entry-level employees' career success because such a relationship performs both career and psychosocial functions $[54, 61]$ . The career functions provided by a mentor include sponsorship, facilitating exposure and visibility, coaching, and protection. Sponsorship means actively helping the individual get job experience and promotions. Facilitating exposure and visibility means providing opportunities for the protégé to develop relationships with key figures in the organization. Coaching involves providing advice in both career and job performance, which is important since supervisors are often the person most familiar with job requirements $[66]$ . Shielding the protégé from potentially damaging experiences provides protection.

The primary psychosocial function is role modeling, which occurs when the mentor displays behavior for the protégé to emulate. Other psychosocial functions include acceptance, counseling, and friendship. Unfortunately, although some companies have formal mentoring programs, junior employees are often left to negotiate their mentoring relationships. The barriers to finding a mentor include a lack of access to mentors, fear of initiating a mentoring relationship, gender differences, and fear that coworkers might not approve of the mentoring relationship $[62]$ .

The link found in this study between career opportunities and career satisfaction speaks to the continued importance of having a variety of career opportunities within an organization. The number of career paths changes rapidly nowadays as organizations struggle to find effective internal structures for their organization $[60]$ . But specific paths may not be as critical as a variety of creative opportunity $[14, 57]$ . In any case, unless organizations hold some promise of advance, they will not retain or attract top-quality IS professionals.

Drawing on these findings, we see at least two ways in which IS management can improve the satisfaction of entry-level IS. These are to provide supervisor support for career development and to promote organizational career opportunities. To support career development for an IS employee, ideally, IS managers and human resources managers should work together to design and implement a career development plan. The plan should reflect the goals of the organization and the employees' expectations. Unfortunately, it appears that careful career planning for IS personnel is not very common in practice [31]. Perhaps this is because managers of IS personnel do not feel comfortable with the career coaching role [57]. IS managers should provide employees with a clear set of expectations and directions for their own career development. For such a program to be effective, IS managerial personnel at all levels must be trained in the fundamentals of job design, performance appraisal, career planning, and counseling. In addition, IS managers also need to be appraised on how well they are doing their job of career support for their subordinates.

The importance of external career opportunities on IS personnel career satisfaction and IS personnel possessing a wide variety of career anchors raises several questions of merit. Will IS personnel who have internal careers that match the external career opportunities provided by the organization have greater career satisfaction than their counterparts? A specific study into possible gaps of internal and external factors should be conducted. Will the importance of external career opportunities be moderated by the individual's central life interests, which may change over time? Are individuals who have other items as their central life interests be less influenced by their external career opportunities? In which ways can IS management effectively tailor their job environments to provide the variety of external career opportunities for IS employees?
