# Trading Volumes with and Without Private Information: A Study Using Computerized Market Experiments

Y. ALEX TUNG AND JAMES R. MARSDEN

Y. ALEX TUNG is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Management/MIS, College of Business, University of Nevada–Las Vegas. His received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Kentucky. His research interests include experimental analy ses of information value, business decision support systems and expert systems, data communication and networks, artificial intelligence in business applications, and MIS research across interdisciplinary functional areas. He has published in journals such as Management Science, Decision Support Systems, European Journal of Operational Research, Journal of Computer Information Systems, Journal of Business Research, Journal of International Business Studies, and Computers & Industrial Engineering.

JAMES R. MARSDEN is Professor and Head of the Department of Operations and Information Management, School of Business Administration, University of Con necticut. In addition he serves as Executive Director of the Treibick Electronic Com merce Initiative. He was formerly the Philip Morris Professor and Founding Chair of the Department of Decision Science and Information Systems, University of Ken tucky. He has held visiting positions at the University of North Carolina, University of Arizona, Purdue University, and University of York (U.K.). His research interests include information valuation, controlled laboratory experimentation, electronic mar kets, dynamic database restructuring, and intelligent systems.

ABSTRACT: Insider trading and asymmetric information have been the subject of a significant body of research since the 1960s. Yet little work has been directed at analyzing the impact of different market regulations. Along with difficulties in correctly identifying trades made on inside information, empirical field study methods have not been capable of analyzing the impact of different market regulations. We develop a controllable networked market trading environment that incorporates accurate iden tification of information possessed by each trader studied and that provides the flexibility necessary to analyze market impacts of different regulatory schemes to limit trading on inside information. We illustrate our methods through a series of controlled induced-value laboratory experiments using human subjects. Subject rewards are performance-based, with cash incentives tied to the outcomes of each market transaction. Experimental results indicate that markets with inside, privately informed traders led to greater trading volumes than markets with traders having access to private information only. In addition to reporting the results of initial sets of the experiments, we use these outcomes to frame future research issues involving the use of IT systems in surveillance and links between trading patterns and insider activity.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: asymmetric information, insider trading, laboratory experiments, market regulations, security market.

THE DEBATE ON INSIDER TRADING has been ongoing for several decades and continues today [17, 27]. In 1942, the Securities Exchange Commission (SEC) prohibited insider trading by promulgating Rule 10b-5 under the Security Exchange Act of 1934.<sup>1</sup> Three research issues continue to be of importance: (1) what impact do informed traders have on trading volumes in a market; (2) what observable information should trigger suspicion that insider activity is present; and (3) what is the impact of alternative regulation on insider trading activity? A considerable amount of research has been conducted on topics related to the impacts of the outlawed trading, with tradi tional empirical field studies reporting contradictory conclusions on whether insiders earn abnormally high returns [10, 25, 31, 39, 47, 48]. At the same time, there has been a paucity of research directed at the three issues listed above. We suggest the limitations of traditional empirical field studies may lie behind both the conflicting results and the paucity of studies concerning signals of insider trading activity and the ef fects of alternative regulatory mechanisms.

Traditional empirical field studies rely on data collected from sources such as Offi cial Summary of Security Transactions and Holdings or Stock Price Tape of the Cen ter for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) of the University of Chicago. While insiders are required to register their transactions, they might not accurately register their activity. They might intentionally use others to complete trades based on inside information. Others, besides those listed as insiders in the SEC regulations (such as board members or senior executives), may come to possess and utilize inside information. A chauffeur, a brother-in-law, or a friend may overhear a conversation or otherwise come to possess and utilize inside information (see “25 Charged with Insider Trading,” USA Today, May 27, 1999). In each case, standard sources based on reporting documents would provide no information on such transactions or returns. Empirical field studies utilizing available data sources face serious questions about whether they correctly identify the presence of insider trading or accurately analyze signals of such activity.

Researchers utilizing empirical data to study insider trading face limited opportunities to study regulatory changes. There have been analyses surrounding legal cases [20] and of one key legislative change, the Insider Trading Sanctions Act (ITSA) of 1984 [2]. The SEC does not rapidly or frequently alter security market regulations. This may reduce uncertainty in the markets, but it also drastically limits the ability of researchers to study alternatives.

In order to overcome the accuracy issues and to enable analysis of questions (2) and (3) above, we turned to the use of IT technology to structure and complete inducedvalue controlled laboratory experimentation [42]. Using the technology and the induced-value approach, we were able to initiate study of insider trading in a setting where we have direct knowledge of the nature of each trader’s knowledge set. In addition, the laboratory enabled us to study the relationship between regulatory op tions and trader activity. Field studies have not been able to tackle such analysis. Rule 10b-5 has simply not changed. Without identifiable variation, it is impossible to com plete the necessary comparisons using empirical field data.

The controlled laboratory setting enables us to accomplish two elements necessary to the intended analysis of insider activity and regulatory options: (1) accurate identification of those really possessing insider information, the nature of the information possessed, and their precise trading activity, and (2) the ability to impose alternative regulatory options. The laboratory capabilities enable us to consider the possibility of identifying relationships between insider activity, trading volumes, and alternative regulations.

As Smith [42] and Smith and Walker [44, 45] detail, induced-value laboratory experimentation involves real markets. The purchase and sale of assets results in rea monetary outcomes. We do not, however, attempt the impossible replication of actual markets. We strive to incorporate salient market features, and to achieve sufficient parallelism, so that the results have relevance for decision-making in the real market. The laboratory stock market we develop is a real market. Trading (asset allocation) decisions result in real monetary gains or losses. As in any abstraction or model, the inferences we draw remain subject to further test and refinement. The questions of when sufficient analysis has been completed and of when implementation should be recommended pose difficulties. Here we present a method and initial sets of results. These help us to frame and posit future necessary research. They do not come close to reaching the threshold of recommending implementation.

First, we briefly review related prior research. Next comes an introduction of our method, details of the developed market shell, and statements of our hypotheses. Then we present the experimental results and analyses, followed by a summary of our work and future research questions.

## Literature Review

THERE ARE TWO SETS OF RELEVANT LITERATURE: (1) field studies on insider trading, and (2) induced-value laboratory experimentation. Appendix 1 and Appendix 2 pro vide a list and brief explanations of key examples of each research direction. As we discuss below, field studies cannot address important theoretical questions regarding insider trading. Because of this, we turn to the induced-value experimentation as a method to analyze issues in insider trading.

As noted in our introduction, the body of empirical literature suffers from two difficulties: (1) the inability to identify true insiders, and (2) the lack of regulatory shifts. Despite the first problem relating to identifying true insiders, several authors have proceeded to analyze market efficiency, resulting in quite mixed outcomes. To date, however, little empirical research has been done on whether observable information (signals) can help indicate the presence of “true” insider activity or on the effects of market regulations on “true” insider trading. What does exist is limited to observations of registered insider activity. Jaffe [20] did such a study on the effects of three significant regulatory events concerning insider trading:

1. the Cady, Roberts decision (November 8, 1961)—penalized a “brokerage firm for trading with secret information on behalf of its clients . . . [and] established the precedent that corporate officials trading on inside information were liable for civil prosecution” [20]

2. the Texas Gulf Sulphur indictment (April 19, 1965), “indicted officials of Texas Gulf Sulphur for trading on, as well as suppressing, information related to the company’s vast mineral strike . . . since Cady, Roberts decision was merely an administrative ruling, the Texas Gulf Sulphur civil indictment can be viewed as an extension of regulatory law.” [20]

3. the Texas Gulf Sulphur decision (August 19, 1966), “upheld most of the counts of the April 1965 indictment . . . [and] showed insiders that the courts, as wel as the SEC, would punish speculation on special information.” [20]

Jaffe investigated both the profitability and trading volume of registered market insiders before and after each of these three important events and found no significant change in the properties of insider trading following any of these three events. A conclusion was made that “the null hypothesis that changes in regulation had no effect on the trading of insiders cannot be rejected.” Jaffe did note that there were other cases, including several Texas Gulf Sulphur Co. appeals, but emphasized tha the three studied were the important ones.

Arshadi and Eyssell [2] tried to test empirically the deterrent effect of the passage of legislation aimed at deterring insider trading—the Insider Trading Sanctions Act (ITSA) of 1984. They examined both the volume and the profitability of registered insider trading before and after the passage of ITSA. Their null hypothesis—“the passage of ITSA had no effect on the actions of registered insiders”—was rejected by their collected field data both from the trading volume and profitability points of view.

Work in induced-value controlled laboratory experimentation has included studies of differing market price models, asymmetric information, and new securities market trading mechanisms [5, 6, 29, 38]. Again, there has been a lack of studies focusing on signals identifying insider activity and the impact of changing regulations. Sunder’s chapter on experimental asset markets in Kagel and Roth’s The Handbook of Experi mental Economics [23] argues for the use of experimental analysis as a potentially valuable tool for studying “poorly understood aspects of asset market behavior,” in cluding insider trading and alternative regulatory policies.

We focus on developing and illustrating procedures to investigate the relationships between stock trading volume, varying degrees of inside information, and alternative regulations. To initiate structured investigation and discourse on alternative regulatory schema, our experiments include variations in monitoring rate and penalty (see below for explanation and precise definitions of these terms). We utilize advanced IT techniques to enable induced-value experimentation (which al lows us to control both the level and availability of inside information in a trading market), to control the regulatory climate, and to carefully track the trading volume for each stock under various inside information/regulation combinations. Our mar kets are electronic in nature and are characterized by asymmetric information. Our research shell enables us to easily change regulatory monitoring and penalty mecha nisms. In summary, our use of induced-value experimentation techniques provides the possibility of overcoming the data problems inherent in empirical field studies of insider trading.

## Method, Market Shell, and Hypotheses

## Method

INDUCED-VALUE LABORATORY EXPERIMENTS SERVED AS THE BASIC METHOD. The approach, pioneered by Vernon Smith [42], emphasizes the use of a reward structure incorporating sufficient monetary value to drive the experiments by motivating sub jects’ performance. Smith argued that well-designed laboratory experiments, which parallel the salient features of real world markets, can offer significant implications for theory development and testing. Smith posited that the results of laboratory ex periments can serve as an empirical pretest of economic theory prior to being sub jected to field testing. He also argued a dual relationship, with laboratory investigations being relevant to the structuring and interpretation of field data. In our analysis, we are using the laboratory to develop data and conduct analyses that we could not do in an empirical field study.

In order to motivate subjects to perform, Smith argued for a monetary reward structure to induce alternative decision choices in a given experiment. Following Smith, we used a direct monetary incentive structure in all of our experiments. The perfor mance payout to participants is directly related to the experimental profits individual subjects attain in the competitive experimental market structure. Each transaction has a financial result impacting total end-of-period profits.

## Market Shell

IT provided the means to conduct the desired analyses. We needed a networked sys tem that included the salient features of stock market trading activities and the ability to conduct market surveillance and impose penalties. Given the limitations of human subject experimentation, penalties were restricted to financial repercussions. Penal ties such as imposition of prison sentences or public embarrassment could not be studied. We developed our research shell to include electronic processes for passing messages, collecting information, performing direct calculations, and integrating ran dom components with direct numerical computations to determine market changes in stock prices, individual participant profits, and overall market performance.

A server machine plays the control role (the “SEC” in the real securities market) while client node machines support human market participants, that is, stock traders. The server (or monitor) responsibilities include: (1) registering market traders, (2) announcing opening and ending of trading periods, (3) complete recording of each keystroke occurring in the system with clock (time) linkage; (4) generation of random components of update elements, (5) recordkeeping of trades and market outcomes, (6) monitoring the market at times randomly generated from preassigned monitoring distribution with alterable mean monitoring rate, and (7) identification of “insider active” status and imposition of penalties. The client node machines pro vide an interface and decision support tools to the human subjects to support their market activities.

Initiation of a specific experimental use of the research shell includes specification of the number of traders (two to eight), initial cash (\$51,250) and stock portfolio endowments (250 shares for each stock), the number of stocks (from one to six), initial stock prices, the expected monitoring rate (e.g., five times per minute), and the financial penalty to be imposed if a trader is detected accessing inside information. Stocks were generically named using three repeated letters, for example, AAA, BBB, CCC, DDD, EEE, and FFF.

In each trading period, the individual client node machines present an interface that provides the following information, which is continuously updated:

1. statement of client’s current profit status (total net return in experiment’s previous trading periods)

2. previous and current period price of each available stock

3. amount of cash and amount of each stock held by the client

4. market transaction summary

5. listing of client’s personal current and previous period trading activity

6. public information (forecasts) for each stock

7. indication of availability of insider information for a stock (insider informa tion must be accessed by use of function [F9] key)

8. mean monitoring rate and penalty imposed if client is caught (electronically observed) accessing insider information [F9 key active]

## The interface is interactive, with subjects able to perform each of the following activities:

1. buy or sell stock shares (available cash must be sufficient to cover requested purchase; if cash is not sufficient, client may sell stock shares to generate cash for subsequent purchases)

2. access insider information (use F9 “hot” key—information remains in visible [pop-up] form until ESC key struck)

3. access history of stock prices (pop-up tableau)

4. access history of market transactions (pop-up tableau)

5. access history of personal transactions (pop-up tableau)

6. access market indices (pop-up graphics)

If a trader (client) attempts a stock purchase that cannot be covered by current cash available, an “insufficient funds” warning appears on the screen and must be cleared by the trader (the requested trade is withdrawn).

The interface includes capabilities to provide traders with two types of informa tion—public and private—relating to forecasts of individual stock performance. In each trading period, public (available to all) information is shown continuously in the information area. This information is similar to the sliding scale analyst recommendation available for free on the NASDAQ website. After significant pretesting, trial subjects indicated strong preference for information in simple numeric form corre sponding to the common five categories shown in Table 1.

When inside information is available to a trader (subject), the availability is noted in the information area window but the trader must strike the F9 key to activate display of this information. The inside information is in the same form as public infor mation, but, as explained below, is more reliable in predicting stock movements. As in actual financial markets, insiders are more likely to outperform the market when the more accurate inside information deviates most from public information. Consider the situation where, incorrectly, poor performance is expected because of publicly predicted low earnings expectations. Private information predicting excellent outlook (or strong earnings) can yield excellent dollar value upon announcement of strong rather than poor earnings. Similarly, if the public expects excellent earnings or an excellent stock value outlook, insiders with information indicating very weak earn ings are likely to gain from selling before such information is publicly revealed.

A trader can only access and display such information for the companies (stocks) for which the trader is an “insider.” When the F9 key is pressed and insider information is shown, the trader is considered in “insider active status.” The trader may exit “insider active status” by pressing the ESC key, which removes the inside information from visibility, returning the trader to “insider inactive status.” The server is programmed to monitor the system at times randomly generated using a normal distribution with mean equal to the expected monitoring rate. When electronic monitoring is operationalized, it performs an electronic “poll” and will detect any trader in active status. A specified monetary penalty is imposed if a trader is caught in “insider active status” by the monitor. If a trader can get in and out of insider active status without monitor surveillance having occurred (i.e., electronic monitoring operation activated) the individual escapes detection and penalty. As the expected rate of monitoring in creases, the likelihood of accessing inside information without being discovered and penalized diminishes. The shell enables us to easily alter both the monitoring rate and the financial penalty imposed if a trader is caught accessing inside information.

As in actual markets, private or inside information available to different individuals may not be of the same level of reliability. A cab driver may overhear that corporation AAA is likely to report strong earnings and the anticipated outcome is “fairly positive for AAA’s stock.” The CEO’s good friend may know that the merger agreement has taken place and a public announcement planned for tomorrow morning should have “very positive impacts on AAA’s share price.” We constructed our shell to enable inclusion of such different levels of inside information. In the experiments detailed below, we actually used two quality levels for inside information. We use “I” to represent higher-reliability inside information, “II” to represent lower-reliability inside information, and “X” to represent no private information (public information only). True signals are structured to take on values over the range 0–1. Type I information signals are drawn from a normal distribution (variance of 0.0279) centered at the true signal value. Type II information signals are drawn from a uniform distribution centered at the true signal with a range of 0.8 (± 0.4). Public information signals are drawn from a uniform distribution centered at the true signal with a range of 1.0 (± 0.5). Figure 1 shows how Type I, II, and X information signals are drawn from the true information. It can be seen from the figure that the relative accuracy level of information Type I, II, and X can be controlled by changing the variance of their individual distributions around the true signal. The 0–1 signal range was divided up into five intervals of 0.2, labeled from “poor” to “excellent.” Generated information signals that exceeded 1 or fell below zero were folded into the closest set.

End-of-period price adjustments are calculated based upon total purchase and sale orders of subject traders, the true information (representing earnings, merger activity, etc.) for each stock, and a random component. The latter two values provide outcomes reflecting the overall market. The true information is the major factor impacting price, followed by subject trader activity and, finally, a moderate random component to reflect external impacts. Consider an individual utilizing Type I (the higher-quality) inside information. This information is more likely than Type II or X to accurately reflect the true information for a given stock. Trades relying on this information are more likely to yield positive financial rewards. However, there is no guarantee that this will occur. In financial markets, even firms reporting excellent earn ings and strong merger or acquisition outcomes often have these results dwarfed by overall perception. This can occur if the chairman of the Federal Reserve speaks in cautionary tones or if an Asian government decides not to act in support of their banking industry. Our random market adjustment thus includes a proxy for such factors.

At the end of each trading period a summary appears on each trader’s screen. Each trader may review the market activities, his or her own activity, and the trading outcomes, including price changes and the trader’s own portfolio performance. After a brief time, the server (monitor) electronically announces the commencement of another period. An updated interactive interface is provided automatically to each sub ject trader. The market continues in the same way until the end of a predetermined number of experimental periods (20 periods, with 120 seconds per period).

Before providing details of the specific experiments reported on here, we briefly describe our research hypotheses.

## Hypotheses

We now illustrate the use of IT techniques to begin an analysis of inside information, trading volume, and regulation alternatives. As noted above, in our approach we are able to accurately identify the type of information available to each trader on each stock and the volume for each trader on each stock.

Our initial analysis also considers the possibility of identifying insider information level and insider activity by comparing trading volume. We use the experimental results to test each of the following three hypotheses:

$H _ { I } .$ On average, insider information stocks will have heavier trading than no insider information stocks.

$H _ { 2 } .$ On average, Type I insider information stocks will have heavier trading than Type II insider information stocks.

Our third hypothesis concerns the trading volumes and alternative regulations.

$H _ { 3 } .$ On average, traders will reduce their insider trading volume as regulations tighten, either making it more likely to be caught or imposing a greater pen alty when caught.

## Experiments and Analysis of Results

## Subjects

THERE WERE TWO SETS OF EXPERIMENTS—one in the United States and one in Taiwan. In the U.S. experiments, four groups of four subjects each were used. Mem bers of each group remained the same throughout the series of experiments and there were no common members across groups. There were no dropouts or absences (prob lems that had plagued previous noninduced-value experiments, see [28]). Group composition was as follows:

Groups 1 and Group 2—each comprised of four doctoral students (no common members). All members of the first group had an MBA degree. Three of the four members of the second group had an M.S. degree in an engineering discipline. The fourth member of this group had a masters degree in mathematics.

Group 3—comprised of four undergraduate business students, two majoring in accounting and two majoring in finance.

Group 4—comprised of four MBA students, two concentrating in finance and two who had not yet chosen a concentration.

The Taiwan experiments were used as a followup. There was one group of four subjects, all of whom were business students at a major national university.

## Structure of Experiments

In the United States, five different experiments were conducted for each of the four groups. The monitor’s surveillance rate and penalty for being caught accessing pri vate information for these five experiments were, in order, (5,50), (25,500), (50,500), (25,1000), and (50,1000). The private information accuracy level for every subject will be described using a sextuple row vector. The first element of each tuple provides the private information accuracy level for the first stock. The second through sixth elements provide the corresponding information for the second through sixth stocks.

In each of the experiments, every trader had two stocks on which they had Type I private information, another two stocks with Type II private information, and lastly, Type X or no private information for the remaining two stocks. We structured these different types of private information on different stocks in a way expected to facili tate our analysis. Table 2 provides a summary of the parameters used in our U.S. experiments.

In Taiwan, three different experiments were completed with surveillance and penalty values of (0,0), (50,1000), and (50,1000). The information structures were such that there was no insider information available for the (0,0) experiment, limited insider information available for the (50,1000) experiment, and broader insider infor mation for the (50,1000) experiment. Table 3 details the information structures used.

Attempting to satisfy the conditions Smith set out, we structured the reward mechanism with an average return of \$12.5, with a range of payouts from a low of \$5 (payment for showing up) to a maximum possible payout of \$30. There are numerous market portfolios for each participant in an experiment (equal to the total number of trading periods). The reward mechanism to induce participants’ efforts to maximize their profits was linked directly to participant performance relative to a market index, with each of the six stocks getting equal weight.

## Test of Hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 3 }$

$\mathrm { H } _ { 3 }$ indicates the following hypothesized trading volume orderings for the U.S. experiments’ regulatory “tightness,” or increasing monitoring rate, increasing penalty, or both:

$$
\text { Ordering   \#1:   Exp   1   (5,50) <   Exp   2   (25,500) <   Exp   3   (50,500) <   Exp   5   (50,1000) }
$$

$$
\text { Ordering   \#2:   Exp   1(5,50) <   Exp   2(25,500) <   Exp   4(25,1000) <   Exp   5(50,1000)}
$$

We performed a series of one-tail hypothesis tests, with the null hypothesis being that trading volume in the tighter regulatory experiment would be greater than or equal to that in the looser experiment (e.g., Exp 2 is a “tighter regulatory” setting than Exp 1). As one might expect from observation of Table 4, the tests provide no support for $\mathrm { H } _ { 3 } .$ While this surprised us somewhat, these outcomes may be tied to the limited alternatives we were able to study. Subsequent experimentation will include more stringent regulatory alternatives.

## Summary and Conclusions

IN THE EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS REPORTED HERE, we are focusing on differences in trading volume and not on performance of insider trading. Using carefully struc tured and controlled experimentation, we are able to track and compare trading volume in the same market on stocks with and without inside information. The results of the experiments in the United States are consistent with increased trading volume on stocks with inside information available. Further, this occurs across regulatory schema. An important aspect of our experiments is the capability to analyze, for the first time, outcomes as regulations change. Our results, however, indicate no regulatory schema impact on trading volume. Further study needs to be directed at considering the robustness of our initial results and determining whether we have not yet pushed far enough out in our regulatory schema or whether other regulatory approaches might yield real impact. The Taiwan experiments support the result of trading activity or volume increasing in the presence of insider information. In the experiment that paralleled the information structure of the U.S. experiments, however, the test results suggest no significant difference between the mean trading volumes of stocks with and without insider information. We suggested that thi outcome might be tied to two traders focusing their insider activity on one specific stock each. Subsequent experimental designs will be structured to sharpen our investigation of this possibility.

Information technology makes it possible for us to overcome limitations of empiri cal field studies. This is especially useful in topics such as insider trading, where the necessary empirical information is not available or is not reliable. In addition, the controlled experimentation approach used here enables the study of regulatory shift that are not feasible to study in the field.
