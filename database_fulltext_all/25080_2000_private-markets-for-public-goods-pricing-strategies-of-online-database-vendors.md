# Private Markets for Public Goods: Pricing Strategies of Online Database Vendors

LAWRENCE A. WEST JR.

LAWRENCE A. WEST JR. is an assistant professor at the University of Central Florida. He received a Ph.D. in MIS from Texas A&M University in 1991. His research inter ests include information economics, e-commerce, and decision support systems, es pecially the use of geographic information systems as DSS technologies. Specific interests that span all three of these areas are the roles of governments as producers and regulators of commercially valuable information and how the nature of electronic information affects its marketability. His previous publications have appeared in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Sciences, Academy of Management Executive, and other journals.

ABSTRACT: The online database industry has annual sales of US\$6.5 billion for a product that can be easily appropriated, duplicated, reused, and redistributed. This paper examines how the industry developed dynamic pricing and delivery strategies as a response to technological and market changes, and shows how each strategy specifically compensated for the public good properties of information. Readers will see that specific pricing strategies reduce the incentive to improperly reuse down loaded information. Thus, these strategies can lead to the sustainability and growth of the online database industry. These findings are then extended to the broader context of information delivery via the Internet.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information economics, information pricing, Internet information markets, online databases, public goods.

A widely retold story tells of a visitor to a fancy hotel who ordered the chef’s special chocolate cake in the dining room. The cake was wonderful and the visitor asked if she might have a copy of the recipe. The recipe was delivered, but, on checking out of the hotel, the visitor was startled to see a charge of \$100 for the recipe on her bill. She protested the charge, but the hotel manager said that they had every right to charge for a copy of the recipe, and since the visitor had seen the recipe there was no way the hotel could take it back. Incensed, the visitor decided to get even by making hundreds of copies of the recipe and giving them away to everyone she knew, along with the story of how she came by the information.

BY 1998 DATABASE VENDORS IN THE UNITED STATES HAD CREATED a US\$6.5 billion market for a product (including cake recipes) that, once provided to a buyer, can be copied and reused or recirculated by the buyer almost effortlessly. Revenues for sales of computer-readable information (CRI)<sup>1</sup> in the United States are projected to reach \$9.1 billion by 2002, after sustaining average compounded growth rates of 8.4 per cent from 1992 through 2002 [29]. Finally, this performance was achieved in an in dustry that did not exist before 1965 [21].

While the rapid growth in the online database industry is not unique in technology industries, two characteristics of the CRI market are of particular interest. First, there are important theoretical and practical impediments to creating a market for electronic information because of the ease with which such information can be replicated and distributed without the consent of the owner. Second, the growth of the Internet, whether measured by number of connected users or by number of information con tent providers, increases the importance of any topic connected with doing business via the electronic transmission of data.

This paper reports on how CRI vendors have used creative pricing strategies to protect their product from unauthorized replication and reuse. It models the market over time and shows how price structures adopted at various times were rational and effective responses to the technological conditions of the moment, and how new technologies and market practices led to new pricing strategies. The paper fills a gap in the literature by showing how a viable market for electronic information products was created and sustained despite the copying problem. The results provide a foundation for analyzing the pricing of specific CRI products and should be of interest to anyone interested in electronic information products.

The next section presents information on the nature of the information-sharing problem faced by the industry and how this theoretical impediment has materialized in a very real way in the CRI market. It is followed by a brief description of the major players in the industry and their interactions. Subsequent sections show how the in dustry achieved a viable market position in the early days of the industry and how technological turbulence created both problems and opportunities with respect to the public good problem. We examine strategies adopted by the industry over time, why these strategies were viable at the time, and why they became problematic in the face of turbulence. A technical appendix presents the analysis as mathematical models and is referred to throughout the paper.

## Information as a Public Good

THIS SECTION INTRODUCES THE CONCEPT OF PUBLIC GOODS and shows why the public good properties of CRI create impediments to marketing electronic infor mation. These ideas form a foundation for the discussion of industry practices in subsequent sections.

## Introduction to Public Goods

A pure public good (or service) is one that is marked by nonrivalrous consumption and nonexcludability[10, 18]. Nonrivalrous consumption means that the good is not depleted when consumed. That is, if I enjoy the benefits of a day at a public beach it does not detract from your ability to enjoy the same beach at the same time or at a later date (unless, of course, we are joined by so many others that crowding takes place). In contrast, if I consume a new sport utility vehicle I have the legal right to prevent you (in some states by application of deadly force) from consuming the same instantiation of the vehicle.

Nonexcludability means that it is impossible to prevent individuals who haven’t paid for a good or service from consuming it. If the beach from our previous example is public property, or in the absence of physical or legal access constraints, it is difficult to prevent the public from using it. It is only the erection of gates, toll booths, or some other method of control that creates the ability to exclude consumers if they haven’t paid for the right to use the beach.

## Information as a Public Good

Information is widely cited as an example of a public good (e.g., see [7, 27]) and the contributing characteristics are quite plain. Consider a person (Person A) who is in exclusive possession of an element of information, such as a bibliographic citation pertinent to a research topic. If Person A shares this information with a Person B, then both have the information and neither’s possession of the information is reduced by the other’s. Similarly, if the information is spread more widely the same effect holds. This behavior is an example of nonrivalrous consumption, an important characteristic of public goods.

Note that some information is not marked by nonrivalrous consumption, leading to the observation that information products are not pure public goods. If the informa tion that Person A possesses pertains to the future price of some other good (such as a stock or commodity), then the private possession of the information has a value to the possessor related to the change in value and the possessor’s ability to capitalize on the data. Once Person A shares the information with Person B, however, each is now in competition with the other to take advantage of the opportunity. They can bid up the price of the subject good and reduce the value of the information.

Nonexcludability becomes a factor because of the difficulty in controlling the spread of information. When Person A (e.g., the hotel in the introductory example) shared his information with Person B (our cake connoisseur) he effectively lost control over Person B’s disposition of the information. A similar problem is familiar to the MIS community in the issue of software piracy.

The manner in which information is instantiated plays an important role in determining its public good properties. There has been a centuries-old market for printed information because the printed version has physical and legal characteristics that allow its owner to protect it as if it were our aforementioned automobile. Medical and other professional educations take so long to acquire that there is little incentive for the average holder of the information to pass on a useful subset of the data to another. On the other hand, specific facts and easily replicated data, such as computer soft ware and CRI, have stronger public good properties and so create market problems.

## Implications of Information as a Public Good

In general, markets do not handle public goods well. The basic problem is that, be cause it is difficult for a seller to collect for his goods or services or to control secondary distribution, there is a reduced incentive for investing in the creation of the good. Three consequences may derive from this situation.

1. There may be no market for the good or service. This situation is called market failure and implies that even though there may be a demand for the good or service no seller will offer it.

2. The government may step in to provide the good or service. In many respects the whole purpose of government may be seen as the provision of goods and services (police protection, lighthouses, roads, etc.) for which an efficient pri vate market cannot exist. It is significant that governments at all levels are prodigious producers of information, including basic scientific research, weather data, commercial data, census data, etc. By 1987 the U.S. federa government had become the largest single producer of publicly accessible electronic databases in the world [33].

3. Modified and less efficient markets may be created to vend products with public good properties. Examples may include tying arrangements wherein a pri vate good, such as a parking lot near a public beach, is created as a revenue generator to capture demand for the public good. Advertising revenue pays fo the free good of public TV and subsidizes the production of other information products, such as newspapers, magazines, and Internet sites. Finally, certain goods can be sold with pricing strategies that account for the demand for paid versus free copies of the product. These strategies are evident in models of software sales in the face of piracy [4, 9].

CRI has both of the properties of public goods: nonrivalrous consumption and nonexcludability. Nonrivalrous consumption is created with modern database tech nology, dial-up network connections, and Internet access whereby many people can simultaneously access the same database. The access of one user does not impede the access of another until sufficient demand creates the electronic version of crowding.

Nonexcludability is the more serious problem. CRI may be copied by anyone with access to the data. Since the online sale of such information necessarily requires ac cess, this practice is a theoretical threat to the industry. In fact, the theoretical threat has materialized as a real problem in the form of downloading [15]. In downloading, a user executes a query leading to the retrieval of records, but the resulting stream of data is then stored locally on the user’s computer. Once stored, the data may be searched, manipulated, or otherwise used without the necessity of connecting to the host com puter and without making any further payment to the database provider. The records can also be circulated to other users.

The Online Database Industry

THE ONLINE DATABASE MARKET IS COMPOSED OF SEVERAL ELEMENTS, including producers, vendors, users, gateway services,<sup>2</sup> and search intermediaries.

Database Producers

Producers create the electronic versions of the information in databases and may or may not be the creators of the original (regardless of format) information content. Many databases arise as by-products of electronic typesetting and transaction processing systems operated by the producers, but some have been created strictly for the online market. Many databases are produced by government agencies, such as the Census Bureau or the National Library of Medicine, while others are produced by a multitude of private profit-seeking organizations. By 1998 there were 3,216 producers of 10,338 publicly accessible databases in the United States [32].

## Database Vendors

Vendors make databases available for public access. They operate computer systems with associated hardware and software to provide data communications, user interfaces, and billing services. Some vendors are also producers, some produce no files, and some vend their own files along with databases produced by others. A few pro ducers, such as the Chemical Abstracts Service, produce and vend their own databases and also make the files available to other vendors. Vendors pay outside database producers a royalty on revenues associated with the producers’file(s). By 1998 there were 2,115 online database vendors in the United States, up from 105 in 1975 [32].

## CRI Users

CRI users are the individuals and organizations who pay to retrieve information from vendors. They connect to the source of the data through a dial-up or other network connection or via the Internet, select a database product, formulate a search, and view the result set—the records returned by the system in response to the search. This process is typically iterative and may include searches of multiple database vendors, multiple databases, and repeated refinement of search terms as the results of successive searches are evaluated. Experience makes parts of the search process easier and reduces connect time, false hits (retrieved records that are not pertinent), and trial and-error searching.

## The Role of Government

As predicted by public goods theory, governments have been key providers of CRI. In 1977, governments or not-for-profit agencies produced 78 percent of all databases [32]. By 1997, however, this percentage had dropped to 20 percent, with the remainder be ing produced by commercial providers. These percentages indicate that forces initially kept the majority of CRI production out of the private market but that some change in circumstances has allowed a private sector market in electronic data to flourish.

## Database Pricing Strategies

The online database market has seen four pricing and delivery strategies during its existence. We will see that each of these strategies was a specific response to technological and market conditions and each mitigated the public good problems of CRI. Figure 1 is a timeline showing the relative sequence in which each strategy appeared, along with key contributing factors, such as modem speeds and the introduction of PCs, as well as statistics on the size of the market. The four strategies are [1, 19]:

Connect-Time Pricing: The primary charge is based on the amount of time the user is connected to a database. The most common variation is a two-part tariff involving one-time or annual subscription charges, or both, in addition to online charges.

Per-Record Charges: This scheme may include all of the charges in the connect-time scheme but also includes a fixed charge per record or other unit of content displayed to the user.

Value-Based Pricing: This scheme may involve all elements of the two preceding schemes but also includes charges for each search term used to formulate queries against a single database. Variations include charging for touching a database with a search term (informing the searcher that the touched database contains a record matching the search term) when searches use a multifile index.

Internet Delivery: Internet delivery may involve any of the above pricing schemes but includes two new components more related to delivery method than to pricing. First, Internet delivery of information can be supported by payas-you-go pricing. Users may use credit cards or Internet-based credit transfer systems to pay for services without being a member of or having a subscription to the service. Second, we will see that the browser-based delivery mode fo data retrieved via the Internet affects the user’s ability to download a copy of the data.

## Methodology

THE PRESENTATION THAT FOLLOWS uses economic modeling and comparative statics techniques from the field of industrial organization economics [5]. As is common in this field, profit- or welfare-maximizing behavior is assumed for each economic actor (decision-maker) and a model relating the factor to be maximized to other element of the environment is constructed. The decision-maker’s actions are then compared to the model’s predicted results. If the actual decisions are consistent with the model’s predictions for profit-maximizing behavior then the model is not rejected. Due to the diverse nature of the products, delivery methods, and commercial value of the industry’s product, the level of analysis must be the industry itself.

Comparative statics techniques also help to validate the model(s). If circumstances faced by one group of participants are different from those faced by another, then valid models should predict different optimizing behavior for each group. Similarly, changes in circumstances—such as new technologies, costs, competitor actions, etc.— over time should also lead to changes in behavior. If the models adequately predict these changes then they should not be rejected.

In this study, the technology available for accessing CRI will be seen to be a major factor influencing behavior. The technological changes will be incorporated into new models and the analysis will show why the change in industry behavior (pricing in this case) is consistent with actions predicted by the revised models.

This approach is useful because it assumes away factors that would be troublesome using statistical methods. For example, when studying the appropriateness of the aforementioned pricing schemes in an industry that sells data about topics from oilfield production to the latest in cancer research to Hollywood gossip, any statistical method would be confounded by diverse valuations and product definitions. This technique places a burden on both the author and the reader, though. The author must present, and the reader must accept, the validity of the models used. In particular, the reader must see how any assumptions used in the construction of the models simplify the presentation and focus attention on the issue at hand. When specific cases (e.g., a specific form or product) are considered, the applicability of the assumptions would then be taken into account.

## Connect-Time Pricing—The Base Case

THE FIRST COMMERCIAL ONLINE DATABASES APPEARED in the early to mid 1970s as in-house database search services began to extend service to customers beyond the boundaries of the vending organization [21]. From this period through the early 1980s, the predominant pricing strategy for database access was a two-part tariff involving a subscription charge and a connect-time fee. The subscription charge was usually an annual charge for use of the system, but the charge could be imposed as a one-time start-up fee. The connect-time charge was imposed for the actual time the user spent connected to the database. Variations of this two-part tariff included connect-time charges of zero giving a subscription-only pricing scheme, and subscription charges of zero giving a fee structure wherein users pay only for connect time. Other variations included discounts for off-peak usage, academic users, and volume usage [19]. The shaded areas of Figure 2 illustrate the user’s cost for a single search under connect-time pricing. A search consists of three time segments, query formulation and transmission time $( t _ { Q } ) _ { \ l }$ , computer processing time $( t _ { P } )$ , and the time to download and view the result set $( t _ { D } ) . ^ { 3 }$ The user pays price $p _ { c }$ for each unit of time in the search.

The way that database vendors avoided losses due to the public good properties of information was simple, and, if it weren’t for the technological changes to follow, almost uninteresting. Recall that through the early 1980s the only technology avail able for connecting to a database was the use of a dumb terminal connected to a central computer. The only way to capture data was dumping a listing of retrieved records to a printer. While some database producers or vendors attempted to limit the reproduction and redistribution of printed versions of downloaded data, most allowed fair use of the downloaded records [15]. In particular, users could not capture electronic copies of retrieved data. A repeated search of the data required a renewed connection to the database vendor’s system and a repeated payment for the service.

In this environment the connect-time pricing approach satisfied the needs of all participants. The industry existed and continued to expand as we saw earlier in Fig ure 1. Many industry participants from this period—users, producers, and vendors— remained in business and are still in business today. Further, connect-time billing was easy to implement by the vendors: Log-ins were routinely tracked by most main frame operating systems and little additional software was needed to convert these log-in records into bills.

## Per-Record Charging Schemes

BY THE MID-1980S TWO TECHNOLOGICAL ADVANCES HAD A CRITICAL EFFECT on the online database industry and resulted in a change in pricing practices. The two effects were the introduction of faster modems and the use of personal computers (PCs) as terminals for online searching. While only one of these changes directly influenced the public good properties of CRI, both led to changes in vendor pricing schemes and so will be discussed.

## Faster Modem Connections

Examination of the terms of the vendor profit function (Equation 3 in Appendix A2) indicates the importance of connect time as a determinant of vendor profits. Consider the mechanics of a transaction, however. Users connect to a database and billing begins. The user types in search criteria and executes the search. The computer per forms the search and returns the results. The user will examine the results and may modify and repeat the search, but at some point finishes and logs off, at which time billing stops. In this sequence of events, the transmission of the search results over a slow (300-baud) modem connection accounts for a significant portion of the overall connect time. Further, the user must remain connected to examine the search result or must dump the search results to a printer. The time needed for the user’s search formulation over the same line also contributed appreciably to billing. The amount of time needed to actually execute the search on the host computer was one of the least significant elements of the time involved.

Now consider a simple increase in modem speed from 300 to 2,400 baud, which cut all data transfer times by seven-eighths. Users spent less time awaiting downloads, resulting in cheaper costs, ceteris paribus, than before. If users were search ing for a specific fact (e.g., a specific bibliographiccitation or company performance report), then the search was accomplished faster and overall connect time was re duced. If the user was pursuing an open-ended search, then he was likely to search more. The marginal cost of a search had been reduced, leading to a lower threshold for the stopping-rule value of retrieved information. (See Appendix A3 for detailed development of this argument.) In either event, though, the user’s activity required a significantly higher proportion of the vendor’s high-cost CPU and secondary storage devices, while the vendor realized lower revenues associated with data transmission time.

## Use of PCs for Searching

Personal computers used as terminals for online search altered two other aspects of the pricing structure for vendors, producers, and users. Experienced searchers quickly learned how to formulate relatively complex queries offline, that is, in an editor available on their computer. They would then connect to the database, paste the query into the terminal session, await the results, and then break the connection. This technique allowed the user to browse the retrieved result set in the buffer of their terminal soft ware or save it to disk and browse it at their leisure. They could then, if necessary refine their search strategy, compose a new search query, and repeat the process.

Figure 3 illustrates the change in profitability attributable to faster modems and offline searching, with the reduced areas for query formulation and download time constituting the vendor losses. The net result was that a combination of prices that were profitable with 300-baud connections and no local storage capability were no longer sufficient to maintain profitable performance. Appendix A3 models these developments.

## PCs and Downloading

The use of PCs as terminals also introduced new capabilities that had a much more direct bearing on the subject of this paper—the ability to capture electronic information for reuse without the permission of the information provider. The essence of this capability was that virtually all software capable of emulating a terminal on a PC has the ability to capture the terminal session to a disk file. Once there, the file could be reformatted, duplicated, and reused at will. It was possible, for example, to formulate a search requesting the bibliographic citations for all articles ever published in a set of journals. Once captured, the results could be reformatted to be searchable on the user’s local machine without paying the database vendor for anything other than the original download. Since the original search required almost no time, the principal costs of this practice were the original subscription cost, the cost of any reformatting on the local computer, and the connect-time cost for transmission of the original data. The user then had free access to a stream of searches. To exacerbate the situation, several terminal emulation products or PC-based data management products provided tools to reformat the output of specific database vendors into locally searchable for mats [15]. Finally, users could obtain updates to their local files by periodically re running their queries with retrievals filtered by date and appending the new results to their existing data.

## The Vendor Response

The vendor response was predictable and addressed both problems outlined above: faster modem speeds and downloading. Many vendors shifted their pricing strategies to a three-tiered scheme with reduced connect-time charges, but had a major portion of the search cost allocated to a new component of price—a per-record charge for each retrieved record viewed by the user. Users entered search terms as before, but the initial response from the system was a count of the number of records retrieved from the search. Users would judge from this number whether the search was too wide or too narrow and refine as necessary. Once an acceptable result set size was found the user would elect to view the records, and they would be charged the per record fee.

This change affected downloading by dramatically increasing the cost of acquiring large sets of records in the hope that they would be useful in later searches. Figure 5- A illustrates the increased costs associated with downloading large result sets, and Figure 5-B shows that a repeated stream of searches yielding smaller result sets can now be the more economical choice. $p _ { \mathrm { ~ } c } ^ { \prime }$ is the new (lower) connect-time price, and $p _ { r }$ is the new per-record charge.

An important effect of this change was that a substantial portion of vendor revenue, the per-record charges, became insulated from the technical turbulence that caused the initial problems, since these revenues were not dependent on connect time. Fur ther, users could remain online longer, since the connect-time charges had been re duced. One study of the policy’s effect on users found that the new pricing scheme resulted in a slight increase in the price of a typical search but that the change was within the range of normal rate increases [32]. With respect to the effect on down loading, one industry observer wrote:

## Value-Based Pricing

ONE OF THE FINDINGS IN APPENDIX A3 is that a user’s incentive to improperly reuse data is reduced in direct proportion to increases in the value of services provided by the host system. (See the discussion leading to the finding that $\lambda ^ { * } = - 1$ following Equation 6 in Appendix A3.) While the per-record charges were seen as the main tool for discouraging downloading, increasing the value of online use of database prod ucts also contributed to this end, while providing competitive advantages to the database vendors. The value of online use can be increased in a number of ways, including reducing search costs, increasing search effectiveness, maintaining database currency, and increasing the breadth of database products offered by a single vendor [2].

One hallmark of the online database industry from the 1980s onward has been the steady improvement in the quality of the products offered. Databases have become more comprehensive; interfaces have been improved; faster and more powerful equipment has been installed; and more powerful search, retrieval, and display options have been implemented.

However, two problems have been associated with the combination of per-record pricing and the increases in vendor and producer investment. First, experienced sys tem users proved to be very creative at designing search strategies to minimize their costs (and therefore vendor and producer revenues). These techniques included use of offline search preparation and use of the free formats (usually title only, with no way to retrieve the full record without paying) offered by many services [16, 26]. Users essentially minimized query time, retrieval time, and per-record charges by doing as much work as possible offline and refining their search to get the result set with the fewest unnecessary records. When they actually retrieved the records, they paid for the desired information but used an unexpectedly (from the vendors’ points of view) high proportion of the high-cost system resources and a lower proportion of the lower-cost (and higher-margin) input/output features.

Second, there was a free rider problem between producers and vendors. Either of these parties had the ability to install value-enhancing features on either the database (producers) or the online service (vendors). If either party took such an action, however, they shared the increased benefits with the other, while absorbing the entire cost of the improvement. If a producer, for example, installs a value-adding feature (such as additional fields offered per record) resulting in increased use, the producer only realizes a small portion of the increased revenues (see the discussion associated with Equation 4 in Appendix A2). The producer therefore had a reduced incentive to innovate. Further, if the licensing agreement had differing royalty percentages for infor mation retrieval and connect time, it was possible for either party to implement system enhancements that skewed the distribution of revenue. For example, some vendors began including a meta-index that allowed the user to determine the likely results of searching various databases before selecting any single database for use. Since producers only received royalties when their database was selected, they lost the revenue associated with the users’ preliminary explorations.

The effects of these circumstances depended on elasticity of demand. The issue was whether lower prices increased use sufficiently to produce increased revenue. Inelastic demand (such as searches for a specific fact) did not increase searching, and revenues fell. While this issue is worthy of further research, the fact remains that both producers and vendors grew increasingly dissatisfied with mixed pricing. In some cases, such as the Chemical Abstracts Service (CAS) license policy revision (see discussion following) and the ESA/IRS price restructuring [12, 17, 23], dissatisfaction turned to action.

Other pricing algorithms went beyond the simple connect-time/record-retrieval schemes to address this issue [19, 23], and the trade literature of the late 1980s to early 1990 called for new pricing methods [1, 16, 23].

The next two subsections discuss illustrative examples of actions taken by major vendors in response to these problems. These responses will be seen to be consistent with a profit maximizing model, to protect vendors from downloading, and to be reasonable responses to the specific technological and business problems discussed here. Each of these new strategies is still in use by the two vendors today, and other large vendors have adopted variations as well.

## The CAS Responses

The Chemical Abstracts Service (CAS) is an operation of the American Chemical Society that has indexed and abstracted literature in the fields of chemistry and chemical engineering for decades. In 1987 CAS shocked the online industry with the unilateral imposition of a new pricing standard on all vendors carrying the CAS databases [24, 25]. CAS reduced connect-time charges but imposed new charges per search term used and for viewing partial records in addition to per-record charges [19].

CAS determined that the value it provided was both the viewed records themselves and the mapping from search terms to the records of interest. Through its indexing and abstracting operations, the service helped users avoid the costs of irrelevant output and missed information. Thus, CAS charged the relatively high prices for search terms, especially for chemical structures, since this capability insulated the user from a significant cost for retrieving unneeded records.

## The ESA/IRS Response

The European Space Agency/Information Retrieval Service (ESA/IRS) is offered by the ESA consortium of countries to provide information services to members. The service displays many of the characteristics of a for-profit organization, and its prices, before the rate change, were roughly comparable to those of Dialog, a U.S.-based for profit vendor offering similar products.

In early 1989 the ESA/IRS initiated a new rate schedule with four types of charges [12, 16]:

1. A housekeeping charge of about \$10/hour based on connect time.

2. A file charge of between \$4 and \$6 per file searched, depending on the file. This charge was assessed one time when a user entered a database but was independent of the length of time logged to the product or the level of search conducted.

3. A nominal search charge for inspecting portions (selected fields) of a search result. This charge allowed the user to refine the search without having to pay much for the information with which to evaluate search effectiveness.

4. A much higher charge for each full record retrieved.

## Internet Delivery of CRI

Contracts with producers were renegotiated so that producers received none of the housekeeping charge but received a much higher share of all database-related charges, session charges, and retrieval/display charges. A stated goal of the algorithm was to have producers receive roughly the same amount of revenues as before, though they were computed differently [17].

## Implications

Vendor and producer efforts that helped reduce the incentive to download databases had the unwanted effect of disrupting the flow of revenues, usually to database producers. Both the CAS and ESA/IRS actions addressed these disruptions while further weakening the incentive to illegally download records. The new pricing schemes had effects in three areas:

1. Both schemes reduced the costs of online searching while further increasing the cost of downloading records. This lowered the incentive for downloading discussed in Appendix A3 (see the discussion associated with Equation 7) while reducing the revenue distribution impediments to improving service in the online version of the databases. Recall that these service improvements also reduced the incentive to download.

One might think that the high price charged by CAS for chemical structure searching would increase the incentive for downloading and private search of the pirated records. However, the index itself was not downloadable and was a very expensive component of the overall database. In order to pirate the database and achieve similar local search capabilities a user would need to pay the new higher per-record charges for the database and replicate the functionality of the chemical structure index.

2. The new pricing schemes had the effect of further insulating both producers and vendors from the effects of technological turbulence and protecting each from actions by the other. For example, providing revenues to database producer whenever their database was searched, whether or not records were retrieved, insulated producers from the effects of better search engines created by vendors. The same can be said for fees for partial record display and enhanced indices.

3. The ESA/IRS and CAS pricing schemes served to allocate revenues to the party most closely associated with the creation of the service being billed. While the system was not perfect, it moved closer to an ideal economic world in which returns are tied to service.

Of interest in this paper is the impact of Internet access on the public good properties of CRI. It turns out that Internet delivery reduces the incentive to pirate data fo unauthorized reuse through two mechanisms.

The first mechanism is prosaic but effective. Most Internet search engines display one page of results at a time and require the user to manually browse through successive pages to view the entire result set. If the result set is large, this manual intervention will not be trivial and has the effect of increasing the cost of downloading, as modeled in Equations 5, 7, or 10 of Appendix A3. A small result set $[ B _ { t } ( \ddot { r } )$ in the same equations] is less likely to provide the long-term value needed to tip the constraint conditions in favor of downloading.

Second, in older systems data was returned to the terminal or PC as text. This display was relatively easy to capture to disk and then parse into database fields on the user’s local computer. The presence of HTML tags in the output stream—including the potential presence of constantly changing advertising banners, instructions, etc.— increases the complexity of a parsing program running on the user’s computer. Thi factor increases the cost of processing the pirated result set for local reuse (c in Equa tion 10), leading to a reduced likelihood that the illegal activity would be worthwhile.

## Conclusions and Limitations

## Conclusions

THE MOST IMPORTANT CONCLUSION FROM THIS RESEARCH is that the theoretical im pediments to creating efficient markets for electronic information have been realized in practice but that pricing strategies adopted by sellers helped to overcome the prob lem. Purveyors of electronic data were able to initiate their businesses in a technological environment that precluded unauthorized reuse of data but, as technologies advanced, the public good properties of CRI appeared as real problems. The paper showed how CRI vendors used pricing strategies to mitigate the downloading problem and, coincidentally, to establish multipart tariffs that more closely approach the profit-maximizing techniques presented by Oi [22]. The implication of these findings is that a viable market for CRI can exist despite the public good properties of the product and, as indicated by the growth in the market as a whole and in the proportion of private sector database producers, the finding is borne out in practice.

Of particular interest is the finding that increasing service levels in data products reduces the incentive to download. This finding is important because it insulates CRI products from unauthorized reuse in almost any technological environment. As long as vendors can provide services that cannot be duplicated on local systems, and as long as these services provide significant value to users, there will be little incentive for users to misappropriate data. The fact that these service enhancements also provide competitive advantages vis-à-vis other sellers is a serendipitous outcome for vendors.

A second finding of the research is that, in the face of technological change, all market participants (users, vendors, and database producers) have continuously adapted their behavior to participate in the market in the most advantageous manner. This finding is no surprise to researchers in economics: Self-interest is commonly assumed of all participants. It should be of considerable interest to MIS researchers, though, to see a new example of how technology changes and participant behavior revolve around the issue of self-interest. It will be especially important for any participant in a technology-intensive market to consider the changes in pricing, incentives, and product characteristics introduced by technological turbulence.

Finally, the paper showed that there is nothing magical about the CRI market. Even though the public good properties of information created problems, the problems and solutions were in line with economic principles and an assumption of profit-maxi mizing behavior.

## Limitations and Future Research

The paper referred to various prices, costs, and demands (usage) with wild abandon, but with virtually no reference to specific values for these figures. How should prices for various information products be set? Should the citation for a legal opinion on tort law be priced the same as a reference to a chemical structure? What about a reference to appellate opinions in capital criminal cases? Recall that the objective of the paper was to show how the public good properties of CRI affect the market for this impor tant product. Recent research has addressed several issues related to CRI pricing, including the role of bundling multiple information products [2], the relationships between Internet content providers and Internet access providers [11], price as a com petitive tool for information goods [20], and the impact of small-scale sharing of information goods [4]. Additional research is in order to address several other issues, including:

 How are specific CRI prices set and why is average-cost pricing so prevalent in the industry?

 Why was CAS able to exercise market power when most database producers lacked similar options?

 How does information product bundling affect pricing and competition in the CRI market, and what determines the products to be bundled? Bakos and Brynjolfsson [2, 3] have work in this area but do not incorporate the public goods problem in their analysis.

 What will be the role of governments in the CRI market as technologies, market size, and industry practices remove barriers to private markets for CRI [31]?

 How will the Internet increase the size of the CRI market, and how will the larger and more diverse market affect its pricing, organization, and performance [11]?

Understanding these issues—especially the growth in demand driven by Internet access to CRI—will be key to understanding the future of this industry. It is also necessary, though, that the issues addressed in this paper be well understood before further research can progress. It is hoped that this work has contributed to this end.

## A3. Modeling the Shift to Per-Record Charging

This section of the appendix models two effects in succession: the impact of PCs and faster modems on costs, and the ability of PCs to enable unauthorized downloading and reuse of data.

Cost Impacts of PCs and Faster Modems

The model assumes that users select an optimal level of search, $t _ { Q } ^ { * }$ , in a terminalbased, slow-modem search environment, and that overall demand and pricing yield positive profits for vendors and database developers.

Consider the impact of an eightfold increase in modem speeds (from 300 baud to 2400 baud) and the user’s ability to formulate queries and review results offline (avoiding connect charges during this period). For any level of search the billable time and transmission cost for formulating queries $( p _ { c } t _ { Q } )$ and for receiving the result of a query $( p _ { c } t _ { D } )$ are reduced, giving a lower marginal cost for any amount of searching. The new optimal level of search, $t _ { \textit { Q } } ^ { \prime * }$ , will take one of two forms, depending on the user’s demand for information, but either form has adverse consequences for the vendor’s profit function.

If the user’s demand for information is continuous, with marginal returns to infor mation declining continuously, then the user will elect a new optimal level of search activity, $t _  \mathrm { ~ \} Q } ^ { \ast }$ , strictly greater than the old level of search activity, $t _ { Q } ^ { * }$ , yielding $r ^ { \prime } \ast$ strictly greater than $r ^ { * }$ , the amount of information retrieved before increased modem speeds. The calculation of vendor profits remains as given in Equation 3, but the reformulation above indicates the nature of the vendor’s problem. An increased proportion of connect time now requires use of the vendor’s expensive CPU and secondary storage devices, and less time is associated with data transmission. If the vendor’s CPU capacity is up to the task then the technology impact will be slight. If demand increases sufficiently, however, then the vendor is faced with congestion. The remedy for this problem is to increase investment in capacity, resulting in a loss of profit. Further, vendors realize a severe reduction in connect-time revenues formerly associ ated with communicating search results $( p _ { c } t _ { D } )$ . Finally, the proportionate mix of connect-time charges has also been skewed, though to a lesser degree, by the ability of searchers to formulate searches offline on a PC and then to connect and transmit the search criteria quickly to the host computer.

The second problem comes about if the user’s demand for information is discontinuous or if the marginal value of additional quantities of information beyond $r ^ { * }$ decreases sharply. Both of these circumstances could arise, for example, if user information demand centers around answering a particular question where, once the answer is found, there is no improvement (and therefore no value) accruing from additional information. If the user continues to find his answer with $r ^ { * }$ records, then no additional search is undertaken despite the fall in effective prices. The vendor is then out revenues for seven-eighths of the transmission time associated with $r ^ { * }$ based on the existing price structure. Since transmission time is an important component of connect time, the effect can be a substantial drop in revenues.

## Downloading

Now consider the ability of the PC to capture displayed data and store it to disk. Once the data is captured, the user can, with some degree of cost and effort, create an application to search the copied database and avoid paying connect-time charges for future use of the online source. The motivation to download can be illustrated by considering the user’s relative benefits from participating in or avoiding this activity. If a user downloads, he appropriates some subset of the target database into a personal system and obtains some stream of benefits from the possession and use of this collection. Call the records that make up this subset r¨. The user seeks to

$$

$$
\begin{array}{l l} S. Faster telecommunication speeds result in a decrease in $t _ { c } ( \ddot { r } )$ and $p _ { c } t _ { D } ( r _ { t } )$ . Depending on the number of repeated retrievals of the same information from the online version of the database, and the number of unneeded records included in $\ddot { r } ,$ the downloaded result set, the relationship between $p _ { c } t ( \ddot { r } )$ and $p _ { c } t _ { D } ( r _ { t } )$ could influence the user’s choice either way. ## The Vendor Response

The vendor response to user tactics was to introduce a new pricing scheme that imposed a direct cost on records retrieved. This policy is illustrated in new user benefit function:

$$
\pi_ {u} = B \Big [ r (t _ {Q}) \Big ] - p _ {c} (t _ {Q} + t _ {P} + t _ {D}) - p _ {r} r\tag{8}
$$

The new cost term, $p _ { r } r ^ { r }$ is the per-record charge imposed by the vendors.

With respect to the effects of faster modems and offline searching, this new term appears in a revision to Equation 2, the breakeven point determination of system use:

$$
\frac {\partial \mathrm{B}}{\partial \mathrm{r}} \frac {\partial \mathrm{r}}{\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}}} = \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{c}} \left(1 + \frac {\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{P}}}{\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}}} + \frac {\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{D}}}{\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}}}\right) + \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{r}} \frac {\partial \mathrm{r}}{\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}}}\tag{9}
$$

First, the cost of searching, processing, and downloading $( p _ { c } )$ has been lowered, leading to a higher level of search than that established in the equilibrium conditions of Equation 2. Second, while the user’s main decision criterion is still the amount of search conducted, $t _ { Q }$ , the user makes a specific decision as to when the per-record charges are incurred. Users lower costs on searching but have new and relatively high costs for retrieved records.

The new charge affects downloading. Consider again the constraint expressed in Equation 7. Adding the new cost term to this constraint yields

$$
\begin{array}{r l r} \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {\infty} \frac {B _ {t} (\ddot {r}) - B (r _ {t})}{(1 - \delta) ^ {t}} & \geq & p _ {c} ^ {\prime} t (\ddot {r}) + c _ {f} + p _ {r} \ddot {r} \\ & & - \sum_ {t = 0} ^ {\infty} \frac {p _ {c} ^ {\prime} [ t _ {Q} (r _ {t}) + t _ {P} (r _ {t}) + t _ {D} (r _ {t}) ] + p _ {r} r _ {t}}{(1 - \delta) ^ {t}} \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

Two effects are associated with the pricing change. The introduction of $p _ { r } \ddot { r }$ on the right side of the constraint means that the user must now pay a per-record charge for the entire set of downloaded records. If, as is common, the ratio of useful records to total records is low, this change will narrow the range of conditions where the constraint will hold. While these per-record charges are partially offset by lower connect time charges for the initial acquisition and per-record charges $( p _ { r } r _ { t } )$ for the alternative stream of incremental online searches, the reduced cost of the incremental search process also reduces the incentive to pirate the database.

## A4. Modeling Value-Based Pricing

The change to value-based pricing adds several new terms to the models of user, vendor, and producer behavior. This section updates prior models to include the new terms, and discusses participant behavior with respect to the updated models.

Modeling User Behavior

Consider a new user benefit model:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \pi_ {u} = B \Big [ r (t _ {Q}, k, s, r _ {e}) \Big ] - p _ {c} (t _ {Q} + t _ {P} + t _ {D}) - p _ {r} r \\ - \sum_ {k} p _ {d k} - p _ {s} s - p _ {e} r _ {e} \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

The summation over available databases, k, of $p _ { d k }$ is the database access charge, $p _ { s }$ is a search term charge, and $p _ { e } r _ { e }$ represents the charges for partial records viewed. Remember that partial records provide enough information to allow the user to determine if the full record charge should be paid, but not enough to satisfy the needs of most searchers. Also notice that users now optimize with respect to new choices in addition to time spent formulating searches. The number of databases to be searched and the number of partial records to be viewed are chargeable elements of the user’s decision space.

Users continue to search until the marginal benefit of an additional record equals the per-record cost of that record or until overall costs exceed overall benefits:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \frac {\partial \mathrm{B} [ \mathrm{r} (\cdot) ]}{\partial \mathrm{r}} \frac {\partial \mathrm{r}}{\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}}} = & \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{r}} + \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{c}} \left(1 + \frac {\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{P}}}{\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}}} + \frac {\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{D}}}{\partial \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}}}\right) \\ S. T. & \quad \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{c}} (\mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}} + \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{P}} + \mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{D}}) + \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{r}} \mathrm{r} + \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{dk}} + \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{s}} \mathrm{s} + \mathrm{p} _ {\mathrm{e}} \mathrm{r} _ {\mathrm{e}} \\ & \leq \mathrm{B} [ \mathrm{r} (\mathrm{t} _ {\mathrm{Q}}, \mathrm{k}, \mathrm{r} _ {\mathrm{e}}) ] \end{array}\tag{12}
$$

The continued reduction in per-record charges will, in the absence of effects from the constraint in Equation 12, lead to a higher level of search if the demand for information is elastic. Searchers can use the new, relatively low-cost, expenditures for connect time $( p _ { c } )$ and partial-record extracts $( p _ { e } )$ to reduce the size of r by eliminating unneeded records. With inelastic demand search will remain relatively fixed.

Experienced searchers limit their search when the marginal benefit of the incremental record equals the marginal cost of the record (the equality in Equation 12). Experience will lead them to minimize the database access charges by knowing which databases are most likely to yield results [13]. They will also use effective and proven search strategies to minimize the set of partial records $( r _ { e } )$ . The continued lower connect-time charges will support refining searches until a reasonable number of records has been identified. Then they will incur charges for a partial-record set. This set will be viewed and the final result set (leading to per-record charges, $p _ { r } r )$ will be selected.

Less experienced searchers may be more likely to be governed by the constraint inequality in Equation 12. They are likely to incur higher database, search-term, and partial-record charges as they experiment to find an effective search strategy.

## Vendor and Producer Revenues

Vendor revenues now accrue from the expanded set of charges received from users, but are also reduced by a richer set of fees paid to database producers:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \pi_ {\mathrm{v}} & = \sum_ {\mathrm{i}} \left[ p _ {\mathrm{c}} (t _ {\mathrm{Qi}} + t _ {\mathrm{Pi}} + t _ {\mathrm{Di}}) + p _ {\mathrm{r}} r _ {\mathrm{i}} + \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} p _ {\mathrm{dki}} + p _ {\mathrm{s}} s _ {\mathrm{i}} + p _ {\mathrm{e}} r _ {\mathrm{ei}} + p _ {\mathrm{fi}} \right] \\ & - \sum_ {\mathrm{k}} \sum_ {\mathrm{i}} \left[ \alpha_ {\mathrm{c}} p _ {\mathrm{c}} (t _ {\mathrm{Qi}} + t _ {\mathrm{Pi}} + t _ {\mathrm{Di}}) + \alpha_ {\mathrm{d}} p _ {\mathrm{dki}} + \alpha_ {\mathrm{r}} p _ {\mathrm{r}} r _ {\mathrm{ki}} + \alpha_ {\mathrm{s}} p _ {\mathrm{s}} s _ {\mathrm{ki}} + \alpha_ {\mathrm{e}} p _ {\mathrm{e}} r _ {\mathrm{eki}} \right] \end{array}\tag{13}
$$

This expression is the result of adding new revenue and cost terms to the simple model first introduced in Equation 3. Revenues are the sum of connect-time charges, record charges, database-access charges, search-term charges, and record-extract charges. Most of these revenue elements have a corresponding payout percentage to the database producer. The database producer’s revenue become

$$
\begin{array}{r} \pi_ {p} = \sum_ {j} \sum_ {i} \Big [ \rho_ {j c} p _ {j c} (t _ {Q i} + t _ {P i} + t _ {D i}) \\ + \alpha_ {d j} p _ {d j i} + \alpha_ {r j} p _ {r j} r _ {i j} + \alpha_ {s j} p _ {j s} s _ {j i} + \alpha_ {e j} p _ {e j} r _ {e j i} \Big ] \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

## Analysis

Recall that the impetus to adopt value-based pricing schemes was not specifically driven by downloading concerns. By preserving the per-record charges at approximately the same level as in previous schemes, the effects of these charges on preventing downloading remain in place. What the new system does accomplish is to recapture revenues that had been dissipated by the presence of database enhancements. Note, for example, that the use of partial-record examination $( r _ { e } )$ and enhanced indices, metadata (multidatabase) indices, and the search terms, s, reduces the size of the retrieved result set and associated revenues $( p _ { r } r$ in Equations 11, 13, and 14). Even though the mechanism for the reduction was the elimination of irrelevant records, the retrieval of these records was previously an important source of revenue. Capturing new revenue associated with these enhancements helped to offset declining revenues. Perhaps most importantly, the new charge terms served to reduce the free rider problem between vendors and producers. Enhancements implemented by either party could generate revenue according to the negotiated split between each party $( \rho _ { c } , \rho _ { r } , \rho _ { e } , \rho _ { s } )$ Finally, the enhancements increased the value to the user of using the online service and so helped reduce the incentive to download.
