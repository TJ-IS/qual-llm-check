# Software Editions: An Application of Segmentation Theory to the Packaged Software Market

SRINIVASAN RAGHUNATHAN

SRINIVASAN RAGHUNATHAN is a faculty member in the School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas. He obtained his Ph.D. in business administration from the University of Pittsburgh. He taught at Bowling Green State University prior to joining UT-Dallas. His current research interests lie in the role of information technology in supply chain management, and in the economics of information goods, the areas in which he has published papers in several journals.

ABSTRACT: Introducing multiple editions of the same software is a relatively recent innovation in the software market. The editions serve to differentiate among different user segments. Introduction of similar low- and high-end products in other markets has been analyzed using segmentation theory. However, the software market is fun damentally different from other product markets in two respects: (1) Software is char acterized by negligible marginal production cost, and (2) the option of offering upgrades also exists. We analyze the problem of software introduction using segmentation theory. Our analysis shows that if cannibalization is low, the vendor should introduce the full software as one edition. This result differs from that obtained in prior research, which showed that the seller should introduce two distinct products in such cases. When cannibalization is high, introducing multiple editions simulta neously is optimal under a variety of conditions. The strategy of introducing a highend edition in the first period followed by the low-end edition in the second period is optimal only when the consumers are extremely impatient and the software is large. A significant result of our analysis is that offering upgrades is clearly superior to other strategies only in a very restricted range of parameters. Our analysis also suggests that the vendor’s profit is higher when it announces the future strategy. Our theoretical results are supported by evidence from the software market.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: economics of software, information goods, market segmentation, software editions, software introduction, software marketing, software pricing.

WHEN MICROSOFT LAUNCHED ITS OFFICE 97 SOFTWARE, it introduced two editions: standard and professional. It introduced four editions—control creation, learning, professional, and enterprise—of its Visual Basic (VB) 5.0 software. Oracle Corporation introduced the low-end Oracle 7.0 “Lite” edition after the regular edition. Powersoft Corporation sold three editions—desktop, professional, and enterprise— of its Powerbuilder 5.0 software. The software editions are different subsets or partitions and are targeted at different consumer segments. For instance, the regular edition of Oracle 7 offers the PL/SQL language—an extension to the structured query language (SQL)—and is targeted at application developers. Oracle 7 Lite doesn’t offer PL/SQL. It is targeted at users who want to deploy applications.

The introduction of high- and low-end products is common in other markets too. Moorthy and Png [11] provide several examples for products such as automobiles, cameras, books, and computers. The theory of segmentation (grouping consumers according to their characteristics) and consumer self-selection (selection of the product that offers the maximum surplus to consumers) has been used to analyze the impact of marketing strategies for these products. Intuition suggests that the same theory may be applicable to software too. However, it is not clear whether the same results apply to software, because of two fundamental differences between software and other products. First, software has negligible marginal production cost (which often is substantial for other products). For instance, according to one estimate, the cost of producing a printed set of Encyclopaedia Britannica is between US\$200 and 300, whereas the cost of producing a CD-ROM containing the same information is \$1.50 ([7], page 150). The marginal cost rapidly approaches zero if the software is distributed over the Internet. Second, a low-end software edition can be upgraded to a high-end edition. Upgrades often are infeasible in other products.

Our goal in this paper is to apply the theory of segmentation and consumer self selection to the software market and to analyze the optimal software introduction strategies. The results are used to (1) provide insights to software vendors in edition design and in formulating introduction strategies, (2) explain some of the empirical observations regarding software editions, and (3) explain the differences in optimal strategies of software and other products. We perform the analysis for a market consisting of two consumer segments: high and low. The high segment values the soft ware more than the low. For instance, business users and home users may be the high and low segments, respectively.

## Prior Research

The segmentation and product design literature generally assumes that products of varying “quality” are targeted at different segments and that the marginal production cost increases with quality. Much of this research is based on Mussa and Rosen’s seminal work [12]—later generalized by Moorthy [10]—on the theory of market segmentation based on consumer self-selection. The major insight gained through this theory is that the seller’s inability to identify a consumer’s type may cause a reduction in profit from existing products when new products are introduced—a phe nomenon known as cannibalization. The seller can reduce cannibalization by de grading the low-segment quality, by enhancing the high-segment quality, and by designing an appropriate price mechanism [15]. Moorthy and Png [11] showed how the sequence of product introduction, in addition to quality, can be exploited to reduce cannibalization. Our model and approach follow those of Moorthy and Png [11] closely. However, we adapt their model to software editions. Padmanabhan et al. [13] analyzed the effect of information asymmetry between seller and consumer regarding network externalities on product introduction strategies. Other significant works include Dobson and Kalish [5] who considered fixed costs in the problem of simultaneous introduction of a product line, and Wilson and Norton [16] who con sidered the problem of optimal entry timing for introducing new products in an exist ing product line.

Recently, Bakos and Brynjolffson [1] analyzed the impact of zero marginal cost of information goods on their pricing and marketing. They showed how bundling of information goods such as software can increase seller profits and eliminate market inefficiencies. While Bakos and Brynjolffson addressed the issue of bundling differ ent products, we deal with the seemingly opposite issue of splitting a single software into multiple editions to cater to the needs of multiple segments.

## Our Findings

Our analysis shows that if cannibalization, which depends on relative segment sizes and valuations, is low, the vendor should introduce the full software as one edition. This result differs from that of prior research, which showed that the seller should introduce two distinct products under low-cannibalization conditions. The explanation for this difference lies in the respective cost structures. As we stated earlier, software is characterized by zero marginal production cost. When cannibalization is low, the introduction of a low-end edition does not reduce the revenue from the high end edition enough to offset the revenue from the low-end edition. As a result, the vendor will find it profitable to increase the number of features in the low-end edi tion. In other products, the marginal cost of production prevents the low-end quality from equaling that of the high-end, even under low-cannibalization conditions.

When cannibalization is high, simultaneous introduction of two editions often is optimal, especially when the software is large or has more features. Introducing the high-end edition in the first period followed by the low-end edition in the second— a sequential strategy commonly used in introducing other products—is clearly superior to others only when the consumers are extremely impatient compared to the vendor and the software is large. A surprising result is that offering upgrades is clearly superior only under very restrictive conditions. One would expect the up grade strategy to be superior when cannibalization is high because it allows the ven dor to limit the low-end edition and provide an upgrade later. However, the nonsuperiority of the upgrade option is consistent with the general industry practice of not offering upgrade options for editions. Our analysis also suggests that the vendor benefits by announcing its future strategy in the first period. Empirical evidence from the software market is consistent with many of our theoretical results.

There are several other important aspects of software marketing that we ignore in this paper. We deal with marketing of editions as opposed to versions that are necessarily developed and marketed sequentially. Similar analysis for versions will include the effects of exogenous factors, such as technology, the development of consumer demand over time as successive versions are introduced, and network externalities. Also, we are not interested in the precise timing of introductions. Rather, we are interested in the broad issue of the tradeoff between single versus multiple editions and sequential versus simultaneous strategy in software introduction.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. The software edition design model is introduced first. Optimal solutions for possible vendor strategies are derived, and then anecdotal empirical evidence is compared with theoretical results. Managerial implications of the results are then presented, followed by concluding comments.

## Software Edition Design and Pricing Model

WE CONSIDER A MONOPOLIST SOFTWARE VENDOR that has already developed a software product and faces the problem of determining the number of editions to introduce and the introduction strategy. The fact that the software has already been developed implies that we do not consider development-related issues [2, 3] but focus on the marketing of the software.<sup>1</sup> The software editions differ with respect to their features. We assume that any feature present in the low-end edition is also present in the high-end edition. For instance, the enterprise edition (the highest edition) of VB 5.0 includes all features of the professional edition, which in turn includes all features of the learning edition, and so on. This characteristic guarantees that editions are upward compatible, an important requirement in software.

We consider two segments, h and $l , n _ { h }$ and $n _ { l }$ in size, that differ in the intensity of their valuations of software features. The valuation functions depend on the number of features in the software. The segment sizes are assumed to be stationary.<sup>2</sup> A consumer buys only one copy of an edition or upgrade. Let $u _ { l } ( f )$ and ${ \boldsymbol { u } } _ { h } ( { \boldsymbol { f } } )$ denote the reservation prices of segments l and $h ,$ , respectively, for a copy of an edition that includes up to feature f. Specifically, we use the quadratic utility functions, $u _ { l } ( f ) =$ $\eta f - q _ { l } f ^ { 2 }$ and $u _ { h } ( f ) = r f \mathrm { - } q _ { h } f ^ { 2 } , r > 0 , q _ { l } > 0 , q _ { h } > 0 , q _ { l } > q _ { h }$ and $f < r / 2 q _ { l }$ , in our analysi for tractability and expositional purposes.<sup>3</sup> The parameter r measures the systematic increase in the consumer valuation as f increases and $q$ measures the consumer sensitivity, which moderates the increase in the reservation price. Higher $q$ implies lower willingness to pay for additional features. All parameters are known to the vendor.

On the vendor side, there is a single profit-maximizing vendor. We assume that the vendor has already developed the software with number of features $F , 0 < F < r / 2 q _ { l }$ The upper limit on $F$ ensures that the marginal utility is declining for all feasible values o $; f . ^ { 4 }$ The vendor has to decide whether to introduce one or multiple editions of the software. If a single edition is to be introduced, it has to decide only its price. If multiple editions are to be introduced, it has to decide the features in each edition, their prices, as well as the sequence of introductions. We also assume that the vendor cannot sell the same edition at two different prices.<sup>5</sup> The unit variable cost of producing a copy of an edition is zero. The development cost has already been incurred and is assumed to be zero for convenience.

A consumer will buy a copy of a software edition only if its reservation price for the edition equals or exceeds its price. Given two software editions, a consumer wil prefer the edition that yields a higher consumer surplus, defined as (reservation price – seller price). When a consumer is indifferent between two editions, it will choose the edition with more features. Similarly, when the vendor is indifferent between marketing a single edition versus multiple editions, it will market just one edition.

Since our model is adapted from that of Moorthy and Png [11], it is worthwhile comparing the two. f in our model is the same as quality q in theirs. Our model doesn’t have a marginal cost of production function, whereas they assume a quadratic mar ginal cost function. We assume a quadratic consumer utility function, but they as sume a linear utility function.

Given our model, the software vendor must decide the editions to be introduced, the sequence of introduction, and the prices. The vendor can serve only one segment, both segments simultaneously, or one segment before the other. We assume that the vendor will market one or two software editions<sup>6</sup> and introduce them in one or two periods. A consumer who has bought a low-end edition in the first period may have the option of upgrading it in the second period. A consumer who has bought or up graded to the high-end edition leaves the market forever.

## Sequential Introduction

For the sequential introduction case, we assume that the vendor announces the features and prices of software editions to be introduced in both periods in the first period itself. We assume that the vendor will keep the commitment and consumers have no reason to believe otherwise. Whether a vendor can or cannot make such a commitment credibly depends on several factors, including its reputation and its willingness to risk such announcements. Later, we consider the case in which the vendor doesn’t announce the complete strategy in advance and analyze the effects on our results.

In the sequential introduction strategy, the vendor introduces one or both editions in the first period, followed by the second edition and/or upgrade in the second. In this strategy, the vendor must wait till the second period for some of its profit, and the segment served only in the second period must wait to enjoy the product. The vendor will discount the second period profit, and the consumer will discount the second period surplus. Let the vendor discount factor be $\delta _ { d } \in ( 0 , 1 )$ , that is, a profit of p in the second period is equivalent to a profit of $\delta _ { d } \pi$ in the first period. Higher $\delta _ { d }$ corresponds to more patience. Let the consumer’s discount factor be $\delta _ { c } \in ( 0 , 1 )$ ), that is, a consumer’s surplus of s from software bought in the first period reduces to $\delta _ { c }$ s when bought in the second. In the sequential strategy, the vendor can introduce (1) the low-end edition first, followed by the high-end edition with or without an upgrade option, (2) the high-end edition first, followed by the low-end edition without an upgrade option, or (3) both editions simultaneously in the first period and an upgrade to the high-end edition in the second period.

The vendor will never pursue the first option of introducing the low-end edition in the first period followed by the high-end edition in the second period. Introducing the low-end edition first will aggravate cannibalization. If the low-end edition is introduced in the first period, the high segment will face a positive surplus of at least $u _ { h } ( f _ { l } ) -$ $u _ { l } \left( f _ { l } \right)$ in the first period and it will discount the surplus in the second period. Hence, the high-end price cannot exceed $u _ { h } ( f _ { h } ) - [ u _ { h } ( f _ { l } ) - u _ { l } ( f _ { l } ) ] / \delta _ { c }$ . The vendor will get higher profit by introducing the high-end edition also in the first period, in which case it can charge $u _ { h } ( f _ { h } ) - [ u _ { h } ( f _ { l } ) - u _ { l } ( f _ { l } ) ]$ for the high-end edition. In addition, the vendor will get this profit in the first period itself. So, introducing the low-end edition followed by the high-end edition is worse than the simultaneous introduction strategy. Hence we consider only the latter two options below.

## Evidence from the Software Market

THE THEORETICAL RESULTS PRESENTED EARLIER IN “Single Edition Introduction” are generally consistent with empirical observations. We collected data about editions introduced in several software packages sold during the period 1995–1998. The data collected included the name of the software, version number, number of editions sold, whether the editions were introduced simultaneously or sequentially, and whether upgrade options existed among the editions. Our observations reveal the following.

## Common Business Versus Specialized Software

(I) Commonly used software packages, such as word processing and spreadsheets, have just one edition at a low price. Even when they are included as components of an application suite—for example, Office 95—and the suite has multiple editions, the difference among the editions lies in the number of applications included, fo example, inclusion of the Access database. The applications themselves have only a single edition.

(II) Specialized software packages, such as database management systems, application development environments, and Web servers, have either multiple editions or just the high-end edition. For instance, the database software DB2 has only the high-end edition, whereas Oracle database software has both high-end and low-end editions.

## Software Version

(III) Multiple editions of a software program are more common in later (highernumbered) versions compared to earlier (lower-numbered) versions. For instance, Oracle introduced the low-end editions only from version 7.2. Microsoft started introducing multiple editions of its VB software only from version 3.0<sup>7</sup> and has been increasing the number of editions with higher versions.

## Prevalence of Simultaneous Strategy

(IV) Both sequential and simultaneous introductions of software editions are observed in the software market. However, simultaneous strategy is more common. The sequential introduction introduces the high-end edition first. Once multiple editions of a version are introduced (whether using the simultaneous or a sequential strategy), vendors introduce multiple editions simultaneously in the subsequent versions—ex amples being Oracle, Domino, and VB.

## Absence of Upgrades

(V) The vendors do not offer the option of upgrading from a low-end edition to a high-end edition. For instance, a consumer cannot upgrade the VB 5.0 standard edi tion to VB 5.0 professional edition. This observation is interesting, because the ven dors always offer the consumers the upgrade option for versions, that is, a user of VB 4.0 professional edition can upgrade to VB 5.0 professional edition.

## Managerial Implications

THE MOST SIGNIFICANT IMPLICATION OF OUR RESULTS TO MANAGERS is that introducing a single edition or simultaneously introducing multiple editions appears to be generally optimal under a wide range of conditions. The type and size of software appear to affect the optimality of a strategy. However, when software gets sufficiently large in later versions, the vendor will find it optimal to offer multiple editions.

When multiple editions are introduced, the strategy of introducing them simultaneously appears to be optimal under a variety of conditions. When the vendor is more patient, the precise optimal strategy depends on spe cific parameter values. However, the tradeoff appears to be between simultaneous introduction and the upgrade strategy. The vendor should estimate carefully data such as the relative segment sizes and the reservation prices before deciding on the optimal strategy. Our analysis also suggests that the vendor should commit to the future marketing strategy rather than allow the consumers to speculate on the ven dor strategy.

Our results also underscore the importance of the values of segment sizes $n _ { h }$ and $n _ { l }$ and their sensitivities $q _ { l }$ and $q _ { h } .$ . If the vendor is uncertain of the segment sizes or the sensitivity parameters, then the vendor strategy is unclear, especially when the vendor is more patient compared to the consumer. On the one hand, the vendor may introduce several editions simultaneously to gather information about the market, but it may face large cannibalization. On the other hand, the vendor may introduce edi tions sequentially to avoid cannibalization, but it may delay the profits. The use of our model by a software vendor requires the consumer valuation data. Various market research techniques exist to elicit consumer valuations of software. The experimental study of Kalish et al. [8] found that consumers were able to provide direct estimations of reservation prices. Other methods based on conjoint approaches [6] and regression approaches [9] can also be used to gather consumer valuation data.

## Summary

SOFTWARE DIFFERS FROM OTHER PRODUCTS with respect to zero marginal cost of making a copy and the availability of upgrade options. A model is presented of seg mentation and consumer self-selection applicable to the software market. The model’s results are consistent with evidence gathered from the software market. The results provide several guidelines for managers involved in marketing packaged software.

How sensitive are the results to the assumptions made in the model? The model was limited to two segments, two editions, and two time periods for tractability reasons. It is conjectured that if the vendor faces more segments, then for sufficiently large F the vendor will market more editions. There are empirical observations consistent with this conjecture. For instance, Microsoft started with one edition of VB, but as the version number increased it increased the number of editions. The number of time periods, and consequently the number of editions for the two-segment case, does not influence the nature of the optimality of the simultaneous introduction strategy be cause more time periods will make the simultaneous strategy more attractive. We also assumed quadratic declining marginal reservation price functions. The quadratic func tion guarantees a unique low-end edition. The results appear to apply to more general declining marginal utility functions. With regard to potential or actual competition, our assumption is that the monopolist vendor faces a captive market. Competition will reduce $\delta _ { d }$ and that will push the vendors even more to adopt a simultaneous strategy. A significant limitation of our model is the restriction of at most two edi tions. Shapiro and Varian [14] point out that information sellers can potentially benefit by selling three instead of two editions and avoid the psychological phenomenon known as “extremeness aversion.” Incorporation of this phenomenon in our model will require additional parameters, such as the proportion of consumers who wil avoid the extreme editions. As Shapiro and Varian also state, “the optimum number of versions to offer is rarely clear-cut” [14, p. 114], and further research is required to provide more insights into the issue of optimal number of editions.

The results of our model apply when the vendor does not know the consumer type and the consumers self-select the edition. As a result, the high segment benefits be cause it gets a positive surplus and the low segment always gets zero surplus. In addition, the low segment may not get the full software even though the vendor does not incur any additional cost. Also, the vendor gets a lower profit. If the vendor knows the type of individual consumers or if the consumers truthfully reveal their type to the vendor, and if it is legal to price-discriminate between the segments, the vendor can get the maximum profit of $n _ { l } ( r F - q _ { l } F ^ { 2 } ) + n _ { h } ( r F - q _ { h } F ^ { 2 } )$ , and both segments will get the full software at their reservation prices. There are examples of such third-degree price discrimination in several industries, including software. Third degree price discrimination will be profitable to the vendor only if it can monitor and enforce the truthfulness of the consumers. An interesting extension of our research is to investigate software edition pricing that takes into account the enforcement cost and incentives to consumers to reveal their true types through price discounts. An other potential area of research relates to the impact of the network effect among editions on the vendor strategy.

Our results suggest that while segmentation theory is useful in analyzing the soft ware market, the results for this market can differ from those of physical product markets. Further research is needed to understand the implications of segmentation on the design and introduction of software products.
