JAHANGIR KARIMI, ANOL BHATTACHERJEE, YASH P. GUPTA, AND TONI M. SOMERS

ABSTRACT: Despite the ever increasing importance of information technology (IT) in firms, the extent to which IT management practices are applied creatively to critical tasks varies widely across firms. For over a decade, firms have employed IT steering committees to manage their IT resources. However, the impacts of such committees on the IT management function have not been examined in depth. This paper hypothesized relationships between the level of sophistication of IT steering committees and level of IT sophistication of management within firms, and tested those relationships empirically via a field survey of 213 IT managers in the financial services industry. Results of the study suggest that presence and roles of IT steering committees are significantly related to the level and nature of IT management sophistication within firms. Firms interested in achieving the most benefit from their steering committees should carefully select their preferred roles depending on the type and the level of IT management sophistication desired. The article concludes with discussion and implications for IT researchers and firms’ executives.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information technology management, information technology management sophistication, steering committees

IN TODAY’S FAST CHANGING and hypercompetitive business environment, multinational and multidivisional firms must be capable of coordinating operational and strategic activities across functional areas, business units, and national borders. To compete effectively and gain flexibility, firms require some capability for lateral organizational coordination, collaboration, and decision making [36]. Such lateral capability may be in the form of formal structures (such as policy boards, committees, or teams) or informal groups (such as virtual teams). Lateral coordination can also be applied to the information technology (IT) management function in the form of IT steering committees (henceforth referred to as steering committees). The growing complexity of IT management issues over the last decade has elevated the role and importance of IT steering committees by virtue of their key role in policy setting and organization wide coordination of IT capabilities.

A steering committee is a high-level team of representatives from multiple divi sions or functions who are entrusted with the task of linking IT strategy with business strategy by setting a strategic direction, matching corporate concerns with technological potential, and building commitment to policies [54]. Chaired by a top executive, steering committees meet periodically to discuss IT direction, approve and rank projects, review performance, formulate or approve technology policies, determine resource levels, and perhaps recommend major initiatives [31]. While steering com mittees of the 1980s and 1990s were instrumental in formulating important IT decisions regarding system development projects, hardware/software selection, outsourcing of IT services, and the like, we believe that steering committees will assume greater importance now as many firms reorient themselves in light of the emerging electronic business industry. The Internet is triggering profound changes in how a firm does business, how it enters new markets, how it communicates across the enterprise, and how it deals with suppliers. While today’s networked economy offers unique oppor tunities to extend their customer reach, market share, and industry presence beyond what was possible in the traditional (manufacturing- or service-based) economy, oc casional missteps along the way can prove disastrous for these companies. The keys to building a successful electronic-age enterprise lie in an effective “marriage” of business acumen with IT competencies, and in extensive collaboration and information sharing of a variety of organizational functions—which are what steering com mittees are expected to deliver. Hence, the issue of steering committees is not only relevant at the present time, but there is a need to reexamine our prior findings in this area in light of the emerging economy.

Unfortunately, successful steering committees are difficult to find, and few firms know what to expect from their steering committees or what roles these committees should play in governing IT management. In addition, different forms of steering committees may be needed for different purposes. Some prior research has examined the relationships between the existence of steering committees and one or more IT management practices [29, 58, 70]. However, there is no study, to our knowledge, that has empirically studied the different roles of a steering committee or the linkage between these roles and the overall level of IT management within firms. Toward this goal, we define three roles of a steering committee and four dimensions of a macrolevel construct called IT management sophistication (intended to capture the overall level of IT management capability), and test interrelationships between these categories via a field survey of 213 IT managers in the financial services industry. The central research questions are: (1) do firms with steering committees have a greater level of IT management sophistication than firms that do not, and (2) if so, which dimension of IT management sophistication is most affected by which steering committee role. Our results not only link the efficacy of steering committees in building higher levels of IT management sophistication in firms, but also link specific steering committees roles to specific dimensions of IT management sophistication.

In the next section, we present the theoretical components of our model. These components are used to derive research hypotheses for subsequent empirical testing.

## Theoretical Background

PRIOR RESEARCH ON IT MANAGEMENT indicates that the extent to which IT management practices are applied creatively to critical tasks varies widely among firms [15]. This is often due to the lack of organizational structures designed explicitly to monitor and enhance effective IT management [13]. Effective IT management requires conscious coordination of the relationship between business strategy and IT resources [38, 65], as reflected in the degree to which IT mission, objectives, and plans support business mission, objectives, and plans [61]. Distinction is made between intellectua and social dimensions of the fit between business and IT domains. The intellectual dimension examines whether IT and business objectives are consistent with each other, while the social dimension refers to the level of mutual understanding of and commitment to the alignment of business strategy and IT strategy. For successful IT management, senior IT planners and the senior business executives must act together on both intellectual and social dimensions of this linkage.

Factors that may influence the linkage between business and IT strategy are the choice of actors, timing, decision making, and communication mode used in the formulation of mission, objectives, and plans for IT and the business. Results of the linkage are levels of understanding of business and IT strategy by business and IS executives. A steering committee is an organizational structure that helps bridge business strategy with IT strategy [61] by embodying an organizational commitment to the alignment of business and IT strategy (social dimension) and by validating such an alignment (intellectual dimension).

## Steering Committees

A steering committee is a formal organizational structure for managing various aspects of business-IT fit [27, 29, 31, 51, 69]. Steering committees enhance IT management capability in at least eight ways [31]:<sup>1</sup>

1. by providing strategic direction to IT operations

2. by providing leadership in exploiting and managing IT

3. by resolving resource allocation decisions

4. by helping top management maintain control over IT activities

5. by securing top management support for IT activities

6. by providing visibility of IT initiatives

7. by deciding which IT are of strategic importance to the firm

8. by centralizing or decentralizing the IT function

Galbraith [36] has posited that depending on their lateral coordination requirements, different firms may employ different types (or roles) of steering committees (lateral organizations) to achieve different levels and types of fit. Management should match the steering committee roles to the strategy-implementation requirements, and build lateral coordination capability so that these roles can be drawn upon when needed. Note that a firm may need and employ multiple steering committee roles to serve different organizational goals [69]. For instance, it may be appropriate to have two different steering committees to address strategic and functional issues [58]. Further, the roles of steering committees may evolve over time and should be managed accordingly [31].

Earl [31] suggested three ideal types of steering committees: steering groups, policy committees, and IT boards.<sup>2</sup> The primary responsibilities of steering groups are (1) to guide and approve IS strategy, (2) to give business direction to IT activities, and (3) to prioritize IT activities. In contrast, policy committees are primarily involved in (1) implementing IT strategy and technology policies across the business and (2) creating policies and procedures associated with IT planning, control, organization and architectural delivery. The roles of IT boards include (1) formulating IT departmental strategy, (2) planning, controlling, and managing the activities of IT units, and (3) ensuring that IT is run as a business. In other words, steering groups are concerned with creating IT strategies (such that they are aligned with corporate goals), policy committees are concerned with implementing IT strategies among user groups across the organizations, and IT boards are focused on implementing IT strategies within the IT department. Distinguishing between these roles is important because different firms may have unique needs that require implementing one or more of these roles. However, as elaborated by Earl [31], these steering committee roles are not mutually exclusive (i.e., they may overlap), a firm need not have all three roles, and even if multiple roles exist, one role may dominate the firm’s overall IT management capability.

## IT Management Sophistication

Effective IT management requires coordinated efforts in planning, organizing, controlling, and directing the deployment and use of IT resources within firms [14]. The concept of IT management sophistication (also called IT maturity) can be used to characterize the effectiveness of the IT management function [53, 63, 64, 83]. Greater IT management sophistication implies that IT managers are aware of the firm’s longterm strategic plans [48, 70], the firm’s future strategic plans are explicitly included in IS planning [18, 31], and IS performance is evaluated based on its contribution to the firm’s overall objectives and not on cost savings alone [18, 70]. Moreover, firms with greater IT management sophistication are expected to have an IT-literate top management [44, 48] who would participate proactively in the IS planning process [48].

The technology assimilation model suggests that IT management strategies evolve as firms move toward greater IT management sophistication [49, 60]. This model segments the IT assimilation process into four distinct phases: (1) technology identification and investment, (2) technology learning and adaptation, (3) rationalization/ management control, and (4) maturity/widespread technology transfer. In the final maturity phase: (1) benefits and experience with new IT are quickly disseminated to other business units, (2) learning is relatively complete, (3) long-term analysis and planning are emphasized, and (4) the technology base is installed and integrated into business strategy formulation, and (5) the IT function evolves from data processing orientation into strategic orientation [50, 64, 70]. IT management sophistication is therefore not a static state, but the result of an ongoing dynamic process, and firms may acquire various degrees of sophistication based on a host of organizational factors. The level of sophistication achieved would constrain the extent of IT-business strategy fit, and therefore their deployment and use of IT and firm success [44]. In firms with higher levels of IT management sophistication, the IT function evolves from a data processing orientation into a strategic orientation [50, 64, 70].

Previous research on IT management sophistication and the technology assimilation model suggests IT planning, organization, control, and integration as benchmarks for measuring firms’ progression toward IT management sophistication [43]. Higher levels of IT management sophistication or IT maturity would imply higher levels of IT planning, control, organization, and integration activities within a firm. The following paragraphs describe these four dimensions of IT management sophistication in detail.

IT Planning: As firms move toward a higher sophistication in IT management, the nature of the IT planning changes from a computing plan with a technology management focus to a long-range strategic plan with a data resource management focus [24, 55]. The primary objectives of IS planning are then to align the IS plans with the firms’ business plans [73], and to extend the infusion and diffusion of IT within firms [68]. New concerns are: What information systems do the firm’s business strategy demand, what strategic opportunities are presented by IT, and how should IT project priorities be set?

IT Control: Control of IT activities has changed drastically over the past two decades, from being loose/informal, project, and technically based to being more tight/ refined, managerially based. New methods of controls are based on benefits, priori ties (selective charge-out), and technical standards, and are based on organizational goals rather than cost [3, 5, 17].<sup>3</sup> As firms move toward higher IT management sophistication, they gain confidence in managing their IT resources, strive to balance the short-term and long-term implications of IT investment decisions, and establish mechanisms to permit key line managers to exercise controls over budgeting, priority setting, and resource planning for the IS function [31]. New concerns are: How much should firms spend on IT, how should IT proposals be evaluated, and how should the responsibility and authority for IT direction, development, and operation be set?

IT Organization: In the early stages of data processing, firms could organize IT activities autonomously because early applications were limited to transaction-oriented functions requiring only limited user awareness and involvement. Today’s in creasing trend toward end user computing requires that users’ ideas be given special attention in the planning and implementation of IT applications [1, 20]. Consequently, the direction, development, and operations are increasingly being colocated in the hands of business units and the IS organization. In this new era, the IT department is likely to be much flatter, with specialized subunits organized around technologies and business needs, responding better to changing user needs and leveraging IT investment more effectively. New concerns are: How will IT affect the firm’s organization structure, should IT have a director, and what should be his or her roles and responsibilities?

IT Integration: Traditional management strategy for automation has employed a bottom-up approach, where various functional areas were automated on an application-by-application basis, without consideration for integration or optimization at the firm level. As a firm moves toward greater IT management sophistication it should institutionalize a formal top-down planning process for linking information systems strategy to business needs, transfer the technology to a wider spectrum of applications, and contribute to a high degree of technology integration for better exploitation of IT within the firm [19, 57]. The end result of the change in IT management perspective from a local bottom-up approach to a global top-down (business-based and across the organization) approach is a high degree of systems integration, which enables these firms to use IT for (1) creating new products and services, (2) altering linkages with suppliers and customers, (3) achieving new levels of performance, and (4) displaying more proactive orientation toward IT [44].<sup>4</sup> Such integration not only requires identification and exploitation of IT opportunities, but also fundamental changes in business practices and culture [44]. Challenges for integrated firms in the new era are adoption of new IT and adaptation of both IT and organizational processes and roles for better fit [19, 31].

## Hypotheses Development

The quest of this paper was to examine how steering committees impact IT manage ment sophistication. Drury [29] posited that steering committees not only communicate potential uses, implications, and benefits of IT investments to top management and user groups, but they also play an important role in strategic IT planning. The presence of steering committees therefore has positive influences on firms’ level of IT control, organization, and integration activities, and is hence positively related to IT management sophistication in general. This expectation is supported in part by prior empirical studies demonstrating that steering committees have positive impacts on the quality of IS planning [39], planning processes [27, 31], planning effectiveness [57], improvement of IS project portfolios [51], fit between IT strategy and corporate strategy [51], and IS success in general [32]. Hence, we propose:

H : The level of IT management sophistication is significantly higher in firms with steering committees than in firms that do not have such committees.

Further, as mentioned before, depending on the lateral coordination requirements of a firm’s business strategy, a firm may employ different types (roles) of steering committees [36], which would then focus on different aspects of the IT management sophistication construct. Therefore, one may expect that different roles of steering committees will have differential effects on the dimensions of IT management sophistication by creating a different IT management mentality within the firm. For example, since policy committees are more responsible for steering IT activities in line with the strategic direction of the firm, one may expect that firms with policy committees will exhibit higher levels of IT management sophistication compared to the firms with a steering group. Likewise, the impact of a steering committee on any single sophistication dimension may also differ based on the type (role) of steering committee employed. Therefore, we propose:

$H _ { 2 } .$ : Levels of IT management sophistication in firms differ depending on the roles of the IT steering committees within those firms.

One may argue that IT management sophistication affects the presence and roles of steering committees, rather than vice versa. Indeed, organizational learning theory posits that firms learn from their experience in deploying organizational innovations (such as steering committees), which then enables them to make more effective utilization of their IT resources [2]. Such experience helps firms build an internal knowl edge base, which helps reduce subsequent barriers to organizational strategy formulation. While admitting that there may be a temporal causal cycle between the deployment of steering committees and the level of IT management sophistication, we also note that the purpose of using steering committees is to enhance the strategic fit between business and IT (i.e., higher levels of IT management sophistication) rather than the reverse. In other words, use of a steering committee is intended to enhance IT management sophistication in a firm, which at a later point in time may influence utilization of the steering committee, ultimately leading to an even higher level of IT management sophistication. A longitudinal study is required to capture such dynamic and cyclical effects. Given the nature of our cross-sectional study, we examined the unidirectional association between steering committees and IT management sophisti cation at any single point in time.

Also notable in this context is that steering committees are only one of several organizational constituents that may impact a firm’s level and nature of IT management sophistication. Other determinants may include organizational and/or IT de partment size, organizational culture, and extent of organizational politics. While we recognize the potential effects of these other factors on IT management sophistication, we wish to emphasize that the presence of steering committees and the roles played by such committees are important determinants that have not received ad equate attention in the literature—hence this study.

## Research Methodology

## Data Collection

DATA WAS COLLECTED FOR THIS STUDY via a mail survey of 213 firms in the financial services sector (e.g., banks, insurance companies). IT is often considered to be a strategic resource in this sector because of its role in expanding the scope and delivery of services significantly (e.g., ATM networks) and creating new services (e.g., PC banking, Internet banking) [4, 21, 22, 23, 45]. Further, occurrences of steering committees are fairly common in the financial services sector, making it particularly suit able for our study.

The initial research instrument (see Appendix) was constructed based on an extensive review of the IT management literature. This instrument consisted of one binary item asking respondents whether or not they had an IT steering committee in their firm, plus 28 fully anchored, five-point, Likert-scaled (“strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”) items measuring various dimensions of steering committee roles and IT management sophistication. IT management sophistication was measured using 20 items corresponding to IT planning, control, organization, and integration, by expanding on the IT maturity scales proposed by Benbasat et al. [6], Earl [31], and Sabherwal and Kirs [64]. Steering committee roles were measured using eight items, based on prior work by Earl [31]. The instrument was pretested for content validity via in depth discussions with senior IS executives in local financial services firms. Some items were reworded based on the feedback received from IS managers.

The 29-item questionnaire was mailed to senior IS executives of 1,035 financial services firms across the country, along with a letter soliciting their participation in this study. Respondents were explained the objective of the study and assured total confidentiality of their inputs. Of the firms solicited, 213 responded to our request, for a response rate of 21 percent, which is fairly typical of mail surveys [60, 69, 72]. Of these, 177 firms reported having a steering committee. All respondents were corporate-level IS executives—CEOs, vice presidents, directors, and managers of IS. The responding firms were fairly well distributed with respect to annual revenues and total number of employees, with median annual revenues of 2.5 million, and median number of employees approximately 500. To test for nonrespondent bias, we compared the means of responding and nonresponding firms with respect to their annual revenues and number of employees. Differences between these means were nonsignificant (chi square = 10.77 and 9.21, p > 0.05), indicating absence of any systematic nonrespondent bias in our data sample. ## Instrument Validation

Psychometric validation of the research instrument was performed in two phases. In the first phase, we conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on the data sample to see if the different steering committee roles and IT management sophistication dimensions can be identified uniquely, and also to assess whether individual items loaded up appropriately on their hypothesized constructs. Items with poor or ambiguous factor loadings were deleted from subsequent analysis. In the second phase, the refined scale was subjected to confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and factor loadings of each item were reassessed. Both EFA and CFA were performed using EQS for Windows Version 5.4 [10].

EFA was performed separately for steering committee roles and IT management sophistication items. Principal components technique was used in each case to identify the underlying factors, which were then rotated to obtain the final solution. An oblique rotation was used because the underlying factors were expected to be correlated. Correlation matrices for steering committee roles and IT management sophistication dimensions are presented in Tables 2 and 3, and their factor loadings are listed in Tables 4 and 5. A common rule of thumb for assessing construct validity is that individual items should have a factor loading of at least 0.6 on their hypothesized construct (for convergent validity) and less than 0.3 loading on all other constructs (for discriminant validity) [56]. Based on these criteria, 9 of the 28 items failed to load cleanly on any single factor, and were deleted from further analysis. Of the remaining 19 items, 15 items met the convergent validity criterion, while the other 4 items demonstrated factor loadings fairly close to the 0.6 threshold (see Tables 4 and 5). The discriminant validity criterion was met by 49 out of 50 cross-factor loadings. The shortened scales therefore demonstrated satisfactory levels of construct validity.

## Data Analysis and Results

TWO STATISTICAL TECHNIQUES WERE USED FOR TESTING the two research hypotheses stated earlier. Hypothesis 1 was tested using multiple analysis of variance (MANOVA) on the complete data set of 213 respondents, while Hypothesis 2 was tested using structural equation modeling (SEM) of the 179 respondents who reported having an IT steering committee in their firm. MANOVA was justified for Hypothesis 1, since this hypothesis examined whether presence versus absence of steering committees (on a categorical scale) resulted in significantly different levels of IT management sophistication, measured along the four dimensions of this construct. SEM was appropriate for Hypothesis 2, since this hypothesis was concerned with the strength of causation between the three steering committee roles and four IT management sophistication factors, with all seven variables being measured using multiple-item interval scales. SEM analysis was performed using EQS, a covariancebased approach similar to LISREL, which is especially appropriate in areas with strong a priori theory, where further theory testing and development is the goal [35]. Results of MANOVA analysis are presented in Table 7, while the structural equation portion of EQS analysis is depicted in Table 8.

Hypothesis 1 examined whether firms with steering committees differed significantly with respect to IT management sophistication from those without steering committees.

The multivariate effect of presence versus absence of IT steering committee on IT planning, control, organization, and integration was significant, as observed from Pillai’s, Hotelling’s, and Wilk’s statistics. These statistics had values of 0.155, 0.183, and 0.845, respectively, and the overall MANOVA had a statistical power of 0.999, indicating that presence of IT steering committees indeed had a significant effect on overall IT management sophistication.

Follow-up ANOVAs were conducted to determine which of the four hypothesized independent effects contributed to the overall MANOVA. As shown in Table 7, all four ANOVAs (independent effects of the presence of steering committees on IT plan ning, control, organization, and integration) were significant, with statistical powers of these tests ranging between 0.89 and 0.99. Collectively, the MANOVA and followup ANOVA indicated that the presence of steering committees added explanatory power to IT management sophistication as a whole, as well as individually to the IT planning, control, organization, and integration dimensions of this construct. Therefore, Hypothesis 1 was supported.

Hypothesis 2 examined whether individual steering committee roles had any significant effect on any of the four dimensions of IT management sophistication. This was tested using an EQS structural equation model that linked steering group, policy committee, and IT boards as independent variables to IT planning, control, organization, and integration as dependent variables. Model estimation was done by first examining the goodness-of-fit of the overall model, and then examining the standardized path coefficients and path significance for each hypothesized association. These estimates are provided in Table 8.

EQS uses several goodness-of-fit measures to evaluate the quality of a hypothesized structural model. A chi-square test is used to estimate model fit, in which the model chi-square statistic is compared with the chi-square from a comparable baseline model of uncorrelated variables [8]. However, chi-square tests are especially sensitive to sample sizes, and the probability of rejecting any model increases as the sample size increases, even when the model is minimally false [9]. Consequently, in very large samples, virtually all models are rejected as being statistically untenable. Therefore, Bentler [8] recommended using the \*<sup>2</sup>/df ratio (df: degrees of freedom) as a better measure of model fit. This ratio should not exceed 5 for models exhibiting reasonable fit with observed data, and was estimated as 3.52 in our hypothesized structural model (see Table 8).

EQS provides three additional goodness-of-fit measures: Bentler–Bonett normed fit index (NFI), Bentler–Bonett nonnormed fit Index (NNFI), and comparative fit index (CFI). The NFI metric should fall within the 0–1 range, with higher values indicating better fit [8]. NFI is, however, affected by sample size and may not reach 1.0 with small samples even if the model is accurate. To overcome this limitation, Bentler and Bonett [9] recommended a NNFI metric, which adjusts NFI for degrees of freedom. NNFI may fall outside the 0–1 range, but it is a better measure of fit since: (1) it is invariant to sample sizes, and (2) it incorporates model parsimony (via degrees of freedom) in fit estimation. Both NFI and NNFI assume that the underlying data follows a central chi-square distribution or at least can be approximated in large samples by a noncentral chi-square distribution—an assumption that is reasonable for true models or models with small misspecifications. Under circumstances when this assumption is not met, Bentler [8] suggested a third measure of model fit called CFI, which compensates for underestimation of fit by NFI and NNFI in small samples. In general, NFIs, NNFIs, and CFIs greater than 0.90 are indicative of good model fit [10]. In this study, NFI for the hypothesized model was estimated as 0.88, NNFI as 0.90, and CFI as 0.91, indicative of acceptable levels of model fit (see Table 8).

Since the overall model exhibited reasonably good fit, the next and final step in data analysis was to examine individual path coefficients for each hypothesized effect and its significance. As indicated in Table 8, 10 of the 12 hypothesized associations between steering committee roles and IT management sophistication dimensions were significant at $p { = } 0 . 0 5$ , suggesting that steering committee roles are generally related to IT management sophistication dimensions (IT planning, control, organization, and integration) within a firm. However, the effect of a steering group on IT planning and that of an IT board on IT integration were found to be nonsignificant. Though unexpected, this result suggests that steering groups, by having a more strategic (as opposed to operational) focus, are generally not involved in the day-to-day IT planning activities, which are operational in nature. In contrast, IT boards, responsible for implementing the IT strategy (after its formulation) within the IT units are more concerned with planning, controlling, and managing IT activities of IT units. Therefore, they have little impact on IT integration, since integrating IT strategy with business strategy and integrating IT across business units are not their prime responsibilities.

Earl [31] had suggested that (1) different types of steering committees’ roles are not mutually exclusive, and (2) there may be overlapping reasons for the existence of each type. In this study, based on the examination of the path coefficients, we have found that the steering group is more strongly associated with IT organization than IT control or integration. Since the primary responsibilities of steering groups are to prioritize IT activities and to give business direction to IT activities in order to align IT strategies with corporate goals, one may expect steering groups to have greater impact on IT organization than on IT control or integration. On the other hand, policy committees, though significantly related to all four IT management sophistication dimensions, had the strongest association with IT organization, followed by smaller but significant associations with IT control, integration, and planning. Since policy committees are primarily responsible for implementing IT strategies among user groups across the organizations, one may expect that they have significant impact on policies associated with IT planning, control, organization, and integration activities in an organization. IT boards’ roles had the strongest association with IT planning, followed by IT control and IT organization. As mentioned before, IT boards are more responsible for implementing IT strategies within the IT department, and therefore one may expect that they have greater impact on IT planning, control, and organizational issues within IT units rather than the IT integration issues across the business units.

In summary, our analysis found that IT planning is determined primarily by IT boards, and to a lesser extent by policy committees. IT control is mostly predicted by policy committees, followed in decreasing order by IT boards and steering groups. IT organization is predicted strongly by policy committees, and then by steering groups and IT boards. Finally, IT integration is predicted mostly by policy committee and then by steering groups.

## Discussion and Implications

RESULTS OF THIS STUDY INDICATE THAT THE PRESENCE of steering committees indeed help enhance the level of IT management sophistication within firms. Higher levels of IT management sophistication ensure a good degree of fit between IT and business strategies, which then serves as the foundation for appropriate selection and utilization of IT resources. As firms enter the electronic era, accurate identification and use of IT resources (technology and people) are absolutely vital for their sustenance. For instance, many firms today are trying to redefine their business models by using Internet as a new, low-cost distribution channel to reach a vast population of global customers, or by using an intranet/extranet to streamline procurement, customer relationship management, collaboration with strategic partners, and other mission-critical processes. As companies are increasingly realizing, deploying electronic business solutions is not a universal panacea for their business problems and there may be more pitfalls to this strategy than is evident on the surface. Hence a careful orchestration of IT and business strategies is required to reap the benefits of electronically enabled processes. Steering committees can provide this much-needed linkage by bringing IT and business units together, and promoting a shared understanding of each other for common organizational good.

Firms that are involved in strategic applications of IT (e.g., electronic business, enterprise resource planning) should consider using steering committees and making full and effective use of such committees. While steering committees were previously used to coordinate IT outsourcing, system development projects, and IT selection and implementation, as argued before, they can potentially serve a very critical role in managing organizational transformation from the traditional business models to the new electronic ones. The need for unprecedented speed in enterprise decision making represents a huge organizational and cultural change in many firms. It requires breaking down internal walls between business and IT, walls between functional units, and walls between the inside and outside of the firm. Our finding that steering committees help enhance the overall level of IT management sophistication within firms is important, in light of the fact that very few studies have addressed this aforesaid linkage.

We also demonstrated that steering committees or IT management sophistication are not singular, unidimensional constructs. Depending on their responsibilities, steering committees may have three roles: steering group, policy committee, and IT board. Likewise, we found that IT management sophistication can be viewed as an aggregation of IT planning, control, organization, and integration. In addition, we observed that the three steering committee roles influenced the four IT management sophistication dimensions in different ways. Firms interested in squeezing out the most benefit from their steering committees should carefully select their preferred roles depending on the type and level of IT management sophistication desired. For example, if IT organization is a primary IT management concern, a steering group will be helpful for giving business direction to IT activities, for setting priorities, and for funding infrastructure development. Its membership should, therefore, comprise senior executives with functional and enterprise experience plus the IS director and executives in charge of strategy and development in the IT department.

As is evident from Table 8, firms with policy committees tend to have significantly higher levels of IT planning, control, organization, and integration than those without, establishing the efficacy of policy committees in achieving a higher IT management sophistication. If improving overall IT management sophistication within a firm is the primary objective, a policy committee should be appointed in order to steer IT activities in line with strategic direction of the firm and to set policies and procedures associated with IT control, organization, and integration within firms. Members of this committee should be knowledgeable about IT relevant to the business, have experience using IT, and have the conceptual ability to see how IT can create value for the businesses. Finally, if information system planning sophistication is the major rationale for creating steering committees, an IT board should be appointed to clarify departmental strategy, and to facilitate IT planning, control, and organization within the business unit. IT boards should include the senior executives of the IT business unit, and one or more directors appointed from customer departments or divisions. As observed before, steering committee roles are not mutually exclusive. A firm can employ multiple steering committees performing different roles, or it may have a single steering committee performing more than one role.

## Limitations

ONE CRITICISM OF OUR STUDY is the threat of single-respondent bias, since our subject pool was restricted to one IT executive from each participating firm. It is possible that the perceptions and opinions of this IT executive may be different from the collective opinions of business executives in the firms. However, given that we were interested in examining IT management practices (as an effect of steering committees) within firms, we felt that the CIO or IT manager was best positioned to respond to our inquiries. A multiple-respondent approach (from each company) may have increased confidence in the observed data target via data triangulation, but at the same time it may have biased the data against IT management practices.

A second limitation of the study is the lack of a prevalidated research instrument for measuring steering committee roles and IT management sophistication dimensions. This is not unexpected, given the lack of prior research in this area. Although our IT management sophistication scale was constructed by expanding on items proposed by Benbasat et al. [6], Earl [31], and Sabherwal and Kirs [64], and steering committee roles were created using prior work by Earl [31], we had to drop several items based on our initial data analysis due to poor psychometric properties. Future research should attempt to theoretically construct and empirically validate research scales for conducting further empirical work in this area.

Third, this study focused specifically on the effects of steering committee roles on IT management sophistication. There are potentially several other variables related to steering committees—such as their size, experience level, representation of business units, and chairperson’s organizational position—that can conceivably impact IT manage ment sophistication. In light of our finding that steering committees impact IT management sophistication in a complex manner, future research can be directed at examining how other steering committee variables affect IT management sophistication.

Fourth, our hypothesized model was focused on the nature and direction of association between steering committee roles and IT management sophistication, but did not examine contingent factors (such as organizational size, culture, and politics) that may have mediated the hypothesized effect. Future research should examine the type and effect of mediating factors affecting the association between steering committee roles and IT management sophistication.

## Conclusions and Future Research

THIS STUDY ADVANCES OUR UNDERSTANDING of steering committees’roles and their impact on the level of IT management sophistication within firms. Specifically, we found that (1) firms with a steering committee exhibit a higher level of IT manage ment sophistication than those without, and (2) specific steering committees’ roles influence specific dimensions of IT management sophistication. We offer some insights into the nature of these associations, and their implications for IT management within firms. However, our work is only a first step in building a body of knowledge. Many more studies are required to conceptualize additional attributes of steering committees—such as their agendas, membership, chair responsibility, and organizational level—and to test their impacts on IT management and organizational performance.
