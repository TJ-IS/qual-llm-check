# Institutional Bridging: How Conceptions of IT-Enabled Change Shape the Planning Process

JOHN TILLQUIST

JOHN TILLQUIST is Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at the Gary A. Anderson School of Management, University of California, Riverside. Dr. Tillquist’s expertise is the area of computerization and organizations, strategy, electronic commerce, telecommunications, Internet banking, and the impacts of informa tion technologies. Prior to earning his Ph.D. from the University of California, Irvine, he spent ten years as senior engineer and corporate staff manager for GTE Data Services, Ameritech, and US West. Dr. Tillquist has published articles in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Global Information Systems, and the Communications of the ACM, and frequently speaks at business and academic conferences dealing with the Internet and the impact of technological change on business. Dr. Tillquist has been widely quoted in the media as an expert in information systems and has provided expert opinion in the United States and Canada for high technolog legal cases.

ABSTRACT: Organizations are continually influenced by notions of management pro moted through broadly held visions of managerial practice. These notions often incorporate models that generally prescribe information technologies as enabling agents for directed organizational change. Such concepts reflect highly cohesive, self-referential systems of beliefs, goals, and rules that structure perspectives about com puterization and work in organizations. To achieve “breakthrough” changes in efficiency, performance, or competitive advantage, organizations must translate these high concepts into a specific model of change appropriate for their organizational context.

This study shows how abstract and institutional-level conceptions about change are translated into actionable and individual-levelrealities, and how within this translation the organization’s ability to reform can be locked into a constraining process. As consultants bridge institutionalized conceptions of management to discrete organizational activities, participants of change adopt not only the vision of change but also new ways to talk, act, and plan. This adoption may inhibit change by blocking effective discussion and forcing compliance to ill-fitting prescriptions.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: business process reengineering, change methodologies, interpretivist field study, IT-enabled organizational changes, IT planning, organiza tional transformation

## Planning Technological Change

THE CHOICE OF PLANNING METHODOLOGY significantly influences organizational and technological planning. Portrayed in the highly abstracted functionalism of popular books and trade magazines, notions for planning organizational technologies are often conceived in terms of economic optimization or production efficiency [56]. But in reality planners must work in the many worlds of polity, negotiation, and the other more “organic” considerations of technological implementation [5]. The success or failure of a technological development often depends upon the ability of planners to realistically assess a proposed technology against the existing technical and organizational setting. This paper presents two case studies where organizations struggle to reconcile the prescriptions and visions of the popularized Business Pro cess Reengineering (BPR) change model with their specific needs and settings. These cases reveal how broadly held management prescriptions structure the process to plan change using information technologies (IT).

This research study follows corporate managers planning technological change for two major reorganization projects, projects intended to leverage information tech nologies in streamlining services and improving customer relations. The planning ideals managers were pursuing appeared rational and attainable, and in each case experienced consultants were contracted as a reasoned approach to gain specific knowledge of IT-enabled change. The two organizations shared in their choice of BPR as the prescription for change.

Like many, these organizations looked to consultants as holders of highly specialized knowledge [63]. Consultants are expected to translate concepts of IT-enabled change into actionable plans for their client firms. Businesses see consultants as having broad exposure to leading edge management ideas and as having experience in guiding other firms through to the realization of those ideas [62]. The methods forwarded by two independent consulting firms followed here built on features of BPR that were broadly generalizable across clients, tractable and auditable in their results, and offered clear, measurable deliverables.

Understanding how change plans are formed from diffuse prescriptions for IT-enabled change holds important insights for both theory and practice. For practice, managers can act to assure that the change methodology is formed and shaped in ways that lead to successful adoption and change. For theory, this study details spe cific mechanisms for bridging institutionalized conceptions of management and the formative processes of discrete technological planning.

## Leveraging Organizational IT

IT-ENABLED ORGANIZATIONAL CHANGE is the explicit forwarding of information technology for the purpose of enabling organizational change. Computer and communication technologies network geographically distant work groups, disperse decision-making authority among appropriate organizational agents, centralize control over operations, delayer vertical hierarchies of bureaucracy, and engage new product and service markets through electronic commerce. Using information technologies strategically allows firms to streamline operations, exploit new business opportunities, and globalize production operations. These unique features make organizational planners keenly interested in gaining expertise in using computers to establish new forms of work and work organization. Organizational planners are, however, struggling to identify which IT to use and how to deploy technology investments effectively [27, 70].

The popular press and trade journals extol the change capacity of IT. Depictions of ITenabled organizational change suggest that a strategic redeployment of the information technologies infrastructure—the hardware, software, networks, and organizational and technical support systems—can effect deliberate, wide-ranging changes to an organization [6, 49]. Yet understanding the implications of using information technologies for organizational change is difficult and often frustrating. Organizational planners hear anecdotal “bankruptcy-turned-boom” cases where computers enable “order of magnitude” improvements in strategic agility and customer service and they wonder why they have not been able to achieve such dramatic success with their own IT efforts [70].

The research literature offers some guidance about how IT can enable large-scale business transformations. The work of N. Venkatraman [68], Jeffrey Pfeffer’s perspectives of technology and organizational order [57], and Stephen Barley’s [3, 4] influential depiction of technologies shaping organizational structure provide keen insights into the value of IT used strategically. Much of the research has attempted to show how structures of interpretations shared within organizations may influence computerization development and adoption decisions [19, 26, 44, 45, 50, 54, 58], primarily as explanations of failure [8, 53] or to isolate key social and political factors affecting technological design and development [63]. Other studies have focused on social, political, and organizational issues, such as the adoption and diffusion of innovation [9, 17, 27], characterizations of mobilizing metaphors [33, 36], or barriers to creativity and acceptance [10]. Focusing on organizations as systems of interpretation and interdependencies has resulted in innovations in organizational learning [55], creativity [51], decision-making [25, 52], usability [46, 69], negotiation and commitment [48], strategic formulation [37], and competition [12]. Often, however, these studies have led to diffuse prescriptions and cautionary tales with strong intuitive pull but without detailed systematic approaches for application to other situations and cases.

The popular literature appears to take a more detailed prescriptive approach to ITenabled organizational change. Re-engineering the Corporation [31], Paradigm Shift [66], The Balanced Scorecard [34] and articles from the Sloan Management Review, the Harvard Business Review, and the more practitioner-oriented Business Week, Fortune, and Forbes magazines carry broad claims about how information technologies can “reinvent” organizations [17, 20, 30, 35]. These authors specifically target organizational managers to adopt new conceptualizations about IT, the organization of work, and their relation to each other. While the practitioner-oriented literature offers systematic approaches to IT-enabled change, the approaches tend to be highly rationalized and functionally focused, often under-determining key social and political factors that shape computerization development and adoption in actual organizations.

This study investigates how conceptions of IT-enabled change from research and prescriptions for practice in popular management discourse influence planning for technological and organizational change. The paper is structured as follows. The first section describes the research design and looks at the phenomenon of BPR. Following that introduction, two cases of large-scale organizational change are presented. The observations and insights from each of the research sites are then used to analyze how prescriptions of technological and organizational change come to influence and structure the change planning process.

## Research Design

A QUALITATIVE RESEARCH DESIGN [43, 67] was chosen as the method to uncover the forces and elements in the process of strategic planning at the research sites. This approach was particularly suited to analyzing interactions within planning meetings and uncovering the complex dynamics of the planning process. Much of the data collection arose from direct observation of steering committee meetings, planning meetings, technical committees, work sessions, and work flow analysis sessions where the processes of planning took place. Observations included participant discussions and patterns of interaction in 64 hours of planning meetings. Field notes and audiotaped discussions were transcribed into electronic text and collated with the various hand outs, overheads, and reference materials from the meetings. After each contact, field notes were analyzed and a contact write-up report was produced for further analysis.

In addition to direct observation of the planning meetings, over 44 hours of indepth, directed interviews with participants of the change process were conducted. These interviews were to probe individual perceptions and to validate and deepen a growing understanding of the planning process. Interviews at the research sites spanned the organizational hierarchy, from senior management to clerical workers, tapping a broad range of views held by the stakeholders of change. A detailed description of each of the research sites is included below.

## Site Selection

Possible research sites were selectively limited to organizations planning IT-enabled change under a common management planning strategy. Described in detail below, the research sites, referred to here pseudonymously as Domestic Tel and Coast Pharmaceuticals, were chosen to elicit an understanding of the process of organizational planning under the BPR model of technical and organizational change. These re search sites were carefully selected to be distinct in form, goal, polity, and process. Domestic is a telecommunications firm with a strong engineering ethos and a long and stable history. Coast is a pharmaceutical manufacturer and marketing operation, well known for an open and flexible management style and a history of constant organizational change.

Selecting research sites from different industries and with distinct management styles accentuated the shaping influences and accommodation processes in adopting

BPR by revealing consistencies and contrasts across the disparate cultures. Choosing Coast and Domestic enabled an investigation into a diverse array of social and politica action and a broad range of influences on the organizational planning process. As the BPR change model was adopted at each research site, some aspects of the model were expected to be adopted relatively evenly, while other aspects were expected to be adopted quite differently. Studying the consistencies and differences across these two diverse settings enabled an increased depth of focus on the implementation of BPR.

## Business Process Reengineering as IT-enabled Change

BPR IS A PARTICULARLY STRONG EXEMPLAR of an IT-enabled organizational change [7, 9, 58]. Because of its popularity and strong prescriptive nature, it is an especially interesting case for studying how adoption of a planning model shapes the organizational planning process. Hammer and Champy [31] center information technology as “an essential enabler” for business process reengineering. There is, in their words, a “disruptive power of technology, [an] ability to break the rules that limit how we conduct our work.” BPR portrays information as a ubiquitous asset, appearing anywhere it is needed to coordinate and simplify work, and depicts information technologies as uniquely malleable avenues to workplace intervention and competitive advantage.

## Exploring the Model of Business Process Reengineering

Stoddard and Jarvenpaa [64] summarize BPR in five major precepts, eliciting several themes and articulating many of the basic organizing principles constituting business process reengineering: radical transformation; don’t automate, obliterate; processbased change; top-down direction; and information technology as the critical enabler. (See Table 1.) These assumptions have helped galvanize many managers to choose BPR as a model for IT-enabled organizational change. They have sought to achieve what Taco Bell and Federal Express exemplify in radical, quantum-leap business trans formation [31].

Understanding how organizational planners envision BPR is important to understanding the planning process [8]. The interactions that take place in the process of planning, however, are theoretically complex and their richness is only hinted at in the absence of a means to properly frame the findings. Here, Social Rule System Theory theoretically grounds the analyses and frames the findings from the two re search sites.

## Theoretical Foundation

## Social Rule System Theory

SOCIAL RULE SYSTEM THEORY (SRS) provides an explanation of the formation and implementation of social rule systems that regulate participants in social relations [13]. SRS is a theoretical approach to understanding and characterizing differences in assumptions about the conceptualization, definition, and regulation of social action. SRS defines three rule categories that structure social relations: These are the classificatory, evaluative, and prescriptive rules that order behavior.

SRS posits that patterns of social action are strongly influenced by social rule systems. Social rule systems are collectively shared structures of rules acting upon and constituted by social systems. They include cultural, organizational, and institutional constraints on individual and group action, their knowledge, power, and social role, and metarules for the maintenance of the rule systems and interpretive frames. They “specify to a greater or lesser extent who participates (and who is excluded), who does what, when, and how, and in relation to whom” [13].

Analysis of organizational processes highlights both similarities and differences between settings, past and present, and between participants. Patterns of discord and concurrence among participants about the structuring of social relations reveal underlying assumptions and beliefs about the nature of work and organizational change. In terms of SRS, participants may differ about how a work setting should be con structed (classificatory differences), what roles the participants should take on and how they should be conducted (evaluative differences), or about what courses of action are to be considered legitimate (prescriptive differences).

Participants may also agree on how they define themselves and their social setting (classificatory similarities), what goals are worth pursuing (evaluative similarities), and taken-for-granted ways of “how things get done” (prescriptive similarities). The underlying assumptions revealed by this process can then be used to infer characteristics of the visions held by the participants.

The rules that regulate and structure social activity fluctuate, changing with the actions and interpretations of the social agents who apply them. Rules are fluid structures whose application depends upon their interpretation. When gaps in rule systems create situational ambiguity, interaction of these social choices force adaptation of the rule systems to the new situation. Where rule systems conflict, participants choose between antinomies or create new rules. The structuring of social relations continuously regulates and is reproduced by the action and the interpretation of action of the participants [13].

This fluid interpretation creates opportunities for participants. Well-positioned so cial actors will often advance rule systems to their advantage. Dominant political players advance and legitimate perceptions, criteria, and action patterns from mosaics of often conflicting rule structures. Nondominant participants may disagree or misinterpret the application of the received rule systems, further compounding the constitutive conflicts and ambiguities within the system.

Social rule system theory is well suited for describing social phenomena at the level of IT and organizational planning [1, 29]. Discrete actions can be traced to systems of descriptive, evaluative, and action rules that guide workplace activity. These rule systems can be examined for consistency, completeness, and bias from individual inter ests. Dominant political ideologies can be tied to specific arrangements of social order. Social roles, conflicts, consensus, and legitimization are centerpieced as explanators of social structure.

## BPR as a Rule System

BPR embodies a cohesive system of rules that governs organizational functioning and expression. BPR forwards a system of rules and regulations that defines the con stituent actors and their relationships, an evaluative system for judging performance and obligations, and a normative order regulating actional behaviors:

Classificatory Rules (“Who are we?”) Actors and their relationships are defined explicitly under the BPR rubric. There are the strategic planners, upper level managers planning work centrally to ensure the appropriate breadth of consideration and global applicability of the decisions. There are middle managers, who translate strategic plans into tactical operations and who motivate and guide the workforce. And there are workers, skilled organizational agents who work semiautonomously to accomplish work assignments given to them by their superiors. Workers, middle managers, and strategic planners are organized into teams, transient entities created to resolve problems and identify opportunities. Team members can be, and often are, members of several different teams. Each team or work group is presumed to have access to sufficient organizational resources and expertise to accomplish their assigned tasks. Roles and rules of social interaction are predefined and universally accepted.

Evaluative Rules (“What is good?”) Work performance is evaluated based in comparison with predefined outcomes. Work relations are hierarchical, with increasing spans of responsibility higher in the organization. Achievement is evaluated based on objective measurements of cost, quality, and timeliness. Work relations are presumed to be highly cooperative and geared to the betterment of the overall organization. Workers are presumed to be skilled and forthcoming, and situated appropriately to accomplish their assigned tasks. Workers are expected to be emotionally committed to the organization, gaining fulfillment from the successful accomplishment of the assigned tasks.

Prescriptive Norms (“How should things done?”) BPR carries specific prescrip tive rules specifying how participants of change are expected to behave. Information technology determines the work process, and workers are expected to operate in accordance with the work design. Desired outcomes are defined by iteratively higher organizational entities in accordance with organizational goals. Each level of the or ganizational hierarchy has associated with it a particular set of roles and responsibilities. Behavioral norms and conventions structure the conduct of work processes by prescribing appropriate behaviors and proscribing illegitimate activities. Dissent and deviation are not tolerated.

Together, these conceptions of the organization of work under the popular model of BPR constitute the prescriptions and proscriptions that regulate organizational action and determine an organization’s social structure. These themes define context, guide interpretation, create evaluative criteria, and inform action. They define what is to be considered important, relevant, and material to the organization of business activities.

## Building a Better Model

The broad-based popularity of BPR has produced many analyses and critiques in the academic literature, and has produced several noteworthy advances to the BPR model [22]. Venkatraman [68] heralds BPR as a step toward better management of the strategic capabilities of IT, but emphasizes creating complementarities between the orga nizational and the technological infrastructure. He suggests that rational application of IT can create seamless interconnections (“strategic alliances”) among organizational processes and within business partner networks.

Earl [21], Galliers and Baker [24], Stoddard and Jarvenpaa [64], and Pollalis [58] suggest refinements to the “core” BPR paradigm to address how organizations should be expected to manage radical transformation. They propose several techniques to manage discontinuous organizational change based on organizational learning, where essential elements of the old organization are used to facilitate the implementation of the new design.

Organizational learning also complements the BPR vision. Robey et al. [60] envision a methodology integrating BPR and organizational learning into a mutually com pensating process for achieving IT-enabled organizational change. They propose tapping organizational memory and implicit assumptions of group functioning to tem per the top-down, mechanistic approach of business process reengineering.

Other scholarly articles have been more critical of the BPR model. Research studies have suggested the formal-rational approach of BPR is too narrow, ignoring key social and political aspects of work and work organization [14, 16, 35, 55, 71]. Pentland [55] speculates that the emphasis on cost reduction and processing capacity of these managerial models unnecessarily constrains the expression of key social and political issues. “The social nature of the underlying phenomena gets lost in the rhetoric [of] information processing and managerial decision-making”[55]. Ezzamel et al. [23] find that formal-rational management models overlook vested political interests, and speculate that models of organizational change often simply reinforce existing command-and-control hierarchies. Kantor [35] criticizes formal-rational planning models as unilaterally imposing “mindless” tasks that lack regard for the social and political culture of the workplace. These critiques have raised awareness of the shortcomings of the BPR model but stop short of explaining how BPR is adopted and shapes the process of technological and organizational planning.

The following case studies at Coast and Domestic reveal how the BPR model shaped the planning process by imposing a new system of appropriateness and expression. How these themes organize thinking about organizational and work change are investigated at the Coast and Domestic Tel study sites.

Domestic Tel is introduced in the next section, followed by an analysis of the at tempts at technological and organizational change made there. The Coast Pharmaceuticals research site is described in the following section, and the paper concludes with an analysis and synthesis of the two studies.

## Domestic Tel

“DOMESTIC TEL” IS A PSEUDONYM for a diversified U.S. communications company engaged in telecommunications, cable, wireless communications, and multimedia content and services businesses. As with the Coast case discussed later, Domestic has undertaken a large-scale BPR program in response to impending industrial reforma tion and rapidly changing workplace technologies. Tied to a legacy IT architecture and a history as a government-regulated monopoly, Domestic struggles with a heritage of bureaucratic lethargy and huge, inflexible work systems. At the beginning of this study Domestic Tel was entering the second half of their four-year BPR program. This timing provided a rich opportunity to gain a long-term retrospective on the plan ning effort, while at the same time enabling a perspective of the future of the ongoing processes of organizational change.

Domestic Tel is a holding company for several telephone operating companies with annual sales revenues of roughly six billion dollars. While concentrating on local telephone service provisioning, Domestic has a number of related subsidiaries in telecommunications, entertainment, publishing, advanced technologies, and consulting. From a long history of monopolistic practices under national regulation, Domestic is faced with increasing local competition and a thinning regulatory shield. Alternative telecommunication service providers target the lucrative high-density business zones, bypassing Domestic’s network in providing connection services directly to long distance carriers and high-capacity point-to-point telecommunications between business sites. Domestic’s strategy of cross-subsidizing expensive residential service with profitable business traffic has become increasingly untenable as the high-end market erodes due to these bypass networks. An embedded plant of outdated copper-wire technologies makes the competition even tougher for Domestic, which is facing start up companies deeply capitalized in high-capacity fiber optic transmission and high speed computerized switching equipment.

When this study started, Domestic had already spent U.S.\$290 million on their BPR effort and was planning on an immediate workforce reduction of 800 positions; in two years, cuts in its workforce grew to 9,000 positions and a total project cost of \$610 million [39]. A special organizational division, Strategic Development, was created to manage the change process. The goals driving the BPR initiative were to consolidate customer service and repair centers into one-stop, centralized “megacenters,” and to restructure the Proposal Management arm for large business and government customers.

## BPR at Domestic Tel

A GLOBALLY RENOWNED CONSULTING FIRM (referred to here as “Global Consulting”) was retained to guide the BPR planning process and support the implementation of the recommendations in the affected operating divisions. Global had experience with executing BPR projects and held a reputable position as a change leader for the For tune 500.

The BPR planning process at Domestic has had a twin focus. The first of the two reengineering efforts, the BPR in Proposal Management, entailed the consolidation of the project proposal management function. The intended “quantum leap” opportunities were cost savings associated with the elimination of redundant activities, process standardization, improved cross-project coordination under a single service site, and shared expertise built into an IBM/Lotus Notes repository. Domestic expected the centralization to standardize the project proposal management process, fostering interconnections among the business, government, and residence service teams, and consolidating resources under the single site.

The order fulfillment process effort, the second and much larger of the two organizational processes targeted for BPR, looked to improve responsiveness to customers and reduce support costs by consolidating their 520 customer service centers into 30 centralized service sites. Through the extensive use of INWATS telephone lines and the development of an integrative front-end system allowing access to the numerous order management and service provisioning systems, Domestic Tel expected to increase productivity, streamline operations, and reduce cost through labor reductions.

## Computerization at Domestic

At the crux of the two change initiatives was the information technology infrastructure. Domestic Tel had traditionally been a leader in the adoption of computerization, and IT permeates the operations and management functions at Domestic. In the 1960s and 1970s, huge mainframe systems were created for the management of circuit in ventories and in support of the functions of billing, accounting, and order processing. Domestic’s trunk circuit inventory database is reportedly the second largest data re pository in the world.<sup>1</sup>

Emerging from three decades of industrial, technical, and regulatory change, the business systems of Domestic Tel are complex masses of accumulated modification and iterative patching. While essential to the business, Domestic’s technological infrastructure is unwieldy and cumbersome. Years of refinements to keep pace with changing requirements and the constant addition of dependent subsystems have complicated Domestic’s core technological infrastructure to the point where Domestic has no comprehensive understanding of the detailed functioning and operational rules embedded in these core legacy systems. Documentation is outmoded and incomplete, often lagging behind operational development or left out altogether from the development and maintenance process.

Executive Director, Strategic Development: We take a set of business processes and we, um, we look at them and we say, “Okay, this particular step could be mechanized. So let’s do that.” We have never really done a good job of stepping back and asking the question, Is this step in the process really necessary anymore? So rather than doing automation and streamlining, we’ve done just pure mechanization for years. Which is, you know, like other big companies, the reason you get yourself so embedded in a legacy environment that is virtually impossible to get out of. Everything is so interrelated and intertwined that you can’t really identify what the process really is.

The BPR initiative was aimed at overcoming the obsolete and increasingly complex computerized operations at Domestic. Once these systems were isolated and an integrating interface created for end-to-end process support, Domestic hoped to create “quantum leap” opportunities for customer service, internal operational efficiency, and flexible organizational processes. A Unix<sup>®</sup>-based, client-server architecture was envisioned as the technological infrastructure to integrate the heterogeneous mainframe systems in support of the envisioned new business processes.

## The Change Vision

The planners of change at Domestic, under the guidance and tutelage of Global’s consultants, recognized the challenges to revamping their computerized operations. From the outset, Global’s consultants called for the BPR planners to “stretch the envelope” and expand their “comfort zones” to overcome technological barriers to change. They called for objective process analysis of both computerized routines and workplace operations to capture and redress essential work processes, and to simplify the IT infrastructure into a “seamless, ubiquitous information environment”— IT Manager in Strategic Development.

Domestic’s senior management embraced the BPR model to reengineer the legacy IS systems and to signal that, in their words, “business was no longer to be conducted as usual.” After years of complaining about the problems of their stodgy mainframe systems, the IS group was now empowered to transform the IT infrastructure to serve as foundation for the new organizational order.

Domestic needed to distill the anticipated “order of magnitude” benefits of consolidated organizational processes into fine-grained business practices. To accomplish this, the planning process would focus on uncovering and eliminating process disconnects and redundancies buried within Domestic’s complex work processes. The bulk and inertia of Domestic’s work systems and its legacy computer systems would be overcome by a systematic and objective analysis and a resynthesis of basic business operations. Inefficiencies were to be eliminated, and computer systems redesigned on an integrated GUI-based client-server platform interfacing with the legacy mainframe systems.

Domestic Tel’s senior management was deeply committed to changing their operations. Executives clearly understood the importance of streamlining operations and becoming more flexible in response to competition. Millions of dollars were funneled away from other projects to capitalize the change effort. Highly skilled people were enlisted to create a change plan that would radically reorganize Domestic’s business operations. In setting up the BPR planning body, executives had sought the best industrial advice in retaining Global as a top-rated consulting firm, and had provided their organizational planning team with the authority and expertise to real ize the change.

Claiming significant experience in implementing BPR and IT-enabled change, Global sold their capabilities to Domestic’s executives through a well-articulated methodology and compendium of “best practice” experiences. Historically, Global added value for consulting clients through a rigid task-and-activity analysis and cross-referencing of work systems to supporting applications. Successful client cases were summarized and rolled into their best practices database, which complemented selling and facilitated closing future contracts. With the advent of BPR in the popular management consciousness, Global tailored their methodology and best practice cases to bring in key themes of radical transformation into their consulting repertoire.

## Learning About BPR

Despite expertise in network engineering and organizational administration, Domestic’s executives were unclear about how to develop and implement a reengineering project. Global consultants took the lead and created a core BPR team, a select group of handpicked Domestic managers seconded to the Strategic Development division to learn and execute Global’s BPR process.

Most managers at Domestic had little knowledge of BPR prior to their exposure to Global’s methodology and learned about BPR as they proceeded along the planning process. Core team planners were the first to be trained for BPR change, while paral lel sessions trained lower-level managers and supervisors to take the BPR process to the rest of the company. This “training the trainers” approach was intended to carry the methodology to the field—geographically distributed offices, satellite service centers, network operations, and field engineers—where data about the existing work organization could be gathered. These trainers used materials, data collection forms, and work aids provided by Global.

Surprisingly, these training sessions did not focus on teaching the BPR model. Global’s training and presentations concentrated primarily on the procedural implementation of BPR vis-à-vis the Global methodology. Many of the more fundamental concepts of BPR, which may have come from readings from Hammer and Champy’s book or selected articles from the popular business press of the time, were missing from the training materials and presentations. Instead, training focused on predefined task and activity analysis, from “as-is” work interviews to process redesign, walking Domestic’s people through the notebooks and handouts illustrating how to do Global’s BPR process.

## Strategy-Making for Fun

Core team managers called up to work on the strategic change planning committees were enthusiastic about creating change. Certainly part of the reason was the break from the routine of daily work and the opportunity for organizational visibility. Once a participant, each manager quickly assumed the role of a BPR visionary:

Core Team Member: There are a lot of people who are not visionary. But once you start getting anyone involved in visionary thought or in visionary activities, everyone takes on the role of being a visionary. People relish that. . . . People really rally around identifying change and different ways the business should run.

Assigned to the task of creating organizational change, managers assumed an entirely new role and attitude as a visionary of change. Removed from the daily operations and motivated to create radical improvements, managers assumed the views and character izations associated with the new change process. They were eager to learn and few publicly questioned the appropriateness and implications of the BPR change process.

## Level of Detail

The various local representatives of the various subprocesses—including circuit provisioning, network, quality assurance, and in departments throughout the organization—gathered periodically in teams to discuss how to integrate their work. Their major challenge was to identify and simplify the working interfaces between the organizational subprocesses, breaking down the “toss it over the wall” mentality of the functional hierarchy to create a continuous process environment.

Domestic was facing a set of conceptually standardized but pragmatically diverse order entry and billing systems. Initially developed centrally and distributed among its many operating company subsidiaries, these systems had acquired their own unique “dialects.” Common systems supporting seemingly compatible functions turned out to be irreconcilable. Each geographically based subsidiary computer system had accumulated layers of unique enhancements for local regulatory, political, and market conditions. Conceptually equivalent systems existed on significantly differing hard ware and software platforms and were host to numerous and quite different subsystems. Data repositories were structurally and semantically incongruous. Each system reflected the idiosyncratic operational requirements and unique institutional histories of their host organization.

The integration teams found that subprocesses they sought to streamline in the complicated organizational architecture were in themselves complex, often wrapped in differing languages and subject to broadly divergent internal restrictions and conflicting goals. The detailed technical operations of network administration, for instance, proved too complex and frustrated efforts to create a single-point-of-con tact for order fulfillment and customer service. Numerous interfaces to centralized support and administration groups—such as finance, regulatory support, rates and tariffs, and network engineering—made it difficult to synchronize the differing pri orities, obligations, and schedules under a unified order management process.

It was becoming increasingly clear to planners that creating useful interfaces among these diverse groups required very fine-grained understandings of the interdependen cies among the subprocesses. But since Global’s methodology captured only task activity details about work activities, recognizing and acting upon this detailed operational knowledge proved to be exceedingly difficult.

Executive Director: [Team members] say, “Oh, yeah. We’re working on that. We’re identifying our interfaces. . . .” But you find that they’re really not articulating at a level that you could do anything about it.

## Analysis Paralysis

The creative energy and enthusiasm that marked the beginning of these teams began to bog down within a few months. Many employees resisted changes in their area of operations:

Staff Manager: We always look out and we’re trying to change the way other people do their work. Because it’s always someone else who needs to change, and not me—I’m doing everything really well.

Facing unexpected complexity and difficulties in implementing the change plan, planners would continually be returning to the high-level, “50,000-foot” planning phase. When the work plans generated from BPR planning were compared with the daily operations, planners would abandon the change effort and begin planning anew. Unforeseen requirements, unexpected interdependencies, and uneven levels of analyses for computerized systems and work tasks derailed the implementation of the proposed changes.

Executive Director: When you get down to that, you start uncovering that this is really hard, nasty, ugly, dirty work. Well, we just came from something that was really fun. So our behavior is that when we see, “Oh, God, this is really ugly and nasty, there has to be an easier way.” And we will turn around and we will go back and we will retest the vision, and we will start looking for the silver bullet.

## Executing the Plan

Three years after project initiation, Domestic Tel concluded its contract with Global. Despite remaining problems, the high-level BPR plan was complete and Domestic then prepared to implement the BPR.

At implementation, Domestic’s two-tiered implementation plan was highly abstract. Domestic’s plan was to create an IT infrastructure to usher in the new unified organizational routine and to simplify complex organizational problems. Technically, the implementation was to insert a middle-tier computer platform between the work organization and the legacy mainframe systems to create an integrated system. Organi zationally, there was the revamping the work processes—implementing top-down directives to optimize critical business operations and capitalizing on the integrated information environment.

Domestic’s planners had a vision of change they believed was the best strategy for transforming their regulated bureaucracy into a fleet-footed competitive organization. They believed in their capability to direct change. “I really haven’t found that people who sponsor BPR are anything other than sincere,” says one operations man ager, “They’re always sincere.”

The implementers of the BPR plan were also concerned with the workforce, believing firmly their new design created opportunities for collaboration, creativity, and room for personal and professional achievement. They trusted in the organization’s ability to respond to change, and in their workers’ ability to accept the new work changes instrumentally.

## Overwhelming Complexity

Executive Director: A lot of the assumptions done in that strategic plan were based upon a fact that the processes would change, people would accept those process changes, that technologies would be able to support with new systems and applications the new way of doing business and in the time frames that we were looking to do them. All of those were bad assumptions.

Domestic’s expectations that operational groups could work through implementation problems were seriously challenged by the complexity of continuing problems with the embedded work systems. Individual differences in commonly defined work systems consolidated under the BPR plan were unyielding to integration, leaving workers faced with a mosaic of computer systems, local rules and regulations, and idiosyncratic procedures and practices.

Staff Manager: God, it’s a nightmare maintaining that old code. You can reengineer all the processes you want, but how do you quickly get an infra structure there to support it?

As part of their service center consolidation, Domestic’s planners expected computerized systems to be integrated through a single user interface for their new, full service customer service representative (CSR). The embedded systems supporting the CSR work, however, were not amenable to consolidation. To place an order in the newly consolidated order center, for instance, the newly reengineered CSR was now required to know each of the four different subsidiaries’ order entry systems—each requiring different passwords, slightly variant product and service codes, and very different procedural navigation schemes. Training for the new entry-level CSR posi tion now took six months to a year, a remarkably long time for a position with tradi tionally less than an 18-month average tenure.

## The Results

Despite a high level of commitment from senior management, the information systems division, and the impacted business divisions, Domestic was experiencing many difficulties in meeting their initial expectations of the BPR. After a series of continuing failures and problems, several high-level executives at Domestic were terminated, including a subsidiary president and several vice-presidents. Although the program continued organization-wide, funding was cut back and an appreciable sense of frus tration and fear had grown up from the ranks.

The rise of frustration and fear may have been predictable in retrospect. In the first two years of the BPR initiative at Domestic Tel the most noticeable changes had been in workforce reductions. The customer service center consolidation had begun shrink ing the 520 centers toward the anticipated 30 centralized facilities, while the central ization of proposal management function in the Midwest offices had cut their ranks by one-third.

These labor-cutting initiatives drastically impacted worker morale. Employees be came fearful they would become the next victims of the reductions. They were be coming more defensive, less risk-taking, and notably more staid in keeping “business as usual.”

Executive Director: You sit down with people now that are still around and [they] say, “What can we do? We’re here to help. God, I’m really anxious to help in anything you need help [with].” Okay, so what we need to do is we need for you to commit to delivering this or that or something else. And then, “Well, you know, I’m not sure that I can commit to that,” and, “I don’t have enough detail.”

The changing sentimentality had strong effects on the implementation of the other aspects of the BPR initiatives. When benefits expected from BPR’s process orientation failed to materialize, isolationism and defensiveness replaced enthusiasm and teamwork.

Core Team Member: We put process owners in charge of each of these processes, doing, you know, what we thought was the right thing. What we continued to see is that the processes have taken on the same behavior as the functional organization. . . . They don’t talk to each other as they’re building the infrastructure or developing their products or insuring the network.

More work responsibilities were being forced upon the workforce as their numbers shrank and workloads grew. Workers were less willing to assume the additional risks and workloads needed to integrate islands of work responsibilities into streamlined processes. The “high walls” previously partitioning the functionally vertical organization had been replaced by “horizontal process stovepipes.” “All we did was we turned the stovepipes on their side”—Director, Strategic Development.

## Interpreting Domestic’s BPR Initiative

GLOBAL’S INTERPRETATION OF THE BPR MODEL organized the strategic discourse and planning behavior at Domestic. Planners, initially unfamiliar with the details of executing a large-scale change, had turned to Global consultants to guide the planning process and to define themes of organizational change. Planning participants reframed their behavior and expressions in terms of the Global methodological model, and expressions of personal interest and organizational change were recast to appear tightly bound to Global’s interpretations of the BPR model. Planners adopted the normative assumptions of the BPR methodology to classify, prescribe, and evaluate their actions and expressions.

## Classificatory Rules

Official statements from executives of the goals and objectives of Domestic’s reengineering change initiative were constantly in the foreground of the planning discussions. Domestic’s executives expected “quantum leap” changes to the proposal management and service order fulfillment processes; incremental improvements were not enough. Executives had defined the goals of the project and the legitimacy of the BPR model as the change mechanism.

## Prescriptive Rules

Domestic’s planning process focused on the work process, identifying disconnects and redundancies and systematically replacing them with reengineered tasks and activities. Charged with constructing a plan to radically transform the IT infrastructure to usher in the new organizational order, problems of complexity with the embedded work systems, technical systems, and local adaptations of work processes were marginalized during the planning stages against the centralized topics of cost, quality, and timeliness.

## Evaluative Rules

Compliance with Global’s methodology was motivated both formally and informally. The high-profile BPR project meant participants had more to gain in pay, position, status, or political opportunity by being associated with the BPR project.

But the unusually high and visible senior level management support for the project also raised the ante of implicit rewards and penalties for the BPR participants. Informally, individuals were motivated to comply with the BPR methodology as terms of being a “good team player.”

## The Results of Domestic’s BPR Initiative

The principle outcome of the BPR project was a large-scale labor reduction without changes to the existing work order. The policy statements, administrative practices, and official statements intended to motivate and direct change found only surface compliance among Domestic’s workforce. These fed the growing sentiment that the BPR was “yet another fad,” and as such, should be tolerated until “it blows over”— Satellite Office Staff Supervisor.

Staff Supervisor: It’s just another rearrangement of the deck chairs on the Titanic. We get these programs all the time—quality circles, entrepreneurships— but no one really gets around to making any real change.

Inviting personal change brought with it perceived risks of loss of pay, position, status, and even career for individuals. Ironically, public criticism or reluctance to change was equally risky, because people feared being branded as dissenters. As a result, privately held interests and deeply ingrained defensive behaviors were hidden behind public appearances of support for the change program. Participants claimed strong support for the new work order while blaming technical and organizational complexity for holding up the change process. Participants retained the appearance of good organizational citizenship while simultaneously, and covertly, protecting their own political “turf” and personal interests.

Executive Director: [What you hear is] Well, I would really like to do that, but I’m just really so busy doing these other things. You know, we’ve downsized by fifty percent. We’re all so busy now—I’ve taken on, you know, the work of three other people, because everybody else is gone. So I don’t have time to do that. But you give me a good, solid definition of what it is you want done, and I’ll respond to that.

Domestic’s planners were clearly struggling to resolve conflicting interpretations that arose about the adoption of IT as change enabler. Planners wrestled with Global’s characterization of IT as an agent for quantum leap work improvements and radical innovation against their own knowledge of IT and work organization from daily experience. Committed by the organization to embrace the change vision, planners took on a front-stage/back-stage mentality, where enthusiasm and public bandwagonning for radical change hid private interests and backroom political maneuvering.

The analysis of the structuring of planning IT-enabled change at Domestic (Table 2) highlights these features of Global’s interpretation of BPR at Domestic. Two key features of this analysis are (1) the realignment of authority and legitimization systems from the hierarchy of Domestic’s administrative bureaucracy to the hierarchy of knowledge-stock in BPR, and (2) the substitution of the consultant-based methodological procedure for the reasoned application of BPR principles and concepts.

As the next case reveals, these features of Domestic’s attempts at BPR are not unique to Domestic Tel. At a multinational pharmaceutical corporation, pseudonymously referred to here as “Coast Pharmaceutical,” efforts to leverage change using IT were again shaped through the interpretations of the consultant experts.

## The Research at Coast Pharmaceutical

THE BPR EXPERIENCE AT COAST WAS SURPRISINGLY CONSISTENT with the Domestic initiative. The focus on a task-activity sensibility of work, the attention to costefficiency and customer service, and the implicit assumption of compliant, motivated participants were operationalized in much the same way as had been the case at Domestic. This was at least initially surprising given the stark industrial and organizational differences between the two companies, but it suggests that the underlying BPR rule systems were being applied consistently. Coast’s BPR initiative highlights the importance of the consultant’s role in the interpretation of the BPR message.

At the initiation of this study, Coast was just beginning the planning phase for a large-scale organizational change, one that explicitly focused on IT to create “orderof-magnitude” change opportunities. Coast had always been successful at implementing organizational change. With strong consultant support and a vibrant organizational context, initial expectations were that the BPR at Coast would be the success story to contrast Domestic’s derailment.

The study followed the strategic and technical planning process at Coast’s BPR project over an eight-month period, beginning in 1995 with the inception of planning for the order management process and ending with the disbandment of the planning group and the presentation of the final recommendations to senior management.

## Coast Pharmaceutical

The history of Coast is one of specialization around profitable product lines. High entry barriers to research and development in the pharmaceutical industry, coupled with long periods of patent protection, have traditionally enabled high profitability with relatively little competition. Product and service innovations not developed in ternally are targets for acquisition and corporate merger, while matured product lines are divested, spun off as separate entities, or, more often, purchased in whole by other pharmaceutical conglomerates. Coast had a long history of absorbing and spinning off several smaller companies before reaching the current corporate and technologi cal configuration.

## The Early Stages of Change Planning

Like Domestic, the participants were excited by the change mandate in the early stages of Coast’s order management reengineering project. Most planners were first through third-level general business and IS managers, all with full-time responsibilities in addition to the BPR project. Despite being overloaded, they enthusiastically welcomed the senior management mandate for change and were looking forward to creating innovative organizational change strategies. Excitement grew in these first meetings as planners began to see the opportunity to correct what they had long seen as problems in their workplace.

Andy:<sup>2</sup> I think it’s important that Coast has finally stepped up to the bar and said, you know, we need to move on with some of these decisions; quit dillydallying with it.

## Contract Experience: The Role of the Consultant

As with Domestic, Coast retained a world-renowned consulting firm to guide the BPR planning process (referred to here as “International Consulting”). International Consulting is a direct competitor to Global Consulting. International’s consultant played a key role as the BPR subject matter expert, working in conjunction with Coast’s project cochairs to choose the appropriate elements of the BPR methodology and to direct the planning process. These joint management duties, however, were not spli evenly. The pace and direction of the meetings was often dictated by the consultant in her attempt to produce her contract deliverables within the allotted time.

## Learning to Talk the Talk

It was in the initial meetings that the chief architects of organizational change learned the BPR model. Most had no prior exposure to the BPR process and few had little more than scant knowledge of basic BPR principles. A short two-hour presentation and lecture by International Consulting introduced the meeting participants to BPR; most of their learning, however, came later through interaction with the planning meth odology and the consultant. Through repeated use of prepared workbooks, forms, and planning guides (an example of which is depicted in Figure 1), participants learned what was to be considered important, meaningful, and pertinent in the planning discussions. Their collective concentration focused in on the methodology’s key measures, goals, and deliverables as they worked to make sense of the BPR planning process.

A shared perception of the legitimacy of the BPR change process implied by Coast’s executive decision to adopt the methodology was reinforced through snippets of BPR knowledge garnered from the popular press. These snippets frequently depicted broad popular support with claims of spectacular success. Planning meetings became pep pered with stories of corporate innovation and colorful allegories of “best practice” firms. Within a few weeks, the planners had acquired an operating knowledge of the BPR process and, as a group, had internalized the mainstream BPR process.

## Making the Best with What You’ve Got

After the first month, private concerns about the course of the BPR project began to emerge. Planners balanced a public face of the committed team player with their private set of interests and beliefs. In public settings, planners followed the emerging norms of compliance and enthusiasm for the engineering rationality of the change model. In private, they shared frustrations and fashioned agreements to work around the planning process. In huddled groups during meeting breaks, people would talk enthusiastically about their own visions for change, about ideas to resolve their work place problems, and about the polity of organizational change:

Kim: The [finance division] is very dogmatic: “This is how it was, and this is how it will stay. . . .” You can’t report against payroll detail because they don’t believe you should do that. It’s one of their strong beliefs.

Susie: This is more of a vision problem—the sales force needs to get a common vision [about how orders are to be entered].

Upon returning to the meeting, however, planners reverted back to a vocabulary of strategic change and quantum leap improvements.

Thomas (restarting the meeting): Our primary focus is to, one, set achievable expectations; two, map software functionality with the reengineering vision; three, define functional business processes and flows; and four, develop a “best practice” business model.

As the planning went into the third week, some planners were becoming visibly dis couraged about the lack of progress. In off-line meetings and hallway conversations, planners shared concerns that critical workplace issues were not being addressed:

Karrie: We spend too much time filling out forms and too little time thinking.

Katy: Also you’re not getting the information you need.

Vice-President: There is a process problem. People are [upset]. Let’s get past that and change the process.

As the process continued, complaints about a perception of limited progress were becoming more frequent during breaks and between meetings, and began to spil over into the meetings themselves. An inability to discuss certain aspects of work believed important was becoming problematic for the planners. They began to point to the BPR methodology as constraining the planning process:

Dean: The interview guide is not useful—just the first sheet.

Karrie: [It’s not our job to be] going through the books. The consultant’s job is to fit the method to the current process.

Joan: The [method] binder, as good as the consultants think it is, is inadequate for our function.

For her part, the consultant was continually prompting the group to “move on” whenever the discussion strayed from the BPR program. She kept the meeting pace brisk, even to the point of suggesting charging money from tardy arrivals to planning meetings. Coast’s executive management encouraged planners to “stay on track” with the consultant’s BPR methodology, even while apprehension grew as Coast’s manag ers perceived their own goals diverging from those of the consultant.

Katy:<sup>3</sup> It’s just a matter of you get it done. And [our executives] don’t want to hear that you need to extend deadlines, because they’re working with conflicting deadlines. And all the deadlines have to be met. So it’s not something that people are real receptive to in terms of changing. I think in the BPR, International Consulting was probably less receptive to changes in timelines—understandably so—than anybody else.

Within months, the initial excitement felt at the beginning of the project had given way to a growing sense of stress and fatigue. During one meeting, a planner recounted how overtime at work had her falling asleep at her kitchen table as she prepared for the next day’s activities. While sympathetically evocative, the story raises an issue echoed continuously by team members: Planners were being overwhelmed by increasing work responsibilities. “I feel like a jet pilot,” said one,<sup>4</sup> “We’re flying fast and low. I don’t have the time to think ahead because all of my attention is taken dodging the things right in front of me.” Planners were feeling stretched between the full-time commitment of the BPR project and the equally demanding responsibilities of their regular jobs. They felt that trying to meet their daily obligations left little or no time to address even the most substantive issues.

The constant push to keep up with plan milestones and to achieve project deliverables did not allow participants time to slow down and critically evaluate the impacts of the planned changes:

Katy: My output’s not going to be worth anything if fifty percent, sixty percent of my time is dedicated to [another] project, the rest is supposed to be dedicated to BPR and then somewhere in between there you fit the management responsibilities that you have on a daily basis, plus providing service to your customers.

But, lacking in BPR experience and expertise, planners felt limited in their ability to modify the consultant’s methodology, and were thus tied to its deadlines and deliverables. By default, Coast continued to follow International’s methodology:

Dottie (to Consultant): Tell us exactly what you want done for next week.

Lou (to Consultant): Yeah, is next week to be a comparison of “as is” to “to be” or a validation of the metrics?

Consultant:Try to come up with stretch targets for key areas. The whole point of the [job holder] interviews and summaries is to quantify the work.

Katy, Cochair (to group): Well, we’re agreed to do this, so the question is how can it get done? What do you need to reach a comfort level?

Dottie: Well, we need time to analyze what [information] we have already.

As the study unfolded, it became clear that the change methodology was not working well for Coast. Despite assistance from International Consulting, there was a growing discontent among the participants with the perceived limitations of International’s methodology. Managers were concerned with the emphasis of International’s meth odology on the detailed analysis of job function and IT application support instead of on opportunity identification and strategic brainstorming. Despite this discontent, however, executive management continued to support the consultant and emphasized sticking to the BPR methodology:

Mary: Is International the consultant [group] that we should be using?

VP:<sup>5</sup> Let me make it clear. It’s this group’s responsibility to make the process. I’m hearing we don’t like International, but the problem—

Mary: It’s not International, it’s their method.

VP (irritated): The problem is with this group.

Participants of the planning meetings felt constrained in their choice of discussion topics. They were hesitant to offer up problems and potential alternatives for fear of challenging the expertise of the consultant. During one meeting, a junior planner became agitated about the planning method but quickly censured himself in front of the consultant:

Dean: But we’re lacking even a half-baked test for reasonableness! We’re looking at the pieces but not at the steps or method. (After glancing at the consultant) . . . but we’re trusting you that it will all work out.

Later in the meeting, another planner defers to the consultant’s claim to expert knowledge:

Dean: It seems to me that we should be spending more time on the “to be” [planning], . . . but then I don’t have fifteen years experience doing BPRs [as the consultant claimed earlier].

As a result, many issues of critical relevance to implementation feasibility were being sidelined or tabled from the discussion. Managers were becoming increasingly frustrated with their inability to express themselves in the planning meetings. Surprisingly little of these compounding frustrations were voiced in public discussions as an unspoken taboo on questioning the appropriateness and implementability of the BPR process permeated the meetings.

## Unmet Expectations and Back to the Drawing Board

In their final recommendation, the BPR planners presented seven “quantum leap” opportunities to senior management. Response from the senior managers to these recommendations was tepid. In their response, the senior managers ask the planners to reassess their recommendations and to provide further justification for their implementation plans:

Katy: [The executive steering committee] have asked us to go back to the drawing board and tie in some additional numbers and recommendations of individuals that could champion those quantum leaps and bring it back to them. We think probably they’ll buy into two or three, if we’re lucky.

Despite the best efforts of many, the outcome of this planning process had not brought about the radical changes that had been eagerly anticipated at the project outset. Even though the Coast BPR would not be considered a failure, it was not clear to the planners why it had not been a success. The senior managers felt the recommendations focused too much on streamlining existing operations and too little on creating competitive opportunity:

Katy: The steering committee [was] saying this is not strategic enough. This is not quantum leap enough. If you are going to do a BPR, the incremental things you can do along the way—you’re empowered, make them happen. But we would like recommendations on things that are going to put us where we need to be and give us a competitive edge. So they actually sent us back and said, “Umm, beef this up. You need to really do some more strategic type things.”

The BPR participants, however, blamed the foreshortened planning schedule, thei double-duties as planner and manager, poor consultant support, and a stodgy organizational hierarchy for the disappointing outcome. In a post-decision interview, a par ticipant related:

It’s the same type of management, and it’s the same type of thought process that’s running the company, and no one’s taking a risk, and no one’s really doing things differently. . . . It’s the same good-old-boy’s network that has been here forever. . . . We’re just going to keep making the same mistakes, tripping the same way unless somebody steps up and says, “Let’s do it differently.”And you don’t see that. You don’t see that from the top down.

## Consequences

The BPR model taught to Coast portrayed abstractions of work and the workplace that emphasized tasks, activities, and measurements but marginalized key aspects of the material, social, and political organizational realities. The change vision accentuated the issues and opportunities resonant with the central organizing themes of the BPR model, but at the same time constrained and habitualized a narrowed perspective of work. Essential elements of work were systematically understated in change, limiting the potential for successful implementation of organizational technologies.

In the planning meetings, the BPR-backed methodology defined the “rules of the game” for conducting radical change planning. International defined the relevant elements of work and how they would be organized in the planning sessions. The methodology set the rules for planning, including which goals were appropriate and prescriptive rules that defined the legitimate course of action in pursuing those goals.

Alternative strategies and heuristics for change planning were viewed as heretical, especially under foreshortened planning schedules and the double-duty pace of radi cal organizational change. The opportunities for beneficial change—factors that ini tially motivated and excited planners to engage in change planning—became difficult to articulate as they were filtered and strained through the sieve of a narrow model paradigm. Planners became frustrated with the extra workloads and the constrained discourse of the planning process. The final change plan that emerged looked little like the grand expectations of organizational revitalization initially foreseen by the plan developers.

## Gaining a Foothold

What made Coast’s planners willing to accept the consultant’s perspectives of work and IT-enabled change despite personal reservations and Coast’s history of successful change? One important explanation appears to have been the popularization of the BPR planning model in the mainstream professional management literature.

Coast distributed daily copies of the Wall Street Journal to several dozen managers, and popular managerial magazines as well as trade magazines were visible in offices around the firm. Managers indicated a general familiarity with articles from these news sources. Interestingly, the single most significant effect of the business literature at Coast was the appearance of widespread acceptance and legitimization of BPR and IT to enable organizational change—not the informational content of the articles, as had been expected.

This perception of widespread legitimacy made it easier to mobilize support and garner organizational resources at Coast. The broad popular support of BPR, complemented by claims of spectacular success in “best practice” organizations, predisposed Coast’s management to adopt the consultant’s methodology without a detailed ex amination of the project’s feasibility and without substantial evidence indicating the probability for a successful outcome. Despite a surprisingly low level of active reading of the popular literature, key knowledgeable subject matter experts were able to sell Coast’s senior management on undertaking the large-scale BPR project with little justification.

## Bridging High Concepts

International’s methodology bridged the abstractions of the popularized BPR model to a concrete planning process for Coast. General rules for BPR were introduced to Coast’s planning process through International’s methodological tools and techniques. The training and the methodology did not, however, expose Coast’s planners to a comprehensive view of BPR or the critiques against it. Their methodology simply elaborated upon the basic premises of the BPR model of organizational change, bind ing the model constraints to International’s existing methodology. The basic imperatives underpinning the BPR model—the “epistemological envelope” of the model [41]—remained the critical ingredient in formulating a coherent organizational change methodology.

International Consulting elaborated upon the BPR theme by making the abstract organizational change model vivid for their managerial audience. They added detail, filling out model abstractions with the tools and techniques of managerial change, embellishing on the abstract genre conventions to make their product more immedi ately applicable and readily intelligible. The abstract BPR model was given form and substance; through that form, the methodology bound thinking about work and organizations, portraying and privileging certain views and beliefs in their application (Table 3).

The operationalizations of select BPR model constructs framed the planning discourse and shaped the planning process at both Coast and Domestic Tel. A comparative analysis is depicted in Table 4, showing in both cases how social rules redefined the planning context. This selection and arrangement of elements from the workplace allowed the consultant to structure the definitive and evaluative context of work. Norms of adherence to the methodology and its tenets reinforced the centrality of the model’s key themes as the group encouraged pro-model thinking and discouraged conceptual deviance.

## Conclusions

THE CASE STUDIES AT COAST AND DOMESTIC suggest that planning IT-enabled change depends greatly upon the role of the consultant in interpreting broad themes into concrete practices. The BPR model of change adopted by both Coast and Domestic shaped and constrained the process of planning by creating a particular sensibility about the organization and the role of IT within it. BPR introduced binding rules about how planning was to be conceived, what roles actors played within it, and what means and actions were appropriate. Planners worked within an intellectual framing of relevance, materiality, and importance set in place by the BPR methodology.

The case studies at Coast and Domestic allow for the formulation of four propositions. At Coast and Domestic, adoption of the BPR ideology restricted how organizational IT was conceptualized and disallowed many of the unique affordances and strategic implications of using information technologies to lead organizational change. This analysis leads to the proposition that models of IT carried within the profes sional business management community distinctly influence the conceptualizations of organizational IT held by strategic planners, exerting pressure on organizations to accept IT-enabled change.

Proposition 1. Models of IT-enabled organizational change shape and structure the nature of planning and planning expressions.

This research also suggests that diffuse, supra-organizational models of technological and organizational change play a significant role in shaping local character izations of IT. Broad professional and social discourses provide organizational actors with the foundation for constructing contextual understanding by connecting local discourse to a larger and more diverse community of social actors. The consultant methodologies that guided Coast and Domestic were formed from a model of change that transcended their organizational context. Local perceptions and technological frames were being shaped by models of IT as a strategic enabler that came from the broader professional discourse of business management.

These characterizations embody simple, transportable, and conceptually ambiguous notions for gaining power and control over the organizational change process. Achieving widespread recognition, ideas for change become part of the local management discourse, evoking stories of revolutionary change, organizational revitalization, and spectacular success among practicing professionals.

Proposition 2. Organizational technology planning is shaped by and part of broader shared models of change carried by continued discourse among professional managers and research academicians.

The common mechanism tying the disparate Coast and Domestic cases together is the broad-based stereotypical characterizations of BPR. Planners at Domestic and Coast connected to the language and tacit worldview of BPR in real and specific ways. They adopted new conventions of discussion for strategic planning, they accepted the leading role of consultants as natural, and they bound themselves and each other into a process enforced through social sanctioning and tacit public compliance.

The BPR model was new to the planners at Coast and Domestic. It was clear through their gradual adoption of the language and world vision that the model was one that had to be learned. Through their engagement in the planning process, participants gained an appreciation for what was to be considered meaningful, relevant, and im portant in planning IT-enabled change. This leads to a third proposition:

Proposition 3. Popularized models of technological and organizational change carried in the broad managerial discourse shape the organizational planning process by defining the way participants can talk about computerization and work, and by predefining the taken-for-granted assumptions of IT and work organization.

A fourth proposition about the role of the consultants can also be inferred from the Coast and Domestic cases. Acting as strategic guides for their clients, the consultants from International and Global were tapping the language and worldviews of the professional management community [33]. These consulting firms translated the ab stract BPR vision from a topic in the general management discourse into a specific, detailed planning process common between Coast and Domestic. Calling upon the broader professional management community, consultants were able to gain struc ture and, more important, legitimacy for their methodologies.

The consultant firms used their own process management methods and procedures refashioned to the terminology and stylisms of the BPR model. Assuming the dominant political position in leading the BPR planning at Coast and Domestic, consultants were able to advance certain themes from the embedded rule systems of BPR to their advantage. Consultants were able to coerce support for their actions and legitimate pursuit of their own contract deliverables by selective application of the criteria and action patterns from BPR.

The methodologies promoted by International Consulting and Global Consulting in this study did not reflect the rich scholarly discourse on BPR or IT-enabled change, but instead focused on structured work flow analyses and measurable deliverables. Their engagements lacked evidence of such critical insights as strategic complementarity and broad-based strategic scoping [68], social and political issues in BPR implementation [14, 45, 46], or the importance of organizational learning [21, 24, 60].

The consultant methodology did, however, carry complex rule systems of appropri ateness, political and social power, and consensual behaviors, controlling the content and discourse of the planning process. Often this happened overtly, when the consultants would call upon the need to demonstrate measurable progress in order to redirect discussions or to stifle unwanted commentary. Systems of social sanctions were en acted that restricted deviance from the consultants’ preprogrammed methodology. These regulatory systems checked dissident behaviors and curbed renegade talk within the planning sessions, pushing many key concerns out of the planning discussions into the hallways and disabling opportunities for repair dialogue within the meetings.

With few knowledgeable subject-matter experts within the organization, planners at Coast and Domestic had to learn about IT-enabled change through the teachings and materials provided by the consultants. Participants lacked knowledge and experi ence to challenge the imperatives and rule systems underpinning the change model. These recipients of the imposed methodology remained hesitant to question subject matter experts or to criticize their socially lean portrayals of work and work life in fear of appearing ignorant.

Proposition 4. Consultant firms are intermediaries that translate abstract and institutional-level conceptions into actionable and individual-level realities and thus influence the form and intent of the change initiative.

## Summary

CRITICS OF BPR AND OTHER MANAGERIAL MODELS of organizational change have advanced several critiques of using IT as a change agent, often ascribing failure to achieve dramatic organizational transformations to an overutilization of key resources, fading executive support, or demanding time and resource constraints on the partici pants. There is evidence supporting many of these critiques in this study. Planners at Coast were overburdened by assuming the BPR duties in addition to their normal job functions; planners at Domestic were continually under pressure to meet challenging deadlines. While change programs started off with high visibility and management support, planners from both projects complained of a lack of support as the political and capital costs of change began to reveal themselves. And while the overall planning effort in both cases enjoyed better than average support, it could be argued that even these were insufficient for the task. But these problems are symptomatic, not of managers lacking “the right stuff,” but of the emergence of implicit normative and conceptual constraints on managerial expression during the planning process.

At Domestic, Global’s interpretation of BPR emphasized quantifiable deliverables and the development of “quantum leap” change opportunities that masked the fundamental technical incompatibilities associated with their legacy mainframe systems. At Coast, the consultant-provided methodology played a pivotal role in guiding the planning dis course and structuring participant behavior. International Consulting’s model of IT-en abled change strongly shaped the strategic talk and appropriate behaviors in planning meetings, distorting participant expression to a point where the final recommendations presented by the planning team had little practical relevance to Coast’s operations and sent the planners “back to the drawing board.”

The Coast and Domestic cases show how consultant intermediaries are able to trans late abstract and institutional-level conceptions into actionable and individual-level realities, and how within this translation the intermediary’s interests can reform the inherent institutional message. Consultant intermediaries act as a mechanism for bridging institutionalized conceptions of management and the formative processes of discrete social interaction in technological planning.

It is tacitly assumed in the academic and popular literatures that visions of IT carried within the broad professional discourse add flexibility and opportunity to the planning process. This study questions this presumption, suggesting that models of IT-enabled change carried in the professional discourse can actually systematically negate flexibility and opportunity from the planning process.
