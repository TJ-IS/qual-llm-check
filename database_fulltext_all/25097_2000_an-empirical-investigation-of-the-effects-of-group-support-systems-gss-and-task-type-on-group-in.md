# An Empirical Investigation of the Effects of Group Support Systems (GSS) and Task Type on Group Interactions from an Influence Perspective

WAYNE W. HUANG AND K.K. WEI

WAYNE W. HUANG is Senior Lecturer at the School of Information Systems, Technology and Management, The University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia. His main research interests include group support systems, electronic commerce, and software engineering. He has published papers in IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, Information and Management, IEEE Transactions on Profes sional Communication, European Journal of Information Systems, and some top IS conference proceedings such as International Conference on Information Systems; Hawaii International Conference on Systems Sciences, and European Conference on Information Systems.

K.K. WEI is Professor and Head of the Department of Information Systems, School of Computing, National University of Singapore. He received his Ph.D. in 1986 from University of York, United Kingdom. His main research interests include electronic commerce, group support systems, virtual organizations, and computer mediated communication. His papers have been published in Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, Information and Management, Decision Support Systems, Management Science, and ACM Transactions on Information Systems.

ABSTRACT: This research examines an important issue that has not been specifically addressed in the GSS literature, namely, the effects of GSS and task type on group processes and social interactions. Most previous GSS research has focused on study ing group outcomes, whereas the current research studies group interaction processes using two different coding methods. A conceptual research model is derived from the Theory of Time, Interaction and Performance (TIP theory) and a review of the litera ture on social psychology and GSS. Task and social interactions are studied from an influence perspective. The task type is varied as preference task and intellective task. The support level is varied in a GSS support and non-GSS support (i.e., face-to-face) discussion. Research results report that GSS could significantly influence group interaction processes, and tend to enhance group task interactions but dampen group social interactions.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group interaction, group processes, group support systems

GROUP WORK IS IMPORTANT TO ORGANIZATIONS but it is frequently not as productive as expected [52]. A GSS is expected to provide a solution to this problem. About 200 GSS experiments and dozens of GSS field studies have been conducted during the last decade for this purpose [10, 34].

The research literature shows that a GSS tends to increase group task performance, such as group decision quality, but fails to enhance or even decrease group satisfac tion [e.g., 5, 23, 34, 65]. Theoretical reasons for this failure are not completely known. Most prior GSS research focuses on group outcomes, and group interaction processes are inadequately studied [65, 74, 80]. However, group interaction process is central to a group [70]. In most prior GSS research, the study of group interaction processes has been regarded as a black box [e.g., 46, 89, 109], and therefore, further research is required in order to fully understand the dynamics of these processes.

A review of social psychology literature shows that two types of group interactions exist in group processes, i.e., task and social interactions, which coexist and are equally important to a group [e.g., 3, 7, 8, 16, 63]. The TIP theory [71, 72] posits that a group engages in three group functions simultaneously: production, group well-being, and member support (further discussion of this theory can be found in the section on “Research Hypotheses”). Conceptually, group functional activities of production overlap with group task interactions, whereas group functional activities of member support and group well-being overlap with group social interactions. Previous research indicates that even in task-oriented computer system design meetings, only 40 percent of the meeting time is spent on task-focused discussions and interactions [81].

A review on GSS research literature indicates that GSS can enhance group task activities, and many GSS tools are specifically designed to support group task activities such as brainstorming, idea organizing, and voting/pooling [78]. In fact, most prior GSS research examines group issues of ideation, problem solving, and decision-making [77], which largely involves group task interactions, whereas research on other group issues such as group well-being, member support, and social behaviors has been sparse [73]. Relatively less has been known about the impact of GSS on group social interactions in GSS research literature. In group interaction processes, members interact with each other and naturally (consciously and/or unconsciously) exert certain forms of influence on one another [e.g., 61]. As a result, group interactions are studied from an influence perspective in this research.

Task is an important variable in group dynamics and often moderates group outcomes [e.g., 23, 89]. Hence, it is important to study whether or not task and GSS have an interacting effect on group interaction processes. In summary, this research studies the impacts of GSS and task on group processes (i.e., group task and social inter action processes).

The remaining parts of this paper are organized as follows. The first two sections briefly review previous related research, derive a conceptual research model for this study, and formulate research hypotheses. After that the research methodology is discussed. Then research results are reported, followed by the discussion and impli cations of the research findings.

## A Conceptual Research Model from an Influence Perspective

A GROUP CAN BE VIEWED AS AN INTERACTION SYSTEM that mainly consists of two types of interactions: task and social interactions [1, 2]. Task interactions of a group are directly related to the group’s tasks and are involved in asking for or giving infor mation, suggestions, directions, and possible ways of action, whereas social interac tions are directly related to relations between group members or internal needs (or preferences) of members [1, 2]. Because group interactions are studied from an influ ence perspective, relevant prior research on influence in social psychology literature is reviewed here. In general, two forms of influences have been identified and largely studied in the research literature, i.e., informational and normative influences. Infor mational influence is based on the acceptance of information from others as evidence about reality, and normative influence is based on the desire to conform to the expectations of other group members [29].

A review of social psychology literature shows that the two theories of informa tional and normative influence have become and remained as general models for group influence processes [e.g., 12, 15, 29, 51, 56, 61, 94]. Three main characteristics of the two theories are identified from the literature review and summarized below.

## Assumptions

The normative influence theory posits that human beings often need to seek approval and a sense of belonging, and the aim in human social relations is for harmony and communality. There are generally four sources of normative influence: dominance, majority power, persuasiveness, and hierarchy status [19]. Informational influence theory assumes the centrality of being correct, of knowing and understanding the world, and therefore of needing and processing factual information. Sources of informational influence include factual information sharing [55], factual and task messages/rationales/ arguments [57, 67]. Hence, normative influence theory seems to focus more on interpersonal relationships whereas informational influence theory emphasizes more the exchange of factual information and the search for task truth, which conceptually corresponds to the main characteristics of social and task interactions respectively.

## Key Aspects of Group Discussion

Normative influence centers on concerns about a group itself, its dynamics, and one’s position in it, whereas informational influence is driven by a desire to achieve accu rate and correct decisions. Broadly speaking, factual information can be described as reference to observable events; normative information usually involves evaluative and “good–bad” preferences that are mainly validated by reference to other’s expressions of values/preferences [13]. Hence the two theories also differ in the importance of group discussion. For normative influence theory, it is the preferences and values held by others—that is, the judgments per se—that are important; for informational influence theory, it is the facts, rationales, and arguments supporting the judgments— the component parts of the judgments—that are important [67]. Again, the different importance of group discussion in the two theories overlaps with the different empha sis on group task and social interactions.

## Mechanisms of Influencing

Informational influence theory suggests that effective influence<sup>1</sup> comes from infor mation shared in group discussion that is novel or nonredundant for a given member [58]. Hence the mechanism of informational influencing is that whenever effective influence takes place, it is because one group member incorporates new information that is provided during discussion. This new information can be provided via persuasive arguments [11] and information sharing [55]. Because such persuasive argu ments and information sharing involve asking for or giving information and persuasive suggestions that are largely communicated in task interactions [1, 2], the informational influencing mechanism is more likely to be effected in task interactions.

On the other hand, normative influence theory predicts no differential effect be tween redundant and nonredundant information, but attributes effective influence to the force of the number of group members expressing some preferences or to the importance of interpersonal pressure [58]. Hence, the mechanism of normative influencing is that effective influence takes place because group members conform to normatively preferred views reported by other members, which are motivated by desires to maximize their social rewards, like acceptance, status, and adequacy [85, 90]. Because the social rewards, such as acceptance and status in a group, can be described using relationships between group members, and are directly related to the internal needs of group members, which are expressed in social interactions [1, 2], the norma tive influencing mechanism is more likely to be effected in social interactions.

In addition, based on a thorough review of literature on social psychology, other main characteristics of information influence and normative influence are identified and summarized as shown in Table 1. These include: manifestation, motivation, sources of influences, decision model, type of issue, interactive goal, morality, cultural level, and allocating rewards. The main characteristics of informational influence such as information sharing, factual and task messages, rational decision model, etc., are essentially reflected in task activities of asking for and giving information, suggestions, and directions [1, 2], which are involved in task interactions. Similarly, the main characteristics of normative influence, such as group relationships, morality of care, seeking subjective virtue, group norms, preferences, maintaining harmony, etc., are essentially centered on relationships between group members or internal needs/preferences of members [1, 2], which are involved in social interactions.

Therefore, to summarize the above discussion, from a group influence perspective, task interactions can be studied using informational influence, while social interactions can be studied using normative influence.

The two influences can be better elicited in an intellective task and a preference task respectively (see 8 in Table 1). Hence, these two tasks have been adopted for this research. Kaplan and Miller [59] examined the effect of task type on informational and normative influence in noncomputer support groups. They used an intellective task and a judgmental task and adopted two decision rules: unanimous decision and majority decision. They audio recorded group sessions, and later analyzed and coded group interactions of the sessions in terms of informational and normative influences. Their main findings were: (1) informational influence predominated in intellective task groups; (2) normative influence predominated in preference task groups; and (3) the effect of task type was stronger under the unanimous decision rule than under majority decision rule. The task-influence mode of the finding (1) and (2) has been confirmed by more recent research [60, 61].

A review of GSS research literature has shown that there are a few previous studies that specifically examine influence behaviors. Zigurs et al. [109] studied influence behavior using an intellective task and adopting Putnam’s coding scheme. Their study showed no significant difference of influence behavior and overall influence distribution between GSS groups and unsupported groups. GSS groups used more initiation and procedure statements, whereas unsupported groups used more goal-oriented and integrative statements. Clapper et al. [19] studied impacts of GSS on normative influ ence using an intellective task and a judgment task, focusing on group outcomes rather than group processes. They found that GSS could significantly decrease major ity influence (one type of normative influence) in a group.

Lim et al. [69] studied interacting effects of GSS and leadership using a preference task. They reported that GSS groups had greater influence equality than unsupported groups. Tan et al. [96] examined the effects of GSS on status influence in different national culture contexts (United States versus Singapore). Their research findings reported that GSS reduced status influence and this influence was more sustainable in Singaporean culture than in U.S. culture. They did not focus on group interaction processes but on group outcomes using a mathematical formula to measure status influence.

A conceptual research model of GSS intervention in group interaction processes, as shown in Figure 1, is mainly drawn on TIP theory [71, 72], five basic structures of a general purpose GSS system [e.g., 9], and relevant prior research. In Figure 1, group influence mainly consists of informational and normative influence [e.g., 19, 29, 58]. Before GSS intervention in group interaction processes (see the left-hand side of Fig ure 1), informational influence predominates in an intellective task whereas normative influence predominates in a preference task [59, 60, 61]. GSS can influence group interaction processes profoundly [109], so that after the GSS intervention (see the right-hand side of Figure 1), informational influence in an intellective task could be increased (i.e., [I-i nfluence(II ) > I-influence(I )] and [I-i nfluence(IV ) > Iinfluence(III)]); and normative influence in a preference task could be reduced (i.e., [N-influence(II) < N-influence(I)] and [N-influence(IV) < N-influence(I)]). Since informational influence reflects task interactions and normative influence reflects social interactions, this model suggests that GSS might enhance task interactions but dampen social interactions. The rationale of this conceptual model is discussed below, and the formulation of related hypotheses follows.

## Research Hypotheses

TIP THEORY [71, 72], A GROUP PROCESS THEORY, indicates that a group simultaneously and continuously engages in three major functions—production (akin to what is often referred to as problem-solving or task-performance phases), member support (reflecting relations between individual members and the group), and group well being (reflecting relations among group members). Conceptually, the group function of production overlaps with group task interactions, whereas group functions of member support and group well-being overlap with group social interactions. These three functions are transcended by four group modes of activity—inception (goal choice), technical problem solving (mean choice), conflict resolution (of different preferences and values), and execution (attaining goals). Group activity does not need to follow any fixed sequence of these four group modes. A group’s specific activity path, whether it consists of the above two, or three, or four group modes, depends on the performed task and other contextual factors.

Task type has been found to significantly affect group interaction processes [e.g., 76] and group outcomes [e.g., 39, 84]. Hence GSS intervention in group interaction processes should be analyzed separately according to task type. Furthermore, a GSS normally consists of various structures/tools to support various group activities. However, five basic structures of a general-purpose GSS [e.g., 9, 84] exist in almost all GSS systems [e.g., 22, 28, 102] and have been adopted in many previous GSS re search studies. A GSS is used as a general purpose system for this research as used by Rao and Jarvenpaa in their research [87]. The five basic GSS structures are anonym ity, simultaneity, electronic recording and displaying, structured interaction process, and enhanced information processing [9]. Further, GSS-supported groups are controlled so that they can only use the electronic communication channel to interact with each other in this study.

## The Effects of GSS on Informational Influence

Informational influence can be better elicited in an intellective task [e.g., 59]. In an intellective task, according to TIP theory, groups likely follow the activity path of inception, technical problem solving, and execution [100]; and the key group issue is technical problem solving, which depends on more factual information exchange [68, 70].

First, a GSS structured meeting agenda, which regulate group interactions, [21] could encourage more task-focused information exchange. Second, GSS anonymity can reduce status differences and social norms [78], so that group members can focus on merits of expressed ideas and therefore exchange more factual information with less fear of social disapproval and offensiveness [48, 87]. Third, GSS simultaneity provides equal and open access to an electronic communication channel for each group member, which would be likely to foster member participation and thereby increase factual information exchange. Fourth, prior research has reported that GSS groups tended to exchange more task information than manual face-to-face (FtF) groups [e.g., 18, 75]. All these suggest that GSS could enhance factual information exchange in group interaction processes, thus increasing informational influence. ## The Effects of GSS on Normative Influence

Normative influence can be better elicited in a preference task [e.g., 59]. In a prefer ence task, no correct answer exists [70]. According to TIP theory, groups would go through an activity path involving three modes of inception, conflict resolution, and execution; and the degree of the modes involved depends on group members’ degree of value conflicts [100]. The key group issue is the conflict resolution, which requires the exchange and understanding of members’ different preferences, cultural values, and social norms [70, 73].

In group interactions, conflict resulting from different values and preferences would be better resolved in FtF talks with multiple social cues. For example, personal preferences can be better expressed simultaneously in the tone, speed, and content of verbal statements, and in the facial expression and gesture of nonverbal behaviors. However, the tone and speed of verbal statements and the facial expression and gesture of nonverbal behaviors do not exist within the electronic communication channel of a GSS. Only the content of verbal statements can be communicated in the form of electronically written messages. Hence many social cues are reduced or eliminated in GSS electronic communication [53, 91, 93, 99], which would in turn hinder the exchange and understanding of personal preferences and values.

Furthermore, GSS structures of anonymity and simultaneity enable members to fo cus on the expressed idea itself rather than the idea initiator [78], because it is much more difficult to identify the idea initiator in such a setting [20]. Consequently, as it is difficult to identify the idea initiator, it would be difficult to exchange personal preferences and values held by each group member. In addition, the GSS structured meeting agenda requires group members to state their views and ideas at specific points along the specified decision-making path, which leads groups to systematically go through a rational sequence of decision phases [28]. Thus groups are likely to adopt a rational decision model [109] rather than a social decision model in group decision-making. The above-mentioned personal preferences, values, and social de cision model reflect normative influence. [See (2), (4), and (7) in Table 1.] Therefore, in summary, GSS would intervene in group interaction processes by reducing norma tive influences.

H2: In a preference task, normative influence will be less in GSS groups than in FtF groups (i.e., [N-influence (III) > N-influence (IV)]; see Figure 1).

## The Effects of GSS on Group Influence Behavior and Influence Distribution

GSS intervention in group interaction processes can be further examined by explor ing the effects of GSS on a group’s general influence behavior, which conceptually consists mainly of informational and normative influences [59, 61]. The two dependent variables of influence behavior and influence distribution have been commonly used in prior GSS research [e.g., 69, 109]. Influence behavior is defined as those actions that attempt to affect or determine the course of group behavior; influence distribution is defined as the reciprocal of variance of group members’ influence be haviors in a group [109]. Here the word “attempt” is important because it is attempted influence that is being measured and analyzed, with the emphasis on objective actions that attempt to move or affect a group. Further, influence behavior refers to actions that are expressed rather than implied.

Because informational influence prevails in an intellective task and normative influence prevails in a preference task [61], without GSS intervention it is expected that influence behavior should have a similar pattern of effects on influence distribution as informational influence has on an intellective task, and as normative influence has on a preference task.

## Intellective Task

In an intellective task, before GSS intervention in group interaction processes, informational influence predominates in group interactions [e.g., 59]. After GSS intervention, GSS tends to enhance this prevailing informational influence (H1). Even though normative influence also exists in group interactions, it is the minor component of influence behavior [e.g., 59] (see the N-influence(I) in Figure 1), because the key group issue is technical problem solving [72, 100], which requires more ex change of task and factual information [70]. In other words, normative influence would be required much less for solving an intellective task. Consequently, influ ence behavior, being mainly composed of informational and normative influence [29, 59], might be increased as the prevailing informational influence would be enhanced by GSS (H1).

H3: In an intellective task, influence behavior will be greater in GSS groups than in FtF groups (i.e., [Influence (I) < Influence (II)]; see Figure 1).2

In FtF groups, 20 percent of the group members normally do more than 80 percent of the talking [77], which means that some group members are often more dominant than others in group interaction processes. The research literature on GSS shows that GSS can result in a more even participation from group members [e.g., 5, 77], and therefore GSS would lead to a more even influence distribution.

H4: In an intellective task, influence distribution will be more even in GSS groups than in FtF groups.

Preference Task

In a preference task, before GSS intervention in group interaction processes, normative influence predominates in group interactions [e.g., 59]. After the GSS intervention, GSS tends to dampen this prevailing normative influence (H2). Even though informational influence exists in group interactions, it is the minor component of influence behavior [e.g., 59] (see the I-influence(I) in Figure 1), because the key group issue is the conflict resolution, which requires more exchanges of personal preferences and values [72, 100]. In other words, informational influence would not be required as much for solving a preference task. Consequently, influence behavior might be decreased as the prevailing normative influence would be dampened by GSS (H2).

H5: In a preference task, influence behavior will be less in GSS groups than in FtF groups (i.e., [Influence (III) > Influence (IV)]; see Figure 1).

The key group issue is the conflict resolution for a preference task, and this would require members to clarify their different values and preferences. Before GSS inter vention in FtF group interaction processes, members who are more adept at verbal expressions or possess higher social status could be more likely to occupy the airtime to speak [77], and they are therefore more dominant than others in terms of clarifying their preferences and values. After the GSS intervention, such dominance from these members would be reduced because (1) normative influence reflecting social status [see (6) in Table 1] could be dampened by GSS (H2), which is also supported by the research findings of Tan et al. [96]; and (2) the GSS simultaneity provides an equal and parallel communication channel to every group member. Lim et al. also reported in their study that GSS tended to increase influence equality for a preference task [69].

## Research Methodology

## Experimental Design and Setting

THIS EXPERIMENT HAD A TWO-BY-TWO FACTORIAL DESIGN. Support and task type were the two independent variables. Support was varied using GSS support and FtF manual support. Task type was varied in an intellective task and a preference task.

The GSS system used in this experiment was the SAMM system (Software Aided Meeting Management) developed by the University of Minnesota [28]. The five basic structures of anonymity, simultaneity, electronic recording and displaying, structured meeting agenda, and enhanced information processing were provided by SAMM as a general purpose GSS system. GSS group members could only communicate through the system. Under the condition of manual support, groups only had normal FtF discussion, but were provided with flipcharts as well as with the same structured meet ing agenda as in GSS groups.

Experimental steps for the two tasks were: (1) group members were asked to do a warm-up task; (2) for an intellective task, individual members were asked to learn the task criteria; (3) members in GSS groups were trained to learn the structured meeting agenda and operations of the SAMM system; (4) before formal meetings, group members were asked to fill in pre-meeting questionnaires; (5) groups were asked to perform the tasks; and (6) after formal meetings, group members were asked to fill in post-meeting questionnaires and to go through a post-meeting debriefing. All meeting sessions were videotaped. All groups finished their meetings within two hours.

One hundred and forty first-year students majoring in information systems from a large university participated in the experiment. Subjects were given course credits for their participation, and they were randomly assigned to each treatment and each group to meet the requirement of independent samples. Groups were comprised of five mem bers each.

## Task Type

The intellective task for this study was adopted from Zigurs et al. [109], and required group members to choose from an applicant pool a given number of individuals to be admitted to an international studies program, based on scores (and/or data) of six attributes of applicants. These six attributes were gender, expectations for social success, self-concept, expectations for independence, attitudes about premarital sex, and prior travel abroad. There was a correct answer for this task because the causal knowledge of components of success in the studies program was certain.

The preference task of this study was adopted from Watson et al. [104], and this task of a personal trust foundation asked group members to allocate funds to a list of competing projects based on their personal preferences and values. No correct answer existed for the task. The projects were based on a personality component scheme with six basic interests or motives: theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, and religious. These two tasks adopted can elicit informational influence and normative influence in group interaction processes [e.g., 59].

## Methods for Group Interaction Analysis

Group interaction analysis for capturing insightful information in group interaction processes has become increasingly important in GSS research [82, 89, 109]. There are many different coding schemes for analyzing group interaction processes in the research literature [e.g., 2, 14, 32, 41, 79, 88], but they normally serve different research purposes, such as studying group leadership. Kaplan and Miller’s coding method [59] was adapted for this study because (1) it is one of few coding methods that specifically measures informational and normative influence in group interactions, and (2) the current study can be considered as extending Kaplan and Miller’s re search [59] from a FtF setting into a GSS setting.

Furthermore, Putnam’s influence analysis scheme [86] with modifications suggested by Zigurs et al. [109] was adopted to measure influence behavior for this study. Firstly, this coding scheme has been more commonly used in GSS research literature [69, 109]. The adoption of this scheme enables a comparison of the current research findings with prior GSS research. Secondly, it is important to study the same issue using multiple indices [e.g., 50, 61] from different perspectives [108]. Putnam’s scheme measures influence behavior from the perspective of procedural interaction behaviors, and hence using another sound interaction analysis method, such as Putnam’s coding scheme [86], provides an additional way to validate our research results from a different perspective. It is a sound empirical research tradition to study the same issue using different theoretical perspectives and methodologies [4]. The importance of a multimethodological approach has recently been emphasized by some GSS researchers [e.g., 6, 82, 101, 108]. Hence this study adopted these two interaction analysis methods.

## The Measurement of Dependent Variables

Influence behavior, informational influence, and normative influence were measured in two aspects: verbal behavior and nonverbal behavior. “Verbal behavior consists of the use of language as traditionally defined (the form and meaning of the spoken word); and nonverbal behavior typically means specific categories of actions other than the use of language, such as body motion and facial expression” [109, p. 628]. Each verbal and nonverbal act emitted by an individual member was measured. “Act here denotes one instance of a behavior, usually an utterance of speaking by a group member” [109, p. 631]. Verbal behavior was coded by viewing recorded videotapes and by examining computer log files or flipchart records according to Kaplan and Miller’s coding method and Putnam’s coding scheme, while nonverbal behavior is measured by counting acts of nonverbal procedural behavior.

This study, as in research by Zigurs et al. [109], did not intend to conduct an in depth analysis of nonverbal influence behavior such as eye gazes, hand gestures, and facial expressions. Instead, nonverbal procedural behavior was captured because of its importance as indices of leadership emergence and decision-making processes [86]. Nonverbal procedural behavior is defined as “actions that reorient or change a group’s visual aids through use of supplementary visual aids” [109, p. 628]. It consists of behaviors like changing the current display on the public screen, deleting an item from the system, and using the group communication feature in GSS groups; or changing the currently displayed page of the flipchart, crossing out material from the flipchart, and writing messages in the flipchart for public display in FtF groups [102, 107, 109]. Behaviors like changing the public display or deleting an item were diffi cult to judge as to whether they reflect informational influence or normative influence, and they were therefore coded into the category of “others” when using Kaplan and Miller’s coding method [59]. (See Appendix A.)

Kaplan and Miller’s coding method [59] was specifically designed to measure informational and normative influence. Five categories of the coding method include task facts, inferences from task facts, values/norms, personal preferences, and others. The first two categories measure informational influence and the third and fourth categories measure normative influence. Informational and normative influence in a group are the sum of all five members’ coded acts. Since the research experiment showed that GSS groups had significantly longer meeting times than FtF groups (the shortest meeting time was about half an hour for an FtF group, whereas the longest meeting time was more than one and a half hours for a GSS group), the amounts for informational and normative influence in a group were thus proportioned by dividing the total number of verbal and nonverbal acts.<sup>3</sup>

Putnam’s ten-category coding scheme was used to measure influence behavior to compute influence distribution. It captures procedural messages in group interaction processes, which occupy a substantial portion of group talking time and perform vital meta-message functions [86]. The first five categories of the coding scheme that form the basis for measuring influence behaviors are: initiation messages, goal-oriented messages, integrative messages, implementation messages, and process messages. Influence behavior in a group is the sum of all five members’ coded acts. The coded acts in each experimental treatment were then proportioned by dividing the respec tive average meeting time to correct for the unequal meeting session lengths [83]. Influence distribution was computed as the reciprocal of the variance of group members’ influence behaviors in a group [109].

Two raters worked together initially until they achieved mutual reliability, and then they completed the rating of remaining videotapes individually. (See [24].) The overall average inter-rater reliability achieved was 90 percent. Detailed contents of the two coding methods and the sample coding can be found in Appendix A.

## Discussion and Implications

TWO MAIN FINDINGS OF THIS STUDY are summarized and discussed below.

(1) GSS did profoundly affect group interaction processes by increasing informational influence (H1 supported) and decreasing normative influence (H2 supported). From an influence perspective, informational influence reflects group task interactions, whereas normative influence reflects group social interactions. Therefore, the general purpose GSS used in this study tended to favor group task interactions but dampen group social interactions. These effects of GSS on group interactions were tested using multiple indices (or dependent variables) and two different coding methods.

First, the two types of indices—direct and indirect—were used to measure group interactions in this study. The direct indices included dependent variables of informational and normative influence that directly measured the effects of GSS on group task and social interactions. (See H1 and H2.) The indirect indices included dependent variables of influence behavior and influence distribution that indirectly measured the same GSS effects. (See H3, H4, H5, and H6.) Hypothesis formulations of H3, H4, H5, and H6 were based on the hypothesis formulations of H1 and H2, but these indirect indices were measured using a different coding method (i.e., the Putnam coding scheme).

Second, the two coding methods were used to measure group interactions from different perspectives. Putnam’s coding scheme is derived from the perspective of procedural influence behavior [86] and Kaplan and Miller’s coding method is mainly derived from sources of the two types of influences [59]. [Also see (4) and (6) in Table 1.] Because this research finding was tested and confirmed using these two different interaction analysis methods, which was in accordance with some prior re search results (e.g., 18, 75), these research findings are further substantiated.

These research findings can have some implications for GSS researchers and adopters. For GSS researchers, the finding of enhancing group task interactions by GSS may be due to the fact that most previous research in GSS literature largely focuses on enhancing group task performance (e.g., 5, 25, 23, 65), which may have its historical basis. GSSs originate from earlier work on Decision Support System (DSS) [26], and the goal of DSS design is to enhance the rationality of decision-making [e.g., 62, 92]. Thus the current GSS design, influenced by the DSS design, is based on the assumption that the task performed by a group requires a stronger element of rational decision-making rather than that of social decision-making [27, 47, 109]. Consequently, many theoretical structures specifically supporting rational decision-making and thus group task interactions, such as brainstorming and NGT (Nominal Group Technique), have been studied and incorporated into GSS systems. However, few theoretical struc tures have been developed and incorporated into a GSS for supporting social deci sion-making and thus social interactions. Since both task and social interactions are equally important to a group [e.g., 8, 16, 63], future GSS research should bridge this gap to develop specific theoretical structures for supporting group social interactions.

For GSS adopters, GSS can be used to enhance factual information exchange in group interactions, which is supportive to the group function of production and the group activity mode of technical problem solving [72]. Further, although GSS appears to dampen group social interactions, it is not necessarily an absolute defect in GSS. Rather, the dampening effect of GSS can be applied to certain group work where this effect is desirable, such as to facilitate group brainstorming in creative activities, to encourage equal participation for democratization, or for criticism blind to status [103].

(2) The effects of GSS on group interactions could be moderated by task type. In this study, the moderating effect of task type was demonstrated in two aspects. First, in an intellective task, influence behavior was increased by GSS; but in a preference task, influence behavior was decreased by GSS. Second, the evenness of group influence distribution was increased by GSS for a preference task, whereas the evenness of influence distribution was reduced by GSS for an intellective task.

This moderating effect of task type could have implications for GSS researchers and adopters as well. For GSS researchers, because task type can moderate the effects of GSS on group interaction processes, groups should require different GSS supporting structures/tools when performing different tasks. Hence the potential relationship between task types and GSS structures can be an important factor for GSS design, and also an important research topic for future GSS studies. GSS adopters should carefully analyze the characteristics of different task types before using GSS, so that suitable GSS structures/tools can be selected for supporting their group interactions, and ultimately enhancing their group performance.

## An Attempt to Reconcile Prior GSS Empirical Research

As mentioned at the beginning of this paper, GSS have been found to enhance group task performance like decision quality [e.g., 30, 31, 35, 36, 49, 79], but have failed to enhance group satisfaction and perhaps even decreased it [e.g., 30, 36, 37, 40, 49, 104]. The theoretical reasons for this problem are not fully known. The conceptual research model in Figure 1 and our research findings may provide one possible expla nation for this problem. On the one hand, GSS tends to increase informational influence, encourage factual information exchange, and favor task interactions. (See Figure 1; and H1 supported.) Hence, the group function of production and the activity mode of technical problem solving [72] could be enhanced by GSS, which is one key issue in group task activities [100]. As a result, group task performance, such as decision quality, could be increased by GSS.

On the other hand, group satisfaction often involves group members’ perceptions. Social interactions conceptually overlap with group functions of member support and group well-being [72]. The dampening effect of GSS on social interactions would therefore affect these group functions as well. If members perceive that their group’s functions of member support and group well-being are dampened by GSS, it would be likely that they would feel less satisfied with their group activities even if they think that their group makes a quality decision. Therefore, GSS could fail to enhance or could even sometimes decrease group satisfaction.

## A Possible Explanation of H4

The unexpected finding of H4 reported that for an intellective task, GSS resulted in a less even influence distribution, which contradicts conventional wisdom that GSS leads to a more even participation among group members. A further analysis of the computer log files and post-meeting debriefing records indicated that even in GSS groups some group members contributed more ideas and suggestions to the task solution than others for the intellective task. One possible explanation is presented below.

Although the same task case was given to all group members, due to differences in personal cognitive capabilities, life experiences, and skills in verbal expression, contributions from different group members to the task solution were not equalized. Some group members, such as those who were more adept at verbal expression [109] and thus could elaborate more from the given task case, or who had life experiences related to the task, were likely to contribute more ideas and suggestions to the task solution than others.

Furthermore, those above-mentioned group members could contribute even more ideas than others in a GSS setting, because (1) GSS encouraged more factual information exchange (H1 supported); (2) those members could have less fear of social disapproval, offensiveness, and social status in order to participate in group interactions (due to the GSS structure of anonymity) [87]; and (3) those members did not face the problem of airtime allocation in FtF talks, in which only one member could speak at any specific time (due to the GSS structure of simultaneity) [78]. As a result, those members were likely to contribute even more ideas in a GSS setting than in an FtF setting, thus leading to a less even influence distribution. Post-meeting debriefing records provided some supporting evidence for this explanation, such as:

“I’ve traveled a few foreign countries and I think [that] my travelling experiences helped me in providing more relevant information to the group discussion.”

“I had some experiences in getting along with international students in our university, [and] I should know more about the critical factors for the success in an international studies program.”

“I felt interested in the task, so I talked a lot and elaborated my points using my own life experiences that were related to the task.”

“I can sense out that some guys would be more active than me or others in group discussion, [and] they seemed to have more to say [about the task]. But I didn’t feel unhappy about it, because I had an equal chance to talk and I simply didn’t have more to say.”

Zigurs et al. also reported that in an intellective task, GSS failed to increase overall influence distribution as expected [109].

In addition, our coded data also showed that GSS groups communicated more initiation- and procedure-oriented messages, whereas FtF groups communicated more goal-oriented and integrative messages, in line with findings of the study conducted by Zigurs et al. [109]. A further review on the videotapes and computer log files (fo GSS groups) and flipchart records (for FtF groups) indicated that FtF groups seemed to have difficulty in following the structured meeting agenda because they frequently became confused or argued about the meeting agenda, or talked about something outside the scope of the meeting agenda. On the other hand, GSS groups spent most of their time in following the meeting agenda and discussed task-related issues. Therefore, the data suggested that GSS groups tended to be more comfortable in following the imposed meeting agenda and thus focused more on the task-related discussion than FtF groups in group interaction processes. An interesting finding may be that human beings seem more willing to accept and follow an imposed electronic version of a meeting agenda.

## Limitations of This Study

This study was conducted in a controlled laboratory setting. Laboratory experimental study is normally limited by its relatively low external validity, although its internal validity is high. As a result, the generalization of the research findings into organizational contexts should be treated cautiously.

This research studied group interaction processes from an influence perspective, and attempted to map two types of group interactions (task and social interactions) with two types of influences (informational and normative influences). In general, there are four sources of normative influences: domination, majority power, social persuasiveness, and hierarchy status [19]. But social interaction is a broad concept and may be more thoroughly studied from other perspectives. This initial research from an influence perspective provides some clues for future research from other perspectives, such as from a social psychological or organizational science perspective.

## Future Research

The current research findings suggest a few research issues for future studies.

Although the primary concern of this study is group processes, not group outcomes, it is important to link group processes with group outcomes. This is an unanswered research question in the current study and could therefore provide an opportunity for future research. Our research findings suggest that enhanced informational influence by using GSS would increase a group’s performance for an intellective task, whereas dampened normative influence by using GSS would decrease a group’s performance for a preference task. Future studies could investigate this important issue to examine specifically the link between group processes and group outcomes, i.e., whether a satisfactory group process leads to a better group outcome and why this might occur and under what contextual conditions.

GSS is generally considered to result in more even participation or influence for group interactions [e.g., 5, 77]. Our research findings, however, indicated that GSS could actually either increase the evenness of influence distribution as expected (for a prefer ence task), or decrease the evenness of influence distribution (for an intellective task), which is unexpected. This has been described as the moderating effect of tasks [43]. Future research should be conducted to study why and how GSS could result in less even influence distribution, and under what specific conditions. For example, one fu ture research project could replicate the current study, but choose, based upon McGrath’s task circumplex [70], different task types, such as planning task and cognitive conflict task, in order to confirm the unexpected research finding in the current study.

Because most prior GSS research was conducted in an FtF decision room setting, group social interactions such as group forming and storming activities were often considered to take place outside electronic meeting rooms [77]. Hence, rather than neglecting this important type of group interaction, GSS researchers try to supplement group social interactions by providing a suitable physical social space [77]. As a result, the GSS limitation on social interactions reported in this research might not be as important an issue as it may at first seem.

However, since distributed teamwork and distributed GSS are becoming more important [10], a virtual social space will be an important issue for designing distributed GSS. Specifically, theoretical structures should be worked out in future research in order to support group social interactions such as group forming, storming, and norming activities, or even informal caucusing [77]. Huang et al. [44] proposed a theoretical framework that specifically aims at supporting group social interactions such as issues of group norms, shared values, and preferences. Their further study reported that by incorporating this theoretical framework into an existing GSS, the GSS could support group social activities and enhance distributed group work [45].

Additionally, our research findings should be confined to the context of the general-purpose GSS adopted in this study. Hence the GSS effects of dampening social interactions should not be considered as an inherent feature of a GSS. If further re search is conducted and more theoretical structures are worked out for supporting group social interactions, a GSS embedded with these structures should be able to enhance group social interactions as well.

## Appendix A

Putnam’s Coding Scheme [86], Revised by Zigurs [106]

1. Requests or suggests deadlines, agendas, or lists of activities; action/initiation

2. Requests or makes statements about group goals or group jurisdiction.

3. Summarizes and integrates contributions (excluding signpost summaries).

4. Suggests or requests division of labor or implementation of a course of action; how to implement.

5. Requests or suggests procedural direction; how to use.

6. Changes task-related-topic of discussion by introducing an abstract label or general heading.

7. Continues task-related discussion initiated by a general heading or by an agenda category; simple confirmation or acknowledgment.

8. Changes task-related discussion by switching topics via jumping from spe cific detail to specific detail. Content switches by moving from the details of one category to the details of another category. This category should be used only for major topic changes.

9. Messages that digress from the group task to social-emotional issues. Contri bution that strays from the here-and-now interaction topics of the group. The digression does not have to address social issues—it simply has to indicate a shift from the immediate concerns of the group to other events that have very little to do with this particular group per se.

10. Initiates an interruption or multiple conversations such that the content of the interaction is difficult or decipher—too many people talking at once or several people sustaining interaction simultaneously for a short period.

Kaplan and Miller’s Coding Method [59] with Minor Modification

(The first two categories measure informational influence and the third and fourth category measure normative influence)

1. Task Facts: statements citing task facts that were provided in the task descrip tion.

2. Inferences from Task Facts: statements of facts not given in the task description but that were inferred from those given.

3. Values/Norms: statements of personal values or social norms of right or wrong in actions and outcomes, or statements of appropriateness.

4. Personal Preferences: statements alluding to one’s preference, including both simple declarations and normative pressure to reach a specific consensus.

5. Others: statements not included in the above categories and irrelevant to the task.
