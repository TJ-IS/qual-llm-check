# Stimulating Thinking: Cultivating Better Decisions with Groupware Through Categorization

KELLY M. HILMER AND ALAN R. DENNIS

KELLY M. HILMER is an Assistant Professor of Accounting and Information Systems at Virginia Tech in Blacksburg, Virginia. She holds a doctoral degree in Management Information Systems from the University of Georgia. Dr. Hilmer’s research interests involve applying group support systems to decision-making, brainstorming, focus groups, telecommuting, and other group collaborative efforts. Her research has appeared in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Information Management, and at several national and international information systems conferences.

ALAN R. DENNIS is the John T. Chambers Chair of Internet Systems in the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University. His current research interests include the use of computer technologies to support group brainstorming, decision-making, and collaborative work for teams who work in the same room at the same time, as well as those that work over the Internet. He is an Associate Editor for MIS Quarterly and serves on the editorial boards of Journal of Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Information Systems Frontiers. His research has appeared in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, Academy of Management Journal, and Communications of the ACM.

ABSTRACT: Previous research shows that groupware improves the exchange of information within groups. However, the additional information does not often lead to better group decisions, probably because individuals fail to process the new information they receive. This study explored the use of groupware processes that required individuals in groups to categorize information, in order to induce group members to better attend to the new information received from others and to integrate it into their own individual decision-making processes. Different groupware processes had different effects on attention to and integration of information, and ultimately on decision quality. Groupware processes that provided categories to organize information and groupware processes that required the receiver of information to categorize information increased attention to information and integration of information, which led to improved individual decision quality.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: decision-making, group support systems (GSS), groupware, individual cognition, information exchange, information processing.

INDIVIDUALS SELDOM HAVE ACCESS TO ALL RELEVANT INFORMATION, so when truly important decisions have to be made, a group usually is formed to make the decision or to advise the individual who must make it [15]. Group discussion enables participants to exchange information so that the group as a whole has a larger pool of information than any one person acting alone [36]. Thus, one fundamental purpose of using groups is to enable a more complete exchange and consideration of information.

However, group decision-making is often flawed. Groups seldom exchange all of the available information that is important to the decision [39, 41, 42, 43]. Even when groups do exchange key information, participants often ignore the new information they receive from others and rely instead on their prediscussion preferences [5, 7, 12]. Simply put, individual information processing often fails in decision-making groups.

This failure may be due to the individual group members’ lack of attention to and integration of available information about the decision. Many different techniques exist to assist in successful decision-making. No single decision-making technique guarantees good quality decisions, but using some formal technique may improve the likelihood that important information will not be overlooked by the individuals in a group and increase the chance that the group as a whole will be successful. The goal of this study was to develop and investigate groupware techniques designed to encourage greater attention to and integration of information by the individual group members in order to improve decision quality.

Group Decision-Making

## Groupware Research with Decision-Making

GROUPWARE IS A GENERAL TERM THAT HAS BEEN APPLIED to a variety of computerbased systems designed to help individuals exchange information and vote, whether they work together in the same room at the same time or work across the Internet from different places and at different times. Recent meta-analyses suggest that while groupware can improve decision-making in some environments, it provides only modest benefits and may even impair performance in some circumstances [1, 6].

As DeSanctis and Gallupe [10] posit, groupware should positively alter information exchange to improve decision outcomes. Previous studies have examined the exchange and use of information in group decision-making [4, 5, 7, 18]. These studies found that groupware significantly improved the exchange of information so that larger groups discussed sufficient information to enable them to find the optimal decisions. However, groups in these studies made no better decisions using groupware than when discussing the information verbally, despite identifying and discussing the critical information omitted in verbal discussions. The conclusion from these studies is that group members often discount or do not attend to information from others. Simply put, individual information processing—cognition—often fails in group settings.

## Individual Cognition

Once new information is exchanged, participants must actually process it before it can affect decisions. Individual information processing, or cognition, in decisionmaking groups can be considered as a two-step process [13, 14, 27]. The first step, once others have provided information, is for participants to attend to the information—that is, direct their cognitive focus to the information. The second step is to evaluate the information to determine if it is relevant and useful and integrate it into their decision framework. Thus, two possible explanations for the failure of individual cognition are that individuals do not attend to information they receive, or that if information is attended to, it is not integrated.

Attention to Information. One possible reason for the lack of information processing may be that information contributed to the discussion is not received by the other participants: Important information is present, but participants do not attend to it. This problem exists in both verbally interacting and groupware groups, but appears more strongly in groupware groups [7]. Information presented in groupware groups may be overlooked because it is less salient. When a participant contributes information verbally, he or she is more likely to focus the group’s attention on that informa tion [16]—it can be difficult to ignore a person speaking right in front of you. Information is presented to you (a “push” approach). Verbal discussions tend to loop back and repeatedly discuss issues that participants believe to be important [11, 40]. The redundant presentation of this information means that it becomes salient and there is less chance it will be overlooked. It is attended to and used in making decisions [31]. Simply put, the information that participants believe to be important (but not necessarily the “right” information that leads to the best decision) becomes very salient through repeated verbal presentation, is processed by all participants, and is used by the group in making its decision.

For groupware groups, in contrast, information is contained in paragraphs of text in a pool of information. It is easier to ignore information because no one is actively placing it in front of the participants. Participants must actively search out and review information—a “pull” approach, which requires more effort on their part. Likewise, there may be less systematic redundancy in the groupware information. Information seen as important by participants is less likely to be intentionally repeated when using groupware than it is in verbal discussion. (While there is often redundancy in groupware information, it is not done intentionally to highlight supposedly important information [8].) Thus the combination of the need to exert effort to actively pull information from the groupware, and the reduced repetition of supposedly important information, may mean that important information is not attended to. That is, it is unintentionally overlooked.

One advantage of the parallelism and anonymity provided in groupware is that groupware increases the amount of task-relevant information exchanged [5, 7]. But groupware may also increase the amount of extraneous information to be exchanged. The information overload due to the larger amount of information exchanged may introduce difficulty in processing all of that information [7]. Additionally, if information is received in a format that makes it difficult to process, participants may not exert sufficient cognitive effort to process it [28]. It is more difficult to find and process information when information in groupware is presented in a pool of text [5].

Integration of information. Petty and Cacioppo [28] argue that there are two “routes” by which information is processed. The first is the “central route,” in which members actively assess the information and integrate it into their overall understanding of the situation and their preferences (also called persuasive arguments or information influence). Information processed by the central route must be important and novel (i.e., not previously processed) to change a previous decision (in this case, the individual decision made prior to group discussion) [23]. The second route by which information is processed is the “peripheral route,” in which members’preferences are shaped more by peripheral cues, such as the number of people arguing for a position or their attractiveness, rather than the quality of the information itself.

Processing factual information via the central route requires more cognitive processing than processing information about others’ preferences via the peripheral route [28]. In many cases, participants have sufficient uninterrupted time prior to group discussion to process information, integrate it into their decision framework, and formulate a decision preference. When participants do have sufficient time, the central route should dominate. During discussions, however, responding to others’comments in a timely manner becomes important, so time for uninterrupted evaluation becomes more scarce. Participants may be unable to evaluate the new factual information relative to the other information they know as quickly as they need to and thus fail to integrate the new information into their decision framework.

## Stimulating Cognition

How can we stimulate an individual’s attention to information and integration of information? No one approach will be best for all situations, but just as having a structured approach in solving problems can help individual decision-making [38, 46], so too may having a structured groupware approach help individuals in groups process information better.

Stimulating Attention. We argued above that one plausible cause for the lack of attention to information in groupware groups is that information is presented in a large unorganized pool of information. Groupware can display categories of the information based on the decision alternatives, which may reduce the burden on limited cognition and may structure the information in such a way as to focus attention toward important information. Categories are a way to frame information chunked so that a related bunch of items may be recalled later [22]. Displaying categories of information based on decision alternatives may alleviate information overload because the categories arrange similar pieces of information together for each alternative [37]. Keeping these like items together may make it easier to pay attention to and remember the information because an impression of the alternative can be formed [32].

The organization of information based on decision alternatives may also eliminate extraneous information. The alternative-based categories may keep attention on the alternatives of the decision, not to other, unrelated information. Thus, a group member is less likely to go off on tangents—talking about his cat, for example—but instead focus on the information in the categories [9]. A decision alternative–based categorization scheme is not the only possible way to categorize information (e.g., categories could be criteria-based), but it was used in this study to emulate other groupware (such as GroupSystems) that use similar categorization techniques (e.g., Topic Commenter). Therefore,

H1: Groupware processes that display information within decision alternative– based categories will increase the attention to information.

Stimulating Integration. Increasing the attention to information may be necessary, but not sufficient to improve decision-making. Information must be integrated into an individual’s decision framework before it affects decision-making. Groupware processes that not only contain decision alternative–based categories for the information, but also require participants to organize information into categories may improve the integration of information. In groupware processes that require the receiver to categorize information, participants would have to read and assess every piece of information before assigning it to a category. Categorization and filtering of information provides more efficient use of cognitive resources, which may result in better decision outcomes [1]. Furthermore, requiring participants to assess and categorize information based on its importance to the decision may also focus the efforts of individuals and encourage the information to be better integrated for the group decision [29].

There are two theoretical arguments to explain why the act of categorizing may have significant effects on information integration. First, although displaying information in categories may increase attention to that information, individuals could still ignore information if they do not carefully focus on every category. Information that supports prediscussion information and choices is more thoroughly processed [28]. Therefore, alternative-based categories may focus attention on the alternative of an individual’s preference and cause an individual to ignore other information. If, instead, group members are required to categorize all information received, then all information must be attended to, explicitly decided upon, and not superficially discounted [47].

The systematic approach of evaluating each piece of information often leads to more accurate and efficient decisions [20]. Making judgments on each piece of information ensures that no one piece is overlooked or deemphasized [9]. Individuals have to think about every piece of information, instead of ignoring some information. Otherwise, elements in a decision may be ignored [17]. By evaluating each piece of information, the individual may be induced to consider the factors that make a piece of information important. This may, in turn, produce more successful criteria for the judgment of information.

Second, the categorization of information as important or not important may also impact its integration. The amount of information exchanged may be too much to comprehend. To manage the information overload that groupware often induces, the categorization of information by importance of the information to the decision may allow the individual to focus on a subset containing only the important information. This filtering that the additional categorization based on importance gives may permit the individual to attend to the important information and set aside the superfluous information [2]. In this situation, only the important information should be considered when it is integrated into the individual’s decision framework. Filtering by importance directs the decision-maker to systematically deal with complexity and numerous judgments without losing track of what is relevant [20]. Groupware processes that prompt group members for their preferences (e.g., importance of a comment) may force individuals to think more about each discussion piece of information and to support the selection of alternatives [10].

In summary, groupware processes that enforce the act of categorizing information may help ensure that no one piece of information is overlooked and allow each piece of information to be evaluated and integrated into one’s decision framework. Likewise, groupware processes that enforce the filtering of information by importance may focus the efforts of individual group members and allow the information to be integrated toward the group decision. With the display of an important category, only the important information should be considered when it is integrated into the individual’s decision framework. Therefore,

H2a: Groupware processes that require the act of categorizing information will increase integration of information.

H2b: Groupware processes that enable the filtering of information by importance will increase integration of information.

Improving Decision Quality. Research has shown that adding structure to maintain focus within the group positively affects decision outcomes [10, 38]. Keeping more focus of attention should concentrate the group members on the task at hand. The task focus should lead to higher decision quality [29, 30]. Categorization and filtering of information provides more efficient use of cognitive resources, which may result in better decision outcomes [1].

In summary, the use of groupware processes that increase attention to information may make important information less likely to be overlooked and focus attention toward that important information, thereby leading to better decisions. However, increased attention alone may not be sufficient. Information must also be integrated. Therefore, attention to information coupled with increased integration of information should promote even better decisions. Thus,

H3a: Individuals using groupware processes that increase attention to information will make better decisions.

H3b: Individuals using groupware processes that increase integration of information will make better decisions.

## Method

THE GOAL OF THIS RESEARCH WAS TO UNDERSTAND INDIVIDUAL COGNITION in a group setting—that is, to understand if groupware processes could be manipulated to increase an individual’s attention to and integration of information, and improve his or her decision quality. A laboratory experiment with a four-treatment research design was used. Each treatment examined different groupware processes.

The unit of analysis was the individual participants, not the group as a whole. We could have conducted this study by having subjects interact with other subjects in the traditional manner for groupware experiments and then measure the data at the individual level. However, this has the potential to introduce greater error variance into the process because each group discussion is likely to have a different trajectory, with different information entering the discussion at different times. An alternative would have been to use confederates to type comments into the groupware system using a prepared script (e.g., [3]). However, this is expensive in terms of the number of confederates needed and also has the potential for error if confederates do not conform exactly to the prepared script.

Therefore, a group simulator was used (e.g., see [35]). The groupware simulator behaved essentially like a series of automated confederates (cf., [3]). The groupware simulator was a software tool that looked and acted like regular groupware. Participants typed comments into the system, but instead of exchanging those comments with other participants, the simulator presented participants with comments that appeared to be from other group members but in fact were drawn from a script written by the experimenter. The script contained the task information and discussion about the information, as well as non-task comments typically found in groupware discussions (e.g., “Are we done yet?”). The script used comments from real groups who performed the task in a previous study [7]. In order not to bias the study’s outcome, subjects were not informed that they were using a simulator until the end of the experimental session.

## Subjects

A TOTAL OF 188 UNDERGRADUATES SERVED AS SUBJECTS. Subjects were sophomore, junior, and senior business students drawn from a large core course who received course credit for participating. The average age was 19.9 years. Approximately 53 percent were female. Subjects were randomly assigned into one of the four treatments, with 43 to 52 subjects in each treatment.

## Task

FOR THIS STUDY, WE BEGAN WITH THE STUDENT ADMISSIONS TASK developed by Dennis [4], which required subjects to select one student out of four (fictitious) candidates for admission to the university. We adapted the facts in the task to the university’s current admissions process with the help of the university admissions office by adding and deleting some information and adjusting GPA and SAT scores. The revised task had 17 criteria. We validated the task with the Director of Admissions to ensure that the candidate designed to be the best choice (candidate #3) was indeed the best choice.

The prediscussion information was designed to make subjects choose candidate #1 (an incorrect choice). The information in the simulator was designed to make the subjects believe they were members of a nine-member group, three of whom (including the subject) favored candidate #1, two of whom favored candidate #2, three of whom favored candidate #3 (the correct choice), and one of whom favored the last candidate. We validated the task through several rounds of pilot tests with a total set of 152 students drawn from the same subject pool as those in the experiment (but these 152 students did not participate in this experiment). The goals of the pilot tests were to ensure that the prediscussion information set induced the subjects to select the intended incorrect alternative (candidate #1) prior to “group discussion,” and to make the simulator discussion appear as “real” as possible. By the end of these pilot tests, subjects were choosing the intended candidate 98.3 percent of the time and reported that they believed the simulator discussion to have been a “real group” discussion 83.3 percent of the time.

## Treatments

THERE WERE FOUR TREATMENTS. Each treatment presented the same comments from the simulator at the same time intervals, but the way in which the comments were displayed and the way in which the subjects were expected to interact with the simulator were different.

## Baseline Treatment

The first treatment (baseline) was designed to appear as a traditional groupware tool. It appeared to provide electronic communication with anonymity and parallelism by containing a list of discussion comments in one window. Individuals typed comments, which were appended to the bottom of the discussion list window, and could scroll the comments up and down within the window. No specific process designed to improve information processing was imposed on the interaction.

## Sender Categorize Treatment

The second treatment (sender categorize) was similar to the baseline treatment, except that it displayed the comments in four categories based on the decision alternatives (i.e., the four admission candidates). This treatment was designed to increase participants’ attention to the information, without assisting them in integrating the information. In this treatment, the senders of information (i.e., the subjects) assigned their comments to one of the four decision alternative–based categories before sending their comments to “other group members.” The information that was received from “other group members” (i.e., the groupware simulator) was displayed in the respective decision alternative–based category.

## Receiver Categorize Treatment

The third treatment (receiver categorize) was designed to increase both attention to and integration of the information by requiring subjects to categorize each piece of information as it was received. Instead of categorizing comments when they were sent, the receiver categorize treatment required participants to take each comment they received from a “group comment” window and drag and drop it into one of the four decision alternative–based categories. Sorting the information requires participants to attend to all information (increasing attention) and to make explicit judgments about the information pertaining to the task (increasing integration).

## Filter by Importance Treatment

The fourth treatment (filter by importance) was designed to increase both attention to and integration of the information. This treatment was similar to the receiver categorize treatment, except that this treatment had an extra category filtering out unimportant information and enabling the participants to focus on the information they believe to be important for their decision [44].

## Procedures

THE PROCEDURES FOLLOWED THE GENERAL APPROACH OF DENNIS [5]. A total of 253 subjects reported to the 20 experimental sessions and were randomly assigned into one of the four treatments (i.e., five sessions were conducted for each treatment). Subjects first completed a background questionnaire and experimental consent form. They then received a task information sheet that provided the prediscussion (incomplete) information and were asked to make an initial individual decision. As noted above, this initial prediscussion information was designed to induce them to choose a specific incorrect alternative (candidate #1). If the subject did not choose the intended alternative (a total of nine subjects, 3.6 percent), then the experimental manipulation failed. The subjects continued in the experimental process but their data were excluded from the analysis.

Subjects were then trained in the use of the groupware simulator and worked on a brief practice task. They then were asked to enter their initial choice into the system, and after a slight pause the system presented them with the “results” of the vote (which, of course, was always predetermined to be three in favor of candidate #1, two in favor of candidate #2, three in favor of candidate #3, and one in favor of candidate #4). Subjects were informed that they each had received incomplete information and would need to share information with each other in order to reach a group decision, which might be the same or different from their original individual decision. They were then asked to discuss the task for 15 minutes and at the end of that time vote for their choice (i.e., whom they considered to be the best candidate). Subjects in the receiver categorize and filter by importance treatments were reminded to categorize the group information.

Subjects then completed two separate post-session questionnaires, asking the participants to make their final decision, to recall information discussed and used, and to report several perceptions. The first of these questionnaires asked the subjects if they had any prior admissions experience or had served on any admissions appeal committees. The seven subjects (2.8 percent) who answered yes to either question were discarded from the analyses. The second questionnaire informed the participants that some of them had been working with a simulator, not a real group, and asked whether they believed they had been working with a real group or with a simulator. The 50 subjects (19.8 percent) who responded that they believed themselves to have used a simulator were discarded from the analyses. The subjects were then debriefed, in formed that they had used a simulator, and were requested not to discuss the experiment with anyone. We did not observe any significant difference in the percentage of subjects believing they had used a simulator from the earlier experimental sessions to the later sessions (25 percent versus 18 percent, $\mathrm { F } ( 1 , 1 8 ) = 0 . 7 3 , \mathrm { p = n s ) }$ , so we believe that the majority of subjects did not “give away” the use of the simulator to others.

## Measures

## Attention to Information

ATTENTION TO INFORMATION WAS ASSESSED IN TWO WAYS. First, a perceptual measure was used. A postsession questionnaire included a scale designed to measure perceived attention to information (seven items, $\mathbf { a } = 0 . 8 4 8 )$ , in which 1 = low and 7 = high. This scale, developed for this study (see Appendix), attempted to measure the degree to which the subject believed he or she paid attention to the information.

Second, attention to information was measured by a recall of information on the post-session questionnaire. Subjects were asked to list all task information they remembered. Two independent raters counted the number of pieces of information remembered correctly. Inter-rater reliability, assessed using Cohen’s kappa, was acceptable (0.913). This information was classified as either known information recalled (i.e., information contained in the initial prediscussion task information sheet given to the subjects prior to using the simulator) or learned information recalled (i.e., unique information only received from the simulator).

## Integration of Information

Information integration was also assessed in two ways. First, a perceptual measure was used. The postsession questionnaire included a scale designed to measure perceived integration of information (eight items, $\mathbf { a } = 0 . 9 3 0 )$ in which 1 = low and $7 =$ high. This scale (see Appendix), drawn from previous research [7], attempts to measure the degree to which the subject believed he or she thought about and used information contributed by others.

In the second measure, subjects were asked to indicate which of the task information on the free recall question they used in making their decision. The same raters who coded the attention measure assessed this measure by counting the number of pieces of information remembered correctly that were indicated as being used. Interrater reliability was again adequate (Cohen’s kappa = 0.933). Again, this information was classified as either known information used (i.e., prediscussion information known prior to using the simulator) or learned information used (i.e., information received only from the simulator).

## Decision Quality

Decision quality was coded as a zero–one variable. Subjects choosing the correct alternative on the postsession questionnaire received a one. Subjects not making the correct decision received a zero.

## Results

AS DISCUSSED IN THE PREVIOUS SECTION, THE RESEARCH HYPOTHESES looked at how the experimental treatments affected attention to information and integration of information, in order to improve decision quality. This section presents the statistical results of the hypotheses. ANCOVA followed by post-hoc Tukey tests were used to test H1 and H2. Logistic regression was used to test H3 because decision quality is a zero–one variable. Table 1 presents the means and standard deviations for all treatments. Tables 2 through 4 present the statistical results.

## Attention to Information

There were no significant differences in perceived attention among the four treatments $( \mathrm { F } ( 3 , 1 8 2 ) = 1 . 2 1 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 3 0 8 )$ . Likewise, there were no differences among the four treatments for known information recalled $( \mathrm { F } ( 3 , 1 8 2 ) = 1 . 9 3 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 1 2 7 )$ . Tukey tests also found no significant differences between treatments. However, the learned information recalled differed among treatments $( \mathrm { F } ( 3 , 1 8 2 ) = 3 . 1 6 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 0 2 6 )$ . Posthoc Tukey tests found significant differences between the baseline and filter by importance treatments for the learned information recalled.Therefore, H1 was partially supported. In short, the groupware processes requiring filtering of information received by importance helped focus attention on the new information first seen in the simulator “discussion,” but had no effect on the attention to the initial information received prior to the “discussion.”

## Integration of Information

There were significant differences in perceived integration among the treatments $( \mathrm { F } ( 3 , 1 8 1 ) = 3 . 4 9 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 0 1 7 )$ . Post-hoc Tukey tests found significant differences between the baseline and filter by importance treatments, and between the baseline and receiver categorize treatments. The treatments had no effect on the information already read, considered, and integrated prior to the “discussion” (known information used) $( \mathrm { F } ( 3 , 1 6 4 ) = 2 . 1 2 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 1 0 0 )$ . Tukey tests also found no significant differences between treatments. But once again the treatments had significant effects on the new information first received from “others” via the groupware simulator (learned information used). The ANCOVA found no significant effects due to the treatments $( \mathrm { F } ( 3 , 1 6 4 ) = 2 . 0 6 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 1 0 8 )$ ), but post-hoc Tukey found significant differences between the baseline and filter by importance treatments, between the baseline and receiver categorize treatments, and between the sender categorize and filter by importance treatments. Therefore, H2a and H2b were partially supported.

## Discussion

TYPICALLY, PARTICIPANTS IN GROUP DECISIONS tend to overlook or discount previously unknown information received from other participants [12, 39, 45]. They instead rely on information known to all participants prior to discussion [12, 39, 45]. This study shows that using certain groupware processes can increase attention to and integration of information first received from “others” during group discussion. In turn, the increased integration of this information into one’s decision framework can improve decision quality.

## Attention to Information

It was hypothesized that groupware processes using categories would help people pay attention to information. The results show that people did not perceive that they paid more attention to information, and that in fact, they did not pay more attention to the already known information contained on the task sheets received prior to the “group discussion.” However, the filter by importance treatment did increase attention to the new information first received via the simulator from “others.” In short, the groupware processes requiring filtering of information by importance helped focus attention on the new information received from “others” but had no effect on attention to prediscussion information.

This groupware process encouraged the participants to pay attention to the new information and helped them to avoid the prediscussion information trap. This new information that was attended to was the unique information that was important to the decision. This increase of attention to the new information may be due to the categories allowing the subjects to “see” more new information. The filtering of information into categories by importance of the information did not help participants to remem ber what they already knew, but rather to focus in on what was new.

## Integration of Information

It was also hypothesized that groupware processes that required participants to categorize newly received information would help them integrate the information into their decision frameworks. People perceived that they integrated information when required to categorize information by importance, but again what they integrated more was the new information received from “others” via the simulator. The categorical processes had no effect on the information they already read, considered, and integrated prior to the “discussion.”

As with paying attention to new information, putting information into categories encouraged participants to take that new information and integrate it into their decision framework. The use of categories and requiring subjects to evaluate each piece of information made participants not only look at items grouped together in chunks, but made them consider how each piece of information fit into those chunks. While displaying information in categories encouraged attention, individuals could ignore some information if they did not examine every category. Because information that supports prediscussion information and choices is more thoroughly processed [28], alternative-based categories may encourage participants to focus attention on the category of an individual’s preference and cause them to ignore other categories. Instead, when participants were required to do the categorizing on all information received, all information had to be attended to, explicitly decided upon, and not superficially discounted [47]. Individuals had to think about every piece of information, instead of ignoring some information. Otherwise, elements in a decision may have been ignored [17].

If information is received in a format that makes it difficult to process, participants may not exert sufficient cognitive effort to process it [28]. There is no extra effort needed to integrate previously known information. The cost lies in integrating new information. The treatments that required people to categorize information required them to look at and evaluate each piece of information. This further step caused the subject to make the extra effort on the new information. This extra effort was conscious. That is, the subjects that participated in the treatments requiring categorization reported on a perceptual measure from a postexperimental questionnaire that they integrated more information. In this study, it was seen that the groupware processes requiring categorization and filtering by importance made the subjects actively process the information received from the simulator.

## Decision Quality

It was also hypothesized that the treatments that increased attention to information and increased integration of information would lead to better quality decisions. While there was no improvement in decision quality due to the increased attention to information, increased integration of information improved the quality of decisions. Recall that it was the treatments that required categorization and filtering by decision importance that increased integration of information. The major factor in the improved qualit of decision was the integration of information first received via the simulator (i.e., the learned information used). It was this information received during the simulator discussion that should lead to the correct decision. And it was found that as more of this learned information was recalled and used, the decision quality improved, as expected. The information given in the task before using the simulator (i.e., known information) was intended to produce the incorrect choice. As more known information was recalled and used, the decision quality decreased, again as expected.

It was discovered that increased attention to information alone did not improve decision quality, but increased integration of information did. Therefore, comments (i.e., a whole bunch of words) do not lead to good decisions, but the evaluation of those comments can. Prior research has shown that groupware can increase information exchange (compared to verbally interacting groups), but that this improved exchange does not often lead to better decisions [5, 7]. Instead, careful consideration and evaluation of information is needed to improve decision quality.

## Implications for Research and Practice

Groupware has the ability to make people think differently. It has been found in this and other studies that different groupware processes may induce different patterns of communication and different use of cognitive resources. People make different decisions depending on how the information is framed [19]. This study presented to the subjects the same information at the same time intervals in each treatment, but the display of information was framed in a different way. This study found that presenting information in categories based on the decision alternatives and requiring participants to categorize information increased attention to and integration of new information and ultimately led to better decisions. Thus the implication for practice is to use decision alternative–based categories to organize information where possible. Ideally, participants should be required to categorize newly received discussion comments into categories, rather than having the information categorized by the sender, but this may need to await the development of new groupware tools that simplify this process.

A decision alternative–based categorization scheme is not the only possible way to categorize information. Researchers using the analytic hierarchy process are well acquainted with looking at the decision-making process by breaking down the decision problem by criteria [33, 34]. Therefore, other groupware possibilities could frame the information based on the decision criteria or allow the group members to form their own categories. Additional research is needed to investigate other means of framing information to group members and their effects on attention, integration, and decision quality.

During group discussions, the individual decisions may be such that the group opinion is split as either majority/minority (i.e., a majority of the group favors one decision alternative and a minority of the group favors another decision alternative) or uniform (i.e., group members are somewhat evenly divided among the alternatives in their opinions). This study considered the case where the subjects thought they were in a uniform situation (i.e., there was no group majority). This viewpoint induces certain information processing actions that may differ if the subjects think they were in the majority, in the minority, or if they don’t know an initial group decision, because people behave differently when a majority/minority exists within a group [24, 26]. Normative influence theory argues that participants are motivated to conform to the opinions of others to preserve a favorable self-perception and self-presentation [25]. Future research is warranted to compare the change in information processing and decision quality due to different initial group compositions and the group processes used in this study.

This study looked at the individual in group decision-making using a groupware simulator. The role of simulators in research is an alternative to using full groups and confederates. Simulators can be used for idea generation [35] and for decision-making (as seen in this study). The application of a groupware simulator opens many opportunities for research. Using a simulator allows for fewer subjects to be needed, but at the same time increases the statistical power. Research using simulators may turn out to be less expensive in terms of the number of subjects that must be used. The use of simulators opens many new doors for research because the unit of analysis is the individual.

Simulators also increase experimental control by enabling the researcher to design a very specific and precise experimental environment in which to test hypotheses. Because each subject now receives the same stimulus, there is less random variation than is usually encountered in the group discussion process. Data can be gathered much more efficiently. More research is advocated to reap the above mentioned benefits and control of using a groupware simulator.

This study may also have implications for using similar processes for individual decision-making (i.e., individuals not working in groups). That is, just as the groupware processes investigated in this study helped increase attention to information and integration of information, leading to better quality decisions, the same processes could be used for an individual needing to make a decision based on collected information. The information may be received from several sources, such as e-mails, reports, and the Web. Then the individual can use processes involving categories and enforcing categorization to help with his or her decision. More research is encouraged to discover the implementation of groupware processes in individual decision-making situations.

This study employed a lab experiment with students as subjects and thus suffers from all the normal limitations of such studies [21], including the ability to generalize the result to organizational settings. Another important limitation is the use of the group simulator. While we believe the use of a simulator is an important step forward in precision, this advance also comes with a move further away from the study of “real” groups in natural organizational settings. More research is warranted to reproduce this study’s results with real (non–groupware simulated) managerial groups. Nonetheless, we believe that the results have some implications for practitioners. We believe that system developers, managers, and facilitators should consider the many possibilities introduced by the use of category-based interfaces when building, promoting, and using groupware.

This study has several implications for managers wanting to improve information processing and decision quality in organizational problem solving. This study showed that, with certain groupware processes using categories and requiring categorization, group members could focus on the information given to them and the task at hand. In an organizational setting, it is common to have individual group members with slightly different information about a particular decision to be made, especially with interfunctional teams and task forces. Therefore, if managers want meeting participants to pay attention to and integrate new information into their decisions (i.e., additional information from other constituents), processes that involve active categorization and filtering should be considered.
