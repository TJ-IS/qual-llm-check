# A Framework for Assessing the Relationship Between Information Technology Investments and Firm Performance

SUMIT SIRCAR, JOE L. TURNBOW, AND BIJOY BORDOLOI

SUMIT SIRCAR is Professor and Chairman of the Information Systems and Management Sciences Department in the College of Business Administration at the University of Texas at Arlington and Director of the Center for Information Technologies Management. His doctoral degree was earned at the Harvard Business School and he is also a Certified Computing Professional and a Disaster Recovery Planner. Dr. Sircar has published numerous articles in the area of information resource management in a large number of journals, including the Journal of Management Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, Information & Management, and Journal of Database Management.

JOE L. TURNBOW retired from the Air Force in 1990 after twenty-two years of service. While in the Air Force, he participated in several major feasibility studies and in the planning and implementation of two major computer systems. He was also responsible for evaluating productivity before and after installation of these systems. After retiring, he completed a B.S. in computer science and a B.B.A. in accounting at the University of Alaska, Anchorage, and an M.S. in information systems at the University of Texas at Arlington. He is currently a consultant in the Oil and Gas Division of Computer Task Group Inc. in Anchorage, Alaska.

BIJOY BORDOLOI is a Visiting Associate Professor in the Kelley School of Business at Indiana University, Bloomington, where he also received his Ph.D. in MIS. His current research interests include data modeling, data administration, distributed data base systems, and software project management. His publications have appeared in several journals, including Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Information Science and Technology, Journal of Microcomputer Systems Management, International Journal of Information Resource Management, and International Journal of Production and Operations Management.

ABSTRACT: There have been several attempts in the past to assess the impact of information technology on firm performance that have yielded conflicting results. Researchers have been unable to conclude that IT spending by an organization results in increases in key performance indicators. Two major recent studies have attempted to address the issue by putting greater emphasis on the theoretical underpinnings of the solution to the problem, although they chose different theoretical frameworks. The present study extends that work to yield a framework that shows the relationship between firm performance and both IT and corporate investments. The data used to validate the framework exceeds that used in previous analyses in both quality and quantity, thereby permitting appropriate statistical analyses. A large database consisting of over 2,000 observations of 624 firms was constructed, using data provided by the International Data Corporation, Standard & Poor's Compustat, and Moody's. This allowed us to pose the following research questions: (a) Can the relationship between sets of investment measures and firm performance be demonstrated (as opposed to individual measures)? (b) How are IT investments related to a firm's market value, market share, sales, and assets? and (c) Is there a difference in the effect of computer capital and noncomputer capital?

Seven measures of firm performance were initially incorporated as outputs in the framework, related to sales, assets, and market value. Similarly, seven input measures of IT and corporate investments were initially included. Two output measures and one input were eventually eliminated to formulate a refined framework with strong explanatory power. After careful editing, canonical analyses were performed, resulting in several important findings. Both IT and corporate investments have a strong positive relationship with sales, assets, and equity, but not with net income. Spending on IS staff and staff training is positively correlated with firm performance, even more so than computer capital.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information technology payoff, information technology investment, organizational performance.

OVER A DECADE AND A HALF AGO, KEEN [16] BEMOANED the lack of a “cumulative tradition” in the MIS field of the kind that exists in more established fields such as microeconomics or the behavioral sciences. Since then, a few attempts have been made to build on the works of other researchers in MIS. One such effort is the fairly extensive body of work performed to resolve the infamous “productivity paradox,” although not with the results anticipated in a cumulative tradition. Instead of each additional research project building on previous research, this stream of research has yielded inconsistent and contradictory findings. Part of the problem arises because “productivity paradox” research encompasses both productivity and performance issues.

The productivity paradox is the perceived lack of increased output resulting from investments in information technology (IT). Since the term was popularized in the mid-1980s, many studies have attempted to demonstrate the benefits of spending on IT, with conflicting and inconclusive results. Research concerning this issue has been plagued by a number of problems. One of these is definitional—mainstream IS researchers have almost exclusively focused on the impact of IT investments on firm performance, not productivity. Productivity is measured by the efficiency with which outputs are produced for a given level of inputs and, unless otherwise mentioned, is considered synonymous with labor productivity, that is, output per unit of labor (person-hour). Measurements are made of a nation's output or a firm's output, usually the volume or value of goods and services produced. These values are then divided by the number of person-hours required. This has been the domain of economists, at both the national (macroeconomic) and firm (microeconomic) levels. On the other hand, firm performance has been measured in a variety of ways, including familiar measures such as return on assets or investment, market share, and sales growth, among many others. Hitt and Brynjolfsson [13] argue that the productivity issue is grounded in the theory of production, whereas the issue of business performance has a framework rooted in theories of competitive strategy. Although the framework developed in this paper borrows from both approaches, our emphasis is clearly on firm performance, not productivity.

In view of the lack of adequate data and the sometimes flawed methodology, it is not surprising that research findings heretofore have been contradictory, inconclusive, and usually nongeneralizable. Some researchers have therefore given up on trying to correlate end results with IT investments and advocate focusing on the actual processes that IT is supposed to enhance [21, 29]. Yet another approach was attempted by Weill [34]. Rather than focus on IT spending as a whole (for which he was unable to find any effect on performance), he subdivided it into three types of IT spending and was able to show a correlation between firm performance and IT investments in processing the firm's transactions.

The objective of the research presented here, however, is to build on recent work on the relationship between aggregate IT investments and firm performance conducted independently and apparently simultaneously. These are studies representing what we have termed the “mainstream” IS approach, by Mahmood and Mann [19], and those representing the econometric or production function approach, by Brynjolfsson and Hitt [5, 6, 12]. We have attempted to extend their work to yield a framework representing the relationship between a set of IT investment measures and a set of firm performance measures, which we believe has strong validity.

Firms operate in an exceedingly complex environment, with myriad factors influencing their operations. Their performance is also multidimensional, measurable by many different gauges. Although all this complexity cannot be modeled completely, we believe the framework presented here is reasonably comprehensive, and allows us to pose the following research questions:

\- Is the gestalt approach valid—that is, can we better capture the relationship between an aggregate of investment measures (both IT and non–IT) and a set of firm performance measures (in lieu of individual variables)?

\- How are IT investments related to a firm's market value? Market share? Sales? Assets? Equity? Income? What about non–IT investments (labor and capital)? Do these effects vary by industry sector?

\- What is the impact of computer capital versus noncomputer capital on firm performance?

Using this framework, we are able to address several issues not addressed in the previous studies; we are able to confirm many of the prior findings and not substantiate some others. Moreover, having the advantage of much more data (2,009 observations covering a greater time-frame than both groups of researchers), our study examines several industry groups, which was not done in either of the aforementioned series of studies. The ability to analyze separate industries is extremely important because of their vastly different characteristics, even though some relationships may be true across a broad spectrum of firms. For example, regardless of the industry sector, our study shows a positive correlation between IT investments and a firm's sales revenues, while simultaneously showing a negative correlation with the most common measure of profitability—ROA. Similarly, IT staff spending is positively correlated with multiple performance variables across all sectors. The same is true of some other important spending variables such as computer capital (estimated street value of firm's IT assets); noncomputer capital; and labor, with the exception of one sector. Perhaps of special interest is that even IT staff training expenditures (MIS staff training budgets) are significantly and positively correlated with several performance variables—a relationship not demonstrated in the previous studies.

The following section summarizes previous work on which the current research is based and develops our framework of the impact of IT investments on firm performance. The next section details the special nature of the data used to validate the framework and compares it with previous data collection efforts. Following this, we provide the methodology used to test the framework and then provide our results. Then we attempt either to corroborate or to dispute some previous findings as well as advance some new ones.

## Development of the IT Investment Framework

EXCELLENT REVIEWS OF THE PRODUCTIVITY PARADOX LITERATURE have been provided by Kauffman and Weill [15] and Brynjolfsson [5]. Sircar et al. [28] provide a more recent comprehensive review of the literature covering the impact of IT investments on firm performance. They show that recent research can be classified as studies focusing on specific industries or processes to demonstrate IT's value [22], and those attempting comprehensive empirical studies. The second category is the focus of the present study, in particular the research of Brynjolfsson and Hitt [6] and Mahmood and Mann [19], which provide the basis of the framework developed in this paper. Each of these studies, summarized below, drew on different bodies of work for their theoretical underpinnings.

Brynjolfsson and Hitt's work was based on standard production theory from economics—that is, the output a firm produces is a function of the inputs it uses. This leads to the production function approach, which is rooted in economic theory and has been used extensively. These authors use the most common production function specification, the Cobb-Douglas, as follows:

$$
\operatorname{Log} Q _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \log C _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \log K _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} \log S _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} \log L _ {i t} + e,
$$

where $Q_{it}$ = output of a firm in industry i in year t; $C_{it}$ = computer capital; $K_{it}$ = noncomputer capital; $S_{it}$ = information systems staff labor; $L_{it}$ = other labor and expenses; $\beta$ is a vector of parameters to be estimated; and Log denotes the natural logarithm. Each of the above variables has been incorporated into the framework proposed in this study. The authors were able to report that computer capital and IS labor spending contribute significantly to firm-level output. This research was subject to the drawbacks inherent in the production function: (a) the assumption that the function is quasi-concave and monotonic—that is, output must increase at a decreasing rate with additional amounts of inputs, and (b) constant returns to scale (a limitation of the Cobb-Douglas specification).

In an extension of this work, Hitt and Brynjolfsson [12] enumerate three measures of performance based on past research: (1) total shareholder return, (2) profitability as measured by return on assets (ROA), and (3) profitability as measured by return on equity (ROE). The authors found that computer investment had a significant impact on firm output (sales), but they found no evidence of a positive impact on the three measures of business performance listed. Again, the components of these measures of business performance—assets, sales, net income, equity, outstanding shares, and share price—were included in the proposed framework of this paper.

Mahmood and Mann [19] adopted a more traditional approach for measuring the impact of IT investments on firm performance. They used the Systems Resource Approach [35] as their framework for measuring organizational performance. They examined performance measures used in previous research to use within this framework for their study. Based on this review, they selected six direct measures of organizational strategic and economic performance: return on investment, return on sales, growth in revenue, sales by total assets, sales by employee, and market to book value. In addition, the authors conducted an exhaustive review of the literature to identify IT investment measures. They arrived at five ratios: IT budget as a percentage of revenue, value of an organization's IT budget spent on staff, percentage of IT budget spent on the training of IT staff, and number of PCs and terminals as a percentage of total employees.

The authors then hypothesized a framework interrelating the two sets of variables. A particularly attractive feature of this work was the application of canonical analysis as the technique for gauging the relationship between inputs and outputs. It is known that IT affects many different outputs simultaneously—for example, sales, income, and assets—as well as most of the inputs—for example, computer capital, training, and staff expenses. It thus makes eminent sense to measure the correlation of a basket of inputs to a basket of outputs, which can be done with canonical analysis. This allows the simultaneous analysis of both sets of variables, in order to ascertain whether they are statistically independent of each other in a linear sense, or to determine the nature of their multivariate relationships. Thus, when using correlation analysis, Mahmood and Mann found that the individual IT investment measures were weakly associated with individual organizational strategic and economic performance variables. However, when they applied canonical analysis by considering the combined effect of IT investment measures, the prediction level increased significantly. They concluded that IT investment appeared to be related to organizational strategic and economic performance.

The Mahmood and Mann [19] research has some serious limitations, however. The data collected were from only one hundred firms, the Computerworld Premier 100 for just one year. This was by definition a biased sample, which cut across all industries. There were insufficient data to perform any industry analysis. The choice of variables also invites criticism. All the variables (dependent and independent) were ratios. Indeed, one, book value by market value, was a ratio of ratios! This makes interpretation of correlation very difficult, because numerators and denominators move in different directions for a variety of reasons. The authors did find a significant positive correlation between IT budget spent on training with sales by total assets, market value to book value, and return on investment. However, this ratio was significantly negatively correlated. Such results are difficult to explain. Similarly, they also found that IT budget as a percentage of total revenue was negatively and significantly correlated with return on investment, sales by total assets, and market to book value. Again, such results are difficult to interpret, partly because they are correlations of ratios with ratios.

The present study attempts to meld the Brynjolfsson and Hitt and Mahmood and Mann approaches, focusing on their strengths and eliminating their weaknesses. The single output implied by the production function is replaced by a set of seven dependent variables, all of which are widely supported in the literature. There are also seven independent variables, some related to IT and others not, but all commonly supported. Where possible, ratios are avoided, although their components are utilized, such as income and assets, instead of the frequently used return on assets ratio (ROA). Figure 1 shows the components of the proposed framework. Note that the investments are separated into IT investments and corporate investments (labor and capital). The IT investments, in turn, are categorized as MIS budget variables, which are annual spending variables, computer capital, and PCs per employee.

Firm performance has been divided into sales, assets, and market variables. These reflect, respectively, the firm's output, efficiency, and effectiveness; the firm's capital and financial strength; and the firm's value as viewed by the stock market. As with investments, all these variables have been used in the aforementioned models, which in turn were derived from extensive previous research in the area. The only additional variable that we have introduced for completeness, although it is a ratio variable, is market share. This is a common measure of a firm's effectiveness in the marketplace and has been used by Barua et al. [2] and Courtney [7].

The meanings of all variables are self-evident, except perhaps those of the market variables. Closing price is the average daily stock closing price of a firm throughout the year. Outstanding shares is the average daily number of outstanding common shares throughout the year.

Even though strong correlations are shown between the sets of input and output variables in the framework, it is important to point out that we have carefully avoided claiming causality, because strong correlations by themselves do not imply a causal relationship. Rather, we have focused on finding the character of the relationships among important variables. Of course, we would expect a large firm to have higher outputs and also bigger IT investments, but this is not the point. Our main goal was to determine the magnitude and nature of the relationships between important outputs and inputs, as per the framework. Causality would have to be inferred from other sources of evidence.

To illustrate this point, let us use the following example. It is known that the standard of living in a country is closely related to its consumption of sulfuric acid. Does a high standard of living cause higher consumption of sulfuric acid, or does using more sulfuric acid cause a higher standard of living? What is important here is that this particular relationship is strong and provides a useful indicator of a country's standard of living among thousands of other competing benchmarks. The same logic is true of the strong relationships between certain important variables uncovered in our framework.

Nevertheless, the framework does imply a causal relationship between the input and output variables. We would have to base our inference of causality on all the previous research in the area that has been performed with identical or similar inputs and outputs.

In the next section we provide details on the data collected for the research. It is important to note that, although many factors affect firm performance, given sufficient data and careful statistical analysis, underlying relationships can be uncovered.

## Data

LARGE-SCALE EMPIRICAL STUDIES ARE A RARITY IN THE INFORMATION SYSTEMS field, because of the paucity of data. Indeed, both the Loveman [17] and Barua et al. [2] econometric studies used the Management Productivity and Information Technology (MPIT) data collected by the Strategic Planning Institute over the years 1978–84. This is clearly inappropriate for any current study that purports to determine the impact of IT investments, because of the sea change in the technology since then. As mentioned earlier, the Mahmood and Mann study used 1989 data for only one hundred “premier” firms, from a survey published by Computerworld, based on International Data Corporation (IDC) survey data. The Brynjolfsson and Hitt study used data collected by the IDC for the years 1987–91. The dataset was based on five annual surveys of 380 large firms for a total of 1,164 observations. (Not all firms were represented in all five years.) The authors were able to use about two-thirds of this data.

## Analysis and Results

THE NCSS STATISTICAL SOFTWARE PACKAGE WAS USED TO PERFORM all data analyses. An initial examination of the data (see Table 4 for summary statistics) revealed a log normal distribution and minor problems of linearity and equal variance. Taking the natural log of all variables eliminated linearity and equal variance problems and reduced the influence of a nonnormal distribution. Blocking the data by primary SIC (one-digit SIC) normalized a majority of the data. Large sample sizes further limited the influence of a log normal distribution.

Following normalization, it was necessary to identify and eliminate records with missing data and those that were inconsistent with a majority of the data (i.e., outliers). Outliers were identified with a variety of methods including two- and three-dimensional scatter plots and an NCSS data screening routine. "Count" in Table 4 indicates remaining sample sizes for each variable after removal of outliers.

With data preparation completed, the analysis proceeded in three steps. In step one, a simple correlation matrix was created based on the Spearman-Rank algorithm which is more robust when dealing with a nonnormal distribution. The intent was to answer as many research questions as possible at a less complex level of analysis and to aid in the interpretation of subsequent canonical analyses.

Correlation matrices were produced for the entire data set and for each industry sector noted in Table 5. The count in Table 5 refers to the number of available observations in each sector after removal of outliers. After an analysis of the trends and correlations indicated by the Spearman correlations, step two was performed, that is, a canonical analysis was conducted for the entire data set and each industry sector. The results of both simple correlations and canonical analysis were carefully scrutinized for extraneous variables. In step three, as described below, three such variables—net income, closing price, and PCs per employee—were identified and removed. The refined framework was then used to repeat each of the preceding canonical analyses. The findings for this three-step analysis are detailed below.

## Summary

Viewed as a whole, the question is not if IT measures are correlated with the economic measures—they are clearly and positively correlated. The question is one of identifying the role IT investments play in the different industry sectors. To answer that question we must examine multidimensional correlations.

## Step 2: Canonical Analysis

Whereas simple correlations look at one-on-one correlations, canonical analysis examines related dimensions in the two sets of variables. A dimension is a linear combination of the original variables. Canonical analysis finds a sequence of orthogonal linear combinations from each set that have maximum pairwise correlations. It is consistent with gestalt theory—that is, taken as a whole, one group of variables may be able to explain the variance in another group of variables. The routine is extremely sensitive to outliers. This is why extreme care was taken to eliminate outlying data points that might unduly influence the results.

Much of the difficulty with canonical correlation is due to the jargon [32]. First there are variables, then there are canonical variates, and finally there are pairs of canonical variates. Variables refers to the variables measured, for example, MIS budget. Canonical variates are linear combinations of variables, one combination on the IV side (e.g., MIS budget and computer capital) and a second combination on the DV side (e.g., market share and outstanding shares.) These two combinations form a pair of canonical variates.

Four indices are used to assess the efficiency of the framework: That is, how well is the framework able to account for variability in the data, and, in particular, how well are investment variates able to account for variability in performance variables? Canonical correlation squared and Wilks's lambda react similarly to accounted for changes in variance and to addition of variables to the framework. Both reflect variability in the framework as a whole. Redundancy and individual percentage of variance explained reflect differences in variance accounted for at the level of the variate.

Canonical correlation squared provides an approximation of the $R^{2}$ found in multiple linear regression, measuring the percentage of variance accounted for by the framework as a whole. If adding a variable to the framework produces a significant increase in $R^{2}$ , that variable improves the efficiency of the framework. However, if the variable produces a very small increase (e.g., 2 or 3 percent), then the increase is simply the effect of an additional variable and does not change the proportion of variance explained. In such cases, the variable should be eliminated (or not included) in the framework.

Wilks's lambda is variously described as a multivariate equivalent of the $F$ -test statistic and as a "multivariate generalization of $R^2$ [(11], pp. 339, 590). As the framework increases its ability to explain variability, Wilks's lambda decreases. Like $R^2$ , when the addition of a variable produces insignificant changes to Wilks's lambda, the change is just the effect of that variable and not an indication of additional variance explained.

The individual percentage of variance explained $(pv)$ is the amount of variance in the variables of one variate explained by a second variate. For example, investment variate one for the full data set explains 45.2 percent of the variance represented by the individual variables of performance variate one.

Total redundancy is 40.36 percent. Redundancy “is the percent of variance it (a canonical variate) extracts from its own set of variables times the canonical correlation squared for the pair” [32, p. 203]. That is, the variance in DVs, taken one at a time, explained by the set of IVs times equals redundancy [19]. Thus, total redundancy is a measure of the variance explained by the variate as a whole.

The canonical analysis proceeded in the same manner as the simple correlations. The full data set was analyzed for cross-industry trends and relationships, then a blocking factor (one-digit SIC) was applied to the data set. Not all the results are discussed in this section because refinements to the framework produced improved results, discussed fully in the next section. However, all results from the initial framework are depicted in Table 8.

For the entire data set, $R^{2}$ was 87.23 percent. Variate one accounted for over 97 percent of the 40.4 percent total redundancy. Selection of a value for loadings depends on researcher preference. Tabachnick and Fidell [32] suggest that “loadings in excess of .71 (50% overlapping variance) are considered excellent, .63 (40% overlapping variance) very good, .55 (30% overlapping variance) good, .45 (20% overlapping variance) fair, and .32 (10% overlapping variance) poor” (p. 640).

Per Mahmood and Mann's [19] suggestion, only loadings greater than 0.4 were considered significant for variate one. Three variables failed to meet this criterion—net income, closing price, and PCs per employee. Remaining DVs and IVs were positively correlated. Based on the magnitude of the standardized variable coefficients, MIS staff ranked first among the IT variables, followed by computer capital, MIS staff training, and MIS other. Market share is a suppressor variable—that is, the sign of the standardized coefficient is opposite that of the loading, thereby minimizing (suppressing) collinearity. The strongest correlations between variables in the variate one pair (excluding labor and noncomputer capital) were sales, assets, and equity with MIS staff, MIS staff training, and computer capital. These findings were relatively consistent for each of the seven industry sectors analyzed. In particular, net income, closing price, and PCs per employee seldom produced significant loadings.

For those not familiar with canonical analysis, the following explanation of suppressor variables will help. A suppressor variable in canonical correlation is one for which the sign of the correlation between the variable and a canonical variate has one sign (say, positive), but the coefficient of that variable in the variate is the opposite (say, negative). Superficially, this is illogical. The former says that as the variable increases so does the variate; the latter says the opposite. The apparent paradox results from the “collinearity” among the variables in the variate; much as multicollinearity in regression. Thus, the suppressor variable is mitigating the combined effect on the canonical variate of it and one or more other variables that are themselves correlated (i.e., suppressing).

## Discussion

THE FRAMEWORK FOR ASSESSING THE MULTIDIMENSIONAL RELATIONSHIP of investments with firm performance, especially the refined version, proved to be very efficient at capturing the relationship between a set of IT investment measures and a set of firm performance measures. Because the framework used simple rather than complex variables (i.e., ratios), it was easier to identify and examine relationships, removing the guesswork involved in deciding whether a change originated in the numerator or denominator of a complex variable. Identification and removal of inefficient variables improved the framework's explanatory power by as much as 16 percent. Results from the refined framework permit us to address the research questions posed earlier.

The strongest investment relationships occur with sales, equity, and assets, although this varies somewhat between industry sectors. For example, IT investments in the transportation and utilities sector are strongly correlated with sales, weakly correlated with market share, and not correlated at all with the remaining indices. Such variance highlights fundamental differences in how industries treat IT. These differences are also apparent in the ranking of individual IT variables.

Having an adequate number of qualified MIS staff in the manufacturing and finance, insurance, and real estate sectors has a greater impact on firm performance than any other single IT variable. The MIS staff training budget, an indicator of the required quality of MIS staff, ranks second in nondurable manufacturing. These findings substantiate the work of researchers such as Gupta $[10]$ and Bergeron and Raymond $[4]$ who assert that how IT is used and how well that use is meshed with overall strategic policy is more important than the quantity of IT purchased.

In other sectors, current and future IT assets (i.e., computer capital and MIS other budget) outweigh the effects of sufficient numbers of well-trained MIS staff. This is particularly apparent in the wholesale trade sector in which computer capital is second only to non-IS labor, indicating a strong reliance on IT for successful operations. MIS other and MIS staff budgets were the only significant IT variables in the retail trade sector. This could indicate a rapid expansion of the IT base during the period of observation. Alternatively, it could represent a slower rate of reduction in these two areas than that experienced in other areas studied.

Solomon [30], Schuler [27], and Facteau et al. [9] all indicate the critical importance of IT training to the performance and even the survival of an organization. The truth of this statement is obvious to anyone in business today. Despite this, the value of IT training has never been demonstrated empirically at the firm level. A 1995 study by Lyau and Pucel [18] exemplifies findings at the firm level. They found that corporate training investments had little relationship to labor productivity. Mahmood and Mann's [19] findings, although not strictly comparable, of a positive relationship between the percentage of the MIS budget spent on MIS staff training and firm performance ratios were stronger than Lyau and Pucel's but were still inconclusive.

IT training can be divided into the training of MIS staff as well as training of end users. Our data focused on MIS staff training expenditures, and this study is the first to demonstrate empirically a correlation between IT staff training and a wide range of performance variables. Training is strongly correlated with sales, assets, equity, market share, and shares, and ranks second among the IT variables in all sectors except mining and heavy construction and the trade sectors and in two of those sectors—mining and heavy construction and wholesale trade—linearity problems forced MIS staff training out of the equation. However, the Spearman-Rank correlation matrix indicates staff training is moderately correlated with sales, assets, and market share in these sectors. These results support Strassman's $[31]$ contention that “computers don’t make money, people do.” The rapid evolution of IT and its increasing penetration into organizations will increase the criticality of IT training at all levels for the foreseeable future $[1, 9, 20, 24, 25, 27]$ .

The paucity of correlations with income deserves some discussion. A question that frequently arises is why ROA is often inversely related to IT investments. Mahmood and Mann [19] interpreted this as “some of the highly rated companies used in this study may be spending excessive amounts on IT as a percentage of total revenue without a proper business strategy.”

Our analysis indicated another potential reason for the negative correlations. Net income before taxes—a common ROA numerator—was only marginally correlated with investments and then only in certain industry sectors. However, assets—the ROA denominator—is strongly correlated with IT investments in all areas, implying that for a given increase in investments the ROA numerator increases less than the denominator. This partially explains the negative correlation of IT investments with ROA.

## What is the impact of computer capital versus noncomputer capital?

The non-IT variables—noncomputer capital and labor—consistently displayed the strongest correlations with firm performance. There is evidence that corporate managers are beginning to sense that shifting capital spending from IT to noncomputer capital may be in their best interest.

Businesses have been eager buyers of the latest and greatest in high-tech since the 1980s, spending billions on upgrades with each new generation of Intel chips and Microsoft software. But as the economic expansion ages, some companies are starting to shift technology dollars to other needs. And some big spenders seem to have reached a point of exhaustion. They have plenty of gear capable of getting the job done and are beginning to question why they need much more anytime soon. [36]

The wholesale trade sector appears to provide the exception to this phenomenon. In this sector, noncomputer capital is ranked after all investment variables except MIS staff budget, while computer capital is ranked second behind non-IS labor. This is partly to be expected because the wholesale industry requires far less noncomputer capital than other sectors, such as machinery (manufacturing), airplanes and locomotives (transportation), and stores (retailing). However, the intermediary role of wholesaling emphasizes information processing. Our results firmly indicate that IT is central to wholesale operations and strategies.

## Conclusion

ALTHOUGH THE INFORMATION SYSTEMS FIELD IS NOT GENERALLY characterized by a cumulative tradition of research, this paper has attempted to build on the fairly substantial body of work devoted to the productivity paradox. Two recent models (Brynjolfsson and Hitt [6] and Mahmood and Mann [19]) were merged and extended. These models were, in turn, based upon specific frameworks and extensive reviews of the literature. The Brynjolfsson and Hitt work was based on the Cobb-Douglas production function, whereas the other model was more mainstream, with several dependent performance variables derived from the organizational performance literature.

A major problem in previous research in this area has been the unavailability of sufficient data to perform reasonable statistical tests. The quality of the data has also been suspect in many cases, as has been the age, such as in the MPIT data from the early 1980s. These problems have been handily addressed in this study because the size of the database used in the current study far exceeds previous attempts. The quality of the raw data was checked whenever possible, and found to be surprisingly good. The observations were then carefully culled to eliminate obvious inconsistencies, errors, and outliers, and then appropriately deflated to yield amounts in constant dollars. It was also possible, for the first time, to analyze individual industrial sectors statistically. While there is no denying that a myriad of factors affect organizational performance, it is also true that, given sufficient data, it is possible to uncover underlying relationships through careful statistical analysis.

The hypothesized framework, in its refined form, relating IT and corporate investments to firm performance, proved to have strong statistical validity; in other words, it was empirically validated using data from a broad array of firms. It can thus serve as a theoretical framework for evaluating the relationship between firm performance and IT and corporate investment. This validated framework also permits us to revisit the productivity paradox.

The results of the analyses support Brynjolfsson and Hitt's findings that computer capital and IS labor spending contribute significantly to firm-level revenues. This occurred in spite of using different analytical techniques. However, we could not corroborate their findings of greater impact on the part of IT investments compared with noncomputer capital; in fact, we arrived at the opposite conclusion. It is only natural for investments in rolling mills and factories (manufacturing), airplanes and trucks (transportation), or branch offices (finance) to have a greater impact on revenues than IT investments. Although some results such as these were generally true across industries, there were several differences among industry sectors that could be explained based on their unique characteristics, such as the heavy reliance on noncomputer capital by manufacturing industries.

The value of IS staff and staff training was also quite apparent and exceeded that of computer capital. This confirms the positions of several authors, that the effective use of IT is far more important than merely spending on IT.

On the whole, the findings of this study are generally consistent with the beliefs of the IS community—that is, that IT investment is a very important contributor to a firm's performance, and that spending on IS personnel and training makes good sense. The result that noncomputer capital and labor are also very important should not come as a surprise to anyone. Furthermore, several of the IT effects were found common to most or all industry sectors, although expected variations could be found on account of the characteristics of particular industries.

There will always be room for refinements in approaches to studying the impact of IT investments, such as factoring in the effect of lags between the time when the investments are made and when the resultant benefits are felt, or tying benefits to specific types of IT investments. Measuring all the effects of IT investments on firm performance is a complex research problem that is exacerbated by the increased expenditures that are taking place for outsourcing and end-user computing. These expenditures are not reflected in our figures for IT spending, but they clearly must have an effect on overall corporate performance. Outsourcing expenditures may not be difficult for future researchers to collect, but end-user computing expenses are notoriously difficult to gather.

Whereas firm performance is affected by a multitude of factors, the impact of IT is often felt in areas not directly affecting common measures of firm performance. Ives [14], for example, points out that IT has improved the quality of all our lives in ways few would be willing to sacrifice. Indeed, modern computer transactions offer much richer services than in the past, with greater quality, convenience, reliability, timeliness, safety, flexibility, and variety $[23]$ . Our focus on directly tangible measures of output does not therefore take the full effect of IT into account.
