# Strategic Payoff from EDI as a Function of EDI Embeddedness

AKEMI TAKEOKA CHATFIELD AND PHILIP YETTON

AKEMI TAKEOKA CHATFIELD is a Research Fellow of the Fujitsu Centre for Managing Information Technology in Organisations at the Australian Graduate School of Management in the University of New South Wales. She holds a Ph.D. from Texas Tech University. Her current research interests include strategic IT management, the impact of interorganizational systems (IOS) on performance, IOS-enabled business network redesign, and e-commerce in the Asia and Pacific region. Dr. Chatfield has published articles in Journal of Management Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, and Database. She was involved in 1995 as a technology advisor in the Australian government's international EDI initiative with the Japanese government.

PHILIP YETTON is Commonwealth Bank Professor of Management and Executive Director of the Fujitsu Centre for Managing Information Technology in Organisations at the Australian Graduate School of Management in the University of New South Wales. He has degrees from Cambridge University and Liverpool University, and received his M.B.A. and Ph.D. from Carnegie-Mellon University. His research interests include strategic IT management, dynamics of IT-based change, decision making, and strategic leadership. Dr. Yetton has published over sixty research articles and books in these areas. In addition, he has extensive international management consulting experience.

ABSTRACT: A key issue facing IT researchers and practitioners has been the difficulty in realizing strategic payoff from IT investment. This study, drawn on sociological theories of embeddedness, addresses this key issue, with particular attention to the perspective of EDI network initiator. Cross-case analysis is conducted comparing three initiators of sophisticated EDI networks, who realized different levels of strategic payoffs. Results reveal that the achievement of strategic payoffs is a function of EDI embeddedness, which is defined as how central or peripheral a specific EDI network is to managing interfirm interdependence. In a model of EDI initiator strategic payoff, we argue that EDI embeddedness, which is influenced by existing interfirm relationship, moderates the impact of adopter EDI use on initiator strategic payoff derived from the EDI investment. Specifically, while high embeddedness motivates adopter strategic use, low embeddedness deters such use. The model is validated against three reported cases in the literature.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: electronic data interchange, embeddedness, interfirm cooperation, interfirm interdependence, IT investment, strategic payoff, time-based competition.

INCREASINGLY SINCE THE 1980S, ORGANIZATIONS IN THE PRIVATE AND PUBLIC sectors have developed electronic data interchange (EDI) networks to gain a competitive advantage from cooperative relationships with their suppliers and customers. Supporting this strategy, Cash and Konsynski [10] conclude that new strategic opportunities are created by interorganizational systems (IOS), $^{1}$ asserting that these information technologies are a new strategic weapon that “redraws competitive boundaries.” Against that, Benjamin, de Long, and Scott Morton [5, p. 35] conclude: “Gaining competitive advantage from EDI applications is much more difficult than it might appear.”

Certainly, the difficulties described by Benjamin, de Long, and Scott Morton are common in practice. In her study of Fordnet, a proprietary EDI network initiated by Ford of Europe, Webster [67] observed the absence of cooperative buyer–supplier relationships, which are presumed in the literature to emerge through EDI use. This gap between theory and practice was also observed by Clemons and Row [21] in their case study of the impact of manufacturer-initiated EDI and other information technologies on manufacturer–retailer relationships in the consumer packaged goods industry. They report limits to the potential strategic payoff from new EDI-enabled logistics management practices because of “considerable resistance” by expected adopters. Finally, Bensaou [6] finds mixed results of the impact of EDI use on interfirm cooperation in the automotive industry. He reports that EDI use was positively related to the improved interfirm cooperation among the Japanese buyer–supplier relationships, whereas he found little impact of EDI use on the U.S. counterparts.

In contrast, there are well-known classic examples of success, such as McKesson $[19]$ and American Airlines $[22]$ , as well as new examples of strategic use, including Wal-Mart with its key supplier, Proctor and Gamble $[26]$ , Japan Airlines with its network of vertical keiretsu suppliers $[14, 15]$ , 7-Eleven Japan with its network of retailers $[7]$ , and Brun Passot with some of its large buyers $[36]$ . Overall, the literature reveals large variation with regard to the impact of EDI on interfirm relationships and the resultant EDI strategic payoffs. A key issue facing IT researchers and practitioners is the common difficulty realizing strategic payoff from the EDI investment. However, EDI research has not adequately addressed this key issue.

This study, drawn on sociological theories of embeddedness, addresses the key issue by exploring the large variation in strategic payoff derived from strategic EDI initiatives, with particular attention to the perspective of EDI network initiators. In a cross-case analysis, we compare three case studies of initiators of sophisticated EDI networks $[12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]$ . The results of our analysis are organized into a model of EDI initiator strategic payoff. The model development draws on three complementary theoretical perspectives concerning sustainable competitive advantage: Brian Uzzi's $[64, 65]$ sociological theory of embeddedness; organizational fit $[48]$ and the MIT 90s model $[59]$ ; and the work begun by Stalk and Hout $[62]$ on time-based competition. The resultant model explains differences in time-based competition, specifically, cycle time capability, as a function of EDI embeddedness, where embeddedness is defined as a measure of how central or peripheral a specific EDI network is to managing the interfirm interdependence that results from joint economic actions taken by the initiator and the adopter.

## Theoretical Background and Review

MANY RESEARCHERS HAVE SUGGESTED THAT EDI CAN BE A STRATEGIC WEAPON (see, e.g., [35, 36, 37, 50]). In general, improved buyer–supplier relationships through EDI are presumed to reduce design cycle times, facilitate JIT manufacturing, support quick response to changes in consumer demand, and enable initiators to compete in time. However, despite the promise of EDI network technology, the potential strategic benefits of improved interfirm relationships through EDI are often not realized in practice [5, 6, 21, 67].

The EDI literature identifies two types of benefits: strategic benefits, such as time-based competitive advantage discussed in this paper, and tactical benefits, such as operational efficiency gains that may result from automating existing interfirm information flows and decision processes $[1]$ . These accrue to two distinct types of EDI users: initiators and adopters $[52]$ . The term “initiators” refers to EDI-initiating organizations, which are also referred to as sponsors in the literature. Initiators can be suppliers (e.g., Brun Passot) or buyers (e.g., Ford). Initiators invest significant resources to develop EDI applications and to promote the EDI networks to their trading partners. The term “adopters” refers to those organizations that adopt EDI and join the initiator’s EDI network and hence are sometimes referred to as network members in the literature.

From the perspective of the initiator, the problem of realizing strategic benefits in part stems from interdependence in the payoffs derived from EDI $[9, 54]$ and, as a result, from the importance of adopter strategic use. While initiators can use coercive power to demand adoption $[67]$ , in general, initiators cannot control how different adopters use EDI in their internal processes. Our case analysis shows that, for the initiator to realize strategic benefits, adopters need to use EDI strategically in ways by which they also create value through interfirm joint action (see $[46]$ ).

This strategic use of EDI by adopters typically requires adopter cooperation in undertaking joint economic action with the initiator, such as new product design and just-in-time (JIT) delivery of components. To do this, the adopter frequently has to make significant changes to its internal processes. Strategic payoffs are unlikely when initiators and adopters use EDI simply to automate existing interfirm information flows and decision processes $[5, 21, 37]$ . In practice, many initiators simply do this and avoid internal restructuring, leaving inefficient processes intact or ignoring overall interdependence across processes $[23, 30, 68]$ . It follows that a firm's unwillingness to reengineer its internal systems is a barrier to capturing the strategic benefits of EDI.

The integration of EDI with existing internal information systems (IS) is likely to present problems for small firms that do not have adequate financial and IS knowledge resources $[34]$ . But evidence from case studies of six Fortune 100 firms suggests that organizational and sociological factors, rather than financial and IS knowledge resources, are the critical sources of resistance to change [42]. Many large firms, despite their financial and technical resources, experience difficulty integrating computer-aided design (CAD) with computer-aided manufacturing (CAM). A lack of internal systems integration between these technologies and the resulting difficulty of transferring design data across functions limits the firms' use of information technologies, and they are unable to realize the potential strategic payoffs.

So, to achieve the potential strategic payoffs from EDI investments, we argue that organizational changes need to be made by the initiator and the adopters, integrating EDI with both internal information systems and internal business processes to take advantage of the interfirm electronic links. To model these necessary conditions for capturing EDI strategic benefits, we introduce the concept of fit as described in the MIT 90s model [59] and its development by Yetton and his associates [38, 69, 70]. Combined with Uzzi's [64] concept of embeddedness, this shows how increases in the degree of external fit between the initiator and the adopter influence the nature of the fit within each of them.

## Cooperative Relationships

The cross-case analysis reported below shows that EDI payoffs, particularly strategic benefits, cannot be captured unilaterally by the initiator and must be considered within the context of interfirm relationships. These vary from market-based, arm's-length relationships to cooperative relationships, which are often referred to as “strategic alliances” or “partnerships” [24, 39, 51, 55]. Cooperative relationships often evolve over time, for example, through repeated economic action [64] or through repeated face-to-face joint problem-solving experiences [66].

In order to understand firm-level variation in gaining time-based competitive advantage, we need to examine this relational context in which EDI is implemented and used. While the importance of trust and power in adoption and use is recognized $[31, 67]$ , the existing EDI literature is silent on the role of the relational context in realizing the strategic benefits of EDI.

To explore this, we draw on Uzzi's [64, 65] sociological theory of embeddedness. Uzzi has advanced the concept of embeddedness developed initially by sociologists such as Granovetter [27] to combine organization theory with social network theory [57]. He argues that the structure and quality of social ties between firms shape their economic action. Here economic action includes joint action undertaken by two or more firms collaboratively, such as joint ventures and strategic alliances, as well as economic behavior unilaterally decided by the focal firm or by a trading partner.

The theory suggests that the level of embeddedness can vary considerably. In market-based, arm's-length relations, embeddedness is low. Here social ties between firms are not formed because the logic of the traditional arm's-length relations is based on a "buyer beware" contract. In contrast, economic action is highly embedded in close-knit networks of firms that maintain ongoing and exclusive social relationships with one another. Here the logic of embedded relationships is based on mutual trust born out of shared experiences. This enables the partners to respond flexibly in areas not covered specifically by the contract. For example, Uzzi quotes a manufacturer: “With people you trust, you know that if they have a problem with a fabric they’re just not going to say ‘I won’t pay’ or ‘Take it back.’ If they did, then we would have to pay for the loss. This way maybe the manufacturer will say, ‘Hey, OK, so I’ll make a dress out of it. Or I can cut it and make a short jacket instead of a long jacket’” ([64], p. 678).

Uzzi identifies three characteristics of embedded interfirm relationships: exchange of sensitive information, joint problem-solving arrangements, and trust. First, the two-way exchange of information has an impact on their joint performance. In embedded relationships, information exchanged between firms is more proprietary and more tacit than the information exchanged in arm's-length relationships. Proprietary or tacit information is “sticky.” It is difficult to transfer across the traditional firm boundaries in arm's-length relationships [66] and instead requires face-to-face interaction for its exchange.

Second, embedded relationships support joint problem-solving arrangements. These provide fast and explicit feedback and enable firms to work out problems as they occur and to innovate. In this case, the joint problem-solving arrangements of embedded relationships can accelerate learning and innovation across firms. In addition, in embedded relationships, close social ties make proprietary and tacit information credible and interpretable, which enhances joint problem solving, synergies, and value creation.

In his field study of apparel firms in the United States, Uzzi [64] found that trust, not the contract, acted as the governance mechanism of highly embedded relationships. Trust facilitates voluntary, nonobligating exchanges of organizational resources, services, and information between firms. This reinforces both the exchange of sensitive information and productive joint problem solving that supports high performance.

## MIT 90s Model

Embeddedness is concerned with the relationship between two or more organizations. To examine how these interfirm links affect the organization, we introduce and extend the MIT 90s model [59] to model the relationship between the initiator and the adopter. The MIT 90s model of fit is reproduced in figure 1.

The model is underpinned by the theory of fit [48], which states that fit contributes to high performance and that optimal performance occurs only if there is a tight fit among the five domains of strategy, structure, management processes, individual roles and skills, and technology.

Although the idea of fit and a framework such as this are essentially static and do not themselves imply any sequencing, most applications of such models assume that determining the strategy is the first step in establishing fit. Indeed, the idea that fit leads to high performance has been most comprehensively developed around the link between strategy and structure. While some, such as Mintzberg [49], have argued for emergent strategies, the dominant model in both the strategic-management and organization-theory literatures has assumed that changes in business strategy precede structural adaptation [11, 58], with structural adaptation driving a realignment of management processes [48].

Figure 1. MIT 90s Model of Fit

Researchers into the management of IT have implicitly, and often explicitly, adopted this dominant view about the dynamics of strategic change. They add IT to the conventional model at two separate points. First, they advocate an active role for IT in strategy formation by emphasizing the potential benefits it offers in redesigning the business or competition $[33, 41]$ . Second, the literature recommends that IT be incorporated into the strategy–structure fit by aligning it to the dominant organization design. Here, we relax the implicit assumption of one or two paths to change and adopt the findings of Yetton and his associates $[38, 69, 70]$ that there are many feasible paths to achieve a new fit. The key assumptions are that high-performing organizations have a high fit among strategy, structure, roles and skills, management processes, and technology, and that when one or more of these components changes, a new fit must be achieved across all components to sustain high performance.

## A Concept of EDI Embeddedness

By integrating Uzzi's [64] sociological theory of embeddedness and the MIT 90s analytical framework described above, we define a concept of EDI embeddedness between an initiator and an adopter. Uzzi asserts that a cooperative relationship, as compared with an adversarial relationship, shapes economic action differently and hence has differential strategic implications for performance. Likewise, in this concept of EDI embeddedness, we suggest that a cooperative relationship, as compared with an adversarial relationship, shapes the initiator's EDI investment action (the process of EDI design and network promotion) differently, which influences the likelihood of realizing strategic benefits.

This is shown in figure 2, which illustrates three salient links across organizations: IT/EDI links, strategic links, and people (individual roles and skills) links. Information sharing across organizations is facilitated by IT/EDI links. Joint economic action across firms such as shared product development or JIT supply is undertaken through strategic links. Face-to-face joint problem solving is carried out through people (individual roles and skills) links. Ongoing interactions may lead to trust and close social ties between people across organizations. Of course, strategic alliances and other such interfirm links exist without EDI, and not all such links and associated benefits depend on EDI. It is not claimed that EDI is critical to all strategic interfirm links. Rather, the question here is under what conditions can initiators generate strategic benefits.

High embeddedness is characterized by the patterns of interfirm relationships shown in figure 2. In contrast, low embeddedness is modeled as a reduced form of figure 2 by excluding the joint economic action and social ties. It is likely found in an arm's-length relationship, where the initiator–adopter relationship establishes EDI links without having either strategic links or ongoing, close people links. Such EDI links are typically limited to automated exchange of routine transaction data (e.g., purchase orders). Here, neither the initiator nor the adopter is voluntarily motivated to share proprietary information through EDI. Such information is not considered as important to the interfirm relationship. As a result, the adopter rationally resists further investment for making necessary internal changes, particularly systems integration of EDI applications with internal strategic information systems (e.g., a manufacturing planning system). The strategic implications of such links are trivial.

In contrast, a high-level EDI embeddedness is found in a cooperative relationship with established strategic links and people links. Here joint economic action and internal systems integration are high. As a cooperative relationship develops, value-adding joint economic action, such as JIT production systems between the automaker and the supplier, such joint action requires firms to share proprietary information, such as production schedules, quality-control data, and inventory levels to support joint problem solving. Typically, such information sharing includes internal systems integration between EDI with corporate database and other strategic information systems to support joint economic action. This is shown in figure 2 as the link labeled "Interfirm Relations (Joint economic action)" between the organizations' strategic domains. At a minimum level, the initiator would have limited access to the adopter's corporate database, requiring systems integration by the adopter of EDI with its internal IS applications. At a higher level, both the initiator and a selective set of adopters would have access to each other's corporate databases. What matters here is that information flows are bidirectional and information sharing is reciprocal. This pattern of information sharing across businesses underlies the early conceptual frameworks of value creation through IOS [2, 44].

Repeated high levels of successful joint economic action typically lead to social interaction as trust develops among the key participants in the joint problem solving $[55, 64, 66]$ . This is shown in figure 2 as the link labeled “Interfirm Relations (Social ties).” The level of EDI embeddedness is high. EDI has a direct impact on three of the five domains in the MIT 90s model. There are reciprocal influences by the initiator and adopter on each other’s technology (direct EDI impact), strategy (joint economic action), and roles and skills (social ties). In addition, there is an indirect influence on the other two domains as management processes and structure are aligned to reestablish a high fit across the five domains within each organization.

## Time-Based Competitive Advantage

The model of initiator EDI investment strategic payoff, developed and discussed later in this paper, is generic in the sense that it is not limited to one form of competitive advantage as the outcome of strategic EDI use by the initiator–adopter dyads. We limit our analysis here to one specific benefit, namely, time-based competitive advantage. While we do not claim that the benefits of EDI-enabled interfirm cooperation and network collaboration are limited to this specific domain, we restrict the analysis to this domain to keep it tractable.

Over the last 10 years, time-based competition (TBC) has emerged as a dominant strategy in a number of industries such as automobile manufacturing and retailing [61]. TBC is a value-added strategy based on the extension of the principles of just-in-time and total quality management (TQM) to the entire manufacturing system [8, 62]. Although TBC is more frequently found in manufacturing, particularly among Japanese manufacturing companies, it is increasingly being adopted by U.S. manufacturing and service industries.

There are two components to effective TBC. First, TBC is a systemic concept. This means that focusing on fast cycle time or response time as a strategic weapon must cover all functions in the initiating organization (e.g., manufacturing, purchase, sales, accounting, and service). Second, fast cycle time must be achieved not only by the initiating organization but also by the entire network (e.g., the EDI initiator and its value chain) for all to derive the major benefits of TBC. It is difficult to obtain such benefits without taking time out of all parts of the value chain, because the large complex organization is intricately involved in a network of task-interdependent relationships with customers, suppliers, competitors, and regulatory groups. For this reason, the major benefits of TBC are not typically gained by the initiating organization at the cost of partner organizations. All have to cooperate and all benefit.

Within the TBC framework, EDI has received growing attention as an enabler for creating a new organizational capability: fast cycle time $[36, 54]$ . Here, we consider cycle time at two different levels of analysis. One is the response time of the network to a demand from a customer, for example, booking a holiday package. The other is the elapsed time to develop a new family of products or services, for example, developing the next-generation Honda Accord.

## Method

## Initiator Selection

OUR FOCUS ON THE VARIATION IN INITIATOR STRATEGIC PAYOFF required strategic EDI initiatives that were considered a “success” by initiators. This “selection bias” is a direct function of the research question addressed, the cause of differential strategic benefits of successfully implemented EDI initiatives, rather than the source of a potential threat to external validity. Strategic EDI initiatives that failed, due to extraneous factors such as a lack of top management support or inadequate IT/EDI technical knowledge and project management knowhow on the part of initiators, are not relevant to our research. Rather, what this study needed were successful EDI initiatives that varied in terms of their realized strategic benefits. We selected three initiators, Australian Quarantine Inspection Services (AQIS), Japan Airlines (JAL), and Honda, whose EDI initiatives satisfied these conditions. Their EDI initiatives were successful, with high rates of adoption, and their strategic benefits varied from low to high. Table 1 gives a brief profile of the three initiators.

The case studies that provide the qualitative data for the cross-case analysis in this paper are based on in-depth case research on the three EDI initiators: AQIS [12, 13], JAL [14, 15], and Honda [16, 17]. The cases represent a variety of industry types—government, service, and manufacturing—and two countries—Australia and Japan.

## Theory-Building Case Study

Given the contradiction between theory and practice that motivated this research, we adopted the inductive theory-building case study method discussed by Eisenhardt [25] and successfully used in IS research, for example, by Clemons and Row [21] and Henderson and Lentz [32]. Theory-building case research does not develop a priori hypotheses. Instead, it enables us to extract rich data and encourages novel constructs and propositions to emerge inductively through the process of field investigation.

The three case studies were conducted between 1995 and 1996 at various initiator and adopter sites in Australia and Japan. Open-ended interviews were used rather than structured interviews with a priori questions, with the focus on identifying whatever factors were considered important by interviewees. Interviews with senior line managers and EDI/IT managers at the initiators' sites often lasted over two hours, and, typically, multiple visits were required. In general, the managers interviewed had expressed an interest in the research topic.

Initiators were asked to provide a list of adopters for interviewing. In the case of AQIS, which outsourced their EDI systems development and integration to a multinational computer software engineering firm, several interviews were conducted with the project manager in charge of the AQIS contract. With the agreement of confidentiality, internal technical documents were also inspected.

All interviews were recorded, and at least one interviewer took notes during interview sessions. In addition to the initiators' head offices, visits were made to some of their adopters' relevant work sites, including meat-packaging plants, JAL service centers, and auto assembly and component-manufacturing plants. Where possible, case interview data were cross-validated against external sources and documents. These were supplemented with external databases, reports, trade journals, and business newspapers.

## Cross-Case Analysis and Validation

Field case study results were compared and interpreted against existing conceptual and empirical EDI research results. Using the construct of EDI embeddedness developed above, we explored whether EDI embeddedness moderated the relationship between EDI use and the strategic payoff derived from EDI.

This inductive model of EDI initiator strategic payoff was then subjected to cross-validation using three additional case studies: Ford of Europe [67], Brun Passot [36], and 7-Eleven Japan [7]. The new cases satisfied the same conditions as the three initial cases—namely, they were successfully implemented and exhibited variation in terms of the strategic benefits realized. This began to establish the model's external validity, showing that the model could be applied to new case data and explain the cause of differential strategic payoffs reported in research.

# Three Case Studies

## Australian Quarantine Inspection Service (AQIS)

AQIS IS MANDATED BY LAW TO PROVIDE FOOD EXPORT INDUSTRIES with quarantine inspection and health certification on food exports and to monitor industry compliance for export documentation. To provide a nationwide service, AQIS has regional offices throughout Australia, with its head office in Canberra. In 1994 AQIS had approximately 2,000 employees, including 1,400 meat inspectors at 128 permanent sites.

In providing quarantine inspection, AQIS must meet the wide range of different health and safety standards set by the governments of over 290 countries. Health certificates are taken as proxies for quality assurance. In addition, many banks in Asia require a health certificate before they will issue letters of credit.

In the 1980s, AQIS operated its service at 50 percent cost recovery from industry. The meat export industry consists of meat exporters (buyers) and meat packers (suppliers). Since the late 1980s, both the export of Australian beef and the rigor in quarantine and food safety requirements for exporting and importing have increased. As a result, both the size and operational complexity of AQIS have increased markedly, as have its operating costs and lead time for issuing health certificates.

In the early 1990s, AQIS moved to 100 percent cost recovery from industry. Industry reacted by pressuring AQIS to reduce operational costs. The industry trade journals published during that period suggested adversarial relationships. To help AQIS reduce its service costs, the meat-export industry funded AQIS's EDI initiative, which the industry association viewed primarily as a cost-cutting measure.

With the increased volume of meat export and the increased rigor of food safety inspection, interfirm coordination for export documentation became increasingly complex. The meat exporter had to coordinate with a number of government and industry people to complete the required paperwork. Upon receipt of a purchase order from the overseas meat importer, the exporter contacted Australian Customs Service (ACS) for a unique Export Clearance Number (ECN). The exporter contacted the meat packer via telephone or fax, sharing some information, such as the exporter's reference number, ECN, and the importer's country, but keeping the importer's identity hidden from the packer for competitive reasons. The packer then raised Notice of Intention (NOI), signaling to the AQIS inspector on site that the exporter intended to export meat to a given country. AQIS provided the packer with NOI paper forms with preprinted NOI numbers. The packer completed the NOI with information, such as a detailed product description (i.e., type of cut, fat content, weight) and container numbers. An AQIS inspector evaluated the completeness of information contained in the NOI, and, if the form was complete, proceeded to meat inspection. The inspector then checked whether the meat products met the food safety and quarantine standards, which varied according to the importer's country. If the meat products met the standards, the AQIS inspector signed the NOI, adding additional information, such as container seal numbers and export permit. When the exporter received the signed NOI, he or she completed the original health certificate paper form, using information contained in the signed NOI, and added the importer's name, for the first time. Both NOI and health certificate were submitted to an AQIS veterinarian, who signed the health certificate. The original health certificate was sent to the relevant government and used for import clearance. Copies were distributed to AQIS, the exporter, and ACS.

With the marked increase in meat exports, the number of paper forms such as NOIs and health certificates skyrocketed. Both exporters and packers had to type the same information many times on different paper forms. Human errors were common. For meat exporters whose business expanded rapidly, nearly 50 percent of the documents submitted to AQIS contained errors. For meat exporters, the problems of rework often involved interfirm coordination, because the errors were committed by someone from the network of external organizations. Corrections became costly to the industry. The rework also contributed to a longer lead time to market for meat exporters and higher operational costs for both exporters and AQIS. A small sample of large exporters interviewed for the case study indicated the growing importance of reducing this increased lead time to market.

## EDI Applications

AQIS could have used its statutory power and decided unilaterally how to structure export document files (e.g., what information to include and standard codes for products such as “prime cut”). Instead, AQIS consulted with key meat exporters. This contributed to a high rate of voluntary adoption (70 percent+). Nonadopters were small organizations with very low or no IT capability. Later, AQIS mandated adoption by all industry members

AQIS's mission and legitimate power were limited to the regulatory inspection domain. The EDI initiative was pushed and funded by the industry as a cost-cutting measure. The intent was to simplify and minimize the cost of compliance. So the first EDI application system, EXDOC (Export Documentation), was designed to support AQIS's statutory relationship with industry firms. EXDOC did not support any electronic commerce links between the meat exporter and the meat packer or between the meat exporter and the overseas importer. This (legitimate) design choice reinforced the industry's initial perception that EDI would not have an impact on business. Few, if any, adopters made additional investments to integrate EDI with their internal systems.

EXDOC provided efficient electronic links between AQIS and meat exporters and between AQIS and meat packers. To introduce these links, AQIS did not simply automate existing government-to-industry export documentation processes. Instead, it first reengineered the interorganizational work flow, removing the time delay due to the physical transfer of export documents from one organization site to another, by creating digital documents that can be accessed by all who are linked to AQIS through EXDOC.

Even though interfirm interdependence, created by the statutory requirements, is low, EDI is central to the statutory requirements of AQIS to manage its interfirm relationships with a multitude of industry firms. The impact of EDI on AQIS was significant. It reduced its lead time for inspecting and issuing paper-form health certificates through EDI use. By reducing the lead time for its statutory services, AQIS reduced the cost of issuing paper-form health certificates by 30 percent. It became more “client”-focused and provided a cost-efficient service by providing the EDI infrastructure that reduced both the industry error rate and coordination complexity.

The second EDI initiative, SANCRT (Sanitation Certificate) linked AQIS with the governments of a few of Australia's major markets such as Japan and New Zealand. SANCRT sent governments real-time digital-form health certificates (EDIFACT messages). While EXDOC provided the meat exporter with paper-form health certificates that were then sent by Australia Post or document delivery firms, SANCRT interfaced with EXDOC and sent digital-form health certificates directly from AQIS, bypassing the meat exporter. This change provided further time compression and faster import clearance for Australian meat exporters. The success in lead-time reduction through the systems integration between EXDOC and SANCRT led AQIS to expand the adoption of EXDOC to all food exporters such as grain, dairy, fish, and horticulture.

These two EDI initiatives gave Australian food exporters a short-run time-based advantage over other overseas competitors operating without such electronic links. However, other important interfaces across businesses, such as exporter–supplier interfaces or exporter–importer interfaces, were not addressed by the two EDI projects. This is not surprising because the push for EDI was motivated by industry's demand for AQIS's cost-efficient services and, more important, because the EDI initiator was the government regulatory agency whose legitimate power did not extend to client-business operations or foreign importers.

## Japan Airlines (JAL)

With the deregulation of the U.S. airline industry in 1978, airlines worldwide faced increasingly intense time-based competition [47]. After the deregulation of the Japanese airline industry in 1985, Japan Airlines (JAL) was no longer protected by Japanese law from its major domestic competitors, such as All Nippon Airways, and it also lost its monopoly in international air services as the Japanese flag carrier. JAL's response to the deregulated competitive environment was to leverage its IT capabilities. JAL developed EDI-enabled, time-based capabilities to reduce lead times in procurement and product development across the JAL Group firms.

Until 1987, JAL was a semiprivate corporation whose largest stockholder was the Japanese government. The government protection and control eroded much of the company's managerial independence and created excessive complexity, with a highly bureaucratic, inefficient organizational structure. JAL's customer service and cost structure were noncompetitive.

Management's response was to simplify JAL's complex organizational structure and operational procedures. Organizational layers were reduced. Operational procedures were simplified by outsourcing a large number of service functions and by reducing the extensive paper-based transactions. Administrative, reservations, and sales-office personnel numbers were reduced, as were operating costs.

With a new organizational structure, top management decided to broaden its corporate earning base by promoting greater diversity and synergy with its JAL Group firms. Moving away from the traditional hierarchical relationships, JAL moved toward strategic partnerships, increasing the level of interfirm interdependence. It was reflected in the increased number of joint-project teams forged between JAL and the JAL Group firms. These explored different capabilities of the JAL Group firms for synergies and developed many new products and services such as mass customized travel packages and quick baggage-delivery services.

The JAL Group is a keiretsu-based $^{2}$ value chain, including over 130 companies in which JAL directly or indirectly owns an average equity interest of 20 percent. It comprises two subgroups: firms closely linked to the air transport industry and firms outside the industry. The first group includes managers and marketers of package tours, ground handling, in-flight meal service providers, aircraft maintenance, and fueling service providers. The second group includes hotels, information technology and communications services, trading companies, and cultural enterprises.

JAL's own customer response time critically depends on complementary capabilities of the JAL Group firms. In 1991, JAL introduced faster customer and baggage check-in, on-time departure, and quick baggage delivery to increase customer satisfaction. While JAL successfully reengineered its internal processes to satisfy this customer service goal, successful implementation required improved time-based capabilities on the part of its group firms, providing airport ground services such as baggage handling and transfer inside and outside terminal buildings. To support quick baggage transfer from the aircraft to the baggage carousel, JAL, through EDI, provided the group firms with information on ticket class, customer destination, and customer seat assignment. This information was used to load baggage so as to minimize the time for retrieval and delivery. Similarly, EDI enabled JAL's on-time departure program, which depended on time-based capabilities of group firms that provided cabin food catering and fuel services, as well as the improved baggage handling. Similarly, JAL's sales and distribution of end products, such as airline seats and tour packages, also depend on its customers' value chains. Together, JAL's suppliers and customers' value chains have significant effects on JAL's capacity to build time-based competitive advantages. For example, the lead time for baggage delivery at major destination airports was reduced by 60 percent, which made JAL baggage service the fastest among Japanese airlines. Similar lead-time reduction was achieved by interfirm teams working together to integrate air and ground transport services for the cargo of corporate customers.

These specific initiatives were supported by JAL's development of AXESS, a computer reservation system (CRS) and systems integration between AXESS and proprietary EDI applications. Elsewhere, particularly in the United States, large airlines have invested in CRS to reduce customer response time. These systems have made the airline industry information-intensive [22]. The value of CRS to U.S. airlines was explicitly recognized by JAL, which launched a major initiative to develop its own CRS in conjunction with the new interorganizational systems. JAL's AXESS CRS and EDI were developed with a clear strategic intent to support JAL's emerging strategies on customer service, sales, value-chain logistics coordination, and cost reduction.

## EDI Applications

The initial development of its electronic network was JAL's strategic response to cut costs and provide better and faster customer services. Its use of EDI was guided by the top management vision: to “build a leaner, more responsive, and customer-oriented airline,” while sustaining and strengthening its position as a market leader and improving its profitability. Specifically, EDI was used to coordinate the value chain for procurement, outsourcing aircraft maintenance services and JIT delivery of aircraft fuel, aircraft repair parts, cabin food, and beverages. EDI with CRS links enabled JAL to coordinate the value chain to integrate tour packages and cargo transport. EDI was used to reduce operating costs through fewer paper-based transactions and a reduction of administrative staff and to accelerate the development of new products and services.

## Honda

Honda Motor Company is a world-class time-based competitor [8] and a highly innovative manufacturer [43], and has been judged the best-managed global company [4]. Honda, the last entrant to the Japanese passenger-car market, became the first Japanese automaker to build passenger-car manufacturing plants in the United States. Honda has grown from sales of \$230,000 in 1949 to almost \$43.4 billion in 1998. In 1997–98, Honda's sales were up 13.3 percent, and it was one of only two Japanese auto companies (the other is Toyota) to make a profit [18]. Honda's high performance is due, in part, to its superior lead time in launching a new vehicle. While Honda's organizational size is much smaller than that of Toyota or Nissan, it has been rated by many as "the gold standard," using a basic measure of the time it takes a production facility to return to production capacity. For example, Honda was rated first and Toyota second among North American manufacturers analyzed (General Motors, Ford, Chrysler, Toyota, and Honda) in launching a new vehicle [60].

A high level of task interdependence was observed between Honda and its first-tier outside suppliers with regard to components design, manufacturing, and JIT inventory management. Honda spends 70 to 75 percent $^{3}$ of total production cost on subcontracting parts, components, and subassemblies. This means that Honda needs to manage its first-tier suppliers (160–200) and, indirectly through the first-tier suppliers, to manage a large number of second-tier suppliers who produce approximately 11,000 parts per assembled car.

Effective management of subcontracting is strategically critical because of its fundamental influence on the company's product-development efficiency and effectiveness. Lead time for product development refers to the time from initial concept to production for the auto maker to engineer and produce a new car. At Honda, lead times vary from 24 to 36 months, depending on whether the new products are derivative models with high carryover or luxury cars with many new components. Subcontracting influences Honda's lead time for product development. First, first-tier suppliers are regularly invited to collaborate with Honda in major and minor model changes. They design new parts and components or modify previously designed parts and components. Second, they manufacture and deliver parts to Honda assembly plants in a JIT delivery schedule agreed upon with Honda. The time-based capabilities of these suppliers are critical for Honda's time-based competitive advantage in new-product development.

In the past, outside suppliers played an important role in the success of Honda and other Japanese automakers in establishing and sustaining cost leadership in global markets. With the intensifying competition in the domestic and global markets, Honda is now pursuing a value-added, time-based strategy. Viewing itself and its external suppliers as a whole system, Honda manages subcontracting to reduce product development cycle, lead time in production, and time to market. Integrated EDI plays an important part at Honda in supporting the subcontracting activities in Honda's pursuit of its time-based strategy.

The Honda case study suggests a very high level of interdependence across businesses. Honda engages in very high-level joint economic action with a number of suppliers. These suppliers perform various value-adding functions, such as detailed design of key components, manufacturing, total quality control, and JIT delivery of finished components to Honda's assembly plants. Honda's performance critically depends on these actions by its suppliers.

## EDI Applications

A rapid diffusion of Honda's proprietary integrated EDI network system to its suppliers has provided many suppliers with increased connectivity. In particular, systems integration, jointly undertaken by Honda and its key first-tier suppliers, has provided them with flexible information exchange with Honda. Real-time proprietary information is frequently exchanged across firms between Honda and its suppliers through the proprietary Honda EDI.

While the integrated EDI network systems did not mitigate the importance of face-to-face meetings, Honda's EDI network system has enabled the transfer of information of increasingly strategic value such as detailed three-dimensional computer-aided design (CAD) drawings and engineering tests during the early phase of product development. During new-product launching and normal production phases, EDI enables the real-time exchange of daily production schedules, changes to these production schedules, and hourly demand for JIT delivery of parts. For the external suppliers who cooperate with Honda, EDI is critical to update their knowledge of Honda's time-based JIT manufacturing and assembly operations and to synchronize their own JIT production operations.

## Cross-Case Analysis Results

ACROSS THE THREE CASES, WE OBSERVE DIFFERENT LEVELS OF TIME-BASED competitive advantage realized by the initiators, despite the high diffusion and voluntary use of sophisticated EDI applications by the adopters. Honda became a world-class, time-based competitor. This was enabled by a high level of joint economic action with suppliers and a high level of internal systems integration by suppliers. However, the Honda case shows that Honda had built quality interfirm cooperation before its supplier firms made the significant resource investments required by their joint economic actions and internal systems integration. Although JAL reduced new product development lead time by the improved interfirm cooperation through EDI use, the company did not achieve the Honda levels of systemic responsiveness within the company and across the entire value chain (outside the vertical keiretsu firms).

AQIS reduced lead time and cost in issuing health certificates to meat exporters through EDI. While the cycle-time compression reduced cost for the Australian meat-export industry in general, it is similar in form to a reduction in any other regulatory imposed cost. In addition, the organizational mission of AQIS excluded (legitimately) from the design the electronic commerce links between the meat exporter and the meat packer and those between the meat exporter and the overseas meat importer. This marginalized the EDI impact on industry business practice and, therefore, the adopters did not invest in systems integration between EDI and internal information systems. As such, EDI did not stimulate the development of new time-based competitive advantages in the adopters. Rather, it delivered an industrywide, short-run advantage over other countries' meat exporters.

## A Model of EDI Initiator Strategic Payoff

The results of cross-case analysis are summarized as a model of EDI initiator strategic payoff in figure 7. Here we attempt formally to state the arguments developed above. While EDI is modeled in the literature as “coordination technology” [20, 21] and “cooperative technology” [63], our cross-case analysis shows that EDI use by a critical mass of adopters does not necessarily have a direct main effect on strategic payoffs. EDI links alone do not improve cooperation or coordination and certainly do not deliver strategic benefits.

Instead, the model argues, as shown in figure 7, that the impact of adopter EDI use on strategic payoff, such as time-based competitive advantage, is moderated by EDI embeddedness. EDI embeddedness is defined here as how central or peripheral a specific EDI network is to managing existing interfirm interdependence.

Drawing on the sociological theory of embeddedness $[64, 65]$ , we assume that varying forms of interfirm relationships exist between the initiator and the adopter long before the initiator launches its strategic EDI initiative. These range from close and cooperative “embedded ties” to the traditional adversarial, arm’s-length relationships. Embedded relationships are characterized by the prevailing importance of trust, rather than a contract, as the governance mechanism for interfirm economic relationships, the voluntary and two-way exchange of sensitive and often proprietary information across businesses, and well-established joint problem-solving arrangements. These embedded ties evolve from the dynamic ongoing social and economic relationships between business transactors. Close, cooperative relationships characterized by trust, information sharing, and joint problem solving encourage the development of high-level interfirm interdependence and enable the formation of high-level EDI embeddedness. In contrast, distant, adversarial, and traditional arm's-length relationships discourage high-level interfirm interdependence and foster low-level EDI embeddedness. Formally,

The above discussion and the model in figure 7 raise the question of causality. Typically, this issue is interpreted within a simple cause-effect framework. Instead, here, the model is a dynamic process with a feedback loop. While it is easier to embed EDI in a Honda context with existing high levels of joint economic action and trust, it would also be possible to cycle round the feedback loop in figure 7 linking embeddedness, strategic benefits and information exchange, joint economic action, and trust to build a joint high-embeddedness/high-strategic-benefits state. ## Discussion and Conclusion

THE CENTRAL FINDING OF THIS PAPER IS THAT EDI EMBEDDEDNESS moderates the impact of adopter EDI use on initiator strategic benefits. High EDI embeddedness motivates the adopter strategic use so that the initiator–adopter dyad can create value. In contrast, low embeddedness limits any strategic use particularly by the adopter.

Interfirm relationships that are created through people links, mutual exchange of information, and joint problem solving enable the initiator–adopter dyad to realize potential strategic benefits that are not available to otherwise similar but independent firms. In contrast, low EDI embeddedness makes strategic benefits unlikely, because EDI links are isolated experiences. Without joint economic action and trust, the adopter in particular finds it difficult to make internal changes. Adopters in low-embeddedness relationships resist the strategic use of EDI because it entails significant changes to their internal processes, such as systems integration of EDI with internal strategic information systems, without the strategic gains contingent on joint problem solving.

EDI impact on strategic performance, therefore, depends on the level of EDI embeddedness. Specifically, high interfirm interdependence accompanied by high EDI embeddedness enables high strategic performance. Similarly, medium interdependence coupled with medium EDI embeddedness yields some strategic benefits. In contrast, when low interdependence is matched with low EDI embeddedness, there is negligible competitive advantage from EDI.

It follows that strategic advantage, the most commonly espoused reason for implementing EDI [5], is a contingent function of EDI embeddedness. For some organizations, EDI simply automates existing communication and business processes between loosely networked organizations and, hence, has marginal strategic impact, whereas, for others, EDI provides information to the close-knit network members, through their reciprocal information sharing and joint problem solving, and creates strategic value. This resolves the conflict between “the sexy, high-impact Interorganizational Systems (IOS) that clearly have provided strategic advantage for their developers . . . [and] that EDI applications, rather than being a competitive weapon, are increasingly a necessary way of doing business” ([5], p. 29).

The qualitative in-depth, cross-case methodology adopted in this study is particularly useful for studying organizational dynamics $[28, 29, 53, 70]$ . It is especially relevant given the formative state of the field of strategic IT management $[3, 38]$ , as it reveals emergent patterns that inform theory development $[25, 49]$ . However, there is a major potential threat to the external validity of the present findings: The proposed model of initiator EDI strategic payoff may explain only the three cases discussed here.

## Cross-Validation

To examine the model's external validity, we cross-validated the inductive model developed above against three new case studies: Ford of Europe [67], Brun Passot in France [36], and 7-Eleven Japan [7]. They were chosen to satisfy the same criteria met by the three cases analyzed above, namely, successful implementation of EDI by the initiator and different levels of strategic benefits realized.

Ford of Europe has adversarial relationships with suppliers. Webster claims: “Ford does not regard its trading relationships as if they were partnerships made on an equal basis, but [as] relationships involving its domination and their subordination” ([67], p. 34). Adoption of Fordnet, a proprietary EDI, was mandated by Ford, which was unconcerned with how EDI data were used by its adopters in their internal processes. Not surprisingly, EDI use did not change the existing adversarial initiator–adopter relationships.

In addition, Webster reports that about half of Ford's German suppliers and nearly all its Spanish suppliers were not capable of integrating EDI with their internal information systems. EDI, therefore, has had no impact on their internal processes. There were no joint economic actions or social ties. Finally, Webster's case data suggest that the strategic benefits for Ford were marginal. This case is consistent with the model developed above. Low embeddedness moderated the effect of the Ford suppliers' use of EDI and yielded Ford of Europe low strategic benefits.

Since the late 1980s, Brun Passot, a small French office-supplies company, has been developing cooperative relationships with some of its large customers. To do this, it typically spends a year studying the customer's operating procedures and developing trust and openness [36]. Brun Passot's deputy managing director asserts: "In the end, we know their supply patterns better than they do" (p. 341). On the basis of this new relationship, some of its customers have chosen Brun Passot as a single supplier and formed business partnerships. These customers eliminated warehousing and inventory management. But, while the large customers can directly access Brun Passot's supply information systems and order-entry systems, Brun Passot cannot browse its customers' inventory systems for automatic replenishment. Joint economic action and interfirm social ties are low to moderate.

Jelassi and Figon [36] claim that “the return of this (EDI) investment became visible rapidly” (p. 341). In 1984, 18,000 electronic orders were processed. This rose to 22 percent of sales in 1988 (180,000 orders). In 1992, the contribution of telepurchasing was 40 percent of turnover, and Brun Passot had become one of the largest office-supply companies in France. Supply procedures in related administration were simplified. Customer orders could be forecast accurately. This led to faster stock rotations and reduced inventory costs. New customers were attracted and customer retention rates rose. Brun Passot claims that they can reduce their EDI-enabled customers’ costs by over 20 percent.

Finally, 7-Eleven invested heavily in EDI and related information technologies, such as bar-code scanner technologies, to facilitate frequent and JIT deliveries of fresh products (e.g., bread and Japanese lunch boxes) to the stores widely dispersed throughout Japan. Bensaou [7] claims that, in doing this, 7-Eleven Japan established “partnerships” with a large franchise of small retail stores. 7-Eleven Japan allocates specific customer service specialists who frequently visit all the retail stores in their territories. The customer service specialist develops social ties with retail-store owners, and helps the store owners select the best product mix and design the best store layout for the product mix through store-level IT. In addition, 7-Eleven Japan and retailers share proprietary information. This results in a medium-plus level of EDI embeddedness.

Total sales have grown at an annual rate of 16 percent during 1990–95. Major initiatives have been taken to improve sales by item selection based on additional sales analysis, quality, and freshness by implanting a system of frequent and small-lot deliveries. Value-added services such as home delivery and new products such as fast food have also been introduced. All of these are part of a market dominance strategy enabled by its EDI investment, which has delivered significant strategic benefits.

The key issue in this paper has been the difficulty of realizing strategic payoff from EDI network investment, with particular attention on the electronic network initiators. Our main conclusion is that focusing on the quality of the dyad's dynamic and ongoing economic and social relationships is critically important if we are to understand how such relationships shape the adopter's trust and decisions to invest not only in the EDI infrastructure but also in the internal changes, and motivate the adopter's strategic use of EDI. It suggests a new direction for EDI research, which has not adequately focused on the impact of the dynamic ongoing initiator–adopter relationships, despite the interdependence in the payoffs found in the previous research [9, 54].

In particular, we found some evidence of EDI embeddedness as an essential consideration in future research on the strategic payoff derived from EDI. The model of EDI initiator strategic payoff, developed and validated here against the limited sample, offers one viable explanation for why some initiators realize high strategic payoffs, while others commonly experience difficulty realizing strategic payoffs from the enormous investment in building and promoting their EDI networks. Contrary to the implicit assumptions often found in the literature that EDI use by a critical mass of adopters likely improves interfirm cooperation and impacts on the initiator's strategic benefits, we found some evidence of the moderating effect of EDI embeddedness on the relationship between adopter EDI use and initiator strategic payoff. Most important, high EDI embeddedness motivates the adopter to use EDI strategically, whereas low EDI embeddedness deters such strategic use, creating differential impacts of adopter EDI use on the initiator strategic payoffs derived from the EDI investment.
