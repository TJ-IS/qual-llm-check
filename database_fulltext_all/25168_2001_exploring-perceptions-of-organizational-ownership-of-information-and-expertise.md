# Exploring Perceptions of Organizational Ownership of Information and Expertise

SIRKKA L. JARVENPAA AND D. SANDY STAPLES

SIRKKA L. JARVENPAA is the Bayless/Rauscher Pierce Refsnes Chair in Business Administration at the University of Texas at Austin. She served as a Marvin Bower Fellow at Harvard Business School during the calendar year of 1994. Dr. Jarvenpaa serves as a coeditor in chief for Strategic Information Systems and as a senior editor for Information Systems Research. Previously she was a senior editor for MIS Quarterly. At the University of Texas, she codirects a center for Business, Technology and Law and serves as a track chair for an interdisciplinary research center on Customer Insight. Her current research projects focus on mobile computing, electronic commerce, and knowledge management.

D. SANDY STAPLES is an Assistant Professor in the School of Business at Queen’s University, Kingston, Canada. His research interests include the enabling role of information systems for virtual work and knowledge management and assessing the effectiveness of information systems. Dr. Staples is on the editorial board of the Journal of End-User Computing and has published articles in journals such as Organization Science, Journal of Business and Psychology, Communications of the Association of Information Systems, Australian Journal of Information Systems, Journal of End-User Computing, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and OMEGA.

ABSTRACT: Beliefs of organizational ownership relate to whether information and knowledge created by an individual knowledge worker are believed to be owned by the organization. Beliefs about property rights affect information and knowledge sharing. This study explored factors that help determine an individual’s beliefs about the organizational ownership of information and expertise that he or she has created. Four different situations of organizational ownership (information vs. expertise/internal vs. external sharing) were considered. The study found that a belief in self-ownership was positively associated with organizational ownership—suggesting a collaborative type of ownership situation for both information and expertise and for both internal (intraorganizational) and external (interorganizational) sharing situations. Organizational culture and the type of employee also influenced the beliefs of organizational ownership in all four scenarios. We conclude the paper with implications for practice and future research.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information sharing, knowledge management, organizational culture, ownership of information

KNOWLEDGE WORKERS AS INDIVIDUALS are the source of much information and knowledge in organizations [7]. Through their experience in the organization’s key processes, they create, find, and accumulate knowledge. Traditionally, some of this knowledge was disseminated to other individuals in their work group, some of whom internalized it and used it as a component of their individual knowledge and achievement bases. Occasionally, some of the knowledge was distributed through formal feedback systems to groups elsewhere in the organization, but the use of the knowledge, if not distribution itself, was greatly hindered by functional and divisional boundaries [36]. Generally, much of the organizational knowledge remained in the control of individuals in the organization who initially created or acquired it.

Advances in information and communication technology have increased the potential for greater dissemination of information and knowledge beyond its creator. Information and communication technologies (ICT) have increased both technical and social connectivity in organizations, facilitating information and knowledge sharing [17]. Technology has reduced the economic cost of sharing information and knowledge over various boundaries, but has also created social conventions around communication that make it easier to share information and knowledge among diverse groups in an organization or across organizations [2]. Hence, information technology has created the potential for greater horizontal information and knowledge flows. Information technology has also eased the central coordination of information. Through organization-wide systems, such as enterprise resource planning systems, information technology has facilitated greater upward movement of information [15].

Brynjolfsson [8] argues that greater central coordination of information leads to greater centralized ownership of information. A similar change might be also happening to knowledge. Knowledge management involves managing organization knowledge as a corporate asset and harnessing knowledge creation and dissemination as key organizational capabilities [40]. Starbuck [48] defines organizational knowledge as stocks of expertise that the organization possesses and capitalizes on.

A possible conflict arises because much of the organizational knowledge is controlled at the level of individual knowledge workers. Yet knowledge management argues for the management of knowledge at the level of the organization. It is assumed that, either morally or legally, the organization has the right to find, collect, store, and disseminate information that individuals have created or acquired. According to Constant et al., a common organizational norm is that “an information outcome of work such as an idea, process, invention, document, or computer program that an employee creates or acquires at work or using organizational resources actually belongs to the employer rather than to the employee” [10, p. 404]. This norm goes beyond the legally enforceable employment contracts and specifies circumstances and contingencies of ownership rights of organizations that are unforeseen or too expensive to enumerate in enough details. The norm specifies that the organization is the owner of the knowledge asset. The norm is not without its critics. For example, one human resources expert declared, “Organizations don’t own ideas. Whatever idea is worked on, it belongs to the individual who thought of it” [27].

This paper explores the perceptions of organization ownership of information and knowledge by those individuals who have created or acquired the information and expertise. What factors will help determine if a person perceives the information and expertise to be the property of the organization? We equate expertise with knowledge. Following Constant et al. [10], we define expertise involving intangible information embodied in human memory, knowledge experience or skill such as learning a statistical program. By contrast, information is defined as an information product such as a written document or computer program. We will examine both ownership of information and knowledge from the perspective of two organizational situations: (1) the organization the employee works for, and (2) the external professional body that the employee is associated with. Because internal knowledge sharing and external knowledge sharing are fundamentally different in their purposes and consequences [1, 49], we explore if the antecedents to ownership beliefs are different depending on whether sharing occurs internally or externally. The next section develops the theoretical underpinnings for the research question and advances hypotheses. The methodology used for the empirical study is then described, followed by a description of the results and a discussion of the potential implications for practitioners and future research.

## Conceptual Framework

CONSTANT ET AL. [10] ADVANCED A THEORY OF INFORMATION SHARING to understand the factors that support or constrain information sharing in technologically advanced organizations. The theory goes beyond communication and information exchanges among friends and personal contacts to include “organizationally-remote strangers they will never meet in person” [10, p. 401]. Their theory accommodates both organizational contextual factors as well as psychological factors including feelings, values, and self-identities. The model can be used to examine why people are or are not sharing their best information and knowledge regardless of the amount of technology, organizational mandate, or financial incentive.

The Constant et al. [10] theory of information sharing is built on social exchange theory [31]. Models built on the social exchange theory provide a complementary perspective to the economic perspectives of exchange. Social exchanges of information and knowledge are similar to economic exchanges in the sense that there is an expectation of some future return for sharing. But unlike economic exchanges, there is no understanding of the value of what has been shared and no clear expectation of exact future return. Kim and Mauborgne [32] and Culnan and Armstrong [12] argue that the rules of social exchange govern knowledge sharing, whereas the logic of economic exchanges governs market transactions. Individuals participate in social exchanges to maintain future relationships, the balance of power, and image.

According to the Constant et al. theory, among the rules affecting sharing behaviors are organizational norms that information sharing is “usual, correct, and socially expected workplace behavior” [10, p. 404]. Such norms are not limited to internal information sharing. The marketing literature has argued that normative expectations in the organization affect information sharing between external partners [37]. Jarvenpaa and Staples [30] found in a field-setting that individuals’ beliefs about information ownership were significantly related to the sharing of information both internally and externally. Hence, an unanswered question is whether the antecedents of organizational ownership beliefs are the same when sharing occurs internally and externally.

## Organizational Ownership of Information

Beliefs about the organizational ownership of information relate to what Constant et al. [10] argues is a widespread norm that organizations own the labor of their employees or members. The norm emphasizes the idea that information, even if a product of the employee’s hard labor, is not his or hers to give or withhold selfishly, but must be used for the greater organizational benefit. In a series of laboratory studies, Constant et al. [10] found that people’s beliefs about information ownership were correlated significantly with attitudes about information sharing when information products were in question. In fact, they found that “beliefs in organizational ownership of work outcomes caused a transformation of motivation in the exchange of information whereby people weigh the social good of sharing more heavily than their personal cost of displeasure at sharing” [10, p. 411].

The same mediating relationship between beliefs about organizational ownership and sharing was not found with sharing of expertise. Sharing of expertise was not influenced by organizational norms of ownership. Rather, it occurred because sharing provided personal benefits [10]. Sharing of what they possessed personally made them feel needed, wanted, and appreciated. In summary, Constant et al. [10] found that attitudes about different types of information sharing (information product versus expertise) were associated with different antecedents. The results implied that expertise might be perceived less as an organizational possession than an information product would be. We would expect this to hold true in both external and internal sharing situations. In the internal sharing context, the organization is the employing unit that both the giver and recipient belong to. In the external sharing context of our study, the organization is a professional association that both parties belong to.

There are two bodies of psychological theory that might help explain the difference in ownership belief between information and expertise. One is social exchange theory, which argues that people collaborate to gain desired resources through social reciprocity [53]. To maximize resources gained from the organization, individuals define their interests as being the same or similar to those of the organization. The second explanation of collaborative beliefs and behavior is based on social identity theory. By demonstrating consistency with the organization’s goals, individuals revalidate their status in the organizations [53]. We speculate that the exchange of information products might be more readily explained by social exchange theory while the exchange of expertise fits better with an identity-based perspective. Information products are discernible and individuals might be more willing to appropriate organizational rights to them on the expectation of future social reciprocity. Sharing expertise is more ambiguous than sharing information. Sharing of expertise can be related to general organizational benefit and expected future return, but it can also be related to personal benefit and self-esteem. In a study where Constant et al. [11] examined the exchange of technical information among distant employees (strangers) through a large computer system, they found that information providers gave reasons of personal benefit as often as reasons related to general organizational benefits. That is, providing information to strangers helped advice givers gain personal benefits. Sharing increased self-esteem and personal identification with the organization. We expect to replicate this finding in the internal sharing context as well as extend it to the external sharing context. Hence, we advance the first hypothesis:

H1: An individual associates organizational ownership more with information products than with expertise in both internal and external sharing situations.

## Self-Ownership of Information and Expertise

The legal definition of ownership assumes that all rights to assets that are not expressly assigned in a contract accrue to the person called the owner of the asset [8]. Justifications for ownership have received attention from researchers in law [e.g., 5] and philosophy [e.g., 35], but also increasingly from psychology [e.g., 4]. All three fields share the following three justifications for ownership: (1) current possession, (2) initial possession, and (3) labor. The labor justification deals with who made or created the property.

Heider’s theory of association [25] has been the primary basis of study of individual ownership in psychology. In studying material objects, psychologists have found that those who control the asset also tend to assume (or are credited by others with) ownership [e.g., 4]. People who are associated with an asset, either through current or prior affiliation, who have worked on the asset, or whose own identity is tied to the asset are all seen as having ownership rights to the asset. Hence, control and ownership are intertwined. Applying these findings to knowledge management suggests that whoever has the ability to control the information and knowledge might be seen as having a legitimate claim to ownership.

The legal literature suggests a joint ownership situation for ideas and knowledge unless employment contracts state otherwise. The U.S. laws on intellectual property generally maintain that knowledge is held in the name of the individual worker, although the organization has the rights to continue to use the knowledge for no additional charge. For example, if an employee creates an innovation that is patentable, the patent legally belongs to the individual though the organization has the right to use the patent. Hence, from a legal standpoint, the use of knowledge at an organization level does not preclude control and ownership of that knowledge at the individual employee level.

Therefore, we argue that ownership of information and knowledge does not need to be a zero-sum game. An organization’s ownership does not necessarily imply reduction of the individual’s ownership. An individual can share ownership jointly or interdependently with the organization. Moreover, ownership at the individual level can even strengthen ownership beliefs at the organization level. Self-ownership beliefs can increase self-esteem and one’s confidence in the value that the person brings to the organization. This sense of value creation for the organization can breed identification with the organization. In essence, organizational ownership beliefs might help employees feel connected or have a sense of oneness with an organization. We expect the relationship between self-ownership and organization ownership to hold in both internal and sharing contexts. We hypothesize:

H2: Beliefs about self-ownership of information and knowledge are positively associated with beliefs about organizational ownership of information and knowledge in internal and external sharing contexts.

## Propensity to Share Information/Knowledge

PROPENSITY TO INFORMATION SHARING is part of attitudes toward prosocial organizational behaviors. The prosocial attitudes capture the general tendency of people wishing for good outcomes not only for themselves, but also for other employees or the organization [6, p. 710]. Acts like helping, sharing, and volunteering are aimed at maintaining the well-being and integrity of others and the self [6]. Prosocial behaviors are above and beyond those prescribed by job descriptions, are voluntary in nature, and are not directly or explicitly rewarded, but contribute positively to the organization’s performance [39]. Prosocial behaviors might indicate greater commitment to the organization and greater commitment may engender beliefs that the organization has rights to the information and knowledge one has created or acquired. Again, we have no a priori reason to expect a different relationship in internal and external sharing contexts.

H3: Beliefs about organizational ownership of information and knowledge are positively associated with propensity to share in internal and external sharing situations.

## Organizational Culture

Beliefs of organizational ownership of knowledge and information could be related to, or reinforced by, organization culture. Organizational culture in this paper refers to the shared values and attitudes of the members of an organization. Organizational culture has long been argued to affect the consequences from information technology [36, 42, 44]. For example, Orlikowski found that “in competitive and individualistic organizational cultures—where there were few incentives or norms for cooperating or sharing expertise—groupware did not engender collaboration” [42, p. 362].

Goffee and Jones [24] defined and developed measures for two dimensions of corporate culture that relate to producing and maintaining the well-being and the integrity of other coworkers as well as the organization at large—sociability and solidarity. Sociability is a measure of sincere friendliness among members of a community. Solidarity is a measure of a community’s ability to pursue shared objectives quickly and effectively, regardless of social ties. Hofstede et al. [29] proposed a related dimension of employee-oriented (concern for people) versus job-oriented (concern for getting the job done). Scholz [46] identified a dimension of need for achievement. Need for achievement focuses on the importance placed in the organization on advancement and prestige.

It is known from past research that the nature and pattern of individual behavior is influenced by the strength of the relationship between individuals as well as the persons’commitment to their immediate organizations [41]. By immediate organization, we are referring to the individual’s department. The more committed that individuals are to the immediate organizational goals (high on solidarity and job orientation), the more they might be willing to share the products of their labor (information and knowledge) with others both internally and externally. Also, in an immediate organization where social ties (i.e., solidarity) are strong, beliefs of organizational ownership may be strong. Solidarity is associated with unarticulated and unquestioned reciprocity [24]. Sociability fosters teamwork and an environment in which individuals go beyond the requirements of their jobs to help their community succeed. Sociability is also associated with openness, which should mean fewer tendencies for individuals to want to control information and use it to build their personal power bases. Openness and care for others in the immediate organization may increase the beliefs in organizational ownership of information. Finally, an immediate organization with a culture reinforcing the need for achievement might breed stronger organizational commitment and pride and thereby increase the likelihood that a person attributes more ownership rights to the organization. From an exchange theory perspective, a person might believe that attributing more ownership rights to the organization will lead to more recognition and benefits to the individual that is highly valued in the immediate organization. We expect the individual’s immediate work group culture to affect their beliefs of organization ownership of information and knowledge in both internal and external sharing situations.

H4: Beliefs about organizational ownership of information and knowledge are positively associated with an individual’s immediate organization culture.

## Information Culture

Information culture represents values and attitudes toward information and what “to do” and “not to do” related to information processing, publishing, and communication in an individual’s immediate organization (department) [14]. Davenport [14] describes several ways that information cultures can vary: open versus closed, factually oriented or rumor- and intuition-based, internally or externally focused, controlling or empowering. In the marketing channel literature, information sharing between partners is argued to be affected by normative expectations that encourage an open, honest, dynamic, and frequent exchange of information [37]. If the norms encourage open and uncontrolled flow of information, users might be more willing to believe in benefits from social reciprocity and take on a belief that the collective entity that one identifies with internally (employing organization) or externally (professional association) owns the knowledge and information. Open describes the degree to which members can access information and information flows without any restriction imposed by the immediate organization or by members of the immediate organization. Organic refers to the lack of formal structures and order for processing and sharing information in the immediate organization [46].<sup>1</sup> Organic and open can be expected to be coexisting. Hence, we hypothesize:

H5: Beliefs about organizational ownership of information and knowledge are positively associated with an organic and open information culture in the individual’s immediate organization (department) in both internal and external sharing contexts.

## Task Interdependence

Monge et al. [38] suggest that willingness to create and distribute goods for a collective benefit is dependent on the costs and benefits that accrue to the person. Pure rational self-interest suggests that benefits of reciprocity from communicating and sharing with others are increased when the person’s work is dependent on the efforts of other people in and outside of their department. We expect task interdependence to affect beliefs of organizational ownership in internal sharing. People whose work highly depends on others might face frequent changes in their work including the type of information and expertise they need. The interdependence and subsequent change might increase their beliefs of organizational ownership of information and knowledge in their work environment. They would rather have the organization own the information and knowledge—assuming more stability and predictability in the availability. We would expect this relationship to hold in internal sharing, but much less so in external sharing, because the dependence we are concerned with here is on other fellow employees rather than members in the professional body. Hence, we hypothesize:

H6: Beliefs about organizational ownership of information and knowledge are positively associated with task interdependence in the internal sharing context.

## Demographics

Constant et al. [10] found that work experience was significantly correlated with beliefs of the organizational ownership of information. Similarly, years of schooling correlated with information ownership. Others have also suggested that educational level, status, and tenure affect prosocial attitudes and behavior [6, 21]. The higher the education level, status, age, and tenure of a person, the more likely he might value social reciprocity and hence have beliefs of organizational ownership of information and knowledge. Women are sometimes stereotyped as having a higher need to identify and affiliate than men do. If we assume that prosocial behaviors partly occur because of individuals’need to maintain their social identities, then perhaps women might be more likely to hold beliefs of organizational ownership of information and knowledge. We hypothesize the relationships to exist in both internal and external sharing contexts:

H7: Beliefs about organizational ownership of information and knowledge are effected by gender, age, educational background, and tenure in internal and external sharing contexts.

## Method

A QUESTIONNAIRE-BASED STUDY WAS CONDUCTED to quantitatively test the research model. This section describes the sampling method, construct measures, and analysis methods employed.

## Sample

Two universities were selected for the survey setting: one in Australia and another one in Canada. In terms of their national cultures, Australians and Canadians are rated close to each other [28]. Universities are often characterized as knowledge organizations [24]. Large state universities house many different disciplines and work environments with possibly differing organizational cultures, information cultures, and use of collaborative technologies. In both universities, virtually all office-based staff (faculty and administrative) had PCs with network connections. In our study, the unit of analysis was an employee in a department.

In both universities, questionnaires were sent to all academic staff and all general/ administrative staff whose administrative duties regularly involved using personal computers. At the Australian university, 4,253 employees received the questionnaire. At the Canadian university (which was somewhat smaller) 3,165 employees received the questionnaire. To stimulate responses, a reminder letter was sent out two weeks later and respondents were given the option to participate in a drawing at each university for prizes of \$100 each (most people who completed the survey chose to participate). Completed questionnaires were received from 1,125 employees of the Australian university and 810 questionnaires were received from employees of the Canadian university. After adjusting for questionnaires that were returned unopened due to various reasons, such as the person had left the university or was on leave, overall response rates of 27 percent and 26 percent were achieved at the Australian and Canadian universities, respectively. Whereas this was a reasonable response for an unsolicited survey and it was fairly lengthy (i.e., 12 pages), it does raise the potential for nonresponse bias. Use of the procedure suggested by Armstrong and Overton [3] indicated no significant differences between respondents and non-respondents on a variety of demographic variables. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the respondents. Responses from both universities were combined for the analyses reported here, resulting in a working sample of 1,935.

## Construct Measurement

The questionnaire contained multiple measurement items relating to each of the constructs in the research model. A pretest, using faculty, graduate students trained in questionnaire design, and administrative staff, was carried out as suggested by Dillman [18]. Where possible, scales that had demonstrated good psychometric properties in previous studies were employed. Appendix A lists the questionnaire items used to measure each construct and their sources. Table 2 reports the number of items used to measure each construct, the reliability of the combined items, and the associations among the constructs. One should note that one additional factor emerged from the factor analysis that had not been anticipated. Four items from the Hofstede [29] organizational culture instrument loaded together on a factor that we labeled Democracy. These items were part of other factors in Hofstede [29]. The items loading together on Democracy captured the level of employee authority and autonomy in the organization.

The dependent variable, views of the organizational ownership of information and knowledge, was measured in four scenarios: sharing information products within the respondent’s work organization, sharing expertise within the organization, sharing information products in a professional body external to the respondent’s work environment, and sharing expertise in the external professional body. This was done to explore the relationships specified in the research model in both external and internal sharing situations.

Within each of the four scenarios, a contrastive vignette technique (CVT) was used to measure the propensity to share in each of these situations. CVT is an indirectstructured methodology developed by Burstin et al. [9] to measure social attitudes. Directly assessing attitudes is difficult because of the confounding effect of social desirability perceptions. Burstin et al. [9] demonstrated the reliability and validity of using this indirect technique to assess attitudes within specific contexts. Specific contexts are presented to respondents in short vignettes or stories. Constant et al. [10] also used this technique successfully in their study to assess the propensity to share. Our study builds on their assessment method. Vignettes also have drawbacks as they ask people about hypothetical situations that they may have never encountered [10].

Propensity to share is an attitude about prosocial behavior [10]. Since we wanted to assess a prosocial attitude and not simply reciprocity and self-interest, we set up each vignette where the person that was asking for something in the vignette had previously acted destructively (i.e., previously refused to help the person in the vignette). In this way, we were able to capture information about the prosocial transformation—that is, how much did the individual weigh the social good of sharing than the personal cost (e.g., of helping someone who has been previously unhelpful). See Appendix B for a description of the vignettes used.

## Analysis

For the first hypothesis (H1), we ran a paired t-test on the organizational ownership of information product and organizational ownership of expertise. Stepwise multiple regression was used to test the overall research model and to identify statistically significant predictor variables (independent variables) for the other hypotheses. Stepwise regression is a method for identifying which independent variables are good predictors of the dependent variable. The first variable considered for entry into the regression equation is the one with the largest positive or negative correlation with the dependent variable. In the next step, the first variable is examined to see whether or not it should be removed according to removal criterion. Variables in the equation are then examined for entry. This process is repeated until no more variables meet entry or removal criteria. The regression model was run for each of the four different scenarios: sharing products internally within the work organization, sharing expertise internally, sharing products with a professional body external to the work organization, and sharing expertise externally. For analyses where the independent variable was staff type, we ran a MANOVA analysis. Finally, we controlled for the influence of the department to which the individual belonged because we measured individual perceptions of organizational culture and information culture instead of aggregated departmental values. The results of these analyses are described below.

## Results

The predictors explained between 16 percent and 27 percent of the variance in the individual’s beliefs of organizational ownership of information across the four scenarios. Overall, the amount of variance explained (i.e., 26.9 percent) by the model appeared reasonable for the scenario of sharing information products internally within the work organization. The relatively modest $R ^ { 2 }$ values were found for the three other scenarios. The antecedents of ownership studied here are much less predictive in situations of expertise sharing and external sharing in general. This suggests that organizational and social factors studied here are weaker in expertise sharing and external sharing context. The results of each of the 4 regression analyses are presented in Tables 3 through 6 (i.e., one for each scenario).

## Discussion

THE KNOWLEDGE MANAGEMENT LITERATURE paints a perplexing situation. On one hand, an organization has a need for managing knowledge and information at a collective, organizational level. On the other hand, an individual has a need for using information and knowledge as a component of his or her individual power base. Many scholars in knowledge management emphasize the employee control of information and knowledge and the importance of creating a situation that renders the employee willing to share voluntarily. In writing about information ecology, Davenport [14] distinguished information sharing from involuntary information reporting. Information sharing is a “voluntary act of making information available to others . . . sharer could pass information on, but doesn’t have to” [13, p. 5]. Kim and Mauborgne [32] similarly argued that the firm is dependent on individuals’ voluntary will to cooperate and share their expertise.

Greater sharing is not always reached, however. Even in work groups, individuals do not always volunteer information that would allow the group to work efficiently and effectively [26]. Concerns of power, rational gain, and distrust lead people to hide or hoard information and knowledge. In the knowledge economy, knowledge is touted to be the source of power [14]. Information can be seen as an asset that is to be owned and controlled by individuals in order to elevate their own power and status relationships in organizations [36]. Individuals create and gather information for political purposes—to protect their own self-interest or self-promote [19]. The work of Davenport et al. [16] on information politics makes a compelling case for how power can inhibit information sharing in technologically advanced environments. Davenport et al. [16] concluded, “In the most information-oriented companies we studied, people were least likely to share information freely—as perceived by these companies’ managers” [16, p. 54]. After all, “As people’s jobs and roles become defined by the unique information they hold, they may be less likely to share that information— viewing it as a source of power and indispensability—rather than more so” [16].

What emerges from the current study is a somewhat different picture. The results suggest that many knowledge workers believe that the information product that they have created or expertise that they have accumulated is the property of the organization as well as their own. Self-ownership coexists with organizational ownership. The more the individuals believe in self-ownership, the more they believe in organizational ownership. Hence, ownership is not a zero-sum game. In the views of the employees, organizations have rights to the labor of their employees, including their expertise.

Notably, individuals attach organizational property rights less to expertise than to information products. Social exchange theory [50] would support that individuals are willing to define their interests and goals as being the same or similar to those of the organization as long as they gain resources and status from their organization. Since information products are more discernible than expertise, it might be easier for an employee to gain confidence that an organization would reciprocate the exchange of an information product. The same confidence might not exist with expertise.

The study also found that employees are more likely to assign organizational ownership rights to their work if, in general, they have a propensity to share, a form of prosocial attitude. Organizational culture also had an affect on individuals’beliefs of organizational ownership. Individuals that rated their organizations high on solidarity—relationships based on common tasks, mutual interests, and shared goals—rated high their belief in organizational property of their labor. A culture characterized by solidarity perhaps gave them increased confidence that their willingness to share the ownership of their labor would be fairly reciprocated with appropriate benefits or rewards by the organization. Those that characterized their organizations with need for achievement were also more ready to assign organizational property rights.

Surprisingly, the study did not find any effect of task interdependence. We had expected that those with interdependent tasks would believe in greater organizational ownership of their labor. This hypothesis was based on rational self-interest. Great interdependence in one’s job would create a higher need for information from others. Information that was owned by the organization would presumably be more reliably and freely available. We have three plausible explanations for the lack of relationship. First, perhaps the relationship between interdependence (which creates a need for information) and views of information ownership are too indirect to have a statistically significant relationship. Second, interdependence might create a need for tight interpersonal relationships and these interpersonal relationships might substitute for access to organizational data. Third, rational self-interest may be a poor basis for ownership beliefs. Rather, social reciprocity and social identity may be much more powerful bases.

We did find an affect of demographic factors on ownership beliefs. Women, younger employees, and staff members were more likely to assign property rights to the organization than males, older employees, and faculty members. It might be that the former groups see that the recognition of their achievement will actually increase if they share their labor. Women in general have been argued to see power arising more from nonthreatening and noncontrolling actions than men. Power arises less from something that is possessed and more from what one shares with others [45].

## Limitations

ALTHOUGH INTERESTING, THE FINDINGS MUST BE TREATED with caution given the many limitations of the study. The study was very exploratory using a number of new variables and measures. The data was gathered in two university settings and while universities are thought of as knowledge organizations, the generalizability from a university setting to private industry may be questionable. We did control for professional subcultures (e.g., academic function versus administrative function) and found an effect of a subculture suggesting that results might be different by different subcultures. Although we found several of the relationships in our study to be statistically significant, several were not and the lack of significance might be due to the methodological deficiencies in the study. The study used vignettes to capture prosocial behavior, thereby inheriting all the method’s strengths but also its weaknesses. In our measures for ownership beliefs, we attempted to negate the primitive response that could be created by reciprocity and self-interest so that our measure captured the belief due to social and organizational context. However, we had no way of measuring whether some bias still existed. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of our survey design limits our ability to draw causal inferences. Although our survey identified that a number of relationships exist, we are not able to address why they exist.

## Implications for Practitioners

WHAT EMERGES FROM OUR STUDY is a sense that information and knowledge can be seen as a shared good between the individual and the organization. This type of shared possession of information and knowledge most likely builds on trust, or willingness to be interdependent on the other party without immediate reciprocal action from the other party. The co-ownership of expertise and information can be associated with organizational solidarity and commitment to the organization’s goals, values, and procedures. For example, Polanyi wrote, “The personal participation of the knower in the knowledge he believes himself to possess takes place within a flow of passion” [43, p. 300].

Many knowledge management interventions assume that the organization owns the labor of its employees/members. This is likely the case with codified repositories that impersonalize knowledge. Our current findings are a warning against interventions that have a built-in assumption that an organization unilaterally owns the knowledge assets of its employees/members. Separation and impersonalization of knowledge is likely to reduce the beliefs of self-ownership and, over time, the beliefs of organizational ownership. Beliefs of self-ownership are particularly critical for expertise sharing. Hence, organizations need to devise tactics that nurture co-ownership of knowledge and information.

Besides acknowledging employee/member self-ownership, the organization must cultivate an environment where individuals attach high levels of organization ownership to information and knowledge they create. This entails attracting people who have a natural propensity to share as well as nurturing an organization culture that has high levels of solidarity and need for achievement. Solidarity is enhanced by implementing measurable, ambitious performance targets and holding individuals accountable to them, conducting frequent conferences where the organization’s objectives, procedures, and values are communicated, and letting go those employees who do not commit and confirm to the goals [24]. Organizations also need to find the target groups that are less likely to assign organizational ownership rights to their information and knowledge and focus on them for more improvement. In our study, we found that males who are somewhat older are less likely to assign ownership rights to their organization.

Finally, organizations must explicitly manage the ownership issues through their organizational culture and through clear policies and guidelines. Previously, to a large extent, professionals’codes of conduct, intellectual property laws, and standard business practices of the trade were thought to guide the ownership issues of knowledge within and between organizations. There are now many ongoing changes that are affecting ownership issues to require explicit and continuous management of ownership throughout the firm, not just within the legal department.

## Implications for Future Research

BESIDES STRENGTHENING THE METHODOLOGY of the current investigation, future work would benefit from considering additional theoretical perspectives. This study has not addressed the agency theory perspective to information ownership (for a review on the agency theory, see Eisenhardt [20]). Agency theory assumes that employees will behave opportunistically if their interests conflict with the employer’s, that the organization owns the property rights to the employee labor, and the organization has the right to engage in monitoring and metering to minimize employee (i.e., agent) opportunism.

Our initial analysis suggests that knowledge management does not seem to fit the solution space of the agency theory, but further work is needed. Many scholars on knowledge management emphasize that knowledge workers share their best knowledge based more on altruism, trust, and self-control rather than on reasons of rational self-interest [e.g., 23]. Sharma [47] discusses how, in the context of professional knowledge transfer, monitoring and metering can be prohibitively expensive or, in some contexts, impractical. Others [e.g., 32, 33] are also critical of extrinsic motivators for knowledge sharing such as monitoring and metering. Surveillance tends to undermine the very behavior that monitoring is trying to induce or ensure. That is, sharing is encouraged through intrinsic motivation and when people think that they are under the control of extrinsic motivators, intrinsic motivation tends to decrease. Monitoring also tends to increase the distrust of the surveillants (e.g., the organization) [23, 33]. Although it appears that agency theory may have limited explanatory power for knowledge assets, future studies should more directly address and test the agency theory perspective.

We also encourage future researchers to integrate the Constant et al. [10] information sharing theory with procedural justice theory as well as public goods theory. Procedural justice theory outlines the conditions under which employees are likely to cooperate voluntarily in organizations [51]. Procedural justice captures the extent to which the organizational dynamics are judged to be fair or equitable. Three elements contribute to fairness: (1) engagement of people in decisions, (2) explanation for final decisions, and (3) clarity of expectations of the rules of the game.

Public goods theory also has a long history in explaining sharing in both economics and sociology. Recently, public goods theory has been used to understand problems associated with the process of producing and distributing communication and information goods that share characteristics of a public good [22, 34, 38]. A public good results from collective action where two conditions prevail: (1) impossibility of exclusion (specific members of the collective cannot be excluded from using the good even if they do not contribute), and (2) jointness of supply (one person’s use of the good does not diminish the level of good for other users). Communication and information systems can facilitate the accumulation of public goods or sub-goods via providing technical and social connectivity [e.g., 38, 52]. The public goods theory’s vantage point is that individuals make choices regarding information sharing given their interests, costs, and resources [22, 38]. Monge et al. [38] and the work on which they build on [22] did not deal specifically with the beliefs of the participant about information as an organizational rather than individual resource. The point of Constant et al. is that norms of organizational ownership regulate sharing behavior. Future research has an opportunity to integrate the public goods theory with the Constant et al. [10] theory. Like the procedural justice theory, Constant et al.’s [10] theory of information sharing is built on social exchange theory [31].

## Conclusion

MANAGING KNOWLEDGE OWNERSHIP ISSUES are growing ever more complex because of advances in information technology. Technology itself is changing how knowledge assets can be created and shared. Technology has enabled knowledge flows to become more fluid and abundant within and outside the organizational unit. Internal and external communication is almost instantaneous. The technological changes have increased the debates over the control of information and knowledge, be it the context of internal knowledge sharing or external knowledge sharing. Who owns the information and knowledge? The new virtual organizational forms challenge ownership issues because of physical and temporal separation of employees. Physical presence helps establish socially and psychologically the boundaries between an organization and the individual’s property rights to knowledge and information assets. Also, the new organization forms are relying on more input- and self-control, leaving less room for organizational controls. The increase in the use of alliances moves more information and knowledge sharing outside the organization, further complicating control and ownership issues.

In conclusion, there has been a lack of attention to organizational and knowledge worker ownership issues in the current knowledge management literature. We encourage future work that builds on theoretical perspectives to ownership including those from law, economics, psychology, sociology, organizations, and philosophy. There is much that needs to be learned about the nature of information and knowledge and the resulting implications of ownership to ensure maximal leverage of information and knowledge in organizations.
