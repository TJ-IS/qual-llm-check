# Situated Learning and the Situated Knowledge Web: Exploring the Ground Beneath Knowledge Management

SARMA R. NIDUMOLU, MANI SUBRAMANI, AND ALAN ALDRICH

SARMA “RAM” NIDUMOLU is an Assistant Professor of Operations and Management Information Systems in the Leavey School of Business and Administration at Santa Clara University, where he teaches full-time, and Executive MBA courses in Information Systems Management for Electronic Commerce. Professor Nidumolu’s current research deals with the adoption and impacts of new information technologies using knowledge management perspectives. Prior publications have appeared in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Management Science, and Communications of the ACM, among others.

MANI SUBRAMANI is an Assistant Professor at the Information and Decision Sciences Department in the Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota. He received his B.E. in Electrical and Electronics Engineering from BITS, Pilani; a PGDM degree from the Indian Institute of Management, Bangalore; and a DBA from Boston University. His research focuses on the strategic role of information technology within the organization and in interorganizational relationships. His current areas of research are knowledge management and the leveraging of organizational capabilities using information technologies.

ALAN ALDRICH is an Assistant Professor in the Communication Department at the University of Oklahoma, Norman.

ABSTRACT: Knowledge is now recognized as an important basis for competitive advantage and many firms are beginning to establish initiatives to leverage and manage organizational knowledge. These include efforts to codify knowledge in repositories as well as efforts to link individuals using information technologies to overcome geographic and temporal barriers to accessing knowledge and expertise. We suggest that Knowledge Management (KM) efforts, to be successful, need to be sensitive to features of the context of generation, location, and application of knowledge. To this end, we highlight the situated organizational learning perspective that views knowledge as embedded in individuals, in connections between individuals, and in artifacts as a useful lens to examine phenomena related to the establishment of KM initiatives. In an ethnographic case study of an effort to change knowledge-work processes in a market research firm, we apply the situated knowledge perspective to highlight the factors responsible for the limited success of the initiative in the firm. This study suggests that a consideration of the situated knowledge web and the alignment of the initiatives with the features of the knowledge web are central to success in knowledge management efforts in firms.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: ethnographic methods, knowledge management, organizational change, qualitative research, situated knowledge web, situated learning

THERE IS GENERAL RECOGNITION AMONG both academics and practitioners that knowledge has become an important basis for competitive advantage between firms. Some have even suggested that it is the most strategically significant resource for an organization [21, 43]. Knowledge work refers to “a set of activities using individual and external knowledge to produce outputs characterized by information content” [10]. These activities include the acquisition, creation, and application of knowledge, particularly where work is characterized by exceptions and variety, rather than routine actions [9]. Examples of organizations where knowledge work is the dominant activity include market research firms and financial services firms [36]. Recent research suggests that information systems have important implications for knowledge creation, knowledge sharing, and knowledge use in firms as the ready availability of such knowledge management solutions directs action towards problems that these information systems can handle rather than those experienced by participants [22].

Fahey and Prusak [16] highlight some of these common pitfalls, such as focusing on knowledge stocks like databases and patents rather than knowledge flows among individuals, on artifacts and technologies rather than contexts shared by individuals and groups, and on standardizing tasks rather than encouraging creativity and exploration. As they suggest:

If knowledge exists ultimately within individuals and it is individuals participating simultaneously in multiple groups processes who make and execute key decisions, then a fundamental purpose of “managing knowledge” must be to build some degree of shared contexts. “Shared Context” means a shared understanding of an organization’s external and internal worlds and how they are connected. [16, p. 268]

A similar argument is central to the notion of an activity system that is defined as the set of related actions performed in the context, towards the achievement of individual, group and organizational objectives [3], and to the application of situated action perspectives to organizational action [29]. Situated action views learning as occurring constantly with ongoing activity by individuals and the knowledge created by this learning process as embedded both in the mind of an individual actor as well as in how the actor’s environment becomes structured as a result of this activity. In this view, often described as the situated organizational learning perspective [29], the locus of the learning moves beyond the individual. Knowledge is viewed as embedded in the connection between individuals, in rules, divisions of labor and roles, and other artifacts that determine patterned interaction and behavioral regularities [30]. Overall, this perspective suggests a focus on the patterned interactions in the activity system as the key source of insight in examining the impact of knowledge management initiatives or other organizational changes that aim to influence organizational action in a significant manner. Deriving a detailed understanding of patterned interactions in activity systems is consequently an important consideration in the implementation of knowledge management initiatives because such interactions exert an important influence on the nature of the outcomes.

The purpose of this research is to report on one organization’s attempts to introduce changes to the activity system in a particular division to make the firm more responsive to its customers and its environment.<sup>1</sup> Consistent with the aim of explicating the nuances of the external and internal worlds, the way they are linked, and how they are manifested through action, we adopt an ethnographic methodology that allows us to go deep into the organization and see the world through the eyes of participants [13]. We describe a detailed longitudinal study of the firm carried out over a period of 13 months. While we had expected to report on the success of the firm’s initiative, we observed the firm’s attempt to affect the changes to the activity system, sustain the effort for a period, and finally abandon it as organizational priorities changed.

In particular, we believe that our account of this failed initiative may be instructive for several reasons. First, failures are underrepresented in the literature and less is known about failures than of successes. Second, there is some evidence that failures are more useful as sources of lessons than successful outcomes [49]. Analyses of past successes generally reinforce notions of competence in organizations and redirect organizational efforts towards incremental refinement rather than innovative problem solving [30]. Learning from successes is clearly beneficial only when the lessons from prior efforts are generalizable to situations subsequently encountered by organizations. However, in complex and dynamic contexts, the generalizability of prior lessons is often ambiguous and normal learning processes can lead organizations into unexpected states when failures occur [49]. Instances of failures can thus provide clearer lessons for organizations than successes regarding the nexus of specific contextual factors where drawing from the organization’s prior repertoire of lessons is inappropriate or inadequate.

Based on our interactions with participants throughout the process and our examination of archival records, we provide an analysis of the events and articulate the nature of influences that might explain the outcomes observed. We describe how a top-down approach to changing organizational action and knowledge was not sufficiently sensitive to the distributed nature of situated knowledge among multiple actors, the impacts of unrelated organizational actions such as layoffs on the ability to assemble relevant knowledge, and the priorities within social systems. We explain how this mismatch was an important factor in the eventual abandonment of the effort. We conclude by suggesting that similar patterns and phenomena could help explain outcomes of knowledge management initiatives in other organizations.

## Theory

## Situated Organizational Learning

ORGANIZATIONAL LEARNING MAY BE DESCRIBED as the process through which organizational members develop “the ability to discover when organizational changes are required and what changes can be undertaken which they believe will succeed” [14, p. 78]. Theories of Situated Organizational Learning [20, 24, 29] provide a conceptual lens through which we can examine attempts by organizations to transform the nature of action within organizations.

Adopting this perspective has several implications. First, from a situated organizational learning perspective, knowledge in the firm is viewed as emergent, distributed, and resident in people, practices, artifacts, and symbols. This is different from the view of knowledge as primarily resident in the minds of individuals from where it can be extracted into repositories or transferred to others. The performance of cognitive tasks is viewed as involving a complex system that “includes a web of coordination among the media and processes inside and outside the individual task performers” [24, p. 289]. Knowledgeable task performance is seen as involving individuals, their links to other players with related information and related roles, and mediating artifacts such as division of labor, rules, and technologies aiding the task [3]. Task performance is indicated as being reliable due to the redundancies and correction capabilities inherent in the distributed system [24]. Adopting the situated organizational learning perspective consequently moves the locus of the examination of issues related to knowledge from the individual to the activity system within organizations engaged in ongoing processes of situated learning.

Second, learning is viewed as occurring in parallel with ongoing activities that both (a) shape the environment and the resources available to actors, and (b) are reinforced, modified, or otherwise structured as a result of the activity. This perspective therefore directs attention to the context of the action and the external environment as the enabler or constraint on action.

Third, action within activity systems is conceived to involve the combination of resources and knowledge distributed across multiple individuals and roles. Consequently, this view draws attention to communities of practice that develop naturally within organizations [46] and comprise those individuals involved in various ways in task performance. Such a view brings into focus the subtle processes by which the boundaries of these communities are shaped and by the mechanisms by which newcomers become old-timers and assume identities as practitioners in the community [32]. Finally, this view therefore also emphasizes the importance of the creation and elaboration of community agendas and other means of social reinforcement of prior beliefs and priorities that may not always be aligned with those of the larger organization.

## The Situated Knowledge Web

The symbol of a situated knowledge web is useful in framing discussion specifically related to organizational knowledge. The metaphorical symbol of a web has been used before in previous social and organizational research. For example, Geertz [19] drew from Weber [59] in suggesting that culture could be construed as a web of significance spun by the individuals comprising the culture. Kling and Scacchi [27] proposed that the ensemble of equipment, applications, and techniques are social objects laden with meaning and could be viewed as comprising a web of computing.

Similarly, the loosely linked individuals of online communities are described as constituting webs of knowledge [17, 57]. Drawing from these views, we propose the notion of the situated knowledge web, a more restrictive and bounded conceptualization to study an important subset of the broader issues highlighted by the situated organizational learning perspective. This device enables a closer theoretical focus on specific activity systems addressed in the literature on knowledge management such as customer relationship management [8], transfer of best practices [40], and risk management [36].

The relationship between situated organization learning, the situated knowledge web for a specific activity system, and the views commonly espoused in discussions of knowledge management initiatives [23, 52, 62] is presented in Figure 1. In this paper, we suggest that a fundamental understanding of the processes and outcomes involved in implementing knowledge management initiatives can be derived from an examination of the corresponding situated knowledge web. The analysis of this web is an interpretive exercise where the social meanings constructed by individuals in the context of the activity system need to be explicated [19]. In this research, we employ an ethnographic methodology where social meanings were elicited primarily through metaphors and symbols used by the actors to characterize their interactions.

One key difference between current approaches adopted in the literature on KM and the view of the situated knowledge web employed by us lies in the orientation toward new organizational knowledge. The view of planned efforts at transforming organizational learning and knowledge inherent in current research on KM presumes that managers “are the primary source of organizational change, and that these actors deliberately initiate and implement changes in response to perceived opportunities to improve organizational performance or ‘fit’with the environment” [41]. For example, Duncan and Weiss [14] suggest that when a gap is perceived to exist between the organization’s current performance and that desired by its decision-makers, a search for ways to reduce this gap is deliberately initiated. Similarly, Vandenbosch and Higgins [54] suggest that it is important to focus upon the learning undertaken by senior executives because they have the most influence on their organization’s behaviors and performance. This is evident in models of organizational learning in the KM literature that adopt a top-down, manager-driven approach to the process, even when they involve significant autonomous action by individuals involved [7].

The situated view as discussed earlier assumes that organizational transformation occurs in a more emergent form through “an ongoing improvisation enacted by organizational actors trying to make sense of and act coherently in the world” [41, p. 65]. Its application to knowledge management is consistent with our arguments that new knowledge is created primarily through ongoing interactions and improvisations that an organization’s employees undertake in order to perform their jobs. The situated learning literature [33, 53] proposes a greater sensitivity to emergent, unplanned organizational learning and knowledge engendered by the everyday actions of organizational employees, rather than the popular view of knowledge management as taking place through top-down, manager-driven, purposeful efforts at organizational learning. This emphasis on the emergent aspect in understanding how structures in organizations evolve is also consistent with the behavioral tradition in organizational learning [34].

## Methods

## Details of Site and Research Method

ETHNOGRAPHIC METHODS ARE PARTICULARLY SUITED to the goals of our research because they focus on the accomplishments of everyday actions and the underlying cognitive structures of individuals [4]. While we employ many of the naturalistic methods of inquiry common to ethnography (e.g., participant observation and cultural immersion), our research questions are situated in a specific context—an organization attempting to modify its knowledge sets. Within the ethnographic tradition, we therefore chose an ethnographic case study method to studying organizational learning, which incorporates on-site observation of organizational activities with interviews and examination of documents [45]. We went deep into one organization to derive insights on the processes related to a change in the knowledge web of the organization. Our ethnographic data collection method is particularly useful for discovering new insights when little is known about a phenomenon. In adopting an interpretive research method, we follow discovery oriented procedures that are particularly powerful in casting a new light on complex processes whose structure, dimensions, and character are yet to be completely understood. This allows us to focus not just on everyday actions, but also to examine the specific actors and their actions embedded within the context of interest that create the actions we observe.

This enables us to address, for example, differences in top-down (managers) and bottom-up (workers) perspectives as firms attempt to articulate and implement new visions of knowledge work.

Metaphors allow people to express their understanding of one thing through the symbol associated with another [6, 28]. They therefore provide a window into the cognitive interpretations made by participants of their activities. By focusing on metaphors that describe interactions among organizational actors, our approach provides a useful means to characterize the commonalities among different complex sequences of action that we observe. This is a particularly efficient device when the patterns are often embedded within complex processes and are difficult to otherwise articulate or explain. Overall, our approach provides a view into both the structures and rituals inherent in performance within the organization and the relationships formed by interactions among individuals.

To select an organization for study, we made a series of presentations at practitioner forums to outline our interests in studying organizations that shared three qualities: (a) they were primarily engaged in knowledge work; (b) they made intensive use of information technologies to accomplish this knowledge work; and (c) they were currently actively engaged in, or were planning to engage in, transforming the organization with regard to how they worked. A variety of organizations expressed interest including hospitals, semiconductor manufacturers, and data processing firms. We selected Market Research Inc.<sup>2</sup> (MRI), a market research company based in the southwest, for study because it best fit the above criteria. Moreover, MRI provided excellent research site availability and there was a strong willingness on the part of the organization to allow open access of our research team to its organizational members and its work processes.

In January 1994, the management of MRI had articulated a new vision for the organization. In particular, MRI had initiated a redesign of the processes in one of the key divisions—the Data Integrity division—to make the unit more proactive in interacting with clients and reorient it from one that was systems-focused to one that was more business-focused.

MRI is a specialized market research company in the pharmaceutical industry and maintains the largest prescriber-linked prescription database in the world. With this data, MRI creates a variety of specialized reports supplied to sales and marketing divisions of pharmaceutical companies. For example, one of MRI’s products provides detailed information on prescriptions written for drugs in specific markets to help clients identify the impact of their promotional activities in those markets.

MRI purchases a large and continuous stream of data from retail and wholesale sources. Each week, individual pharmacies and pharmacy chains all across the country send MRI data on every prescription that they fill. This includes the date filled, name of the prescribing physician, the drug prescribed, the dosage, and the volume (e.g., a ten-day supply, two-week supply). MRI receives data on approximately 20 million transactions from retail sources every week. In addition, MRI receives data from wholesale sources such as pharmaceutical companies on drug shipments, from hospitals on shipments received and prescriptions filled in clinics and wards, from wholesalers on receipts and shipments, and from government departments (such as the VA Hospitals) on both shipments received and prescriptions filled. MRI receives data on approximately 7 million transactions every week from these wholesale sources. MRI also periodically receives supplementary information on medical personnel such as physicians, nurse practitioners, and healthcare professionals from sources such as the American Medical Association (AMA). MRI uses these multiple sources of data to create a composite picture of the movement of drugs through the pipeline—from the manufacturers and distributors to the final patient—and is thus in a position to provide a variety of useful reports for the marketing and sales divisions of pharmaceutical firms. The products of MRI, packaged in the form of reports, are used by pharmaceutical firms to chart sales trends and identify problems in service areas.

At the time of the study, MRI received data on prescription transactions on media such as diskettes, data cartridges, and magnetic tapes that were physically mailed by their retail and wholesale sources.<sup>3</sup> The data received each week were combined and consolidated weekly reports were generated for customers on the Monday of the following week.

Maintenance of the integrity of the data stream is of primary importance to MRI as inaccuracies in the final product generated distrust on the part of clients relying on the reports to make significant marketing decisions. Within MRI, the Data Integrity (DI) division is responsible for creating and maintaining the data stream. Since all data initially enters the organization through the DI division, this division receives considerable attention by the organization. All data entering MRI has to be checked for accuracy and completeness. Inconsistencies in the data are identified and corrected during entry into the data stream. Because of the key role in maintaining the data, members of DI often refer to themselves as the “custodians of the data.” This belief is reflected by comments made throughout our interaction with DI personnel and is captured in the mission statement for the DI division: “To collect, validate, standardize, and maintain data to ensure the most timely and accurate databases for MRI integrated information needs. To educate and provide information solutions for our clients.”

The DI division in MRI performs the critical task of combining the data from multiple sources (e.g., combining details of drugs provided by the manufacturer with the specific prescription dispensed by the pharmacy), a process that involves identifying and correcting mismatches and errors. The consolidated data is then formatted and partitioned into subsets that are then passed on to the other divisions for further processing in the form required for specific customers.

Validated and standardized data is considered enhanced and forms the nucleus of information used by other divisions (e.g., Systems, Customer Support) within MRI to produce their product. Problems occurring in the data stream within the DI division thus cascade throughout the organization. Such problems can include the delay of time-sensitive reports or generation of reports with misleading or inaccurate information. The DI division is therefore considered very critical to MRI and served as the focus of an organizational transformation effort in 1994–1995. The planned transformation effort specifically targeted the organizational knowledge generated by the DI division regarding the needs of customers.

Since these needs are primarily met through the outputs of the data stream that flows through the DI division, we concentrated on acquiring an understanding of this process flow (Figure 2) using participant observation during the first phase of our data collection. A detailed description of each of these steps is provided in Appendix 1. Overall, the observations of the data stream make clear that everyday work at MRI’s DI division is knowledge-intensive, requires innovative thinking with regard to why problems may have occurred, and calls upon a mix of business and computer systems knowledge in solving them. ## Data Collection

The ethnographic case study described in this research incorporated prolonged engagement, persistent observation, triangulation, and member checking.

Prolonged engagement in the field was undertaken over a 13-month period (June 1994–June 1995) in three partially overlapping phases. During the first phase (June– December 1994), participant observation was conducted by a field researcher experienced in computer systems analysis and design. Much of our understanding of the details of everyday processes came out of this work, since the observations related to how each task was performed by individuals within the DI division at MRI. In particular, the observations also included actual measurement of the process parameters, such as the frequency of errors in the processing, and the amount of time spent by analysts in diagnosing and fixing these errors. Because such diagnosis and correction activities required considerable expertise on the part of the analyst, they constituted knowledge work that was crucial to the execution of the data stream at MRI.

While process measurements are somewhat unconventional in ethnographic field research, they were nevertheless important to our study because they helped distinguish the knowledge work performed by humans from the routine diagnosis and correction performed using computer programs. They also established that most of the effort was spent on error diagnosis and correction by human analysts, which suggested that the process was indeed intensive with regard to knowledge work. More conventional participant observation techniques used in organizational ethnography [55, 56] were difficult to conduct in this study because of the tacit nature of much of the knowledge work performed by organizational members. However, interviews with organizational members were a useful and valid ethnographic vehicle for explicating some of these underlying concepts and feelings [26]. These interviews formed the last two phases of data collection.

In the second phase that spanned a period of 10 months (September 1994–June 1995), another field researcher, working independently from the first participant observer, conducted an extensive series of interviews within the Information Processing (IP) division of MRI. In the first part of this prolonged engagement in the field (September 1994–February 1995), the interviews concentrated on a deep informant [19, 61]—a senior manager of MRI—who was in charge of the organization’s planned effort at transforming its activities.<sup>4</sup> Such informants promote a deeper understanding of the context as well as provide historical background information and details on events occurring outside of the purview of the field researcher [2, 12]. Specifically, the use of this planned transformation effort (PTE) manager as a deep informant allowed greater access to the organizational vision and formal attempts at implementing new approaches to organizational learning at MRI. A total of 18 interviews were conducted with the deep informant over a period of six months. Each interview lasted approximately three to four hours. After this period of intense fieldwork, the interviewer made a temporary withdrawal from the field for a six-week period to consolidate field notes and plan for the next phase.

In the third phase of prolonged engagement (April 1995–June 1995), MRI provided access to each individual within the DI division as well as to individuals from other units such as the Systems division who were key participants in the formal topdown efforts at transforming organizational learning at MRI. The interviews were set up in one-hour time slots. Also, managers of each subunit within the DI division were interviewed on separate days. The interviews were conducted using private meeting rooms to ensure privacy of the interviews. Since MRI made extensive use of meeting rooms, it was quite normal for subjects to be seen in an interview room with the field researcher. The field researchers engaged in persistent observation in the following manner. Field notes were compiled within 24 hours of exiting the field. At the end of each interview, expanded field notes were created using a three-step process. The first step—typing out information directly from the field notes and recorded observations— was performed as soon as practically possible after the interviews were concluded. The second step—reviewing the typed notes and reviewing them for accuracy—was performed within 24 hours of each interview. Any new recall was added at this time to the notes. In the final step, any points that were not understood were identified for future clarification. Both added recall and points of clarification were identified as such [37] when incorporated into the original notes.

The field notes generated by the interviewer included a reflexive component [35] that dealt with observations about the participant/subject and personal feelings about the state of research such as problems or frustrations of the ongoing process. These entries became instrumental in identifying when it was time to curtail interactions with the deep informant [60]. Interviews with informants often reach a saturation point beyond which further interviewing becomes pointless [38]. Such a point was reached at the end of January 1995 when the deep informant said at the beginning of an interview, “We’re falling into a rut.” Reflexive comments in the field notes and direct comments such as this helped to identify when interviews were resulting in socialization through extended “rapport talk” [51] rather than the addressing of substantive issues.

Triangulation of methods was undertaken by comparing the findings obtained from participant observation, interviews, and examination of corporate documents. Particular attention was given to obtaining independent information that could be compared and contrasted. One means of obtaining uncorrelated materials is by using research teams [11]. Our team consisted of three persons. One person conducted participant observation, another conducted interviews with the deep informant and individual organizational members, and the third remained outside the purview of the fieldwork. This third person functioned as a peer debriefer [35, 37], reading and questioning information brought in from the field.

Member checking was accomplished in two ways. First, informal member checking [15] was done prior to the start of each interview with the deep informant. Assumptions and questions arising from previous interviews were either raised with the deep informant via telephone or at the start of the next interview session. Second, formal member checking in the form of a written and detailed report was done after four months of interaction with the deep informant. This report, totaling 40 pages, outlined observations and beliefs held by the field researchers about the organizational culture, assumptions concerning standard work practices and processes for creating change, and the progress to date of the planned effort at organizational learning. This report was read by every senior manager at MRI, including the CEO of the organization. The report’s accuracy was made apparent by comments made to the field researcher by managers who had read the report. For example, one Senior Vice President commented by e-mail, “I found your observations to be accurate (at least from my limited perspective) and they agree with my own impressions and frustrations.” Although member checking is not triangulation [15], it serves a similar function because it can provide additional confidence in the accuracy of the interpretations made by the field researcher.

Ethical considerations were handled in several ways. First, all field work was conducted overtly, in full view of organizational members [18]. The identities and affiliations of field researchers and their interactions with the deep informant were public knowledge within the organization. Great care was taken to ensure a uniform of presentation of the field researcher’s roles to each individual within the organization. Each subject was given an “interview rules of conduct” to read prior to beginning the interview session, which stipulated that each interviewee had the right to refuse tape recording of the interview, to stop taping at any time during the interview, and to go “off record” at any time. Of the 36 people interviewed, 5 chose to go “off record” for at least a part of their interview session. In one instance, a subject took the initiative to review some information with the intent of placing that information back “on record.”

The data set gathered consisted of approximately 500 single-spaced pages of transcripts, 250 pages of single-spaced field notes, and approximately 250 pages of corporate documents. Corporate documents included memoranda, minutes of meetings, sales literature, planning documents, project progress charts, and so on. The data was handled in several stages including transcription, coding, recording, and cross-checking. Each taped interview was subject to a verbatim transcription using a simplified transcription notation system [25] that identified pauses, overlapping speech, and any unusual happenings during the interview such as interruptions or outside events. A three-stage transcription strategy was used. An initial rough draft of the transcript was produced, followed by a fine-grained transcription session. Key sections of specific interviews culled for analysis received a third pass to check for accuracy of the transcribed data. Interviews in which either the recording quality was unreadable or permission to tape was not granted were summarized in the field notes and secondary observations made by the field researcher after each interview.

During data analysis data was separated into initial categories: deep informant field notes, transcripts and notes of subject interviews, participant observation notes, and organizational documents. Each data set received the same analysis. An open coding scheme [50] was used to identify specific activities and key symbols and metaphors employed by participants.

Computing technology greatly enhances the ability of field researchers to code large quantities of qualitative data since key word searches in word processing programs are both efficient and productive [15]. Portions of text from the interviews were cut and pasted under each category, effectively creating electronic data cards for future sorting. Pennartz [42] identifies two types of data, explicit and implicit. Explicit units are drawn directly from the data whereas implicit units require an inferential association linking the data to the concept. Whenever possible, explicit units in the form of key words or symbols used by subjects were used to identify each category. Implicit units were used to further refine each category. The open coding schemes identified basic activity domains with layers of sub domains for each activity. Both the deep informant and subjects interviews were coded separately using this scheme. Coding of organizational documents was narrower in scope as these documents were mostly organic to the learning efforts undertaken by MRI.

## Observations

WE PRESENT THE OVERALL OBSERVATIONS in the form of a framework in Figure 4. The framework highlights the interaction of the top-down view of planned change with regard to knowledge generation and dissemination and the situated knowledge web. We now describe each of these.

## Articulating a Vision: Thinking and Moving “Out of the Box”

In January 1994, MRI management felt the need to articulate a new vision for the organization as a whole. This need for a new vision was driven in part by increased competitive pressures, decreases in client satisfaction, and an anticipated decline in revenues. The MRI corporate vision of the future was embodied in the metaphor, to think and move out of the box. The “box” metaphor was seen as symbolizing institutionally embedded ways of conducting organizational activities [44].

The DI division became the focus of the organizational effort to think and move “out of the box” because of its position in maintaining the data stream. As described earlier, all the data necessary to produce a saleable product enters the organization through the DI division. Any hindrances or impediments to the data stream occurring within DI adversely affect the entire organization. Around the time the vision was articulated, it was widely perceived throughout MRI that their current approaches to work were not functioning. One relatively junior analyst put it this way: “We have to make the changes or we’re not going to survive.”Another person with more seniority expressed the need for change along with limitations on change, “It is more of a crisis. They are things that have to be done, but then it’s a crisis because we don’t have the resources.” One solution was for MRI to look toward its people as the resources for change.

Planned Changes

This vision of MRI’s senior managers of moving “out of the box” was articulated in corporate documents, such as memoranda and strategic planning notes, and in the organizational talk of its members. MRI saw this movement “out of the box” as both a direction toward a desired set of future outcomes and a set of means by which these desired outcomes could be achieved. In movement there is a sense of doing, of action on the part of participants. “Out of the box” stands for new ways of doing, be it learning, changing, or innovating. The actions embodied in this symbol were visible within MRI in at least three different areas.

Becoming Proactive. An important aspect defining what moving “out of the box” meant for MRI is the strongly held belief throughout MRI that becoming competitive meant becoming proactive in relationship to the customer. MRI at this time was in a significant competitive squeeze where its main competitor was using their understanding of data as the basis of competition. Acting proactively requires anticipating the needs of the customer rather than simply reacting or responding to requests from customers. Explicit orientation to this need is evidenced in strategic planning documents developed within MRI: “The value our customers place on service requires that we be proactive early in the contract process and manage client expectations.” This positioning of MRI relative to the customer was also stressed in an internal memorandum sent to all employees by the executive planning group in August 1994: “The primary goal of this new organization is to become, as a corporation, totally client focused . . . concentrate on our business as viewed from the client’s perspective and structures the company to be optimally responsive to client’s needs.”

Being responsive to the client’s needs meant moving beyond simply satisfying customer inquiries regarding MRI products and services. MRI wanted to be able to introduce new products that would solve problems that users of MRI products might not have recognized themselves. MRI’s strategic planning team for customer service oriented toward this desired outcome in asserting, “For the long term, quality must include proactive investigation in anticipation of client questions for existing and future MRI products.” In essence, being proactive at MRI meant not only satisfying customers, but also developing new markets through product innovation and design.

“Ask Why” Thinking. An “ask why” orientation or a natural sense of inquiry on the part of individual members of MRI was viewed as being crucial in MRI’s dynamic business environment and in thinking and moving “out of the box.” People who “ask why” or “what if” questions were perceived to be people who generate new knowledge. The Planned Transformation Effort manager pointed out how many of the new changes and developments in MRI products occurred because a customer asked a “what if” question regarding the functioning of a particular product. To handle this question, a programmer or systems analyst at MRI, seeing the potential for an enhancement or an entirely new product, would submit the needed changes. However, since the initial impetus for change occurred outside of MRI, the organization remained in the role of having to react to customers’ needs rather than anticipating the needs of the customer in advance. The inability to control both the rate and type of change frustrated for MRI managers. The director of the DI division often referred to the process of responding to problems as “trying to shoot a moving target.” The issue was viewed as the need to motivate employees to think of solutions before the problem was recognized by the customer.

MRI initiated an organization-wide campaign in January 1994 to encourage employees to think “out of the box” in order to be innovative. Such thinking would identify new ways for performing daily activities. The focus was on encouraging creativity and innovative thinking. A senior manager described the effort in this fashion: The company had this big kickoff and they said “OK, think outside of the box” and they had this meeting with, you know, they showed people videos on how to think outside of the box . . . some of the presentations they had were very good, it was clear that they wanted people to be innovative.

Outside consultants were hired during this kickoff to facilitate and encourage innovative thinking processes. Their involvement was limited to a few short classes and presentations to MRI employees. Ultimately, both the emphasis and responsibility for generating new ideas and new knowledge was placed squarely in the hands of the members of MRI.

Shift of Knowledge Emphasis. Part of the desire to think and move “outside the box” was evidenced in a fundamental shift in the types of knowledge sought after by managers at MRI. Since its formation, MRI has viewed itself primarily as a data processing company focused on analyzing raw data and transforming it into salable information. The type of knowledge and experience valued most for accomplishing this has been systems knowledge or the understanding of computers and programming processes. However, the desire to produce new products and develop new markets has resulted in a concerted movement away from systems knowledge to business knowledge. Business knowledge consists of a familiarity with products and services provided by the retail sector. The Director of the DI division described MRI as shifting its focus from data crunching to a more human side. This human side meant company representatives would enter into a particular market area and work with that market to determine what information was desired or needed for a specific application. MRI’s managers viewed this shift as a key component of becoming proactive.

This shift was evidenced in the choice of backgrounds of senior level individuals hired in 1994 and early 1995. The DI director was hired specifically because of his background with the retail aspects of the business. Several newly hired vice presidents also brought considerable business knowledge to the organization. The new CEO was also hired in part due to his customer orientation and business knowledge. One supervisor expressed the view towards the CEO this way, “He is from one of the wholesalers, (from) the human side of the business.” The focus of MRI therefore shifted to acquiring people who could think from the customer’s perspective rather than the analyst’s perspective.

Overall, managerial frustrations with the reactive approach to dealing with customers resulted in a vision of the future which can be symbolized as thinking and moving “out of the box.” This vision was articulated in terms of being proactive regarding customer needs, undertaking innovative thinking, and emphasizing business over systems knowledge. In the next section, we describe how this vision of thinking and moving “out of the box” was implemented in terms of transformational learning processes. We then contrast them with the supportive learning processes that were in place within MRI.

## Transformational vs. Supportive Learning Processes

Transformational Learning Processes. MRI management believed that a wellplanned, well-organized transformation effort coupled with innovative individuals would result in thinking and moving MRI “out of the box.” MRI chose to embark on a planned transformation or “redesign” effort that can be considered as an attempt to undertake transformational learning. The transformation effort, though company-wide, focused on the DI division because of its importance to the company. MRI chose to conduct this planned transformation effort almost exclusively with internal resources and people and to structure the effort in the form of temporary cross-functional learning groups. This effort was temporary in that a time frame for achieving a set of goals was put into place. Once the goals had been achieved the transformation effort was to be dissolved. The effort was cross-functional in that individuals were assembled from all parts of MRI with the purpose of providing individual expertise to the planned transformation effort.

An MRI director was appointed as the PTE manager with full time responsibility to the project in January 1994. A ten-person core team consisting of MRI division directors and unit managers was assembled to identify the basic processes in the DI division. The core team also sought to identify natural boundaries between each process. The result was identification of eight basic areas seen as central to the DI division’s mission of regulating the data stream. Sub teams were formed in June 1994, with single teams assigned to each of the eight identified process areas. The mission of the sub teams was defined by MRI as follows:

Each sub team consists of a group of experts determined by the business to have significant knowledge pertaining to each aspect of the Information Processing redesign project. They have been chartered to think outside of the box and identify topics to be listed for consideration during the DI-Redesign.

This group of experts consisted of 47 individuals representing each division within MRI. Though the entire redesign effort focused on the processes within the DI division, only 17 members from this division served on the sub teams. This was because the emphasis was on identifying people organization-wide with long-term historical knowledge of MRI. Assignment to the sub teams was also made regardless of one’s position within the formal organizational hierarchy. For example, corporate vice presidents worked alongside analysts on some of the sub teams.

Each of the eight sub teams had a manager appointed as a group leader. The PTE manager became informally involved with each of the sub teams as coleader because she thought that the sub team leaders were not clear about their goals and were allowing the groups to digress or to focus on irrelevant issues. One core team member, when asked about their understanding of the sub teams’ goals, responded:

They [goals of sub teams] should come through the person chairing the sub team, who should have been a member of the core team but that was not always the case. I think [the PTE manager] was meeting with them on the side.

Each sub team met once or twice every week during June and July of 1994. At the end of this period, a list of recommended changes in how work was performed was generated by each sub team for their area of responsibility. Though hundreds of recommendations were made, 52 items were identified for immediate implementation in two phases. Each of these items was ranked in terms of how it would save money, increase efficiency, improve product quality, increase accuracy of information, and result in faster product delivery. Once each item had been prioritized, it was assigned to a project team for task completion. In addition to the sub teams generating ideas, process models were constructed for the processes within the DI division. The goal of the models was to capture the activities of individuals and then simulate them to undertake “what if” analysis. By examining the interrelationships between different activities, the performance effects of changes in processing could be tracked throughout the DI division. These models were also viewed as a means to identify processing bottlenecks and to suggest radically new business processes.

Supportive Learning Processes. At MRI, two supportive learning processes or loops significantly impact the day-to-day data stream as it moves through the DI division. In contrast to the transformational learning loop, these loops are more permanent in nature and formalized through organizational procedures in place. One learning loop connects the customer to both the DI and Systems divisions (see Figure 5). This loop is activated when the customer makes an inquiry that requests information or seeks a modification in an MRI product. Often, the inquiry results from a customer’s doubts with regard to the accuracy of a particular piece of data. For example, a customer might call the customer service representative to ask why the sales of a particular product in a territory are reflected in MRI reports as being down when he believes sales should be increasing because of a recently launched campaign aimed at doctors to prescribe the drug. For each customer inquiry, a customer service request (CSR) form is filled out. For questions that the representative cannot provide an answer; the CSR is routed to either the Systems or DI divisions.

CSRs represent supportive learning processes as the organization attempts to respond to existing conditions by working within the existing policies and norms. CSRs pose two problems for MRI. First, CSRs are difficult to handle in a timely fashion. Inquiries passed onto the DI division require manual accessing of the data stream for the answer. For instance, answering the question about the drug sale trends would require the primary data on reports on sales within the territory from pharmacies to cross-check the customer’s intuition against the data. The amount of time needed to address inquiries often results in reports produced long after the CSR originated and can build customer distrust of MRI’s information. Second, CSRs from customers requesting changes in products (e.g., manufacturerwise breakdown of a specific generic drug rather than combined reporting of volume for the category) usually results in modifications to the reports delivered to the customer. This keeps MRI in a reactive mode with changes frequently being made to the products to fit individual needs, which becomes expensive and ties up programming and analyst resources.

The second problem arises from the elaborate learning loop that connects the DI and Systems divisions to each other to deal with modifications to the software applications which monitor the data stream (see Figure 6). Analysts from the DI division and programmers from the Systems division interact at all levels in order to answer questions, suggest modifications to the processing of the data stream, and produce customer reports. For instance, at each decision point in the data stream, DI analysts are in contact with systems programmers who set up the “runs” for each set of data.

The analyst is responsible for informing systems programmers about which process or program needs to be run or to identify the specifications for an ad hoc program written to introduce a specific correction to the data. Each analyst has to know what the raw data ought to look like, which process the data must undergo, and what specifications are required to produce the desired outcome.

The DI and Systems divisions work together whenever any modification or new program needs to be introduced that will impact the data stream. A Program Request Order (PRO) is used to initiate changes to a program or process within MRI. The PRO is filled out from the perspective of the analyst as an end user of the systems change. The specifications or end results required by the analyst are identified on the PRO. Analysts need to be very precise when identifying specifications. Once the programming process begins, it is very difficult and expensive to change or modify the PRO. After a PRO is filled out, a series of meetings occur between DI analysts and Systems programmers to ensure each side knows exactly what the purpose of the software program modification is and what it should look like. The user and programmer go over the program specifications together. Both analysts and programmers have to “sign off” on the PRO when they agree with the desired specifications. According to the PTE manager, the signing off procedure accomplishes two functions. First, it provides a “change history” by identifying who initiated and designed the change. Second, it eliminates the pointing of fingers which has followed past projects where an analyst states that the programmer didn’t produce what was desired or vice versa.

Weekly process meetings are held between managers of DI and Systems divisions. Their objectives are to review each PRO as to prioritize the project, identify the resources needed to complete each project, and evaluate the ongoing status of each project or request. An estimate is made on each PRO during the weekly priority meeting to determine where the project fits into the project queue, resources needed, etc. This determines when the PRO can be actively worked on. All changes made to software programs or methodologies used within MRI are tracked via the PRO system. Similar to the CSRs, slowness in the rate of change is a major problem. PROs can have a long shelf life before receiving attention. As one analyst commented, “I’ve seen [PROs] sitting out there for months, I saw requests sitting out there from ’93 and ’94. So I mean they can sit there.”

In summary, a transformational learning loop was initiated by MRI’s management to implement the vision of moving “out of the box.” This loop was temporary and sought to speed up the process of identifying significant changes to the existing way of doing work. By contrast, the supportive learning loops at MRI are relatively permanent, formal, elaborate in nature, reactive to customer requests for change, and emphasize obtaining many approvals (in the interest of data integrity) which serve to slow down the process of making changes. The inherent tension between the two modes, one top-down and the other arising from the situated context, was a key factor in influencing the ultimate outcomes.

## Outcomes of the Planned Transformation Effort

The initial results that emerged from the planned transformation effort, as implemented through the transformational learning loop described above, were positive. Both the core and sub teams were successful at generating tangible results in a very short time. Literally hundreds of suggestions for improving processes were generated by the sub teams in less than two months. The PTE manager pointed out how impressed senior executives initially were with the progress of the planned transformation effort as evidenced by the number of suggestions for improvements. Management also discovered that the notion of cross-functional organizing (or drawing upon expertise regardless of where it lies in the organization) which occurred in the formation of the core and sub teams was an effective way to work, especially when tasks or resources had to be allocated. Meetings that included people from different divisions within MRI proved to be more productive than separate meetings along divisional lines. Cross-functional organization ensured that multiple perspectives and experiences were available to the decision-makers as needed.

The initial success of cross-functional organization during the planned transformation effort led to its diffusion into other MRI processes. Strategic planning sessions at the executive level began to incorporate the idea of including experts from different units and divisions. The emergence of such new ways of performing work may be considered demonstrative of thinking that has moved out of institutionally embedded ways. However, support for such “out of the box” movement would be significant if the transformational learning loop could be shown to have been effective. This would have required the rapid generation and implementation of innovative new ideas and solutions for processing the data stream, which was the stated goal of the planned transformation effort. We believe that this did not happen for several reasons.

“Low-Hanging Fruit” and “Leveling the Playing Field.” In spite of a transformational learning loop that appeared to have the right elements in place (e.g., crossfunctional teams, mission emphasizing “out of the box” thinking, senior management support), our findings suggest that the outcomes belied the initial expectations. Of the 52 items selected for implementation as a result of the transformational learning loop, there was general agreement within MRI that less than four could be considered truly innovative. Both the PTE manager and the director of the DI division expressed surprise at the extent to which old changes seemed to dominate the planned transformation effort. For example, the PTE manager noted that only three projects out of the 52 changes were considered as being “new ideas.” She went on to explain how “in one sense the redesign effort codified the efforts of people who already knew what needs to be changed.” Comments made by two former core team members, from Systems and DI respectively, bear this out:

What came out of the sub teams was pages and pages of projects that needed to be done, we need this fixed, we need to do that. I didn’t see anything innovative, it was rather this is what we need to do to the system to get it to work. It didn’t cover any sort of redesign. Anything that we were doing were things that we had already called out.

We determined that the top twenty were the things we were already working on, and so that was kind of the end of it. It proved to me that these were things we already knew to exist.

In part, the lack of new ideas can be explained by some important decisions made by the PTE manager early on in the transformational learning process. During the beginning of the process, the PTE manager spent time interviewing DI members to identify past problems that needed to be addressed. Quick results, symbolized by the PTE manager as “low-hanging fruit” or changes that could be rapidly accomplished, were targeted for immediate identification. The objective was to increase buy-in on the part of team members during the process of moving “out of the box.” As the PTE manager stated, “Why should people become involved and supportive of new change when old changes have yet to be dealt with?” This early phase of the process was metaphorically described by the PTE manager as “leveling the playing field,” that is, making sure that changes that had been identified prior to the effort, but were not implemented, would now get implemented. The large number of such pent-up change suggestions resulted in rapid progress by the sub teams in suggesting ideas during the early stages of the planned transformation.

Linked Learning Loops. The implementation of the suggestions of the sub teams was slowed down because it had to pass through the same Program Request Order (PRO) process already in place at MRI. This represented a coupling of the outputs of the transformational learning loop with the inputs to the supportive learning loops at MRI. Although the PROs associated with the transformational process were expected to be given priority treatment, there were two problems in such coupling.

First, the requirements of the PRO process presented obstacles to rapid initiation of change within MRI. As described earlier, the PRO has to be written from the user’s perspective and outlines both the requirements and specifications of the desired modifications. The analysts need to have both a clear understanding of the change requirements and how the data stream would be impacted if the suggestions were implemented. However, this process of PRO preparation is delayed because there are only a few members of the DI division who have developed the expertise to write up these requirements. This task required considerable expertise and organizational experience as important contextual details such as “why scheduling a certain process at a specific time was important” or “why the particular set of procedures was preferred over simpler alternatives” were often omitted from the change requests and therefore needed to be inferred by the DI division. Second, the user requirements have to be verified by DI division managers before sending the PRO to the Systems division for programming. However, not all managers within the DI division have sufficient contextual knowledge required for accurate verification of user requirements described in the PRO. Overall, this intermediate step not only introduced delays, but led to a significant loss of momentum in the change effort.<sup>5</sup>

Challenging the “Ask Why” Expectation. A critical event occurred in January 1995 after the sub teams had completed their work and the change suggestions had been submitted for implementation. An analyst running data through the initial matching process noticed that extraneous data had been included for the particular application. A quick change to the program by the analyst prevented this data from entering the data stream. However, another analyst within the DI division noticed that the program being executed was not taking into account the extraneous data and altered the program to include this data in the analysis. A client who received the final report observed that the actual values for some important fields appeared to be different from what was expected and brought this discrepancy to the attention of MRI. Two significant results came out of this event. First, much effort and resources of the organization were expended to identify and correct the problem and to interact with a very dissatisfied customer. Second, people at MRI began to question the ability of DI to accomplish its job. The director of the DI division responded to this criticism by ordering an internal evaluation of how DI staff were performing their jobs. This process evaluation was conducted by the PTE manager with assistance from a director in the Sales division.

The findings from the process evaluation came as a significant surprise to DI management and directly challenged managerial notions that “ask why” thinking would now be occurring at the most basic levels of the DI division. In narrating this process evaluation, the PTE manager expressed intense frustration at the lack of a sense of inquiry on the part of DI staff as they performed their jobs. The PTE manager said, “People in DI seem to be doing things just for the sake of doing” and “There is no why on your mind.” When pressed for examples, the manager said the responses by some people to “why” type questions about basic processes (e.g., “why are you performing this step?”) were either “I don’t know” or “We just do this.” One analyst queried by the PTE manager about the store matching process did not know what the values meant in some important data fields. These findings caused the PTE manager to remark that things were so bad she was afraid to tell [the head of the DI division] the truth.

In summary, some immediate reasons for why the planned transformation effort did not meet its goals of rapidly generating innovative ideas and moving the organization “out of the box” are (a) the initial emphasis on “low hanging fruit” and “leveling the playing field,” (b) the coupling together of the outputs of the transformational learning process with the inputs of the more permanent and slower supportive learning process, and (c) the continued lack of an “ask why” thinking among the analysts at MRI. However, these reasons do not explain why innovative ideas were not generated later on in the process, nor why the skills needed for preparing and evaluating PROs were in short supply, nor why there was the perception of the lack of an “ask why” orientation by the DI division’s analysts to their daily work. For an answer to these deeper, more underlying issues, we focus particularly on the emergent manner in which learning was taking place among organizational members in the DI division as they went about performing their day-to-day jobs.

## The Role of Tacit Knowledge: The Situated Learning Perspective

Our findings suggest that the knowledge most critical to daily functioning at DI is primarily contextual and knowledge is held informally in the group. This is knowledge that is gained through experience and interactions with both processes and individuals. The recognition of this nature of the knowledge is evident in the emphasis placed within the division on “learning by doing,” where newcomers to the organization are expected to gain much of their knowledge and skills through a hands-on approach. One supervisor explained, “I’ll get things handed to me and they would say, ‘Do it’ and I would say ‘Okay, how am I supposed to do it.’‘Well, why don’t you dig for it, you’ll learn better that way.’” When asked how much of the knowledge within DI had to be acquired through this approach, this same supervisor replied, “Ninety percent.”

“New-Timers” vs. “Old-Timers.” Two symbolic roles, new-timers and old-timers, have emerged as part of the informal culture within DI to distinguish between employees with different levels of expertise. New-timers within DI are people who do not possess sufficient knowledge to do their job. One can become a new-timer by being a recent hire or through transferring into the new position within DI. Newtimers at DI were expected to obtain the knowledge to do their jobs through selflearning, which, at least initially, involved accessing the knowledge of old-timers within the organization. This environment created a functional dependency upon oldtimers, which was frequently alluded to by individuals within the DI division. To describe his attempts at engaging a new process one individual said, “I’m learning from other folks what the best way might be to do it.”

Old-timers are people who have been with the organization long enough to know their jobs and to possess business knowledge about the customer environment serviced by MRI products. It can take up to three years to gain the knowledge base necessary to be considered an old-timer. People with a long employment history at MRI are particularly valued as they have historical knowledge. This knowledge includes personal experiences of what has worked or failed to work in the past and knowledge about who has worked on specific projects and would thus be likely to know what was needed. Both managers/supervisors and analysts orient toward these old-timers as knowledge resources. One unit manager in the DI division pointed out, “I don’t know that there’s any kind of formal recognition, you just end up knowing who are the people who have this expertise.” At the same time there is an informal expectation that old-timers will play an active part in this informal learning environment by making their expertise available. “People who do have a wide breadth of understanding will shift to wherever they’re needed.” This expertise is often used during the initial training of new hires within DI.

“Sink or Swim.” Newly hired people undergo approximately two weeks of formal training. They then work alongside the previous holder of the position for approximately two to three months in an informal apprenticeship arrangement. In this fashion, contextual knowledge is externalized or made explicit, through shared experiences between the new and old timers [39]. There are some individuals within MRI who have thrived in this informal “learning by doing” culture. One analyst asserted, “I took it upon myself to learn how to do some programming. I had zero programming skills when I started but now I guess out of three of us I’m the person that some people come to if they had a program that they needed help with.” At the same time, this “learning by doing” culture is also metaphorically described as a “sink or swim” culture by organizational members at MRI. Another analyst said, “I felt like I was learning it all by myself. I was being thrown into the pool and I was learning how to swim.”

While formalized knowledge such as standard operating procedures (SOPs), provide organization-wide explanations of what should be done and the conditions under which something should be done, their availability within MRI is limited. A new-timer trying to learn a position said, “you find this out just by talking to people because, obviously, I haven’t been in this job long enough to know the history of this position. Nobody writes these kinds of things down.” When asked about written knowledge, one supervisor pointed out, “Here those [SOPs] are pretty much nonexistent. There’s literally no documentation. We can’t get answers.” An analyst, when asked about a specific process, asserted, “That’s a big part of our business and it’s something that we don’t really have formal procedures on.” As stated earlier, having individuals sign off on change documents serves to formally create a history of change within MRI. However, a large component of the contextual history exists in the minds of individuals who were involved in the creation of the process rather than in formal, written documents. Details such as “why a procedure is required” and “why a certain process has to be reused as-is and not changed and how it fits within the larger organizational processes” are often not included in the documentation. Newcomers to MRI consequently found little in the way of explicit documentation or formal knowledge to make sense of processes and achieve a larger perspective of the process required to make sense of individual subprocesses. These shortcomings served to reinforce the “sink or swim” metaphor prevalent within MRI and, overall, affected the knowledge that could be brought to bear on management of the data stream.

“Buffalo Chasing.” The dependency upon old-timers for informal knowledge and the lack of procedural documentation create specific costs for both new-timers and old-timers. The first problem is what we metaphorically describe as “buffalo chasing.” Buffalo chasing occurs when new-timers have to identify and chase down the old-timers with the knowledge and savvy to solve a problem or answer a question. But buffalo chasing is time intensive and no guarantee exists that a solution for the problem can be identified. Acquiring tacit knowledge, or externalizing it, is difficult and frequently does not lead to any learning. The costs of buffalo chasing transfer as well to the individual possessing tacit knowledge. Old-timers have to take time away from their tasks to help out new-timers. One old-timer remarked, “When there’s a question or somebody needs something pulled, I’m the lucky one.” Another particularly senior old-timer responded, “I basically answer questions of the entire staff if they have questions about a process. I spend a great deal of time answering questions.” The pressures of having to accomplish jobs that are time-sensitive may lead to the old-timer simply doing the task to get it done efficiently. Consider this from one new-timer’s perspective:

We’re supposed to run around and find people to help us and if they’re busy we take away from their work or we have to wait for them to help us and who is to say that they train people well? They might not be able to explain what they are doing. They know they can do it faster than it takes to explain to me so sometimes they say ‘I’m gonna do this, just let me do this real quick.’They’ve helped you and you have stamped out that fire but I haven’t learned anything yet at that point.

Effectively, the old-timers were serving as important components of the institutional memory. Deploying this memory therefore required significant individual effort on the part of these experienced employees. When task execution was transferred to the oldtimer in the interest of efficiency, it prevented the newer employee from being exposed to the organizational logic and acquiring the knowledge to perform the task independently on subsequent occasions. This predictably constrained the development of “ask-why” as efforts were directed at achieving an immediate problem solution rather than acquiring the knowledge required for problem solving. The reliance on this mechanism of locating a “buffalo” and holding their attention amidst the stream of tasks they were involved in also adversely influenced the relative emphasis placed on proactive vs. reactive action. Proactive actions lacked both the urgency and specificity characterizing reactive customer requests—the latter were viewed as “time bombs ticking away.” The likelihood of attracting the attention of old-timers for reactive problem solving was thus greater than the ability to attract attention and get help to act proactively, a factor constraining the realization of the top-down expectation of the proactive action.

“Earthquakes and Aftershocks.” The efforts at organizational learning were also impacted by dramatic and unexpected changes within the organizational environment. At MRI, the biggest organizational impact was a company wide layoff that occurred in late 1994 between the core team and the sub teams’ meetings. Employees metaphorically described the layoffs as “earthquakes and aftershocks.” As one individual commented, “Yeah, but the biggest change was just morale-wise, how people viewed their jobs and stuff after the layoffs. It’s almost like an earthquake. You’re going to have aftershocks for a while.”

The layoff and ensuing aftershocks had direct and immediate impact upon the organization. The most visible and salient impact was the loss of old-timer knowledge that followed the layoff. “The fallout from that [layoff] has been some of the key and particularly marketable individuals have left the company and never came back.” The aftershocks of such layoffs also affected the knowledge base of the organization. “We have lost a lot of employees in the time since the layoff and now we have allowed a lot of industry knowledge if you will to leave.” Coupled with the lack of extant knowledge codified in formal form, these departures represented a significant loss of organizational knowledge that made it very difficult to move “out of the box.”

“Learning Minefields.” While the obvious problem for the organization from the layoffs was the direct loss of old-timer knowledge, a more subtle problem metaphorically described by “learning minefields” evolved out of both this loss of knowledge and the overall learning climate at MRI. Learning minefields evolved out of the following two conditions. The first condition is the lack of knowledge at MRI regarding how to perform a task, which results in employees moving forward with minimal guidance. As described earlier, this lack of knowledge is caused by the newness of many employees, weak formalized knowledge structures such as SOPs, and difficulty in accessing organizational knowledge either through ineffective buffalo chasing or the loss of old-timers following the layoff. However, in spite of these difficulties in acquiring requisite knowledge, MRI is also characterized by a second condition: people are held accountable for errors and mistakes in performance. Consequently, the individual is placed in a double bind [58]: Mistakes could be made due to lack of knowledge for which the individual is held accountable, but the generation of knowledge requires the individual to take some action. The existence of such a double bind was commented upon by a number of MRI personnel. “We have really walked through a minefield every week and we’re always making different mistakes. It’s not like we keep making the same ones.” A supervisor shared the following observations:

We can’t get answers. And, so, if something goes wrong, you’re held accountable . . . you weren’t even aware that you were doing anything wrong. And that’s really a tough spot to put people in. If you make the error [even though] you’ve been told and you should have known better, you’re still going to make the error, maybe, every now and then. But if you unknowingly make an error, based on no awareness, no knowledge, that’s a whole different thing.

Overall, an examination of the situated knowledge web suggests the importance of the following metaphors or symbols: “old-timers” and “new-timers,” “sink or swim,” “buffalo chasing,” “earthquakes and aftershocks,” and “learning minefields.” These metaphors or symbols emphasize the importance of contextual, tacit knowledge acquired through experience in performing work effectively. Because such knowledge is scarce and difficult to externalize, the day-to-day realities of organizational life at MRI’s DI division is characterized by new-timers’ preoccupation with learning the basic skills needed to perform their jobs and their search for old-timers who can help in performing them. This pursuit is adversely affected by the difficulty in transmitting tacit knowledge, the prevailing atmosphere of “sink or swim,” layoffs, and “earthquakes” that reduce knowledge sources, and a perception of “learning minefields” where mistakes have negative repercussions. The non-canonical role of old-timers emerged as an extremely important element in competent process execution in MRI. Not only are they central to answering questions and vital components of institutional memory, they are also important brokers of “who knows what.” Old-timers were often in a position to not only direct individuals needing help to specific people who would be able to help, but also to legitimize the search for a solution by the newtimers, an important aspect in receiving assistance.

In general, these suggest that the expectations of the top-down initiative in being proactive and encouraging “ask-why” thinking were at odds with the realities and constraints posed by the situated knowledge web. The goals of the top-down effort were consequently undermined and the outcomes reflect the consequences of the lack of cognizance of the features of the situated knowledge web.

## Discussion

THE EXPERIENCES OF MRI in undertaking organizational changes to impact knowledge intensive processes provide a rich illustration of the issues involved in implementing purposive changes to organizational activities that impact the knowledge web, that is, the tensions between purposive vs. emergent learning, transformational vs. supportive processes for learning, and explicit vs. contextual and tacit knowledge. In particular, these findings suggest that efforts directed at changing organizational processes or activity systems involve the attention to and resolving of the conflicting implications of contrasting themes within each issue. Moreover, failures at implementing such changes may be linked to failures in understanding and addressing the tensions between these contrasts.

The senior management at MRI purposively sought to move MRI “out of the box” and identify innovative solutions to its problems, with its attendant emphasis on being proactive rather than reactive, undertaking “ask why” thinking, and valuing business over technical knowledge. As our observations suggest, these goals were insufficiently sensitive to the emergent nature of the ongoing process in the organization that is revealed in an examination using the situated knowledge perspective, that is, “learning by doing” based on knowledge acquired by individuals through day-today interactions with experts who had very little time to devote to this task.

The project to transform the nature of action within DI was intended to identify and implement innovative solutions to MRI’s problems. By contrast, the proposed solutions, rather than being innovative and radical as intended, reinforced the existing setup in at least two ways, that is, they suggested improvements to the existing system in order to make it work more smoothly, and they codified ideas that were already being worked upon. These outcomes can be symbolized as “leveling the playing field” and resulted from the members’ own situated understanding of the day-to-day problems with existing computer systems. Consequently, the findings suggest that attempts at transformational (i.e., double-loop or generative) learning resulted, in practice, in supportive (i.e., single-loop or adaptive) learning.

As illustration of the issues associated with transforming the basic form of knowledge, MRI managers sought to make knowledge formal and explicit, such as, in the form of standard operating procedures, business process models, and written requests for clarification (e.g., CSRs) or computer system changes (e.g., IPOs). By contrast, the situated learning process suggested that critical knowledge needed to undertake everyday actions was primarily contextual and tacit and difficult to codify. Moreover, such knowledge was possessed by “old timers” who had been with MRI for some years and learned through experience. The process of knowledge acquisition therefore consisted primarily of asking “old timers” for assistance, which we metaphorically describe as “buffalo chasing.” This process was fraught with all the problems associated with knowledge acquisition and deployment at DI, as described earlier. The non-canonical and unrecognized roles played by the old-timers was revealed as vital—as repositories of institutional memory and contextual knowledge absent in documentation and as vital knowledge brokers directing and legitimizing the search for solutions in the social network of the firm.

An additional issue (with its associated contrasts) also helps in understanding why the effort was unsuccessful at MRI: the structures deployed for generating new knowledge vs. those in place for their application. As described previously, changes in knowledge and organizational action were primarily sought through a temporary edifice of structures and processes—the redesign effort—whose primary objective was to generate innovative solutions that would lead MRI “out of the box.” Paradoxically, not only were the actual solutions less than innovative but the implementation of these solutions was funneled through the existing edifice for implementing changes, that is, the PRO process. In essence, temporary mechanisms for knowledge generation (even if transformational in intent) were coupled with extant, more permanent mechanisms for knowledge implementation that reinforced the status quo. Moreover, the implementation edifice was itself subject to problems such as inadequate expertise in the DI division for specifying and verifying PRO requirements, which was a result of the everyday learning realities described earlier. An additional factor exacerbating this issue was the loss of context specific knowledge such as why a particular set of processes was superior to other alternatives, which was not part of the documentation. Consequently, the knowledge implementation processes dampened the speed with which ideas (even old ones) that participants could be expected to be familiar with were carried forward. These findings illustrate the importance of distinguishing between knowledge creation and its application, and of emphasizing the latter along with the former [47]. Overall, our findings provide a perspective on why the expected changes in the activity system did not take place as expected at MRI.

The priorities of the organization changed unexpectedly with the CEO resigning in February 1995, eleven months after the change initiative was set in motion. The new CEO, hired from outside, initiated a new set of priorities. The planned change effort was abandoned with the assignment of the PTE manager to a different unit and a different set of responsibilities. There was no formal winding down of the effort or any attempt at a formal evaluation as the focus of managerial attention changed to the new priorities.

The situated change perspective suggests that organizational transformation can be understood in terms of the “situated practices of organizational actors as they improvise, innovate, and adjust their work routines over time” [41]. Our observations suggest that MRI’s lack of success in transforming its organizational base of knowledge can be understood in terms of the ongoing actions of individuals as they sought to acquire the knowledge necessary to perform their day-to-day tasks. These microlevel actions are a key feature of the situated change perspective and therefore suggest a bridge between individual learning and organizational learning efforts at MRI. These actions (and associated apprehensions) were characterized by metaphors and symbols such as “sink or swim,” “buffalo chasing,” “earthquakes and aftershocks,” and “learning minefields.”

A focus on the situated knowledge web directs attention to the everyday efforts at knowledge acquisition and deployment that organizational actors undertake to fulfill their tasks. Our research suggests that the following aspects of a situated knowledge web will be useful to managers when initiating change efforts such as knowledge management programs that impact activity systems in their organizations:

1. Conceptualization: The situated knowledge web can be viewed as a network of people, knowledge resources, and techniques (formal and informal) for acquiring knowledge.

2. Role of infrastructure: Organizational context is inseparable from the knowledge context with which it forms a social system. For example, organizational procedures such as CSRs and PROs at MRI were an integral part of the social system comprising the knowledge web. The services obtained from the web depended on many human actions and judgments carried out within this social setting, such as buffalo chasing and navigating learning minefields.

3. Limited control over web: There is limited administrative and political control over the web and its infrastructure on account of the dispersion of knowledge sources. In the case of MRI, such knowledge was dispersed among “old-timers” and customers and the planned transformation effort illustrated management’s difficulty in exerting managing the change, such as, in promoting “ask why” thinking among the analysts.

4. Limited value of formal aspects: The formal features of the knowledge web, such as the formal learning loops and the explicit knowledge contained in it, are only fair-to-poor in highlighting the web’s capabilities and how it can be used. At MRI, much of the critical knowledge was context-dependent and the approaches used by organizational members to acquire this knowledge were primarily informal.

The situated knowledge web described above illustrates perhaps the key lesson that emerges from MRI’s experience: While the planned attempt at thinking and moving “out of the box” concentrated on specific problems, such as how to be more proactive with MRI’s customers, it did not focus on understanding the character and the fragile interconnectedness of resources underlying the knowledge required to execute the processes. Consequently, the top-down vision of thinking and moving “out of the box” was dissipated by the everyday situation confronting organizational members as they traversed the web in search of knowledge to perform their tasks. To use an ecological metaphor, the findings illustrate the need, when attempting organizational changes such as knowledge management initiatives, to focus on “managing the habitat, rather than the species.”

The results of our study need to be interpreted in the light of its limitations. Field research requires the establishment of relationships with key insiders and the maintenance of these relationships to obtain access to people and data from the organization that can influence the findings in subtle ways [1]. In our case, we built an extremely supportive relationship with the manager charged with the transformation effort who helped us immensely during the two-year period of data collection and initial analysis. While we employed a variety of mechanisms such as persistent observation, triangulation, and member checking to minimize biases, our observations and our analysis may be colored in ways that we could not control. In addition, relying on this deep informant who was heavily vested in the change and gave us a view of the change process from the perspective of the manager responsible for the change may have biased our understanding of the effort and consequently, our analysis. Further, as is the case with qualitative studies, the detailed focus on the particular context and the processes limits the generalizability of our results.

## Theoretical Contribution

Our main contribution in this research is to articulate one view of how changes to activity systems unfold as a result of initiatives at transforming organizational learning and knowledge. We contrast planned top-down efforts at change with the situated individual efforts to acquire and deploy the knowledge needed to perform day-to-day tasks. In developing this perspective, we used metaphors and symbols to reveal some of the underlying representations that guided the activities of organizational actors in IT-based knowledge firms. These included “out of the box,” “low-hanging fruit,” “leveling the playing field,” “sink or swim,” “old-timers vs. new-timers,” “buffalo chasing,” “earthquakes and aftershocks,” and “learning minefields.” Our observations suggest that the edifices in place for knowledge application (rather than just knowledge creation) also have an important bearing on the outcomes. We suggest that the overarching symbol of a situated knowledge web is useful to understand the outcomes when firms attempt organization changes in learning and action that are central to knowledge management initiatives. Figure 4 illustrates these concepts and can serve as a conceptual framework for future research.

The results of our study suggest that the situated knowledge web is a useful device to examine change efforts in organizations. Further qualitative examinations of different facets of the tensions between top-down efforts and the features of the situated knowledge web are likely to yield useful insights both for research and practice. The examination of contexts where the expertise for task performance is embodied in individuals and efforts to systematize and manage expertise deployment (such as customer service and delivery of financial services [8, 36]), are underway and would highlight the factors important in enabling these transitions. In addition, the qualitative examination of efforts at unlearning by organizations [48] where specific elements of standard procedures are purposefully discarded and of learning in interorganizationalsettings [31] where multiple knowledge webs are involved would reveal important nuances related to the ongoing transformation of knowledge resources in organizations. Cumulative theory building efforts in these areas would provide a clearer understanding of the cycle of expertise creation, expertise deployment, and expertise institutionalization in organizations that are central to the generative dance [5] between knowledge, knowing, and organizational action.<sup>6</sup>

## Appendix 1: Details of Processes Performed by DI Division

Data Input: Technicians in DI receive the data, sort it, log it in, and start the initial processing on the MRI mainframe computer. This is accomplished by either a technician actually uploading the data or by forwarding the tape to the Systems division to be uploaded. A backup tape is made after the data has been uploaded into the MRI mainframe. At this point, the technician turns over the process to the analysts within the DI division.

Data Cleanup: Before the data is processed, analysts examine the data files to clean up the data. For instance, a common problem is that pharmacies often send MRI data on a variety of products that are not drugs, such as Q-tips<sup>®</sup> or sanitary napkins. A variety of similar problems occurs for data sent in by wholesalers. Analysts comb through the data to eliminate such records and clean up the data sets for further processing.

Error Checking: An analyst begins the processing by running standard verification programs that are applied to the data set. These programs check the information fields of the data set against the corporate databases for accuracy and completeness. There are three important steps in error checking:

a. Product Match: In this step, the product identification number for each product purchased by the customer is matched with the products carried by the retailer who provided the information to MRI. This latter information is stored in control files in the corporate database. Records without product matches are pulled out of the data stream and reported by the software program. The analyst in charge of “product match” is faced with the first decision: modify the data records in order to “pull in as much data as possible” into the reports to corporate customers or pass the error records to other analysts who diagnose the problem and initiate computer programs to make necessary corrections to the control files. Choosing the first option requires halting the data stream while changes are made to the data records, although it eventually results in a richer, more informative data stream for that particular run. Choosing the second option results in less data being used for analysis, but also ensures that the data flow is not interrupted and that the root causes of product mismatch problems are identified. Choice of either option is dependent upon factors such as the number of data records that have errors and whether the process needs to be completed by a specified time.

b. Manufacturer Match: In this step, manufacturer-related aspects of the incoming data records, such as manufacturer ID and manufacturer suggested retail prices (MSRP), are checked against the control files for the manufacturers. Any record that doesn’t have a clear match is tagged for later action. These mismatched records are passed on to the analysts responsible for correcting manufacturer-related problems. Manufacturer-related mismatches are typically difficult to diagnose because of the considerable variations that can be found around product prices. The analysts in charge of diagnosing such problems subsequently initiate programs to modify the control files for the manufacturers to include these prices for future processing.

c. Store Match: This third step of processing relates to matching the store-related information in the data records with control file information on the retail stores that provide information to MRI. A discrepancy in point of sale matching can occur because of a change in the store number (a common occurrence when one retailer takes over another retailer) or the store number was inaccurately recorded at the point of sale location. The analyst responsible for store identification has to decide whether to halt the data stream until the errors in store identification can be reconciled or to continue processing the data without the mismatched records. Reconciliation requires hand keying the information and is time consuming. Moreover, if reconciliation is chosen, the processing cycle has to be repeated with the corrected information. Incoming mismatched data records that are not reconciled during that particular processing cycle are sent to analysts responsible for diagnosing and fixing store-related errors. These analysts either correct the data record or initiate programs to update the control files with the new information on retail stores where the products were sold.

Formatting Enhancements: Once the above three steps of error identification and fixing are completed, formatting enhancements are executed. During this process, the data is partitioned into different formats corresponding to the information reports that are generated by MRI for its customers.

Two of these reports are particularly important: the weekly summary report prepared at the end of each week and the monthly summary report, which summarizes the sales during the month. This latter takes two weeks to prepare and is delivered around the middle of the following month. These reports form the basis for more detailed analyses performed for MRI’s customers.
