# Information Goods and Vertical Differentiation

HEMANT K. BHARGAVA AND VIDYANAND CHOUDHARY

HEMANT K. BHARGAVA is on the faculty of The Pennsylvania State University’s Smeal College of Business Administration as professor of management science and information systems. Prior to that, Dr. Bhargava was a visiting professor in The Heinz School of Public Policy and Management at Carnegie Mellon University and served on the faculty of the Naval Postgraduate School in Monterey since 1990. He serves on the board of directors of the INFORMS Computing Society and is an area editor (covering computing and decision technology) for Operations Research, and an associate editor for Decision Support Systems. Dr. Bhargava has a Ph.D. in decision sciences from The Wharton School of Business, University of Pennsylvania. He has also received degrees in mathematics at Hindu College, University of Delhi, and in management at the Indian Institute of Management, Bangalore.

VIDYANAND CHOUDHARY is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at GSIA, Carnegie Mellon University. ABSTRACT: Second-degree price discrimination, that is, vertical differentiation, is widely practiced by firms selling physical goods to consumers with heterogeneous valuations. This strategy leads to market segmentation and has been shown to be optimal by many researchers. On the other hand, researchers have also demonstrated, under certain restrictive conditions, that vertical differentiation may not be optimal for information goods. We analyze vertical differentiation for a monopolist, continuing the practice of modeling consumer valuation as a linear function of product quality and consumer type but generalizing assumptions about marginal costs and consumer distributions. We show that the firm’s optimal product line depends on the benefit-to-cost ratio of qualities in the choice vector. We find that a vertical differentiation strategy is not optimal when the highest quality product has the best benefitto-cost ratio. Many information goods satisfy this property.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information goods, market segmentation, multiproduct monopoly, price discrimination, versioning, vertical differentiation.

RESEARCH IN PRICING AND ECONOMIC THEORY has shown the effectiveness of price discrimination and product differentiation strategies when a firm serves a market of heterogeneous consumers [10]. Firms can generate greater profits by segmenting consumers, exploiting differences in their willingness to pay for a product. If the firm can observe valuations of individual consumers and isolate them, it can charge each consumer a different price for the same product equal to her valuation for the product. This strategy is called first-degree price discrimination. When the firm can observe a signal about aggregate demand of certain segments of consumers, then it may be able to practice third-degree price discrimination by charging different prices to different segments.

This paper is concerned with second-degree price discrimination, which is a useful pricing strategy when a firm knows only the aggregate distribution of consumer valuations and not the valuations of individual consumers or of consumer segments. In this case, the firm creates multiple versions of the product with different quality levels and offers these at different prices. When all consumers agree on the rank order of the different versions, these qualities are said to be vertically differentiated. Rational consumers will buy the version that maximizes their consumer surplus. This separates the consumers into segments with different segments buying different versions and paying different prices. This pricing strategy, based on vertical differentiation, is called second-degree price discrimination [10].

Vertical differentiation is commonly observed in the real world. Firms offer television sets with different screen sizes, washers and dryers with varying capacity, and airline tickets based on class of service. Consumers buy different versions based on their utility functions, with consumers who have greater valuations choosing higher priced products. Consider a simple numerical example. A firm sells a basic massproduced bicycle and a high quality lightweight bicycle. The marginal cost of production for the basic model is \$200 per unit and for the high quality version is \$590 per unit. There are two consumers. A wealthy consumer, A, is willing to pay \$1,000 for the high quality bicycle and \$500 for the basic model, while another consumer, B, is willing to pay \$800 for the high quality model and \$400 for the basic model. The firm can maximize profits by using second-degree price discrimination, pricing the basic model at \$399 and the high quality bicycle at \$898. Consumer A purchases the high quality bicycle earning a surplus of \$102 while consumer B buys the basic model with a surplus of \$1. The firm earns a profit of \$507. It can be verified that the firm would earn lower profits if it sold the bicycle at only one quality level, either the basic or the high quality bicycle.

This example also illustrates a basic insight into why price discrimination may or may not be profitable for a firm. Suppose, in the example above, that the bicycle manu facturer had only the high quality version. It would then price this product at \$799, selling to both consumers for a total revenue of \$1,598, resulting in a profit of \$418. Comparing this with the case where the firm sells both qualities, we see that the addition of the low-quality basic bicycle caused the low-type consumer B to switch from the high-quality bicycle to the basic bicycle. This, in turn, caused a reduction in the firm’s total revenues to \$1,297. Yet, the firm’s profits increased, because it’s marginal costs fell from \$1,180 to \$790. In general, when the net effect of the reduction in marginal cost and revenues is positive, the firm finds it advantageous to price discriminate.

This paper focuses on the application of second-degree price discrimination in the context of information goods. We define information goods to be products that can be digitized and for which the primary source of value to consumers is the digitizable portion of the product, rather than the medium on which the information may be presented. For example, we consider a book to be an information good when consumers value the information content of the book. However, if the book is an ancient manuscript, such as the recently discovered original Greek copy of Archimedes’s Theory of Flotation or his Method of Mechanical Theorems, the balance of value shifts to the actual medium, here the paper that constitutes the manuscript. Therefore, according to our definition, such a manuscript is not an information good. One of the characteristics exhibited by information goods, as defined by us, is that the marginal costs for such goods are likely to be a concave function of quality. The costs of duplicating a higher-quality information good are not likely to be much higher than that for a lower-quality product. We state, specifically, that the cost-quality ratio is likely to decline with increasing quality.

We develop analytical models of consumer choice under vertical differentiation to show how a monopolist endowed with versions of a product with different quality levels should choose the quality levels to offer to consumers whose valuations are linear in quality. We show that the firm’s decision is based entirely on the cost-quality ratios of the available quality levels and is independent of the nature of consumer distribution. We also find that if the highest quality product has the lowest cost-quality ratio then the firm would not use vertical differentiation, offering consumers only one level of quality—the highest quality product.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews prior literature and results in price discrimination. Then we present a simplified version of the problem where a monopolist has just two product qualities to offer to consumers whose valuations are drawn from only two possible values. In this simple example we assume that the product has negligible marginal costs. In the following section we generalize the problem to the case where the mass of consumers at each valuation point follows a general continuous distribution, and the firm has available product qualities with positive marginal costs. Our results relate the optimality of price discrimination to the nature of the marginal cost function. Finally, we interpret our results and place them in the context of prior findings, and then conclude with ideas for further work.

## Literature Review

MUSSA AND ROSEN [5] WERE THE FIRST TO EXAMINE vertical differentiation in a market with heterogeneous consumers. They model heterogeneity of consumers with a continuous parameter $\theta ,$ and use a linear utility function $( U ( \theta , q ) = \theta \times q )$ , that is, utility is proportional to consumer’s type parameter and the product’s quality level $q .$ In the context that marginal costs increase more rapidly than quality (i.e., the derivatives $c ^ { \prime } \left( q \right)$ and $c ^ { \prime \prime } ( q )$ are both positive, where $c ( q )$ is the marginal cost of producing quality q), Mussa and Rosen conclude that the monopolist would find it optimal to price discriminate by offering multiple qualities.

Moorthy [4] considers the case where the heterogeneity of consumers is modeled with a discrete parameter. Moorthy’s work generalizes the utility function and assumes only that marginal costs increase with quality $\left( \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , c ^ { \prime } \left( q \right) \right.$ is positive), but has a restrictive assumption on the distribution of consumers. Under these conditions, Moorthy shows that the threat of competition “within the firm’s own product line” means that optimal prices of products must be determined simultaneously (rather than independently) and that the firm may reduce the variety of products offered.

Several authors have examined price discrimination for sellers of information goods. Varian [11] and Meyer [3] consider price discrimination for information goods that have zero marginal costs. Their analysis considers that the market has two types of consumers (those with high valuations and those with low valuations) and that consumer valuations are linear in the quality level $( U ( \theta , q ) = \theta \times q )$ . Setting marginal costs to zero, they demonstrate that price discrimination would not be optimal.

Jones and Mendelson [2] also examine price discrimination in the context of a linear-valuation function and zero-marginal costs, but assume a continuous uniform distribution of consumers. They also find that it is not profitable for the monopolist to price discriminate.

In related research, Salant [6] examined the Mussa and Rosen model but with discrete consumer types and determined that if the cost function satisfies certain conditions, it would be suboptimal to employ second-degree price discrimination. He links this work to that of Stokey [9] who analyzed an inter-temporal model of discrimination and found that price discrimination is not optimal when the firm can commit to future prices. Salant also links this work to that of Spence [8], who examined price discrimination through quantity-dependent prices.

This paper generalizes prior work along two directions: we formulate our model with a generalized consumer distribution and an arbitrary cost function.

## Continuous Distribution of Consumers

IN THIS SECTION WE CONSIDER THE CASE where there is a continuous distribution of consumer types and the firm can offer an arbitrary number of quality levels of an information good. Following prior literature (e.g., Mussa and Rosen [5]), we characterize consumers in terms of a type parameter $\theta \in [ \underline { { { \Theta } } } , \overline { { { \Theta } } } ]$ on the real line. We assume that a consumer of type q has utility $U ( \Theta , q ) = \Theta \times q$ for one unit of a product of quality q, and zero for additional units.

## Results

In this section we demonstrate that a firm should sell only one quality $q _ { N } ,$ if and only if $q _ { N }$ has the best cost-quality ratio $( c _ { N } / q _ { N } )$ . All marginal cost functions that are concave in quality satisfy the condition that $q _ { N }$ has the best cost-quality ratio. Since this characteristic applies to our definition of information goods, it follows that price when consumer valuations are linear.

## Discussion

Models of vertical differentiation for a monopolist can differ along three main characteristics: (1) the consumer utility function, (2) the distribution of consumer utilities, and (3) the nature of marginal costs as a function of the quality level. Following earlier work in this area, our formulation assumes a linear utility function. For the consumer distribution, we assume only that the hazard rate function is nondecreasing, which allows for a broad class of continuous consumer distributions. The fourth section demonstrates our results for such distributions, and the third section illustrates the result for discrete consumer distributions. Regarding the nature of marginal costs, while our work is focused on information goods for which marginal costs are concave in quality, we derive our results without making any assumption about marginal costs.

The result of Theorem 1 is illustrated in Figure 3, where the points $( q _ { N } , c _ { N } )$ and $( q _ { 0 } , c _ { 0 } )$ represent, respectively, the highest and lowest quality products. Other quality levels $q _ { j }$ can similarly be represented by a point $( q _ { k } , c _ { k } )$ . Theorem 1 states that vertical differentiation is optimal if and only if there exists a quality $q _ { k }$ such that $( c _ { k } / q _ { k } ) < ( c _ { N } / q _ { N } )$ . How does Theorem 1 relate the marginal cost function to the optimality of price discrimination? Note that the marginal cost function is simply the curve that connects all the points $( q _ { j } , c _ { j } )$ in Figure 3. Any concave cost function implies a quality choice vector such that all quality levels lie in zone B (since $c ^ { \prime \prime } ( q ) < 0$ implies $( c _ { N } / q _ { N } ) \leq$ $( c / q _ { j } ) )$ , hence the firm should offer only $q _ { N } .$ . Any convex function implies that all quality levels lie in zone A, hence price discrimination is optimal. For an arbitrary cost function, price discrimination is optimal if and only if the marginal cost function passes through zone A.

In this paper, we have analyzed second-degree price discrimination incorporating arbitrary marginal costs of production and arbitrary consumer distribution. Theorem 1 states necessary and sufficient conditions for the optimality of price discrimination for a monopolist endowed with quality differentiated versions of a product. However, for many firms the optimal level of quality is also an endogenous decision variable and there are substantial development costs for higher quality. Previous work, including that of Jones and Mendelson [2] and Meyer [3], incorporated fixed development costs and determined the optimal level of quality. These models illustrate that the development costs have no impact on the firm’s decision whether or not to price discriminate and on product pricing. Consider the case of a software vendor that is developing a new version of its product while in possession of older (lower quality) versions. The software vendor must determine the optimal quality level of the new version given that development costs are usually increasing and convex function of quality. If $D ( q _ { N } )$ is the development cost of quality $q _ { N }$ (see Appendix C for details), then the optimal level of quality can be determined by solving $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { D } } ^ { \prime } \left( \boldsymbol { q } _ { N } \right) = ( 1 - F ( \boldsymbol { \Theta } _ { N } ) ) \boldsymbol { \Theta } _ { N }$ jointly with the optimal indifference points stated in Equation (2). Note that

$$
\theta_ {N} = \partial \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \theta_ {i} (q _ {i} - q _ {i - 1})\right),
$$

where

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \theta_ {i} (q _ {i} - q _ {i - 1}) = P _ {N}
$$

(from Equation (10)). Therefore, the optimal quality level $q * N$ is such that the rate of increase in the development costs (with increasing $q _ { N } )$ is equal to the marginal revenue from increasing $q _ { N } .$ A similar condition was derived by Meyer [3] in the context of a product with only two versions of different quality.

The contribution of this paper is to extend our understanding of second-degree price discrimination to a broader set of conditions. Mussa and Rosen [5] established the optimality of price discrimination for traditional goods that exhibit convex marginal costs. Salant [6] demonstrates that price discrimination is suboptimal when the marginal cost function is linear in quality. On the other hand some researchers [2, 3, 11] have focused on goods with zero marginal costs. Many information goods have positive marginal costs which do not fit either of these assumptions. The results of Jones and Mendelson [2] were derived using a uniform consumer distribution and those of Varian [11], Meyer [3], and Salant [6] apply to the case of discrete consumer types. Our results are derived using a general consumer distribution assuming a nondecreasing hazard rate that applies to uniform as well as many other continuous distributions. In addition, we show that prior results derived for information goods with zero marginal costs also hold for information goods that have a concave marginal cost function.

Why is price discrimination optimal for traditional goods but not so for many information goods? Introduction of a low quality product into the market has two effects: it causes some low-value consumers (who would not otherwise have purchased a product) to enter the market, but it also causes some high-value consumers to shift to the low-quality product. The overall effect on revenue is determined by the distribution of consumers and the quality levels. Interestingly, the net effect may be a reduction in revenue. When marginal costs are negligible, the reduction in revenue is equivalent to a reduction in profit. Even when there are positive marginal costs that increase at a slower rate than quality, the cost savings from consumers shifting to the low-quality product are often too small to offset the reduction in revenue. The overall reduction in profit makes price discrimination suboptimal.

## Conclusions

THIS PAPER HAS FOCUSED ON second-degree price discrimination for information goods, defined as products that can be digitized and provide value primarily due to their information content. The marginal cost functions for such goods are typically concave in quality. Extending prior results by generalizing the consumer distribution and marginal cost functions, we conclude that a firm selling information goods will not benefit from inducing consumer self-selection through vertical differentiation. The firm should therefore offer a single quality to all consumers. Examples of this phenomenon include Adobe’s Distiller Server (presently priced at \$5,000) and Acrobat (\$249), Apple’s WebObjects (\$699) and Final Cut video editing program (\$999), and UBI Soft’s Jungle Book gaming software (\$32.99). In each case there is a significant difference between the firm’s price and its marginal cost, raising the possibility that the firm could capture more buyers by offering an additional lower quality version at low price. However, in each case the firm offers only one version suggesting that, were the lower quality version also offered, cannibalization would cut into the firms’ revenues without adequate cost savings.

There are additional factors, not included in our analysis, that may motivate firms to offer information goods at multiple quality levels. For example, factors such as network effects, advertising revenues, nonlinear utility functions, threat of entry, and multi-period market dynamics, may make it advantageous for the firm to sell different versions to different user segments. It would be of interest to extend our modeling framework in order to analyze the implications of all of these factors on pricing strategies, price discrimination, and product differentiation. We are presently pursuing such extensions.
