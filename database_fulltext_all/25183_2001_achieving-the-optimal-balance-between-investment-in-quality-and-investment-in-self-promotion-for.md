# Achieving the Optimal Balance Between Investment in Quality and Investment in Self-Promotion for Information Products

RAVI ARON AND ERIC K. CLEMONS

RAVI ARON is an Assistant Professor of Operations and Information Management at the Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania. His current research investigates the impact of information-based pricing strategies in electronic markets in both B2B and B2C contexts.

For biographical information on ERIC CLEMONS, see the Special Section Introduction.

ABSTRACT: When producers of goods (or services) are confronted by a situation in which their offerings no longer perfectly match consumer preferences, they must determine the extent to which the advertised features of the product reflect the product's actual attributes. We find that the two important determinants of sellers' advertising strategy are the Repeg Cost Ratio, and the Repeat Sales Coefficient. The interplay of these two factors gives rise to four possible strategic scenarios. We show that sellers' strategy is clearly explainable in three out of these four scenarios. In the ambiguous fourth scenario, we show that sellers' strategy for information production goods will differ considerably from information consumption goods based on product complexity and cost of product return (borne by the buyer). Finally, we demonstrate that markets are often characterized by self-reinforcing limits on the extent of opportunistic advertising by sellers.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information products, internet advertising, product positioning, signaling.

PRODUCERS OF ALL GOODS AND SERVICES—from magazines in the popular press to electronic newsletters, from online data to distributed database management systems, from graphic design packages to portfolio management services—have frequently confronted a situation in which their offerings no longer perfectly match consumer preferences. The extent to which the two are mismatched could vary from marginal to catastrophic misalignment (as in the case of Living.com or eToys.com). $^{1}$ In all cases the sellers face the same dilemma: to what extent should they respond constructively to market place demands by changing the product or service (or the value proposition itself in more extreme cases of misalignment), to what extent should they advertise the virtues of their product (in an attempt to change market preferences), and to what extent should they use advertising deceptively (to attempt to change the perception of their offering to a less accurate, but more favorable set of attributes, more in line with market preferences).

## Problem Statement: The Seller's Dilemma

The sellers' dilemma represents the problem of choosing between two basic strategies that are open to them—product modification and advertising. Product modification involves making changes to one or more attributes of the product to bring it in line with the market's preferences. Advertising presents two different sub-options. Sellers may advertise either the virtues of their product (in an attempt to change market preferences), or they may use advertising deceptively (to attempt to change the perception of their product to a less factually accurate set of attributes more in line with market preferences). Each of these strategies has different cost implications, depending on the nature and complexity of the product, the size and spread of the market, and the gap between desired perception and current market perception of the product. However, as we shall see below, the nature of products and services, the costs associated with changing them, and the costs associated with communicating these changes to consumers, have all been profoundly altered, even transformed, by the Internet.

The selection of the optimal strategy from among the three has always been dictated by costs and benefits—changing the product is expensive. If done well and communicated well to consumers, however, the benefits will include current sales, credibility with consumers, and future sales. This strategy offers an annuity value, akin to the value of good will or of brand strength created by a satisfied set of buyers who will probably be repeat buyers and who will also help persuade other buyers to adopt the product in the future. As an example, consider firms that invest in quality and constantly bring out high-quality upgrades of the product. They often do so for one of these two reasons: (1) it is possible to extract higher rents from a customer base that is convinced about the product's quality, and (2) the firm's reputation for quality will often be transferable to a variety of products and brands offered by the same firm. The firm's reputation for delivering customer satisfaction acts as an annuity for the firm, representing a stream of rents that will accrue to the firm in the future.

Changing market preferences sometimes is both expensive and time-consuming, and is thus rarely effective. Given the costs associated with changing the market's preferences, a firm may attempt to do this only if the benefits of engineering such a change can be disproportionately reaped by the firm itself. If, after an expensive campaign to change the market's preferences, the profits could possibly be competed away, the firm's incentives to undertake such a campaign would be diminished. Schwab made considerable investments in technology to make investors feel secure enough to switch away from full service brokerages. However, many online investing services have, in turn, lured Schwab's customers away. Finally, in some instances, changing the perceptions of the market may be infeasible. For example, this would be an awkward time to convince retail investors that pure play Internet retailers can make healthy profits in the near future.

In some instances, changing the perception of the product may be the least expensive option available, or it may be the most valuable. Even if the purchaser is dissatisfied with the initial purchase, collateral sunk investments made by the customers may preclude them from returning their purchases. In this case, this option is quite attractive to the firm. Moreover, if the product purchased has long-term switching costs, then consumers may be forced to buy upgrades or supplies from the original seller—providing the firm with an annuity value and further justifying its choice of this strategy. Alternatively, if returns are high and the damage to consumer credibility is long-lasting, lying would be a very poor strategy.

These choices are even more significant today. This is due in part to the many ways in which the Internet reduces the barriers to entry and decreases the costs of providing information to consumers. However, these choices have also become more significant due to the nature of information products, which are used to produce information (such as database management systems) and the higher switching costs associated with consumers' initial selections. There is a clear distinction between the options open to the dissatisfied buyers of an information product such as an encyclopedia on a CD-ROM and an information product like a relational database. The significant switching costs associated with latter drive sellers to adopt very different strategies in marketing these products.

We make a distinction between two kinds of information products: information consumption products and information production products based on the end objectives served by the products. An information consumption product (information consumable from here onward) is one that the buyer reads (or watches or listens to) to acquire (or experience, as in the case of music or the visual media) information. The online forums Slate.com, Salon.com, and the New York Times are all information consumption products. An information consumable is a repository of information ready for instant consumption by the buyer.

As opposed to information consumption goods there is a class of information products that we call information production products. They are used primarily for the production, creation, or the reorganization of information. Products like relational databases, compilers, application development software, HTML editors, operating systems, and so on, are examples of information production goods. These products are not used as end consumables as much as to produce and deliver information that will be consumed by buyers.

The advent of the Internet has for many categories of information products lowered barriers to entry to an extent that has resulted in a proliferation of products and services. This proliferation has in turn imposed a cognitive cost on the buyer who wishes to inspect products and services in order to make an informed choice. Thus the role played by a brand in mitigating the “cognitive clutter” is becoming increasingly significant. In the financial services industry, the reduction of barriers to entry allows anyone to offer financial services, and, therefore, the role of a brand as a surrogate for more complex measures of quality becomes increasingly important.

## Role of Brand for Information Goods

Brands play different roles in different classes of products. The importance of brand in the case of an information consumption product arises from the ease of communicating any statement to the consumer and for what the brand signifies. When a consumer is considering paying for one of the two information consumables, The Interactive Wall Street Journal and BusinessWeek Online, it is not possible for her to sample every article in both electronic forums for an extended period before she decides on one. Instead, she relies on the reputations (brand image) of the two forums to give her meaningful information about their coverage and content. A consumer's decision problem while shopping for an information consumable, is twofold: (1) how does she decide which product to buy? and (2) once she identifies a service or product that she likes from the many that she has sampled, how can she be sure that the attributes that led to her purchase decision will remain invariant?

For the sellers of these products, the primary use of a brand may be to assure the consumer that the product has not changed and will not change. The risk of brand dilution in this class of products stems from buyers' perception that the seller has changed the product or that the brand no longer signifies immutability of the product attributes that mattered to them. Products such as the Wall Street Journal, the Encyclopaedia Britannica, and CNet's electronic product rating service are all examples of information consumables. Information consumables are characterized by a cost structure very different from that of the physical consumables. Information consumables are a high first copy-cost product. This means that it is costly to produce but cheap to reproduce [9]. It has a very low marginal cost that is often negligible, especially in the case of digitizable information products, but is characterized by a very high investment in acquiring the capability to produce a specific kind of information.

It is often exceedingly difficult, and in some cases impossible, for the seller of an information consumable to change the product's defining attributes. Business periodicals in the trade press such as Forbes and Fortune have invested heavily in hiring and training journalists and writers who can write on topics that are of interest to an upscale business information consumer. For either of these periodicals to change or reposition itself to cover something that is completely unrelated, such as sports or fashion, would require very high up-front investment in addition to the costs associated with moving away from its current area of expertise. For these magazines there are only limited opportunities for repositioning available. They reach a well-defined segment of consumers whose tastes are well understood by the periodicals. Most readers of these magazines are also aware of the fact that a major shift in product attributes is a costly task for the seller and, therefore, is unlikely. As a result, these publications use branding as a tool to signal quality rather than continuity. The Wall Street Journal, for instance, has carefully built up a reputation for accurate business reporting with minimal emphasis on personalities and avoidance of nonbusiness content. It has taken care to give its interactive, online edition the same look and feel of the paper version. The role of advertising in this context is simply to assure the buyer that the product has not changed. That is, this role is to assure the buyer that the product's reputation for accuracy and timeliness, and the editorial policies and beliefs are intact. Any significant discrepancy between the advertised product image and the actual product as experienced by the consumer is likely to result in returns and loss of future sales.

Information production products have a cost structure very similar to that of information consumption products. They have a very high fixed cost and a negligible marginal cost of production. An important difference between these products and consumables is that information production products almost always require some investments on the buyer's part in terms of acquiring the necessary level of expertise and infrastructure to operate the product. The role of advertising is more nuanced in the case of information production products and the choice of a suitable strategy depends on a variety of factors and their interplay. In this case, the seller may choose an advertising strategy that involves making deliberately false claims so as to capture an initial installed base of buyers. The producer of a HTML editor may advertise that his product has a look and feel that is consistent with a popular word processing program, while in fact, the HTML editor may only have vestiges of similarity with the word processing program. Buyers who master the product sufficiently to be able to spot the differences will find it difficult to switch to the firm's competitors for their future purchases, since there is a very real switching cost associated with the learning curve. Furthermore, these buyers find that returning the product is possible only in rare cases and that too carries a significant cost (of a period of low productivity until the user becomes an expert user of the new software). Since the buyer has to make significant up-front investments in acquiring the capability to operate the product, the option of returning the product carries with it the attendant risk of gutting the investments already made. Sellers here are less easily punished for making exaggerated and even patently false claims. Sellers' strategy may therefore be driven by the extent of switching costs and the number of subsequent purchases that the buyer is expected to make.

We investigate how the costs of reformulation vs. costs of repositioning will affect the seller's choice of advertising strategy.

## Advertising and Signaling—A Review of Prior Work

THE ROLE OF ADVERTISING in the formation of consumer beliefs about products and services has attracted the attention of researchers from such diverse disciplines as economics, psychology, and marketing. Much of the work that we will rely upon within the field of marketing is based on the work done in the field of information economics. Prior work in marketing that is relevant to this problem falls within the modeling space of information asymmetry and signaling games developed by researchers in information economics.

Research in information economics has modeled advertising as a stylized signaling game. The recent work of Milgrom and Roberts $[7]$ explores the role of advertising under conditions of information asymmetry. Shapiro $[8]$ has examined the conditions under which a seller would invest in gaining a reputation for producing high quality products and the use of a quality premium to encourage firms to refrain from short-term profit taking by cutting product quality. Information economics has also addressed the problem that arises from the true product attributes and quality being only imperfectly observable by buyers while they are perfectly known to the firm. The resulting problem of adverse selection has been modeled by several researchers including Grossman $[5]$ , whose work focuses on warranties and private disclosures about product quality. The research in marketing differs from the work that we review here in that it principally addresses a different problem—that of communicating the best possible message while avoiding loss of credibility. We address the optimal balance between changing the product and changing the message.

Unlike the relevant work in marketing, we do not assume that the buyer has accurate information on the seller's costs and can reverse engineer the seller's decisions and accurately infer the true state of product attributes. Thus, the problem that we seek to solve here is to analyze the drivers of sellers' strategies in a market and not the buyer's strategic response at equilibrium.

## Two Parameters That Define Seller Strategy

IN EARLIER SECTIONS WE MENTIONED HOW THE COSTS of changing the product and repositioning it differed from one product class to another. We observed that while it is easy to modify a product line of consumer perishables (such as cookies), it is much more costly and more time consuming to alter an information production product (such as a relational database management system) or an information consumable (CNet.com's product rating service). Adding a feature to a relational database is both expensive and time consuming. When Sybase Inc. and Oracle Corporation came out with their versions of the Replication Server, it took several iterations to make these features work as per the claims made by the producers. (The annual report of Sybase Corporation indicates that the design and development of the Replication Server was indeed a major effort [10].) On the other hand, it would be relatively easy to launch an advertising campaign to reposition the product, to stress those attributes that are preferred by the market in the current time period. We will discuss in this section the two critical parameters that determine seller strategies and when it makes sense for sellers to try to modify the product as opposed to simply repositioning it through advertising.

## The Repeg Ratio

As seen in the previous example about relational databases, the ratio of two costs, the cost of repositioning the product, and the cost of rebuilding it, is an important element in the mix of factors that determine the truth content of advertising. We define this ratio as the Repeg Ratio given by

$$
\theta = \frac {\text { Cost   of   Repositioning   the   Product }}{\text { Cost   of   Rebuilding   the   Product }}.
$$

A low $\theta$ ratio would indicate that it is cheaper to reposition the product via advertising than it is to rebuild the product, while a high $\theta$ ratio would favor the opposite strategy. In a market for information production goods such as digital audio-rendering applications or a server-side scripting languages, it is less costly to reposition the product through advertising than to rebuild the product.

The $\theta$ ratio, however, is not the sole factor that drives seller's advertising strategy. A seller would also be aware of the costs associated with falsification of claims. The cost could take the form of expensive returns, lost consumer goodwill leading to loss of repeat sales, and loss of brand equity. The greater the extent of falsification of claims the greater the resulting buyer displeasure incurred in the market. A seller of an Internet-based Web hosting utility or a scripting language may, therefore, trade off the prospect of lost future sales against the immediate gains that might accrue from opportunistic behavior. The seller of an information consumption product, such as a business periodical or a fashion periodical, will be aware of the potential loss of future revenue stemming from blatant misrepresentation of the magazine's content.

## The Repeat Sales Coefficient

Thus, a second factor plays an important role in seller calculations about the extent of truth in advertising—the prospect of repeat sales. The number of times that a buyer buys a product (or an upgrade) after the first purchase has a significant impact on seller behavior. We define a quantity called the Repeat Sale Coefficient, which is given by: Repeat Sale Coefficient S = Number of repeat sales after the first sale. For instance, if a buyer is expected to buy four versions (or upgrades) of a multimedia editing package in her lifetime, the Repeat Sale Coefficient, S = 3. The higher the value of S, the greater the seller's truth content of advertising.

## Other Considerations

Reputation and Word-of-Mouth Effects. In the context of information goods, there are several important features that need to be considered about the nature of word-ofmouth publicity. First, word-of-mouth publicity and reputation-based effects on sales take some time to manifest themselves. In the case of simple products, such as magazines in the popular or business press (examples of information consumables), the reputation of the product will spread to potential buyers in a relatively short time. For complex information production goods, such as a compiler or a relational database, the spread of reputation is slower. As the complexity of the product increases, the time taken for an accurate reputation to form increases.

A second factor that is important in the consideration of word-of-mouth effects is the complexity and context of usage of the information product. The buyer of enterprise-level software will not be able to go by the recommendations made by an existing buyer since the context of usage of the product could determine the true extent of fit between the buyer and product. For instance, a supply chain management application may have a poor degree of fit with one buyer's existing ERP software due to compatibility reasons, but may work very well with another's ERP implementation. There is, therefore, a trade-off between the accuracy of a product's reputation in the marketplace and the time that it takes for the reputation to form. This trade-off is particularly important in the case of information production goods that are characterized by significant product complexity and versatility of usage contexts. We will factor these effects into the analysis in the sections that follow.

## Four Strategic Scenarios

WE ANALYZE THE FOUR CASES that arise from the interplay of these two factors.

## Defining the Strategic Scenarios

Case 1—Low Repeg ( $\theta$ ), Low Repeat Sales Coefficient (S)—Lie Like Hell. Since the cost of repositioning the product is much less than the cost of modifying it and the discounted value of repeat sales is low, the seller has no reason to be truthful or accurate in this case. The seller’s optimal strategy is best summarized in this case as the “Lie Like Hell” Strategy. We would expect that generating the purchase is all important and only the threat of returns and lost sales from the adverse word-of-mouth effects on future sales may act as a fetter on the extent of exaggeration in seller claims. If the product has low marginal cost, falsification becomes even more attractive.

Case 2—High Repeg ( $\theta$ ), High Repeat Sales Coefficient (S)—Truth, Whole Truth, Nothing But the Truth. Since the cost of repositioning the product is much higher than the cost of modifying it and the discounted value of repeat sales is high, the seller has no reason to be anything but truthful and accurate in this case. For instance, electronic community sites such as Epinions.com and RateItAll.com, which primarily provide content related to customer reviews (of products), would find it difficult to reposition their businesses as techno-portals, like CNet.com. Since these services operate on the Internet, they can acquire the content that is necessary to become a techno-portal site through syndication $[11]$ at a relatively modest cost. However, they have an entrenched position in the minds of the consumers as electronic communities that provide consumer reviews rather than as techno-portals. It is actually more expensive to change the image of these services than the actual content, while the product also has a high repeat purchase potential. The services use advertising to emphasize their consumer reviews rather than the third party product information that they provide. Most information consumables fall in this category.

Case 3—Low Repeg ( $\theta$ ), High Repeat Sales Coefficient (S)—Ambiguous Case. Since the cost of repositioning the product is much less than the cost of modifying it, the seller would be inclined to take liberties with the facts in repositioning his product. However, since the discounted value of repeat sales is also high, the seller has reasons to fear lost future sales and therefore mitigate the extent to which he would exaggerate product claims. The seller’s strategy is likely to depend on a number of different factors in this case. These are discussed in the following subsection below.

Case 4—High Repeg ( $\theta$ ), Low Repeat Sales Coefficient(S)—Generally Tell the Truth. In this case, since the cost of repositioning the product is high relative to the cost of modifying the product, the seller would generally be inclined to make truthful product claims. This is reinforced by the fact that there are only limited opportunities for subsequent sales, that is, not only is effective lying expensive in this case, but the expected benefits of this lying are relatively low. Sellers' incentives to be truthful in making product claims are strengthened by the threat of lost future sales should the product acquire an adverse reputation in the market.

The exception to this rule may be when the product is so catastrophically mis-aligned with the market's preferences that a very high degree of product reengineering needs be achieved before the modified product can be brought to the market. In the time that it takes the seller to achieve radical redesign, the market preferences could once again have changed. In a situation like this the seller would be inclined to make exaggerated product claims. Moreover, information-based products, especially those with network effects, may also encourage exaggerated or false claims in order to capture market share as early as possible. Without these false claims, and with the need for a lengthy redevelopment effort, a competitor may be able to release a product that acquires a large installed base. This would make the seller's product less attractive when it is actually released. The phenomenon of advertising “vaporware,” wherein sellers make claims about products that they may not even be working on, can be seen as an extreme form of preemptive announcement that seeks to prevent rivals from benefiting from network effects in information-based products.

## An Analytic Model to Determine Seller

## Optimal Strategies: Preliminaries

We next present an analytical model and provide comparative statics to investigate the sellers' optimal strategy, focusing especially on the ambiguous Case 3 above. Figure 2 presents a diagrammatic representation (of the product space) of a market for a differentiated product.

The product space is represented by a horizontal solid line. We use a model similar to the one used by Hoteling [6] to model buyers and sellers distributed in geographical space. Buyers have heterogeneous preferences and their ideal products are uniformly distributed in the product space. The distance between a product's location and the buyer is a measure of the misfit between the buyers' preferences and the seller's offering. The unit misfit cost is given by the parameter $t$ . The reserve utility of the representative buyer for his ideal product is given by $U$ . The ideal product of every buyer has a unique location in product space. $^{2}$ The buyer experiences a misfit cost of $xt$ when he buys a product that is located at a distance $x$ from his position in product space. The maximum a buyer will pay for a product that is at a distance $x$ away from his location in product space is given by $U - xt$ . Therefore, the monopolistic seller will set a price $P^{*} = U / 2$ and the induced demand is given by $Q = U / t$ .

Table 2 provides a summary of all the variables and parameters used in the analytic model.

We model a market in which a monopolist's product is catastrophically misaligned (see Figure 2) with the market's preferences. The product's positioning yields suboptimal revenue and it has to be repositioned toward the center (not necessarily at the center) of the market. The extent to which the monopolist has to reformulate or reposition the product so as to be able to maximize revenues is given by $D$ (a unit independent measure). It is obvious from the discussion above that $D = U / 2t$ . As a result, the seller can choose from three strategies:

1. Reformulate only. Completely reformulate the product.

2. Reformulate and reposition. Partly reformulate the product and reposition the product through advertising so as to occupy the optimal product position.

3. Reposition only. Rely on repositioning through advertising and so not reformulate at all.

We assume that the extent of repositioning is x and that of reformulation is D - x. If the optimal strategy results in x = 0 or x = D, we will conclude that Strategic Option 1 or 3, respectively, was the optimal one. If it turns out that 0 < x < D, then we will conclude that a hybrid option consisting of some reformulation and some repositioning was the optimal one.

We next introduce additional notation that will be necessary in a moment. The cost of the pure reformulation strategy is given by $C_{1}$ and the cost of partial repositioning (and partial reformulation) strategy is given by $C_{2}$ .

The gross incremental revenue to the seller from moving to a changed position is given by $\pi(D)=U^{2}/4t$ (incremental demand times price $(U/2t)\cdot(U/2)$ ). Since the gross revenue is the same for all three options, we will compare only the costs associate with each strategy to determine the optimal strategy. Two key costs are worth noting:

\- Costs associated with the three strategies. The cost of reformulating the product through unit distance in product space is given by $C$ , and the cost of repositioning it through advertising is given by $\theta \cdot C$ . We will normalize $C$ to 1 without loss of generality and the cost of repositioning will therefore be given by $\theta$ .

\- Product return-related costs. In the case of information production products such as relational databases or network management software, buyers will very often have to make substantial up-front investments to even be in a position to evaluate the product.

## Model Development

In our model, buyers experience a cost when they return a product given by $R$ . $^{3}$ When a product is repositioned by an extent $x$ , only those buyers whose net surplus is improved by returning the product, will return the product. This implies that of those buyers who experienced a negative buyer surplus, only those with a negative buyer surplus greater than the return cost will return the product. All buyers at a distance of $D$ or more units from the actual location of the product experience negative surplus. Of these, those at a distance of $D + y$ or greater, where $y : y > R/t$ will return the product when the product is repositioned by an extent $x$ . Therefore, the segment of the market that will return the product is given by $x - R/t$ .

We simplify our notation by adopting the following convention. The revenue from a unit segment of the market is $m \times P^{*}$ , where there are m buyers per unit segment of the market and $P^{*}$ is the price that each pays. We will use $P = m \times P^{*}$ to denote the revenue from a unit market segment. The cost of returns to the producer is therefore given by: $(x - R/t)P$ for x > R/t and = 0 for $x \leq R/t$ .

Recall that the Repeat Sale Coefficient is given by S. The life of the product is given by n, where n measures the number of (unit independent) time periods during which the product is available in the market. $^{4}$ The buyer will buy the product S times during this period (should he want to). For the sake of tractability we will assume that the buyer will buy the product in S time periods equally spaced apart. The price paid by the buyer in each time period is given by $P^{*}$ . $^{5}$

The annuity value of the sales to an individual buyer in the current time period is therefore given by:

$$
V = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {s} \frac {P ^ {*}}{(1 + r) ^ {\frac {i n}{S}}} = \frac {\left((1 + r) ^ {n} - 1\right) P ^ {*}}{(1 + r) ^ {n} \left[ (1 + r) ^ {\frac {n}{S}} - 1 \right]},
$$

where $r$ is the inter-period discount rate. See Appendix A for notes on the derivation. In our model all buyers who experience a negative surplus (whether or not they return the product) as a result of the producer's claims not being fully accurate, will never buy the product again. This represents the extent of loss of repeat sales to the producer from repositioning the product. We do not explicitly model the loss of sales from adverse word-of-mouth publicity since this takes multiple periods to build up.

In our model all primary sales take place in the first period and only repeat sales take place in subsequent periods. All potential buyers in subsequent periods have already experienced the product and do not require word-of-mouth recommendations to evaluate the product.

The annuity value of lost sales, V, for each buyer that experienced negative surplus is given by:

$$
V = \frac {P * \left(\left(1 + r\right) ^ {n} - 1\right)}{(1 + r) ^ {n} \left[ (1 + r) \frac {n}{S} - 1 \right]}.
$$

The total (annuity) value of lost sales from the market segment that experienced negative surplus is given by $V_{L}$ (also see Appendix A for notes on the derivation):

$$
V _ {L} = V (x m) = \frac {(x m P *) ((1 + r) ^ {n} - 1)}{(1 + r) ^ {n} [ (1 + r) \frac {n}{S} - 1 ]} = \frac {x P ((1 + r) ^ {n} - 1)}{(1 + r) ^ {n} [ (1 + r) \frac {n}{S} - 1 ]}.
$$

Cost of partial repositioning is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} C _ {2} = (D - x) + x \theta + \left(x - \frac {R}{t}\right) P + V _ {L} \\ \Rightarrow C _ {2} = (D - x) + x \theta + \left(x - \frac {R}{t}\right) P + \frac {x P ((1 + r) ^ {n} - 1)}{(1 + r) ^ {n} [ (1 + r) \frac {n}{S} - 1 ]}. \end{array}
$$

The trivial case is when only one of the two strategies is possible. If complete product reformulation is infeasible (costs dominate revenue), that is, when P < 1 we will be left to analyze the partial reformulation case. We will instead consider the nontrivial case, that is, when both strategies are feasible and then derive conditions under which one of these is optimal. This is the richer problem and will provide deeper insights into sellers' strategy. If both strategies are feasible, then $C_{1} \leq DP$ and $C_{2} \leq DP$ .

## Model Application: Comparing Strategies

We will now compare the two strategies and derive the conditions under which each dominates. The difference in costs between the two strategies $G = C_{1} - C_{2}$ is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} G = C _ {1} - C _ {2} = D - \left[ (D - x) + x \theta + \left(x - \frac {R}{t}\right) P + V _ {L} \right] \\ = x (1 - \theta - P) + \frac {R}{t} - V _ {L}. \end{array}
$$

Substituting for the total (annuity) value of lost sales, $V_{L}$ , we have

$$
G = x \left(1 - \theta - P - \frac {P \left[ (1 + r) ^ {n} - 1 \right]}{(1 + r) ^ {n} \left[ (1 + r) \frac {n}{s} - 1 \right]}\right) + \frac {R}{t} \text {   for   } x > \frac {R}{t}
$$

and

$$
G = x \left(1 - \theta - \frac {p \left[ (1 + r) ^ {n} - 1 \right]}{(1 + r) ^ {n} \left[ (1 + r) ^ {\frac {n}{s}} - 1 \right]}\right) \text {   for   } x \leq \frac {R}{t}.
$$

If G > 0, $C_{2}$ is the preferred strategy, and if G < 0, $C_{1}$ is the preferred strategy. When G = 0, the seller is indifferent between the two strategies.

The first question that we address is: Can a pure repositioning strategy be the dominant one?

Proposition 1: A pure repositioning strategy may be the dominant strategy only if the cost of return exceeds the mismatch cost experienced by a buyer for a product D units away in product space from the buyer.

A proof of this proposition is furnished in Appendix A.

Note: This is a necessary but not sufficient condition. That a pure repositioning strategy may be dominant does not imply that it is dominant.

The term R/t is an upper bound for the extent to which the product will be repositioned. When R/t < D, while in theory it is possible to reposition the product by an extent D (pure repositioning), this will never be optimal. The extent to which the product is repositioned will always be less than (or equal to) R/t. This, in turn, implies that the extent of reformulation will be greater than (or equal to) D - R/t and, therefore, pure repositioning cannot be a dominant strategy.

The above proposition states that a pure repositioning strategy may be optimal only when $Dt < R$ . The implications of this result become clearer when we consider what kind of products have a Return Cost (to the buyer) that exceeds $Dt$ . In a market where the producer's product is not catastrophically misaligned, the buyer who is at a distance of D from the product is the marginal buyer. In other words, half this buyer's surplus is lost in the product misfit cost that he bears. $^{6}$ For the cost of return to exceed this, it is necessary that the buyer lose more than half his utility from the product when he returns the product. Very few information production goods, and no information consumables, are characterized by this return cost structure. As a result, it becomes clear that only in the rarest of information production goods, characterized by an exceedingly high cost of return, is it optimal for the seller to rely on repositioning alone. It is, therefore, reasonable to argue that for most products, pure repositioning is not an optimal strategy. Moreover, the conditions under which a pure repositioning strategy is dominant are characterized by extreme values of model parameters. In the case of most information production goods and all information consumption goods, the extent to which a seller will reposition the product is bounded from above by the value of R/t. $^{7}$

This leads us to our next question: Can a pure product reformulation strategy ever be the dominant strategy?

To examine the condition under which this question can be answered, let us begin by outlining an important condition. For a large enough market, the value of P should be discounted after a finite time horizon should still be greater than 1. This means that it is reasonable to assume that $P/(1+r)^{n} > 1$ .⁸

We will now analyze the problem under this constraint.

Proposition 2: If $P/(1+r)^{n} > 1$ , then pure product reformulation may be a dominant strategy.

A proof of this proposition is furnished in Appendix A. From Proposition 2, it follows that pure reformulation can indeed be a dominant strategy. However, the extent of reformulation is bounded from above by D, as opposed to repositioning, which has an upper bound of R/t. This, in turn, leads us to observe that even though pure reformulation remains a viable strategy, the pure repositioning strategy (making completely false claims) is rarely optimal.

To complete this discussion, we must ask: Are there self-reinforcing limits to opportunistic advertising? When we consider the implications of Propositions 1 and 2 taken together, it is clear that while pure reformulation may be an optimal strategy, it is almost never optimal to rely on repositioning alone. It is important to note that a market such as this will exhibit self-reinforcing limits on the extent of false product claims by sellers. Only in the case of a few unusual information production goods (such as a mission critical transaction processing systems) will the cost of returning the product be so high that the seller will be able to rely on a strategy of acquiring market share by making false claims.

## Conclusions and Directions for Future Research

WE HAVE EXTENDED THE TRADITIONAL MARKETING PARADIGM to provide a framework for analyzing the key determinants of sellers' advertising strategy. Our analysis integrates the interplay of the two key determinants of seller strategy with the forces that drive buyer behavior in information goods (both production and consumption) such as switching costs and network effects. Furthermore, we have been able to provide qualitative and analytical explanations for why sellers' product claims have differing levels of truth content for different classes of products (such as information production and consumption goods).

Our framework handles both the sales and marketing of information production goods (and services), and information consumables. We find that the two important determinants of sellers' advertising strategy are the Repeg Cost Ratio and the Repeat Sales Coefficient. The interplay of these two factors gives rise to four possible strategic scenarios. We have disaggregated the impact of the different factors in the ambiguous strategy scenario (i.e., with low $\theta$ and high S) and specified a hierarchy of dominance where seller strategy is driven by the dominance of one of the two ratios and the resulting interplay of the two ratios with the extent of lock-in (as manifested in the cost of product returns). We show that sellers' strategy is clearly explainable in three out of these four scenarios. In this ambiguous fourth scenario, we show that sellers' strategy is characterized by the market's self-reinforcing limits on the extent of false claims that sellers can make. Further, we show how the information complexity of the product (which generates high return costs) can play a significant role in determining sellers' strategy in the case of some information production goods.

Our findings show that for information consumables, which are characterized by low return costs and high potential loss of sales, sellers will generally tend to be truthful in advertising the product's attributes and exaggeration if any will be likely made about the quality of the product. While marketing information production goods on the other hand, sellers will have a strong tendency to make inflated claims both about the attributes of the product and its quality. Where sellers may be naturally inclined to make inflated product claims, they will be forced to moderate their claims so as to preserve the annuity of future sales. We find in these cases, that seller propensity to inflate claims will be considerably influenced by the fear of lost sales in the future and by the cost of returns. As a consequence, sellers' advertising will focus on persuading buyers to “adopt a set of beliefs” that make the product valuable (changing the hedonic vector of buyer preferences) rather than on misleading the buyer about the nature of the product.

A limitation of the analytical model that we have developed in this paper is that we have ignored the role played by reputation in driving the demand for the product. We offer two important observations about the analytical model that we employ in this paper. First, in our model all primary sales to the monopolist accrue in the first period and in subsequent periods, only repeat sales take place. In other words, we consider a market in which new buyers do not arrive in the second period and all buyers who will buy the product already have information about the product and do not require word-of-mouth information to evaluate it. A second issue is the impact of word-of-mouth effects. In any market, word-of-mouth publicity adversely impacts the seller that makes false claims and tends to reward the producers of accurately represented products with higher revenues. The impact of reputation is to provide disincentives to sellers for making inaccurate product claims. Our model establishes that markets are characterized by self-reinforcing limits to opportunistic advertising even in the absence of reputation effects. We establish a stronger result without considering reputation-based disincentives. The addition of the reputation and word-of-mouth publicity as a factor will only strengthen the findings of this research.

We will use the framework developed here to model the behavior of sellers and buyers at equilibrium. We will develop an analytical model where seller strategy is driven by the two important strategic determinants discussed above. Furthermore, we will invest buyers with estimates of some of these costs that will act as a prior on which the buyer's strategic response can be formulated. Sellers anticipating the buyer's strategic response will adopt either some signaling mechanisms (along the lines of the model developed by Cho and Kreps [3] or Banks and Sobel [1]), to signal the truth content of their claims or will have to deal with buyer incredulity. We will use the four scenarios outlined previously to drive four different equilibria and use closed form analytics to comment on total welfare, efficiency of the signaling mechanism, and effectiveness of seller advertising strategies for the four different product classes. We will also incorporate the roll played by external information and rating providing third parties such as CNet.com [4] and BizRate.com [2] and comment on the extent to which these third parties can solve the problems of information asymmetry. Finally, we will provide a welfare ranking of the four equilibria and comment on the policy implications of enforcing advertising standards.
