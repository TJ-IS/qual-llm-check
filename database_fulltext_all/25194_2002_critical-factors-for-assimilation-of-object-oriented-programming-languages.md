# Critical Factors for Assimilation of Object-Oriented Programming Languages

IHLSOON CHO AND YOUNG-GUL KIM

IHLSOON CHO is a consultant of IBM Korea and a Ph.D. candidate at the Graduate School of Management of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology in Seoul. She received her M.S. in Computer Science from the Graduate School of Engineering in Yonsei University and B.S. in Chemistry from the Seoul Nationa University, Korea. She has over fifteen years of experience in information system development. Her research areas are: data modeling, object-oriented software engineering, and business intelligence

YOUNG-GUL KIM is an associate professor at the Graduate School of Management of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology in Seoul. He received his B.S. and M.S. in Industrial Engineering from the Seoul National University, Korea and Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Minnesota. His active research areas are knowledge management, information technology innovation, and customer relationship management. His publications have appeared in various journals, such as Communications of the ACM, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Information & Management, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, among others. He presented several papers at ICIS, HICSS, and DSI conferences.

ABSTRACT: Object-oriented (OO) technology was expected to rapidly replace tradi tional functional software technology due to its productivity and quality improvement potential in software development. Still, OO technology is not yet fully understood and utilized by information systems (IS) organizations. Despite the growing interest and attention of the IS researchers and practitioners, empirical research on the assimilation process of OO technology has been limited. The present study assesses the current status of OO technology assimilation in IS organizations and identifies the factors influencing such assimilation from a software process innova tion perspective.

Innovation attributes and organizational characteristics were tested as determinants of the organizational OO technology assimilation based on a survey of 220 organizations. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the relationships of innovation and organizational variables with the level of OO technology assimilation. The findings indicate that, among the innovation characteristics, perceived complexity and perceived maturity of technology have been found to have positive relationships with organizational assimilation of OO technology. Among the organizational characteristics, intensity of new technology education was positively related to organizational assimilation of OO technology, and satisfaction with existing technology was negatively related to organizational assimilation of OO technology.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information technology innovation, object-oriented technology assimilation, software process innovation, technology assimilation.

OBJECT-ORIENTED (OO) TECHNOLOGY ENABLES modeling and solving of a given problem with features of object, class, inheritance, polymorphism, and message passing [5, 32, 50]. With OO technology, real world problems can be modeled seamlessly, and information systems (IS) can be constructed by reusing standardized software components [32, 54]. As the need to innovate the organizational software develop ment process is critical, the role of OO technology becomes more prominent.

OO technology assimilation in IS organizations, however, has been slower than expected by the technology experts [23, 45]. Although there has been active research on the technical aspects of OO technology, few empirical studies have been conducted on the organizational assimilation of OO technology. Fichman and Kemerer looked into the assimilation of OO programming languages [23]. However, the study was performed at a time when OO technology was less mature and most organizations were in the early stage of OO technology assimilation. Since that study, OO technology has evolved to have a more substantive role in IS development, thus the question of what factors are related to the assimilation level of OO technology re mains an interesting and unanswered question. To capture the context of maturing, OO technology attributes pertaining to the environment for adoption (such as, managers’ perceptions of market trends or maturity of the technology) were included.

The objective of this research is to understand the organizational use of OO technology and to identify the critical factors that facilitate or inhibit OO technology assimilation from a software process innovation perspective. Among the diverse OO technologies, this study focuses on the most representative one, OO programming languages.

To differentiate between innovation diffusion and innovation assimilation, this study will use the term “diffusion” to describe the spread of an innovation across organizations, and use the term “assimilation” to represent the process by which an organization adopts and deploys an innovation.

## OO Technology

## Benefits of OO Technology

OO TECHNOLOGY CAN ADDRESS CHRONIC SOFTWARE QUALITY and productivity prob lems by enabling the construction of application software using standardized quality building blocks [33, 54]. From the software user’s viewpoint, quality of software means meeting users’ functional requirements, high reliability, ease of use, performance, and maintainability. From the developer’s viewpoint, however, the most important software quality is modularity [41]. OO technology is ideal for building modularity. Class, encapsulation, and message-passing features of OO technology promote the creation of modular components. Such components perform coheren functions, hide private information, and communicate with external entities through explicit interfaces [5, 41]. In OO technology, a system is modeled after the objects that correspond to the real world entities. Therefore, the model is stable, and changes in the system objectives are confined to a limited number of relevant objects [32].

The other important goal in business computing is the enhanced productivity in software development. The key to productivity improvement is the reuse of common software components for multiple application development projects. OO technology holds significant promise in software reuse [32, 42], since it is ideal for constructing software components. The component can be used as a “black-box” building block for composing large software. A component also can be extended to handle special requirements through inheritance [41]. As OO technology spreads in the software industry, standard classes can be developed and accumulated in a class library. As more and more class libraries are built and become available, software developers can create new IS applications by assembling components from such libraries rather than creating them each time [54].

## OO Technology as Software Process Innovation

In understanding an innovation, the following questions need to be answered first. What does an innovation change? What is the affected area of a change and how radical is it? Who are the people involved in such change? OO technology changes the process in which software is developed and maintained. Therefore, it has been classified as a software process innovation for IS organizations [46, 53].

Research on software process innovation found that factors, such as top management attitude, organizational receptivity toward change, perceived barriers to innovation, and satisfaction with the existing system [8], are related to the adoption process. Additionally, organization size, technological diversity, professionalism, and IS slack were studied in the previous research [25].

Technology assimilation can be considered to be a process of organizational learning. Thus organizations that can accommodate the costs of organizational learning are more likely to initiate and sustain the assimilation of a complex process technol ogy [2]. The variables learning-related scale (having a level of activity that can achieve an economy of scale in learning), related knowledge, and diversity of technology were associated with higher levels of OO technology assimilation [23].

It is generally agreed that environmental characteristics, such as uncertainty, are less important for explaining assimilation of software process innovations [8, 53], whereas characteristics of the IS organization, especially the role of technical knowledge resources, are more important. OO technology has high knowledge burden and interdependencies with the user [2, 11, 21, 23]. Prior knowledge about OO concepts and methodologies is important for successful adoption and implementation [23, 49].

When OO technology is introduced into an IS organization, significant changes take place. Due to the importance of the class library, building, testing, and maintaining components are the core of OO software development [32]. The development process would change from integrating works of individual developers to an assembly line type of software construction using standardized components [32].

In consideration of the knowledge burden and extent of change from the existing practices, OO technology should be defined as a radical innovation [16, 19, 21]. It was found that unique organizational strategy and structure such as aggressive tech nology policy, concentration of technical specialists, extensive knowledge depth, and organizational size are required for a radical innovation [16, 19].

Even if an organization has experienced client-server or object-based technologies, such as graphical user interface (GUI), thereby having accumulated the related knowledge, introducing OO technology in earnest means sweeping changes, such as expanding IT personnel’s skill sets or replacing project management systems. Changes such as developing an in-house standardized development methodology or introduc ing a reuse system to enhance the utilization of OO technologies require significant time and knowledge accumulation.

## Theoretical Framework

AMONG THE NUMEROUS DIMENSIONS that affect the assimilation of an innovation are organizational, technological, and external environmental factors [56]. The effect and significance of these factors on an innovation process will vary, depending on the type of an innovation. For instance, in case of IT tools for end users, critical factors might include the characteristics of the users who actually use the products, the host organization, or the external environment. Whereas, when it comes to IT process innovation, IS organization as well as the IT industry will have a more direct effect. Given such characteristics, factors meaningful to the adoption and assimilation of OO technology, can be narrowed down to three—IS organization, technology, and IT industry. Characteristics of an IS organization, such as IT infrastructure, staff, man agers, and control systems, have an effect on identifying, adopting, and implementing an innovation [56]. Relevant aspects of a technology, such as comparative advantages and complexity, can influence its spread in an organization [57]. The major factors of the IT industry are the uncertainty of a specific IT field related to the technology, the role of the relevant IT field as a resource pool, and a source of knowledge on the technology [34]. These innovation attributes and organizational characteristics comprise the basis for our research model and hypotheses.

## Innovation Attributes

## Innovation Attributes of the Organizational Contex

Although some variance exists in the literature, several innovation attributes have been found to have significant relationships with the organizational assimilation of an innovation [57]. In an organizational innovation, relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility are the important attributes that are known to affect innovation assimilation [8, 12, 31, 35, 48, 55, 57]. This corresponds with the individual acceptance of technology model where the technology’s perceived usefulness and ease of use affect the user’s behavioral intention and ultimate system acceptance [1, 14].

## Innovation Attributes of the Environmental Context

In the IT industry, where technology changes rapidly, it is also important for an organization to understand an innovation in the technical trajectory of the IT industry [18]. Forces of an emerging technology and drag of the existing technology form the technology trajectory of the IT industry, which then influences organizational assimilation behaviors [36]. Organizations try to strike a balance between an emerging technology and its existing technology base. Without appropriate consideration of this issue, an organization runs the risk of becoming a captive customer of an inferior technology without market standards or economy of scale. Thus, managerial expectation for a certain technology’s future market trend is an important factor for innova tion assimilation.

The industry as a source of knowledge and resource is also important [22, 34]. An adopting organization discerns maturity of the technology in the IT industry, accu mulation of knowledge about the innovation, and procurement of experienced re sources for its implementation.

## Organizational Characteristics

Organizational characteristics for innovation assimilation were studied from multiple perspectives in the past research. Organization structures, such as specialization, cen tralization, formalization, formal or informal communication networks, organization policy, and innovative culture or receptivity toward change, were found to affect innovation assimilation in an organization [13, 34]. The innovation literature also deals with a broad array of organizational variables, ranging from primitive organizational measures, such as organization size, to a very complicated or abstract concept, such as managerial innovativeness.

This paper will explore these organizational variables from the innovation life cycle perspective. We will examine variables related to the organization’s need for innova tion, their exposure to innovation opportunities, and their innovation implementation capabilities, as well as variables related to the environment that can facilitate the innovation to mature and be assimilated into the organization.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Based on the theoretical discussion of the previous section, a research model on OO technology assimilation in IS organizations is proposed (Figure 1). The model defines the innovation attributes of OO technology and the characteristics of the IS organization as determinants of the assimilation of OO technology.

The innovation attributes reflect the environmental (IT industry) and organizational contexts. Attributes of the environmental context are expectation for market trend and maturity of technology. Attributes of the organizational context include technol ogy compatibility, relative benefits, and complexity.

The characteristics of IS organization reflect the innovation needs and the willingness of the IS organization. The selected characteristics are managerial innovativeness, intensity of new technology education, satisfaction with the existing technology, and average years of IT professional experience of IS staff.

To eliminate the spurious or interfering effects of external variables, IS organization size, industry type, and elapsed time since awareness of innovation were controlled. Since the level of OO methodology (for analysis and design activities) use may affect the main relationship of the research model, we set it as a moderating variable. The dependent variable is the level of OO technology assimilation in IS organizations.

## Expectation for Market Trend

Expectation for market trend is the degree of expectation that the target technology will be pervasively adopted in the industry in the future [22]. Decision-makers in an IS organization take into account both the technology characteristics and position of the technology in the industry trend when adopting it. In the case of a programming language, the position of the technology in the industry trend is important to secure investment in the enterprise-wide application software. Transition to OO technology requires investment in training, replacing technology components, and attaining experience in the new technology. This investment is most beneficial when the adopted technology becomes dominant in the market. Getting abreast of the technology trend is also important for gaining support from the technology providers, as is avoiding the cost of not using the dominant technology [22].

H1: Expectation for market trend will be positively related to organizational OO technology assimilation.

## Maturity of Technology

Maturity of an innovation is a function of technology uncertainty and inexperience [35]. When adopting an emerging technology, immaturity of the technology can be a risk factor. Decision-makers, in most cases, tend to avoid the trial-by-error approach caused by adopting an immature technology. Maturity of technology is important for a programming language in gaining experienced development and maintenance resources from the community when the capacity of the resource pool is limited. When a technology is mature, an organization can gain quality resources more economically as the community accumulates experience in the new technology and can access support tools and related products more easily.

H2: Perceived maturity of technology will be positively related to organizational OO technology assimilation.

## Technology Compatibility

Compatibility is how the new technology is consistent with the existing values, experiences, and needs of the adopters [49]. Introducing a new technology requires changes to the existing development procedures and skills. One of the major reasons for slow acceptance of OO technology is the resistance to the paradigm shift [27, 45, 51, 54]. Development in an OO programming language requires a new way of thinking and a different approach. Modeling approaches and techniques may have to change [20, 21]. With lower compatibility, barriers to technology introduction, such as transition costs and resistance to change, would be higher and inhibit the new technology assimilation.

H3: Perceived technological compatibility with the existing technology will be positively related to organizational OO technology assimilation

## Relative Benefits

Relative benefit is the degree to which a new technology is perceived to be superior to the current technology [49]. Relative benefit was studied in the meta-analysis of innovation characteristics [57], open systems adoption [8], adoption of EDI (electronic data interchange) [31], and diffusion of EDI [48]. Primary decision criterion in a new technology adoption is the transition costs to the new technology and benefits from its introduction. When greater benefits are perceived compared to the current technology, it is more probable for an organization to adopt the new technology. Since OO technology promises unprecedented improvement in software develop ment productivity and quality, its perceived benefits will facilitate the organizationa assimilation.

## Complexity

Complexity is the difficulty in understanding, learning, and using a technology [57]. Despite its relative advantage, OO technology was expected to be a slow mover in the industry due to its inherent complexity and lack of compatibility [22]. OO technol ogy is also perceived to be more difficult in modeling and management [21, 45, 51]. In the similar context, OO technology is perceived to be an innovation that requires knowledge burden to potential adopters [2, 11]. Difficulties in understanding and applying a new technology may result in slower recognition of its value, fear of failure, and resistance.

H5: Perceived complexity will be negatively related to organizational OO technology assimilation.

## Managerial Innovativeness

Although some innovations bring direct and observable benefits to the adopting organization, others do not. Software process innovations are an extreme example of the latter. Process changes, like OO programming or structured design/structured programming, may not show explicit or immediate benefits to the adopting organization. Benefits of adopting OO technology may be observed in the long-term, with utilization of OO features and accumulation of the class library [22]. Furthermore, OO technology brings about fundamental changes to an organization. Research in radical and incremental innovation reveals that managerial attitude toward change and technical knowledge resource are important in radical innovation [16]. Managers who place emphasis on attaining leading-edge technology and are willing to take risks in decision-making constitute an environment that promotes innovation [36].

H6: Managerial innovativeness in an organization will be positively related to organizational OO technology assimilation.

## Intensity of New Technology Education

For technical innovations that present a knowledge barrier to the adopting organization, learning and organization knowledge perspectives are important [2]. Technica innovations that share these properties are OO technology, CASE, and other software process innovations.

Fichman and Kemerer [23] concluded that learning-related factors, such as learning-related economy of scale, greater knowledge related to the focal innovation, and diversity of technical knowledge, determine the assimilation stage of OO programming languages. A key construct related to learning and organizational knowledge [29, 30] in the innovation research is absorptive capacity. Cohen and Levinthal [11] defined absorptive capacity as the organizational capability to recognize the value of innovation and assimilate it to productive ends. Boyton et al. [7] empirically studied the relationship between absorptive capacity and IT use, and concluded that manage rial IT knowledge was dominant in predicting the organization’s IT use. Organizational knowledge formulates the basis for searching innovation opportunities and sets constraints for organizational decisions and actions. An organization with higher intensity of new technology education is more likely to obtain the necessary IT knowledge it needs to introduce an IT innovation such as OO technology.

H7: Intensity of new technology education will be positively related to organiza tional OO technology assimilation.

Satisfaction with the Existing Technology

Frequently, a new technology is introduced to fill the performance gap arising from the current technology [36, 49]. Requirements that cannot be met with the current technology, or can be implemented more effectively with the new technology, in terms of functionality, flexibility, and speed of development, can expedite the new technology’s introduction [8].

H8: Satisfaction with the existing technology will be negatively related to organizational OO technology assimilation.

## Average Years of IT Professional Experience of IS Staff

Staff with long IT professional experience and those with short experience are expected to have different attitudes toward a new technology like the OO programming language [45]. New IS professionals are familiar with OO concepts and techniques, since most of them learn OO programming languages, such as C++, in their programming courses. Frequently, they do not get to experience the traditional functional languages at all. On the other hand, experienced IS staff may have to sacrifice most of their programming experience to adopt the OO programming language. Thus, it is expected that organizations with staff composed of short IT professional experience are likely to adopt the OO programming language early and assimilate it more actively.

H9: Average years of IT professional experience of IS staff will be negatively related to organizational OO technology assimilation.

## Assimilation Stage: Dependent Variable

Assimilation of innovation is the process spanning from an organization’s first awareness of an innovation to its potential acquisition and widespread deployment [23, 40, 47]. The process consists of awareness, interest, evaluation, trial, commitment, and finally, deployment of the new technology. Assimilation focuses not only on adoption or deployment rate, but also on the earliness of the initiation of assimilation activities, speed of assimilation activities, and absence of rejection, stalling, or discontinuance [23, 40]. An organization may consider adoption or trial of a technology and later decide to reject the technology.

## Level of OO Methodology Use: Moderating Effect

With different levels of OO methodology use, it is expected that the above determinants would affect OO technology assimilation differently [3]. When adopting an OO programming language along with an OO analysis and design methodology, stability and reference cases of the OO technology application in the industry are more important than when adopting only an OO programming language. This is because the level of investment and the risk of introducing a new technology are greater in the former case. Here, maturity of technology is expected to have a stronger relationship to organizational assimilation of OO technology.

Similarly, when adopting both an OO methodology and an OO programming language, perceptions on complexity are expected to be higher because additional knowl edge is required for mastering the technology. The ability to evaluate and utilize outside knowledge is largely a function of the level of prior related knowledge [11, 23]. Adopting and implementing an OO methodology and programming language requires a higher degree of new knowledge [16, 21]. Accordingly, complexity and intensity of technology education would have a stronger relationship to organizational assimilation of OO technology when adopting both the methodology and language.

Implementing an OO technology takes time to get tangible results, since assimilation of an OO programming language requires investment in training, experience in the new technology, and replacement of the existing system. Thus, managerial innovativeness would be more important for organizational assimilation of OO technology for the case of methodology plus language adoption.

H10: Level of OO methodology use moderates the relationship between indepen dent variables and dependent variable.

## Control Variables

Some variables that are expected to affect the dependent variable outside the scope of this research were selected as control variables. They are industry type, IS organization size, and elapsed time since awareness.

Depending on the industry to which a certain organization belongs, importance of IT within the organization might differ. Such difference will have an effect on the adoption and spread of a new technology such as OO technology. Also task characteristics of a specific industry would result in different functional or performance requirements fo IT, thus it could facilitate or inhibit a certain technology’s assimilation.

Other factors within an organization, such as slack resource [43] and specialization [34], can also have an effect on the adoption and spread of technologies [13]. IS organization size is likely to be related to organization characteristics such as slack resource, specialization, and others that are beyond our research scope. To contro these factors, IS organization size was set as a control variable.

Since different organizations may be exposed to the same innovation at different points in time, and any innovation may get assimilated to some extent over time, we decided to control the “elapsed time since awareness” to eliminate the potential spurious effect of time in organizational assimilation of a technology [48].

## Research Method

THE UNIT OF ANALYSIS OF THIS STUDY IS THE IS ORGANIZATION. We assume that a decision-maker’s perception of a technology affects its assimilation in an organization. Respondents were IT professionals in charge of assessing and selecting a programming language for their IS organizations.

## Development of Measures

Operationalization of the independent variables was based on the existing literature on OO technology characteristics [Table 1, Appendix A]. Items for relative benefits and complexity were developed by adapting Davis’s [14] measures for perceived usefulness and ease of use for OO technology characteristics. The instrument for measuring the assimilation stage was adopted from the existing literature on OO programming language assimilation [23].

## Construction of Questionnaire

A structured questionnaire was used to measure the variables. The respondents were asked to provide information on their IS organization’s programming languages and the percentage of the applications developed in each language. Languages such a C++, Java, Delphi, Developer 2000, SQL Windows, and PowerBuilder were classified as OO programming languages. Support of object, class, encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism, and message passing was used as the criteria for determining the programming language’s object-orientedness. Visual Basic was excluded because the version of the language at the time of our data collection did not support some of the core OO features such as inheritance. Whereas some researchers confine OO programming languages to pure OO languages such as C++, Smalltalk, Object C, this study extended the range of OO programming languages based on the criteria mentioned above to reflect the importance and interests of the IS organizations. To exclude cases for nonvoluntary adoptions, respondents were asked whether the adoption decisions made by their IS organizations were voluntary or not.

## Results

THE DATA COLLECTION WAS CARRIED OUT in the form of a field survey. The primary source of the sampling frame was a list of the 1,000 largest firms based on revenue reported in an influential economic daily in Korea. We removed the system integrator companies, and complemented the company lists with the Chief Information Officers (CIO) mailing list from a local IT magazine. Finally, the survey questionnaires were mailed to 903 IS department managers of the companies. Perceptions of the managers who were in charge of assessing and selecting a new programming language were measured. Therefore, the manager of the IS department, manager of IS planning, or team leader of development was asked to complete the survey. Following the Total Design Method (TDM) of Dilman [17], a postcard follow-up was conducted three weeks after the original mailing and the same questionnaires were mailed again five weeks after the original mailing. The survey was performed between July and Sep tember 1999.

After the three rounds of solicitations, 236 responses were received, representing a response rate of about 26 percent. In Korea, the average response rate for a mail survey is around 15 percent. Among the respondents, 16 responses were eliminated from analysis due to incomplete data, and 220 responses were used for the final analysis. Among the discarded 16 responses, 7 cases were eliminated for nonvoluntary adoption. The respondents and nonrespondents were compared [17] with regard to two key organizational features: total revenue and number of employees. We randomly selected 50 companies from the nonrespondents and compared their total sales and number of employees with those of all respondents, respondents from the first mailing, and respondents from the second mailing. The results of the t-test showed no difference in all four comparisons (total return versus nonrespondent, first return versus nonrespondent, second return versus nonrespondent, and first return vs. second return), suggesting the findings from the study sample can be generalized to the sampling frame used for the research.

## Sample Characteristics

In Table 2, profiles of the respondent companies by industry, by revenue, by IS organization size, by primary OO programming language, and average years of IT profes sional experience are shown. Manufacturing firms comprise 44.5 percent of the companies and 16.8 percent are financial companies. The most frequent range for the number of IS people was from 20 to 50 (25 percent). In the profile of average years of IT professional experience of staff, 5 to below 7 (44 percent), was the most frequent range, followed by the 7 to 10 (29 percent) range. In Table 3, a profile of the respondents by position is shown. Most of the respondents were managers or directors.

Among the OO programming languages, PowerBuilder (86 cases) and Delphi (34 cases) were used most frequently as the primary language. The most frequent OO pro gramming languages the respondents planned to use in the future were JAVA and C++.

About 45.5 percent were using an OO methodology. Out of the 124 companies with at least limited deployment stage, 117 (94.4 percent) of the total companies were using class libraries from a moderate to high degree. Regarding the specific methodology, 35 companies were using an in-house OO methodology, 19 companies used the OO information engineering [39], 18 companies used UML (unified modeling language) [6], and 15 used Coad and Yourdon’s OO methodology [9, 10]. Among them, Information Engineering [38] was the most frequently used (52 cases) methodology, followed by the structured analysis and design method [15] (12 cases). In 50 percent of the organizations, external consulting firms or service companies influenced selection of their program ming language. This indicates the importance of consulting and service companies in introducing a new information technology to an organization [2]. Education on OO concept/usage and OO programming was performed in about 70 percent of the sample organizations. Education on OO analysis and design was performed in 50.9 percent of the organizations.

## Reliability and Validity of Measurement Instrument

Table 4 shows the results of the survey instruments’ reliability and validity test. Before the survey, content validity was tested through reviews by IS professionals with at least five years of industrial experience. A pilot survey was performed with the 44 organizations that do not overlap with the organizations in the main survey. For internal consistency, Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.7787 to 0.9330. For explorative research, it is recommended that the alpha value exceed 0.6. ## Discussion

## OO Technology Diffusion

OUR RESPONSE PROFILE SUGGESTS that approximately 50 percent of the companies currently apply OO technology at a limited scope or have not introduced it at all, with the remaining 50 percent putting OO technology to an enterprise-wide use. When the research was conducted, OO technology usage was moving toward mid and latter stages of innovation diffusion. This provides the backdrop against which the results of this study may be interpreted, as well as offering a comparison with the outcome of a set of research executed at the earlier stage of OO technology diffusion [23].

## Innovation Attributes of the Environmental Context

Although the hypothesis on technology maturity (H2) was supported, the hypothesis on expectation for the market trend (H1) was dismissed. The dismissal of H1 may have been due to the fact that, at the time of the survey, OO technology diffusion in the Korean IT industry was already high, and most of the IS groups perceived that OO technology would become a dominant and standardized technology for IS development in the future. Expectation for market trend for a new technology may have a higher effect on organizational assimilation at the early stage of the technology’s diffusion when there exists a wider range of expectation values among the different organizations.

Support for the technology maturity (H2) indicates that either perception of higher technology maturity leads to the higher assimilation level or, as the organizations get more assimilated with a technology, they tend to believe the technology to be more mature.

## Innovation Attributes of the Organizational Contex

Rejection of hypothesis on compatibility (H3) suggests that, at the time of the survey, there was no significant relationship between compatibility and the level of assimilation. This may have been due to the unique nature of the organizational adoption of a programming language. Although organizations tend to operate a fairly diverse, heterogeneous set of hardware components (mainframe, UNIX servers, NT servers, disk drives, network equipment, and so on), many of them adopt and use one programming language as their primary development standard. Thus, whereas compatibility may be a critical factor in new hardware assimilation, when organizations replace the existing, old programming language with an OO programming language in a relatively short period of migration by outsourcing or recruiting/retraining OO develop ment personnel, compatibility with the existing programming language may not affect the OO technology assimilation process.

The relative benefits construct failed to contribute to assimilation. Among the innovation diffusion research, relative benefits had different findings in different technol ogy contexts [8, 24, 31, 48, 57]. This leads to the theoretical question: What is different about this context from other contexts where this construct was relevant? When a technology is expected to be dominant, the primary consideration for adopting the technology would be how an organization could implement it effectively and efficiently. Thus, they would focus on their readiness to implement the innovation, on good references, standardization, and skilled resources, rather than on relative benefits. Thus, we may reason that, when a technology is perceived to be dominant, the maturity level of the technology and knowledge level of an organization exert greater influence in the adoption decisions of an organization than the intrinsic advantages of the technology.

Moreover, in the case of an OO programming language, its main relative benefits, such as high software quality and increased development productivity, can materialize only after IS organizations accumulate the critical mass of software objects and can reuse them for the next rounds of IS development projects. This delayed percep tion of relative benefits may provide explanation on the rejection of the hypothesis on relative benefits (H4).

Complexity (H5), to our surprise, turned out to have a positive relationship with assimilation. Whereas the conventional wisdom suggests that difficulty in learning, acquiring, and mastering technology becomes a hurdle to adopting an innovative technology [57], the current study shows that a highly assimilated group has a relatively high awareness of technology complexity. This result leads us to interpret that, contrary to our belief, inherent complexity of OO technology may become obvious and distinct with more use of the technology.

As the development methodologies of OO technology seem to be seamlessly connected throughout the entire development life cycle, they require excessive and com plicated definitions at the initial stage, especially in the development of a large-sized system. The fact that these definitions are not properly linked to the implementation phase and there are no appropriate templates or examples [45], might serve as a main cause. Another reason might be found in the prevalent practice in the development field where the efforts to set up and familiarize with the management procedure are overlooked, with emphasis only being placed on generating the outcome first. In many cases, projects are launched even before the OO development methodologies are well understood. Thus, IS organizations seem to realize the fundamental complexity of OO technology as they move through higher assimilation stages.

## Organizational Characteristics

The hypothesis on the managerial innovativeness (H6) was not supported. Although a full implementation of OO technology should require support and innovative attitude of the management and supervisors, as well as substantial investment, it could not be verified by the current research. This may be explained by the fact that, unlike in the United States, most CIOs of Korean organizations came from a manageria background, frequently serving as the chief financial officer or chief operations officer along with the CIO duty. Few of them are lifetime IT professionals. This may have reduced their role in affecting the organization’s technology assimilation.

Training on the new technology (H7) was significantly related to assimilation. In the IS department, the most common method for obtaining knowledge is through training courses. In particular, when it comes to OO technology with a high knowledge barrier, intensity of training on the new technology is directly associated with the overall knowledge level of the organization, acceptance of new technology, and the capability to use it. How members of an IS organization are trained can be a fundamental base in understanding the assimilation of IT innovation in the organization.

The hypothesis on satisfaction with the existing technology (H8) was supported. A predicted, IS organizations at more assimilated stages expressed higher levels of dissatisfaction with productivity, quality, flexibility, and implementation functionality of the traditional programming languages.

The average years of IT professional experience of IS staff (H9) appears to have no association with assimilation. It was anticipated that more experienced IS staff would resist changes driven by the new technology, but this study could not find such association.

## Moderating Variables

We found that the level of methodology use (H10) significantly interacted with the relationships between independent variables and OO technology assimilation. It was meaningful to distinguish different adoption patterns since the OO technology assimilation process may proceed differently under the different adoption contexts [3]. With higher level use of OO methodology, an organization can utilize OO technology for its full potential. On the other hand, in the event that OO methodology is not fully employed, the advantages of OO programming language would not be completely appreciated.

The result suggests that adopting both methodology and programming language requires different levels of knowledge and support from the management. Technology maturity seems to play a bigger role in such a case.

## Meta-Analysis of Innovation Diffusion and Assimilation Research

Since this study was executed in the mid and latter stages of OO technology diffusion, a comparable observation with other research conducted at the early part of the diffusion can be made. In the initial stage, there were several different anticipations toward dominance of OO technology. In particular, there were many differences in the prospects on its application in the IS department’s development work. OO technology was perceived as a technology that can be applied in only limited areas, such as GUI, and in applications that have complex data types. Thus, IS managers did not have confidence in the idea that the technology will be used to develop the business applications. Fichman et al. [22] categorized OO technology as an experimental technology. Furthermore, an assimilation survey conducted in 1994 shows that 87 percent of the surveyed companies were positioned from the awareness stage to the evaluation/trial stage [23].

This study shows that 82 percent of the surveyed organizations are concentrated in the stages from commitment to general deployment. The average of “expectation for the market trend” construct is 4.1 on a scale of 5, illustrative of the fact that organizations tend to agree on the dominance of the OO technology.

In addition, we suggest that during the technology-dissemination process, the poin of time when the study was conducted has an effect on the interpretation of the results. The past studies on innovation diffusion dealt with technology attributes, such as relative benefits, difficulties, and complexity, but each of the variables showed different significance in different contexts [8, 24, 31, 48, 57]. In this study, traditional innova tion attributes, such as relative benefits and compatibility, did not contribute to the assimilation process. What differences in each context can explain this result? Studies on innovation assimilation are conducted at a specific point in time across early to late stages of the specific innovation’s diffusion. In the end, technology grows into the dominant position in the market or finds the niche [22]. At the initial or conception stages, it is difficult to have a common agreement on whether the technology will become dominant or not. Most of the decision-makers at this stage will focus more on the immediate benefits of the technology and its outcomes [4]. It will be easier for an organization that already possesses a similar technology to accept the new technology [4]. As time passes, when the dominance of the technology is established, the factors that influence the decision-making process will differ from those in the initial stage. When it is already taken for granted that the new technology will replace the current one, what is emphasized is how the organization can most effectively introduce the new technology. Organizations will need to check whether the technology is standardized within the industry, whether there are cases they can leverage to minimize risks [36], and whether they have reached a certain level of knowledge base that is necessary to effectively implement the technology [2]. In summary, we suggest that the two considerations in interpreting the innovation diffusion and assimilation research are the diffusion status of the industry when the research was conducted, and whether the technology was dominant or not at that time.

## Conclusions and Future Research

CONSIDERING THE EFFECT OF OO TECHNOLOGY on IS development and management, understanding the assimilation of OO technology is important for practitioners and academics. IS managers are wrestling about the proper level of adoption of OO technology, and this study can help them to understand the situation and future position ing. Organizations that are considering the adoption or deployment of OO technology need to understand how technology and organization-specific factors affect the OO technology assimilation process. This way, they can prepare against the barriers expected and can utilize the facilitators for successful OO technology implementation. Contrary to the theoretical expectations, technology complexity was positively related to assimilation of OO technology. Therefore, it seems necessary to introduce an appropriate migration plan along with sufficient education to mitigate the perceived complexity of OO technology. Significant organizational learning is required to overcome the compatibility and complexity problems and utilize the OO technology. This capability is built over extended periods through investment in research and develop ment [11]. It is both time-consuming and cost-inefficient to develop the knowledge base of all interested parties to apply the technology. In this regard, the leading group should set up the methodologies and management structures that fit the organization, run a pilot project for assurance, improve on the application of the technology, and disseminate it in a phased manner [20].

There are some limitations in this study. In measuring the innovation attributes and organizational characteristics, a cross-sectional analysis was performed. Thus, the result of the survey may have been affected by the fact that perception of technology changes as the organization uses the technology. At higher assimilation stages, the perception would be based more on experience whereas, at lower assimilation stages, such perceptions may have also been based on their expectation as well as experience. This study used the survey sample limited to Korean IS organizations. Between countries, maturity of a certain technology and the decision process for adopting a technol ogy could be different. This difference may affect the assumptions in this research. The cases in the survey sample are mainly concentrated on commitment to general deployment stage (82 percent). We believe that the concentration of samples in particular stages reflects the situation in Korea at the time of the survey. Thus, our results may not be generalizable for situations with a significantly different diffusion status.

Future work on the assimilation of OO technology needs to consider individual level acceptance [52] and use to complement the organizational level assimilation research. In addition, in order to fully utilize OO technology, the academic circle should develop methodologies that will make its application easier and emphasize the development of templates and examples for OO business systems. If OO methodologies are improved into a parsimonious method, application of the methodology and integration of the analysis/design phase and the implementation phase will be come easier. Finally, although this study could not check the validity of the expectation of market trend variable because of the high convergence of the variable, future works on other technology at the initial stage of diffusion can study whether this attribute acts as a critical criterion in determining the introduction of the technology.
