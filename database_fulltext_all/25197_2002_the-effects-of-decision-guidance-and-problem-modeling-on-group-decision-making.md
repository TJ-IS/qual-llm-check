# The Effects of Decision Guidance and Problem Modeling on Group Decision-Making

REZA BARKHI

REZA BARKHI is an assistant professor in the Department of Accounting and Information Systems at Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University. He obtained his Ph.D., MBA, and MA from the Business School at Ohio State University. He also obtained his B.S. in CIS from College of Engineering in the same university. He has published in such journals as Decision Support Systems, Group Decision & Negotia tion, European Journal of Operational Research, Journal of Educational Technology Systems, Computers and OR, Location Science, and Information & Management. He has also published and presented his work in major national and international conferences. He is a member of Decision Sciences, INFORMS, AIS, and other professiona organizations. He has served on the program committees of major national and international conferences.

ABSTRACT: Despite the advances in group decision support system (GDSS) research, few GDSS studies concentrate on problem-modeling tools to support decisions that cross boundaries of functional areas within the business. These decisions have a substantial effect on the profitability of the firm and account for much time and effort of senior management. This research investigates the effect of problem structuring and modeling with a GDSS on coordinated decision-making of managers in a group faced with a mixed-motive production-planning task. In a laboratory experiment, a GDSS with features supporting problem modeling is contrasted with a GDSS without such features. The results indicate that the groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool outperformed the groups using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool, but they were less efficient with respect to the time and number of messages it takes the group to converge to a final solution. User confidence in the solution did not differ between the two groups. The results of this study indicate that the problem modeling feature of a GDSS significantly influences group decision process and outcomes.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group decision support systems, human-computer interaction, problem modeling.

GROUP DECISION SUPPORT SYSTEMS (GDSS) utilize the computing and communication capabilities of computers to provide communication and decision technologies for group interaction and collaboration. Providing decision aid to individuals in a group is more difficult than providing decision aid to an individual who does not need to get the consensus of others [13, 23]. Each individual in a group has his own set of objectives, private information, and interpretation of the problem. Nevertheless, he has to develop a shared interpretation of the problem consistent with the interpretation of the other members. Therefore, although GDSS should enable the necessarily collaborative process of group decision-making, GDSS systems often lack features supporting the critical activity of problem modeling for collaborating individuals in a group. GDSS features should aid individuals in a group to exchange information and make coordinated decisions.

Although a GDSS may help disseminate information to group members, it canno force the group to “think” [7]. Thinking is a form of information processing and providing a problem-modeling tool that will process the input data parameters to gener ate information can aid the group members to “think.”However, group members often do not “process” the information they receive during the group problem solving interaction [8]. The effectiveness of a problem-modeling tool depends on the quality of the underlying parameters. When members in a group representing different functiona areas collaborate to formulate a model and solve it, they each have to provide some parameters of the model. A GDSS with a problem-modeling tool cannot be an effective group decision aid if group members do not share their private information. When members do not share information and the problem is modeled for each individual based on some estimate of each member’s private information rather than the aggregation of the shared information, each member’s search of the solution space results in an individual local optimal solution rather than the Pareto optimal solution.

The Pareto optimal solution is a solution that is optimal in a multiple participant solution space. That is, an increase in the payoff to anyone will diminish the payoff to at least one other person. The Pareto optimal solution can only be identified if the information distributed across the group is properly processed and integrated into the model of the problem. Providing a problem-modeling tool for a group can provide value, if the members share information and build consensus. Alternatively, it can lead the group to a set of individual optimal solutions, thus making the convergence to a mutually acceptable Pareto optimal solution more difficult. This research investigates the effect of a problem-modeling tool of a GDSS on coordinated decision making of managers faced with a mixed-motive production planning task.

When each member of a group uses unshared private information, he may find it difficult to construct a shared understanding of the problem that facilitates collaboration and convergence. Problem modeling involves organization of certain elements of reality into an abstract structure that exhibits features and characteristics of the reality [21]. Problem modeling involves postulating what the variables in a problem are and how these variables fit together and interact [17]. An example of problem modeling is a mathematical model of a problem, which clearly identifies the decision variables, the objective function, and the constraints. Modeling problems when mem bers have asymmetrical information and different objectives can result in different models of the problem.

Although problem modeling is a standard feature of many individual decision support systems (DSS) (such as, Excel Solver, Lindo, SPSS, and so on), it is not a standard feature of many GDSS (for classifications based on features see [9, 15])—we acknowledge that some popular GDSS (such as, GroupSystems) are classified as systems that have problem-modeling capabilities, but in almost all published studies, the full-fledged modeling capabilities have not been used. The majority of today’s GDSS used in research are Level One GDSS—that is, the emphasis is on the communication, rather than decision aiding aspects of group interaction [10]. The decision aid component of the GDSS has received scant attention in the literature, and whether the decision aids lead to greater decision-making effectiveness, efficiency, or both, is an open question [25].

We suggest two opposite arguments about the usefulness of a problem-modeling tool of a GDSS. On one hand, a GDSS that aids individuals to model a problem helps the group to properly exchange and process the information distributed among the group members. On the other hand, a GDSS that aids individuals to model a problem can result in a search for individual optimal solutions, thereby making convergence to a group solution more difficult. Hence, the usefulness of a problem-modeling tool is not as clear in a GDSS setting as it is in a DSS setting.

The next section describes problem modeling in groups. In the third section, a set of hypotheses on the effect of a problem-modeling tool in group decision-making is presented. The fourth section describes the experiment. The fifth section presents the results of the experiment. The sixth section summarizes the findings and their impli cations for the design and use of the next level GDSS systems, and the seventh section concludes the paper and suggests future research directions.

## Problem Modeling in Groups

A DSS IS DESIGNED TO DIMINISH THE DYSFUNCTIONS associated with information overload, incomplete use of information, and incomplete task analysis. In addition to diminishing these dysfunctions, a GDSS should deal with coordination problems, conformance pressure, evaluation apprehension, free riding, cognitive inertia, socializing, and domination [16]. The use of GDSS has been advocated to facilitate group decision-making by diminishing group process losses and magnifying group process gains [1].

Studies on individual decision-makers have shown that quantitative modeling tools improve performance [2, 3]. Such tools may improve process gains by reducing the problems arising from incomplete task analysis, incomplete use of information, and information overload. Appropriate models identify the important decision variables, the objective function, and the constraints, and may reduce information overload. DSS models help decision-makers to distinguish between the “trivial many” and the “significant few.” Although problem modeling and quantitative tools have been studied in the individual decision-making literature, they have been overlooked in the GDSS literature.

A group is not just the aggregation of individuals [13]. Hence, it is not clear if the results of the studies that have found value in problem-modeling tools for individua DSS users can be extended to GDSS users. In a group decision-making situation (that is, GDSS), models require input from multiple decision-makers. The models used in GDSS may not be effective if decision-makers misrepresent or bias the model’s parameters. This may happen if members are not cooperative [13].

We compare a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool and one without a problemmodeling tool. The problem-modeling tool we study in this paper provides a systematic search of the solution space (such as, corner points of the solution polytope such as performed in linear programming solvers). The GDSS that does not provide a problem-modeling tool provides a “what-if” capability that is a heuristic search technique. A what-if method scans the solution space by allowing the members to incrementally change the parameters and evaluate the solutions that are in the vicinity of their current solution in the solution space. This search is usually done in a semi-random fashion and often will not identify the best solution. Hence, systematic search of the solution space incorporated in a problem-modeling tool is preferred to incremental approaches like what-if or other greedy heuristics. It is noted that there are some problems like matroid problems (that is, minimum spanning tree), where the greedy heuristic results in the optimal solution. One version of the GDSS provides decision guidance, and the other version imposes a restricted version of the GDSS. These different features are expected to influence the behavior of the users differently [22].

The systematic search of the solution space implies that the use of a problem-modeling tool is likely to result in better solutions relative to the use of what-if methods alone. However, if the group members do not exchange truthful information to incorporate in the model, a problem-modeling tool can lead the group to misguided local optimal solutions. This could subsequently undermine the usefulness of a problemmodeling tool of a GDSS.

Given that decision-makers adapt their strategies to the decision aids [24], it is important to understand how decision aids influence solution strategy, and subsequently solution quality. A decision aid for individual decision-makers (that is, DSS) may impose an induced restrictiveness on solution strategy [4]. For example, spreadsheet use imposes an incremental solution strategy. Given that specific tools may affect the solution strategy of the users, it is important to investigate how various tools affect the solution strategy and decision process of users.

Previous research on individual DSS has generally reported positive benefits associated with the use of problem-modeling tools compared to “what-if” methods [4]. However, in group decision-making situations, one member’s optimal solution is generally not acceptable by others. Furthermore, individuals may use their individual optimal solutions as their reservation price—not subject to negotiation. This may make convergence to a group solution difficult. Hence, it is not clear if a problemmodeling tool in a group decision setting is a useful tool. If the members use the individual optimal solutions as a reservation price, then a problem-modeling tool may induce each member to stay close to his optimal solution, and thereby miss an alternative solution that may be better for all members. On the other hand, if members exchange truthful information with others, a problem-modeling tool can help them to improve their solution strategy and subsequently solution quality.

## Solution Strategy

A decision aid may induce a particular solution strategy on the user [24]. For example, spreadsheet use induces an incremental solution strategy [4]. Providing both a problem-modeling and a what-if tool may induce a two-phase strategy. In the first phase, a group member uses a problem-modeling tool to find the optimal solution from his perspective, using his private information and his interpretation of the problem. Note that in a group problem-solving situation, the notion of optimality needs to be qualified because a solution that optimizes one objective, in general, will not optimize other objectives [5, 23]. The set of Pareto-optimal solutions consists of solutions that cannot be dominated by another solution.

After group members independently find their individual optimal solutions by us ing a problem-modeling tool, they start phase II. In the second phase, they use the what-if tool to evaluate the neighborhood of individual optimal solutions to converge to a consensus solution. Members in a group may share information that may reveal their preferences. Based on the information members receive from others, they may make inferences about the preferences of others and adjust their solution to identif compromise solutions. This may reduce equivocality [6] and enhance convergence to a mutually acceptable solution. Equivocality is reduced as each member makes incremental modifications to the problem parameters or revises his expectations of what constitutes an acceptable solution. This process may help each group member to identify the commonalities in his view with that of others in an attempt to form a consolidated perspective and a group view of the problem [18]. Such a convergent approach may help the group to reach a mutually acceptable solution [11].

Convergent approaches promote high consensus, whereas divergent approaches can lead to conflict that hinders the ability of the group to achieve consensus and commitment to a final solution [11]. A divergent approach promotes improved quality of outcomes, as it encourages a more comprehensive understanding of the issues. However, because it hinders the ability of the group to achieve consensus, the problem solving process may not converge. The dilemma resulting from the trade-off between decision quality and convergence may be overcome by following a two phase strategy. In the first phase, each individual generates solutions from his perspective, and in the second phase each individual shares his solutions with others and evaluates other individuals’ proposed solutions [14].

Following a two-phase strategy, each member, in groups supported by a GDSS with both a problem-modeling tool and a what-if tool, iterates between a divergen phase and a convergent phase until at some point the convergent phase leads to a solution that is acceptable to the group. Next, we describe the steps in a divergen phase and a convergent phase.

Phase I: Divergent Phase (Problem-modeling tool)

 find one’s individual optimal solution using a problem-modeling tool.

Phase II: Convergent Phase (What-if tool)

 propose solution to others,

 check the neighborhood of solutions using the what-if tool,

 evaluate the solutions that others propose,

 check the neighborhood of the solutions proposed,

 update private information with the new information.

Each member can evaluate the solutions proposed to him utilizing the what-if capability and can make incremental changes to the solution, in an attempt to converge to a mutually acceptable solution. Each time the group members optimize with new parameters, they “land” in a new region of the solution space. Then, using the whatif capability, they perform a local search around that optimal solution to see if any solutions exist that are in the vicinity of the optimal solutions and are also acceptable to others. This process continues until the group decides that the best solution identified thus far should be accepted, so that the problem-solving process may converge.

Each decision-maker using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool can only use the what-if capability. She does not have a problem-modeling tool to find her individual optimal solution. Given that a problem-modeling tool may cause each decision-maker to rigidly stick to her individual optimal solution (that is, viewed as a reservation price), the GDSS with the what-if tool alone may make convergence more efficient than the GDSS with a problem-modeling tool. However, a problem-model ing tool and a what-if tool together induce a two-phase solution strategy that is likely to result in an improved solution.

Groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool should more effectively search the solution space, if they exchange truthful information. Use of a problem-modeling tool and the two-phase strategy improve problem identification and understanding that can result in an improved solution. The groups that only have the what-if capability are more likely to remain in one region of the solution space and generate less diverse solutions—solutions that are similar to other solutions—given that the whatif capability is used for making incremental changes to a previous solution. The groups without a problem-modeling tool are more likely to converge faster because they wil generate less diverse solutions and search only one region of the solution space. Each individual in these groups does not know her optimal solution to use it as a reservation price. They do not evaluate a diverse set of solutions, and are likely to converge to a lower quality solution relative to those who use a two-phase method. The next section presents a set of hypotheses based on these decision theoretic concepts.

## Hypotheses

Effects on Solution

THE GROUPS WITH A GDSS that provides both a problem-modeling tool and a what-if tool are more likely to generate more diverse solutions than groups that incrementall move in the vicinity of a solution using only the what-if capability. Selecting a com promise solution involves less conflict and less equivocality when one can use a problem-modeling tool that supports a systematic search of solution space that leads to a larger set of diverse solutions [14]. A GDSS that promotes a two-phase problem solving strategy should lead the users to a higher quality solution than a GDSS that only supports the what-if strategy.

The groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool follow a two-phase solution strategy and will generate more diverse solutions than groups using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool. The latter groups incrementally move in the vicinity of local optimal solutions. This suggests the following hypothesis:

H1a: Members in groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool will generate more diverse solutions than those using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool.

The GDSS with a problem-modeling tool facilitates a systematic search of the solution space and promotes a two-phase solution strategy. This should lead to a higher quality solution [11] that will be reflected in a higher task performance, suggesting the following hypothesis:

H1b: Member task performance, on average, will be higher in groups supported by a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool than in those supported by a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool.

In order to have a better insight into the nature of the group outcomes, Sambamurthy and Poole [19] recommend a process-oriented approach. A process variable related to solution quality in groups where members have asymmetrical information may be the level of truthfulness in the information that is exchanged among members. This is directly related to solution quality, given that the information exchanged is used to formulate the model of the problem. Furthermore, exchanging truthful private infor mation is the only way to identify the Pareto optimal solution for the group. Each group member using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool can identify the best solution from his perspective. Hence, each member knows what constitutes a good solution for him. A member using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool canno easily identify his best solution. The members using a problem-modeling tool are provided with task-specific templates that explicitly ask the group members to supply their private information so that the collaborative model can be constructed. The problem-modeling tool provides a systematic way of exchanging information using the task-specific templates that request model parameters from the members. It has been previously shown that members in groups either do not exchange information or do not process the information that is exchanged during the meeting [8]. The taskspecific templates that explicitly ask them to provide their private information should be useful. Although both GDSS provide these task-specific templates, the GDSS with a modeling tool requests that members fill the task-specific templates by providing the model parameters. This should enhance information exchange among members. Thus, we suggest the following hypothesis:

H1c: Members of groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool will repor their private information to others more truthfully than members of groups using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool.

Effects on Perception

How individuals perceive their group process and outcomes can be important indicators of the success of a decision technique [20]. Decision models and techniques can be implemented as GDSS features, and individual confidence in the solution influenced by specific GDSS features can be an important indicator of the success of the GDSS.

Each individual in a group using a problem-modeling tool can find her optimal solution and propose it to others. Members may find the merit of solutions proposed by others and perform what-if analysis to search the neighborhood of those solutions. Thi generates more candidate solutions. When each decision-maker optimizes with her data parameters and proposes the solutions to others, all group members generate different solutions in parallel. Hence, they search the solution space more thoroughly. Given that the decision-makers consider many different alternatives, they are likely to become more confident with a solution that they select from many different alternatives. In other words, the alternative generation phase will improve as a result of using the problem-modeling tool of the GDSS. Decision-makers may react more positively toward the group interaction process and develop a higher confidence in the solution when they generate more solutions and have more alternatives to select from. Therefore, a problem-modeling tool should positively affect a user’s confidence in the solution.

Confidence in the solution may be affected by at least two factors. First, do the decision-makers believe that there exists a better solution compared to the compromise solution they have adopted? Second, how much better is the best solution that they can identify relative to the solution that they finally negotiate with others and adopt? If each member is aware of a solution that is much better than the mutually adopted solution, then having to compromise to accept the mutual solution can result in lower confidence in the solution. That is, a member’s confidence in the solution is likely to increase as the deviation between the solution adopted by the group and her most preferred solution widens.

When the system enables the group to generate many solutions and the group members find that everyone is compromising their most preferred solution, they may be confident that the solution adopted is a best compromise solution, given the diverse set of solutions. Therefore, the groups using the GDSS with a problem-modeling tool will generate more diverse solutions, making the adoption of a compromise solution easier. This should result in higher confidence in the solution for groups that have a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool than those that have a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H2: Member confidence in the solution will be higher in groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool than those using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool.

Effects on Efficiency

Problem-modeling capability directs the conduct of information search and helps reduce equivocality, making the process more efficient [14]. As individuals with a problem-modeling tool generate optimal solutions and use the what-if capability to search the vicinity of the optimal solutions, they learn new information about the solution space. This information may be used to revise the model parameters, find new solutions, and continue searching the vicinity of the new solutions for new compromise solutions.

On the other hand, the GDSS without a problem-modeling tool facilitates the construction of new solutions by incremental revisions to previous solutions. This incremental solution strategy will prevent the search from jumping from one region of the search space to other regions that are far away, and hence, will result in searching for solutions that are similar and lack diversity. Given the lack of diversity in the solutions, the search is likely to prematurely converge, as significant improvements to a previous solution occur infrequently when one only uses an incremental solution strategy. The search will be focused on only one region of the search space. Therefore, decision-makers that search a small region of the solution space using the incrementa what-if method are likely to converge more efficiently (that is, less time, and less messages) than those who search a large region or multiple regions using a problemmodeling tool as well as a what-if tool. This suggests the following two hypotheses:

H3a: The time to converge to a solution will be higher in groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool than those using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool.

H3b: The number of messages exchanged will be larger in groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool than those using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool.

## The Experiment

PARTICIPANTS IN THIS STUDY WERE 54 executive MBA students enrolled in a decision-making course at a major U.S. university. Subjects were randomly assigned to three-member groups where each member had the role of a department manager. Subjects were provided with a structured 30-minute presentation explaining the experimental task. The task was designed to relate to the material covered in the course, and therefore, the subjects were familiar with the task. On the day of the experiment, to further familiarize the subjects with the task and the GDSS, each group was invited to a conference room to listen to a 30-minute structured presentation about the task, the GDSS, and the model. After the presentation, each subject was taken to a computer station and was assigned to a computer separate from others. The subjects did not have face-to-face or verbal contact with others in the group, and all their interaction was recorded by the GDSS.

Each participant was given a one-page case description. The case explained the problem and asked each subject to maximize her task performance—this will be explained further, after the task is introduced. In addition to this case description, each subjec received a separate sheet that included the cost data for his or her department (Table A2). This way, each subject had incomplete information resulting in some ambiguity about the true preference and cost data of other members. Daft and Lengel [6] propose that ambiguity confronting managers in organizations seems important to decisions regarding structural design and information processing. The negotiators, to reach a solution, had to consolidate their perspectives by exchanging private information.

The case study was required for the course and represented ten percent of the course grade. The grade each member received on the experiment depended on her performance on the task. Once the group agreed on a solution, the task performance of each individual was compared with that of others in the same treatment condition. The distribution of task performance within each treatment condition was then used as the basis for allocating grade points. Grade points could range from a maximum of 10 points to a minimum of 0 points, out of the total 100 points allocated for the course grade.

We conducted the experiment early in the term. Completion of the experiment was one of the first components for which the students earned a grade. Consequently, students had no grade “cushion,” which might lead to their not treating the experiment seriously. The grade on this experiment was a substantive contributor to the student’s final grade. It provided strong motivation to do well in the experiment.

Each student was randomly assigned to a role in a three-person group. Each group was asked not to discuss the experiment with other groups and was asked to apply the university “honor” system to this project. This study was a part of a larger set of experiments, and the subjects did not know what experimental condition they would be assigned to prior to the experiment. To alleviate the problem of unfair comparisons across treatment conditions and roles, each subject was compared to the subjects in the same treatment condition. Student grades were determined with respect to how well others with comparable roles and treatment conditions performed. This polic deterred students from discussing the project with other students.

To acquaint the subjects with the GDSS, a training session was held before the actual experiment began, and a sample problem was solved. The formal experiment began after the subjects indicated that they fully understood the problem and the features of the GDSS. The author was present during all sessions to answer any questions.

The independent variable in this study is the variation in GDSS capabilities, namely a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool and a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool. The dependent variables are solution quality, member confidence in the solution, solution diversity, truthfulness of information exchange, and the efficiency of the problem solving process. Solution quality was measured by an objective criterion, which will be explained later in this paper. Member confidence in the solution was measured by a Likert scale question posed to users after the experiment. Finally, the time and the number of messages it took to converge to a solution measured efficiency.

## The Task

The task is one of the most important factors affecting the group process and outcomes [13]. Decision-makers faced with a mixed-motive negotiation task are likely to encounter high stakes associated with decision outcomes. As each decision-make attempts to maximize her payoff while working as a part of the group, she may de velop a dual motive: to compete as well as to cooperate. Competition arises due to payoff conflicts, and cooperation results from the need to exchange information. Competition could result in untruthful and biased information exchange that may, in turn, compromise the effectiveness of the GDSS.

The decision-makers in a group need to coordinate their activities and resolve their payoff conflicts. A group meets to develop a production schedule. Group members have to select a subset of orders, with differential payoff effects for each member, from a larger set of customer orders. Each member is instructed to maximize her own payoff. The orders selected should require resources that do not exceed various capacity limits. The mathematical model of the problem is provided in the Appendix. This model is used as a problem-modeling tool of the GDSS that can help members to identify either individual optimal solutions or the Pareto optimal solution, if they exchange truthful information.

This task was selected because one can easily obtain an objective correct answer by modeling the problem, using data from the three departments, and solving the problem. This solution provides a theoretical and objective benchmark to compare with the experimental results.

## The GDSS

For the purposes of this research, we designed two versions of a GDSS. The two were identical in all aspects except for one feature: a problem-modeling tool. One GDSS had a problem-modeling tool and the other did not. Both GDSSs run in a PC Win dows Client/Server network environment. Common features include information exchange facilities, what-if capability, and the capability to capture group memory. We classify the GDSS with a problem-modeling tool as a Level Two GDSS, whereas the one without this tool is a Level One GDSS [9]. Based on Nunamaker et al.’s [15] proposed framework, both GDSSs provide process support (that is, group memory), and task support (that is, what-if capability and information exchange via task-specific templates). However, only the GDSS with a problem-modeling tool provides task structure (that is, problem modeling capability).

We revised the GDSS based on the comments we received from our pilot experi ments to assure task/technology fit [26]. The GDSS consisted of three main screens:

The problem-model ing capability uses the information in a group member’s database to formulate a specific problem instance and to solve it from the perspective of the member at that terminal. Because each member has to specify the effort he would exert, a preliminary screen asks each member to assign a specific effort level (from lowest = 1 to highest = 4) to each order. The GDSS then uses the costs (Actual Departmental Costs and Uncompensated Departmental Effort Costs described in the Appendix) associated with these effort levels to formulate the model of the problem that is shown in Appendix. The GDSS then uses an optimization module to compute the optimal solu tion to the problem from this person’s perspective and displays this information, along with the corresponding payoff to him. The optimization module of the GDSS formulates the mathematical model of the problem as shown in the Appendix and then calls Lindo solver. The GDSS optimization module will then parse the Lindo output to display the payoff and the solution to the user as shown in Figure 1.

Participants can use task-specific templates to propose solutions to others. A participant could evaluate another participant’s proposed solution by using the GDSS’s “what-if” capability. The what-if capability allows each person to examine the incremental effect of changes to a solution (Figure 2).

The “group memory” capability keeps a history of all proposed solutions. Group members have information about the solutions that have been proposed to date by the various group members. This feature aids in the negotiation process by providing a participant with information about the preferences of other individuals and how these preferences are changing over time.

Therefore, the sample size is nine only when the appropriate unit of analysis is the whole group but higher when the unit of analysis is an individual in the group. In addition, when we do find significant differences with these sample sizes, it means that there is a difference, and if we had a larger sample, the significance level would just be more pronounced. A problem-modeling tool affects solution quality as measured by the number of diverse solutions generated, the task performance, and the truthfulness of the information that is exchanged among the group members. Groups using a GDSS withou a problem-modeling tool were more efficient with respect to both the time, as well as the number of messages it takes to converge to a solution. Higher efficiency, however, came at the expense of solution quality. Lower diversity of solutions suggests that the groups using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool did not search the solution space as thoroughly as did their counterparts that had used a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool. The latter groups were induced to use a two-phase solution strategy, and did not converge to a solution as quickly as did the former groups that used a GDSS that induced a purely convergent strategy. The groups following a twophase solution strategy using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool generated better solutions as they diverged in the solution space before they converged to a mutuall acceptable solution [11].

Incorporating a problem-modeling tool as a GDSS feature increased the number of diverse solutions generated. A problem-modeling tool also provided a systematic way for group members to exchange and process private information to integrate their information into a model. As a result, a problem-modeling tool enhanced the truthfulness of the information that was exchanged among the group members. The information exchanged among group members was incorporated, as model parameters, in the modeling of the problem. That is, a problem-modeling tool did provide a vehicle for group members to communicate their private information and aid them in processing the information that was received from each member. This implies that the lack of a problem-modeling tool may be one explanation of why group members do not “process” the information they received from others in their group [8]—they may not understand how to use this information, or may assume that it is not important to exchange it with others.

Our results support previous results that decision aids may have direct effects influencing the behavior of users through the types of features that are available and used [25]. The result that a problem-modeling tool influences the solution strategy is consistent with previous research that suggests a decision aid may affect the way a decision-maker thinks about alternative problem solving approaches [24]. Hence, we question the theories that emphasize the ability of groups to determine their own behavior regardless of technological intervention. We acknowledge, that in repeated experimentation, humans tend to learn to modify their behavior and may not be subject to limitations imposed on them by the technology [12]. However, given the rapid changes in technology and the fact that suboptimal decisions are costly, organizations cannot afford to provide systems that lead to suboptimal decisions and assume that the users will eventually learn to overcome the limitations of the system. Technologi cal intervention seems to affect the strategy and performance of decision-makers. Thus, in designing specific GDSS features, one has to examine the effect such features may have on decision strategy and, subsequently, performance. It may not be sufficient to assume that decision-makers will learn how to use a GDSS to overcome its induced restrictiveness and limitations. The specific GDSS features available can easily influence the solution strategy of GDSS users.

There was no difference in member confidence in the solution between groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool and those using a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool. We speculate that the lack of difference may be that decision makers rated their confidence in the solution when using a GDSS tool and did not really distinguish between specific features of the GDSS. They simply viewed both a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool and one without a problem-modeling tool as a “computer supported decision aid.” Hence, their confidence in the solution was not significantly different using one GDSS or the other. The fact that the groups used either one or the other GDSS did not give them a basis for comparing between the two GDSS to rate one relative to the other. There is another explanation for the lack of difference. On one hand, the groups with the problem-modeling tool may have more confidence in their solutions because they searched a large solution space. On the other hand, the groups with only a what-if tool may have been more confident in their solution because they had picked the best solution from the small region of the solution space that the what-if tool made visible to them, although they were not aware of other regions of the solution space. As a result, the confidence ratings may have converged. Future research should investigate this further to gain a better understanding of the real difference in user confidence in the solution.

The results of this study show that including a problem-modeling tool as a GDSS feature improves solution quality, whereas it does not seem to negatively affect decision-maker confidence in the solution (that is, confidence was not significantly dif ferent between the two treatments). Given that this feature aids decision-makers in making better decisions and does not negatively affect their confidence in the solution, this study presents evidence that a problem-modeling tool can be a useful GDSS feature. The fact that the research on problem modeling has received scant attention in GDSS literature [10] given that such a tool can be a useful GDSS feature points to an important, but overlooked, area of GDSS research. Future research should investigate effective ways of incorporating problem-modeling tools into the design of next generation Level Two and Level Three GDSS [9].

## Conclusion

THIS RESEARCH STUDIED THE EFFECT OF A PROBLEM-MODELING TOOL of a GDSS and discovered that a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool is more useful than a GDSS without a problem-modeling tool for generating a Pareto optimal solution. Specifi cally, the results show that when there is task/technology fit for both types of GDSS, the use of a problem-modeling tool improves solution quality as defined by number of diverse solutions, task performance, and truthfulness of the information that is exchanged. Furthermore, this study shows that technological intervention into the decision process can change the decision outcomes. Despite the importance of problem modeling, few GDSS studies have investigated this phase of problem solving in groups. The GDSS with a problem-modeling tool provides capabilities that the decision-makers can use to scan the search space more thoroughly to generate more di verse solutions. The guided information search results in more complete alternative evaluation and the generation of more options. Therefore, members of groups using a GDSS with a problem-modeling tool consider more diverse solutions before they converge to a final solution. Although this results in better solutions, it also requires more time, interaction, and communication. Therefore, incorporating a problem-modeling tool as a GDSS feature can be valuable, but requires improved communication tools to prevent efficiency losses.

Future research should address the issues related to the design of the next generation Level Two and Level Three GDSS, and a problem-modeling tool is at the core of these systems. Providing problem-modeling tools for groups is more complex than providing these tools for individuals. Group members need to exchange infor mation and communicate, need to achieve consensus, need to coordinate their activities to operate in the same problem solving phase, and, in general, need to deal with issues that individual decision-makers do not need to be concerned about. Al though we studied the influence of a problem-modeling tool of a GDSS in this study, we only focused on a specific mixed-motive task and a specific GDSS to assure tasktechnology fit. There are many other issues that need to be addressed in the future, to understand effective ways of incorporating various types of problem-modeling tools into the GDSS that would aid groups engaged with various types of tasks.

As organizations move toward becoming virtual organizations, where dispersed members have to collaborate, decision support systems that aid dispersed groups become increasingly important. If these systems provide adequate problem-modeling tools, along with efficient communication tools, group decisions can improve. In addition, incorporating problem-modeling tools in the design of a GDSS can increase the range of problems that can be supported by a GDSS. Further research is required to study problem modeling and how it influences group dynamics.

We studied information exchange in this paper and found that problem-modeling tools can be designed to facilitate information exchange in groups, help them process the information, and promote a decision strategy that can lead to improved decision outcomes. It should be noted that despite the internal validity of the results, given that we used executive MBA students that used a specific GDSS applied to a specific task, one should be cautious about extending the results of this study beyond the experimental conditions described in this paper.

## Appendix

THE TASK USED IN THIS EXPERIMENT is a production planning problem. A company manufactures four products. Although each product has a set of basic characteristics, they differ in some features, depending on customer requirements and specific orders. A customer order consists of some combination of all four products, each tailored to the customer’s specification. Associated with each order is a total revenue value that depends on the number of products and the complexity of modifying the products to satisfy the specific requirements. For example, an order might consist of 200 units of product one, 450 units of product two, 200 units of product three, and 400 units of product four. Total revenue generated when this order is filled might be \$865. The information available to the subject consists of the details of the order and the total revenue the order generates. A decision to fill an order means that the order is satisfied completely; no partial orders are shipped.

The group (represented by subjects playing the roles of the managers of marketing, production, and purchasing departments) will meet to decide which orders to fill. Associated with each order is a departmental projected cost that is an estimate the organization has regarding the departmental cost incurred if an order is filled. This information is available to all departments (all group members). It represents an estimate one member (department) has of the costs of the other members. Each department, however, has information about its internal costs. The actual departmental cost (ADC) is not available to the other departments, unless the department wishes to reveal these costs. ADC is not necessarily equal to the projected departmental cost. Further, each department has information about uncompensated departmental effort cost (UDEC), which represents the intangible cost the department incurs for filling an order for a specific level of effort. The UDEC may be viewed as an individual’s opportunity cost of extra time and effort. In general, the harder the employees work, the lower the ADCs become. However, the harder they work, the more personal resources they will provide. The UDEC captures the extra cost of such personal re sources as expending more energy due to a more intense workload. In essence, the ADC is a decreasing function of effort, whereas the UDEC is an increasing function of effort. We summarize the four key variables below.

Projected cost of filling an order (PC) is an estimate of the cost that a departmen incurs for filling an order.

Actual departmental cost of filling an order (ADC). Note that for different levels of effort, the ADC differs and this information is known only to the department.

Uncompensated departmental effort cost (UDEC) is the cost that the departmen incurs by filling an order at a specific level of effort that is not directly compensated by the organization. This information is internal to each department.

The revenue (REV) is the dollar amount generated by filling each order.

Organizational profit is the revenue minus the costs incurred at the three depart ments, the sum of the ADCs. Each member receives the same percentage of organizational profit (POP)—to create a specific example, without loss of generality, this percentage is set equal to 15 percent for this experiment. The real payoff (reward) to each member, however, is the member’s percentage of organizational profit minus the UDEC. Thus, the reward each member receives depends on the ADC of other members as well as his own. Due to the interaction between a member’s reward and other members’ costs, when a member lowers his effort level, the corresponding ADC goes up and hence, other members’ bonuses decrease too. Each member of the group receives the same percentage of profit as a bonus. The reward is the bonus minus the cost of extra effort (UDEC). Recall that a standard projected cost is assigned to each order, and this is an estimate a member has regarding other member costs of filling each order. Each member may update the default projected costs, if he wishes to communicate actual cost information to other members. The members only have information about default projected costs at each department except their own. The problem for the group is to decide which subset of all orders to fill in order to maximize the reward each person receives, without violating product capacity constraints.

For the sake of this experiment, we select four levels of effort as legitimate options for the effort levels. This is to avoid overly complicating the problem while still capturing the information asymmetry property of the task to be able to study information exchange. In essence, each member is solving a problem that can be mathematically modeled as follows:
