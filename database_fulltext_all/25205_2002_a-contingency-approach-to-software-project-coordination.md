# A Contingency Approach to Software Project Coordination

HAYWARD P. ANDRES AND ROBERT W. ZMUD

HAYWARD P. ANDRES is an assistant professor in the Information Systems area in the School of Business Administration at Portland State University. He earned his doctorate in Management Information Systems from Florida State University. His cur rent research interests include the effect of information technology on organizational processes, organizational and social factors that affect software project success, electronic commerce, and computer-mediated communication.

ROBERT W. ZMUD is Michael F. Price Professor of Management in the Michael F. Price College of Business at the University of Oklahoma. His research interests focus on the effect of information technology in facilitating a variety of organizational be haviors and on organizational efforts involved with planning, managing, and diffusing information technology. He holds a Ph.D. (Management) from the University of Arizona, an M.S. (Management) from M.I.T., and a B.A.E. (Aerospace Engineering) from the University of Virginia.

ABSTRACT: Before software project managers can enhance productivity and satisfaction of the software project team member, the effect of task characteristics, goal orientations, and coordination strategies on design and coding-task outcomes must be understood. A research model, which suggests that task interdependence, goal conflict, and coordination strategies significantly affect productivity and satisfaction associated with software design and coding activities, is presented. Issues such as contingency/design misfit, conflicting contingencies, and the extent of deviation to theoretically prescribed coordination mechanisms applied to contingencies are used to make predictions on productivity and process satisfaction. A 2´2´2 factorial experiment was utilized. Overall, projects characterized by low task interdependence exhibited greater productivity than projects with high task interdependence. Also, in general, organic coordination was more productive than mechanistic coordination. There was also a significant interaction between task interdependence and coordination strategy. Low goal conflict and organic coordination each lead to greater process satisfaction. Productivity results for the goal conflict manipulation was opposite to the hypothesized direction. Unconflicted contingencies addressed with consistent coordination and partially conflicted contingencies, regardless of the coordination used, exhibited significant gains in productivity. In comparison, unconflicted contingencies with inconsistent coordination and conflicted contingencies, regardless of the coordination applied, resulted in lower productivity. This suggests that there are instances where multiple contingencies, which warrant the use of different coordination strategies, can be adequately addressed with a specific coordination strategy.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: coordination, contingency theory, goal interdependence, goal conflict, multiple contingencies, process satisfaction, software project management, task interdependence, team productivity.

OFTEN, SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS still prove to be both a costly and risky endeavor. Poor software project execution continues to result, in the best cases, in missed deadlines, and, in the worst cases, in escalations in commitment of additiona resources as a cure-all for runaway projects [22, 34]. Traditionally, most project management tactics for improving software development performance have emphasized technical innovations involving the development of new and enhanced methods and tools [12, 18]. A more recent tactic involves improved project management practices applied to the software development process [8, 20, 24, 52]. Here, concern focuses on issues, such as task decomposition, task assignment, and work group coordination, as well as the social context (such as, goal orientations, conflict) surrounding software developer participation and interaction. It is this second tactic that is the focus of the study described in this article.

A central theme in the design of work group coordination strategies is that the work group’s information processing needs, as defined by the nature of the activities performed, must “match” or “fit” the information-processing capacity associated with the coordination strategies utilized [30]. The contingencies faced by a work unit (such as, task interdependence, task uncertainty, goal conflict) dictate the extent of infor mation exchange and decisional autonomy required to effectively complete project tasks [17]. In addition, Gresov [17] noted that there are instances where multiple contingencies of relatively equal importance must be simultaneously addressed. In such instances, management may have difficulty achieving an optimal work design, or the context may dictate that no design is optimal. Here, it is assumed that conflicting contingencies (that is, conflicting design solutions among two or more contingencies) can be a given, over the short-term, and that the design imperatives of one contingency may be more salient to productivity and satisfaction than other contingencies. Consequently, by assessing the effect of deviation to theoretically prescribed coordination mechanisms applied to a set of contingencies specific to project type [41], work unit tasks, or project phases [17], it is more likely that a more appropriate coordination strategy could be applied.

Software development is a highly information-intensive work activity, and projects can vary in the extent of overall task interdependence [8, 13, 23, 25]. For example, applications can be classified as emphasizing either online analytical processing (OLAP) or online transaction processing (OLTP). The design and development of OLAP systems (such as, executive support systems, decision support) require collaboration among a broader knowledge/skill base than do OLTP systems (such as, order processing, reservation, customer account maintenance). Furthermore, in OLAP systems, the functionality of a software module developed by one designer/coder may produce an event (such as, data value update, data value exchange, or module shutdown) that will significantly affect the proper execution of software modules implemented by other designers/coders. OLAP systems require more integration among subsystems. Clearly, the design and coding phases of OLAP system projects, in particular, generally exhibit greater task interdependence than that of OLTP system projects [8, 52].

Similarly, a project’s goal structure is a social context contingency, which might also dictate the application of the most appropriate coordination strategy. Studies have shown that the stakeholders associated with software development projects espouse quite different goals. For instance, end users desire user-friendly systems with adequate functional task support; programmers focus on overcoming imposed technical constraints and developing competencies to enhance their careers; and, project managers desire to meet schedules and budget expectations [5, 25, 44]. Software designers and coders can disagree on choice of design methodology, programming language, database server, or appropriate deadlines [51]. Such competing goal situations can induce a variety of counterproductive behaviors (such as, project sabotage) among a project’s stakeholders. In addition, competing goals have been shown to limit productivity through its potential to diminish motivation, withdraw commitment, and lower expectations of ability to reach personal goals [26].

The view of software development taken in this study finds software development to be a highly social, interactive process [44]. Here, project coordination strategies must exhibit communication mechanisms and decision-making structures that “match” or “fit” the task and social context associated with specific work units and project phases [8, 20, 24, 25, 52]. In addition, when simultaneously addressed, multiple contingencies can assume configurations that are either unconflicted (that is, all contin gencies warrant the same design solution), partially conflicted (that is, design deviation to one or more contingencies yields suboptimal performance), or conflicted (that is, any design yields minimal performance). The goal of this study is to identify fit relationships among potential conflicting contingencies and their resultant influence on software design and coding task outcomes. More specifically, the interactions between task interdependence, goal conflict, and coordination mechanism in determining successful software design and coding outcomes are examined in a controlled setting. Such research can help to identify ideal design configurations that produce optimal performance and show that deviation from these ideal configurations will result in lower performance.

We begin by deriving the study’s research hypotheses utilizing information processing theory, goal interdependence theory, and multiple contingency theory. Then, the research design and results are discussed. Finally, these results are interpreted, limitations of the study are described, and implications for both research and practice are offered.

## Main Effects

As described above, the three variables identified are expected to exhibit direct effects on software project success: task interdependence, coordination strategy, and goal conflict.

## Task Interdependence

Task interdependence refers to the extent to which a task requires organizational units to engage in workflow exchanges of product, information, skills, or resources, and to where actions taken in one unit affect the actions and work outcomes of other units [15, 32, 45]. Under conditions of low task interdependence, each department’s or team member’s contributions are additive. Increases in task interdependence is char acterized by a department’s or team member’s need to integrate their effort with others and the output of another is needed as input to do their tasks. What do we know about the performance of work teams engaged in high interdependent work tasks? Straus and McGrath [42] noted that increased levels of task interdependence required greater instances of information exchange needed to clarify task assignments, project requirements, and progress. Productivity was diminished because of time spent in reconciling goal differences and the establishment of a shared mental model of the overall task requirements needed for successful integration of efforts. Saavedra et al. [40] noted that progress of a team member is often held up when output, produced by another, is needed as input required for completion of their segment of the task. Under these conditions, greater frequency and volume of information exchange and mutual decision-making was required in order to assure that the effect of a decision regarding one task segment is accounted for in all other interdependent tasks [7, 8, 49].

In software development, low task interdependence is evidenced in the design and coding of stand-alone modules typical to OLTP systems. Here, task interdependence is minimal because there is no need to consider extensive real-time data exchange or system state dependency (such as, running, shutdown, database updated) generated among modules. Consequently, there is no need for time-consuming discussions aimed at assuring that one module’s functionality does not conflict with the requirements of modules developed by others. Individual tasks are additive and group performance is the sum of individual performances. Alternatively, in the design and coding of soft ware characterized by high task interdependence (such as, OLAP systems), task execution requires collaboration among a greater diversity of technically skilled personne (such as, executive users, programming language specialists, database administrators, network administrators, third party vendors, subcontractors), or across multiple cross-functional teams. These specialists must work in concert to complete the design and coding tasks. The development of such software modules is characterized by reciprocal workflows (that is, high task interdependence) where delays and errors in one module negatively affect the completion of another module [23, 25]. These arguments lead to the study’s first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Software design and coding activities characterized by low task interdependence will be more successful than software design and coding activities characterized by high task interdependence.

## Coordination Strategy

Coordination refers to the mode of linking together different parts of an organization to accomplish a set of collective tasks [48]. According to coordination theory, tasks are allocated across organizational members, and communication and control mechanisms must facilitate the necessary information exchanges and decisional autonomy needed for effective collaboration and decision-making [45, 48]. McCann and Galbraith [30] suggest that coordination strategies vary along three dimensions—formality (vertical versus horizontal communication), cooperativeness (extent of shared decision-mak ing), and centralization (locus of decisional autonomy). Informal, cooperative, and decentralized strategies reflect an organic coordination strategy, whereas formal, controlling, and centralized strategies are seen as a mechanistic coordination strategy [6].

Information processing theory asserts that increased information exchange is essential to overcome task uncertainty and task interdependence faced by an organization [30, 48]. Informal horizontal communication channels enable the timely sharing of problem-solving expertise and clarification of one’s task outputs, which must ultimately be integrated with the work of others. For the execution of project tasks, information exchange is essential for the timely sequencing, scheduling, and synchronization of activities of team members. In addition, effective coordination of tasks reduces th performance of redundant activities. Moreover, delegation of decision-making autonomy to levels most sensitive and responsive to problems minimizes decision time and increases efficiency in task execution [27]. Essentially, the mechanisms by which coordination affects task productivity and satisfaction are role clarity, task sequencing, expertise/information exchange, and timely decision-making. These four mechanisms have been found to significantly affect software development task pro ductivity [23, 24, 25, 38]. This suggests the study’s second research hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: Software design and coding activities managed via an organic coordination strategy will be more successful than software design and coding activities managed via a mechanistic coordination strategy.

Goal Conflict

Rahim [37] classified instances of organizational conflict on the basis of its sources. Among the classes of conflict defined were: substantive conflict (that is, disagreement on task or content issues regarding the solution to a problem), interest conflic (that is, same understanding of a problem but preference for incompatible solutions), and goal conflict (that is, preferred outcomes or end-states that are inconsistent). Studies examining the effect of goal conflict on productivity and satisfaction have found mixed results. For example, Amason and Schweiger [2] found that conflict disrupted information exchange, undermined commitment, and reduced team cohesion, all of which reduced decision quality and team member satisfaction. In contrast, Priem et al. [36] noted that, with conflict present, structured decision-making techniques de signed to enhance the expression of substantive conflict paradoxically increased member satisfaction with interactions and decision outcomes. Apparently, recognition of conflict may promote constructive controversy where discussions, aimed at resolving disputes regarding task allocation and resources, enhances satisfaction.

Organizational conflict has also been conceptualized as goal interdependence where goals are either cooperatively linked or competitively linked [46]. Alper et al. [1] observed lower productivity and process satisfaction when perceived competitively linked goals lead to undermining and obstructing the goals of others. Motivation for collaboration can be lowered when there are perceived impediments to the pursuit of one’s personal goal. This is because there is a minimal expectancy of the ability to achieve individual goals [28, 29]. Furthermore, synergy diminishes when individuals proceed to develop strategies that focus on maximizing their own performance. In contrast, cooperatively linked conditions, lead to accurate communication, mutual goal adjustments, and task orientation, because team members are motivated to collaborate with each other [1].

End users, system designers, and programming language specialists can arrive at very different views of a software system’s structure and functionality, and its environmental context [9, 39, 43]. Boehm and Ross [4] noted that: end users desire a robust and user-friendly system; customers desire a product delivered on time and within budget; maintainers desire well-documented, easy-to-modify systems with no bugs; and programming language specialists are interested in technical challenges and fast career paths. Such competitively linked goals can lead to significant software project management difficulties, both at the strategic (such as, setting goals, establishing major milestones and responsibilities) and tactical (such as, resolving day-today conflicts, adapting to changes) levels. Productivity becomes impaired when group members persistently work toward incompatible personal goals or withdraw commitment because expectancy of personal goal attainment is lowered. This leads to the study’s third research hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: Software design and coding activities characterized by low goal conflict conditions will be more successful than software design and coding activities characterized by high goal conflict conditions.

## Interaction Effects

The primary contention of this study is that software development project success is dependent on the “fit” or “misfit” among the contingencies characterizing a software project. Misfit among contingencies occurs when the characteristics of the contingencies (such as, high uncertainty, low dependence) warrant competing or inconsistent theoretically prescribed design imperatives (such as, organic versus mechanistic coordination). The current study extends such a research direction by examining contingent relationships among coordination strategy, task interdependence (a task-context variable), and goal conflict (a psychosocial-context variable) in a software design and coding context.

## Task Interdependence and Coordination Strategy

Increasing task interdependence generates a requirement for higher frequency and volume of communication and decision-making between work units [17, 45]. If a mechanistic coordination mode were used with high task interdependence, information-processing capacity would be insufficient and task efficiency and worker satisfaction would decline. Alternatively, an organic coordination strategy provides a greater capacity to exchange and process information in a timely manner. Random, informal peer-oriented information exchange needed for task clarification, alignment, and integration results in superior productivity and satisfaction [7, 8, 24, 52]. Organic coordination under conditions of low task interdependence can overload the decision-making structure with superfluous information and unnecessary interactions, which can disrupt ongoing, already effective tasks, consuming both time and effor and frustrating workers. Instead, established task-related rules, procedures, and for mal work plans that minimize peer involvement prove to be more efficient for low task interdependence. This suggests the study’s fourth hypothesis, which is illustrated in Figure 2:

Hypothesis 4: As software design and coding tasks become more task interdependent, the greater (lesser) the effect of an organic (mechanistic) coordination strategy on task outcome success.

## Goal Conflict and Coordination Strategy

Interest (goal) conflict has been suggested to interact with managerial coordination strategies in determining organizational effectiveness [27, 47, 49, 50]. Gersick and Davis-Sacks [14] noted that, when high levels of inter-unit conflict become disruptive and create an impasse, organizations tend to use more centralized decision-mak ing along with formal rules and policies to manage relationships between work units. Detailed job descriptions, formal chains of command, and specific rules and procedures (that is, mechanistic coordination) are emphasized to objectively dictate appropriate behavior and minimize disputes regarding task responsibilities and allocation of resources. Alternatively, low to moderate levels of conflict can be productive by enhancing team performance [10, 14]. Moderate conflict can trigger increased task focus and collective problem solving, challenge to the status quo, and result in the introduction of novel alternative solutions. However, moderate conflict can only be constructive when management approaches afford teams with information processing capacity, interactions, and decision-making authority needed to openly identify disagreements, develop solutions, and work through trade-offs without alienating team members (that is, organic coordination) [2, 10, 21]. The resultant movement toward mutual goal adjustments increases motivation, team cohesion, feelings of self-determination, and expectancy of goal attainment [31]. However, if moderate conflict is not appropriately addressed, it can escalate to disruptive conflict where controlling measures become more appropriate. This suggests the study’s fifth research hypoth esis, which is illustrated in Figure 3:

Hypothesis 5: As software design and coding goal linkages move from low to high goal conflict conditions, the greater (lesser) the effect of a mechanistic (or ganic) coordination strategy on task outcome success.

## Goal Conflict and Task Interdependence

Job design research has shown that there is a relationship between the motivational features of jobs (such as, autonomy, task interdependence, goal setting practices) and important outcomes such as productivity and job satisfaction [19, 31, 40, 53]. This theoretical perspective suggests that task interdependence and goal conflict can inter act with each other through their mutual effect on motivation and the extent of required interactions. For example, the skill variety and challenge posed by moderate levels of task interdependence can promote intrinsic motivation because individuals are provided with an opportunity to satisfy their self-competency and self-determining needs [19]. In other words, workers feel that their contribution is distinct and discernible. However, Wong and Campion [53] noted that at extremely high levels of task interdependence, motivational features of a task decrease, resulting in lower productivity and satisfaction. This is attributed to the fact that skill variety and challenge posed by tasks become more complex and less interesting. Furthermore, job significance and identity suffers when a worker’s distinct contribution is diminished and complex interactions require a perspective shift to the overall product or task. Hirst [19] and Mossholder [33] found that if individual and group goal misalignment causes a perceived locus of causality to shift from within individuals to a group, individuals are unlikely to feel self-determining, and therefore, intrinsic motivation is likely to decline. Perceived impediments to personal goal attainment can direct attention away from strategies that would facilitate effective group performance toward maximizing one’s own performance. Consequently, group performance and affective outcomes decline.

Task interdependence requires greater interaction among workers and team members need to develop cooperative task strategies that will give rise to task success. A group goal (that is, cooperative goal) can improve productivity on an interdependent task by aligning effort on assigned tasks and facilitating collaborative problem solving by promoting a sense of cooperativeness among group members [31]. In contrast, members adhering to competing individual goals tend to be more disruptive, competitive, and less cooperative, all of which undermine synergy and collaboration needed in interdependent task execution. In summary, problems attributed to competing goal alignments can only be exacerbated by increased interaction and negative motivational influences associated with high task interdependence. ## Goal Conflict, Task Interdependence, and Coordination

Multiple contingency theory explores and predicts the extent to which theoreticall prescribed designs (such as, locus of decision-making, vertical versus horizontal communication mechanisms) fit their task context (such as, task uncertainty, task interdependence) [17, 45, 48]. Variations in contingency configurations can either be unconflicted (that is, all contingencies warrant the same design solution), partially conflicted (that is, design deviation to one or more contingencies yields suboptimal performance), or conflicted (that is, any design yields minimal performance). For example, high task interdependence and competitively linked goals represent conflicted contingencies. High task interdependence requires more interactions and extra information processing capacity (that is, organic coordination) needed to realize synergy. In contrast, when competitively linked goals lead to disruptive behavior, centralized decision-making, formal rules and policies, and restrained interactions (that is, mechanistic coordination) are required to dictate appropriate behavior and minimize disputes regarding task assignments and allocation of resources. Here, multiple-contingency research suggests that, when addressing both contingencies simultaneously, either coordination mode will result in minimal performance [17]. This is because, for each of these contingencies, minimal performance occurs when inconsistent coordination mechanisms are applied.

Similarly, low task interdependence (warrants mechanistic coordination) and low goal conflict (warrants organic coordination) also represent conflicted contingencies. However, a suboptimal design is possible. Deviation in designing to low task interdependence with organic coordination (that is, surplus information processing capacity, decisional autonomy) involves fewer trade-offs than deviation in designing to high task interdependence with mechanistic coordination (that is, diminished information processing capacity, centralized decision-making). Here, a surplus of information processing is considered less detrimental than the lack of information processing. Likewise, deviation in designing to low goal conflict with mechanistic coordination (that is, centralized decision-making, formal rules and policies) involves fewer trade-offs than deviation in designing to high goal conflict with organic coordination (that is, increased interaction and dialogue). In other words, applying unwarranted controlling methods to low goal conflict is less detrimental than the lack of needed controlling methods under high goal conflict conditions. Consequently, the low task interdependence/low goal conflict configuration is partially conflicted because suboptimal performance may possibly be attained utilizing either coordination strategy.

There are no contingency conflicts in the low task interdependence/high goal conflict and high task interdependence/low goal conflict combinations. The individua contingencies in each of these combinations dictate the same design solution, respectively, mechanistic and organic coordination. Because design imperatives are clear and consistent, appropriately addressed unconflicted contingencies afford maximum performance. We propose that, if equally important and simultaneously addressed, task interdependence, and goal conflict can assume configurations that are conflicted, partially conflicted, or unconflicted. Multiple contingency theory suggests that unconflicted contingencies addressed with consistent designs and partially conflicted contingencies, regardless of the design used, both afford gains in productivity and process satisfaction. We refer to these conditions as non-constraining contingency configurations because suboptimal to optimal performance can be achieved. However, applying inconsistent designs to unconflicted contingencies will minimize performance and process satisfaction. Further, conflicted contingencies, regardless of the coordination strategy applied, will also result in minimal task outcomes. These two conditions are referred to as constraining contingency configurations. This suggests the study’s seventh hypothesis, which is illustrated in Table 1.

Hypothesis 7: Non-constraining contingency configurations will lead to more successful projects than constraining contingency configurations.

## The Study’s Research Design

THE RESEARCH STRATEGY involved a laboratory experiment utilizing a 2<sup>3</sup> factorial design. The factors were interdependence, goal conflict, and coordination mode. The dependent variables employed were task-process satisfaction and team productivity. A laboratory experiment was used to discriminate the effects of task interdependence, goal conflict, and coordination strategy through experimental manipulation of these variables. In addition, a laboratory study can: (1) provide the use of controlled manipulations not easily and consistently observed in the field, (2) test hypotheses derived from related field studies under controlled conditions, and (3) afford the comparison of the relative efficiency or effectiveness of the experimental manipulations.

## Subjects and Sub-Team Composition

EIGHTY SUBJECTS WERE DRAWN FROM A POPULATION of undergraduate management information systems, computer science students, and graduate computer science students. All subjects had either successfully completed, or were currently enrolled in, a computer programming course that emphasized structured software design and basic knowledge of the C programming language. Any experience at working in teams was limited to explicitly assigned group projects typical to programming courses and random group formation in a computer lab setting. A comparison (t-test) of the undergraduate and graduate subjects revealed no significant differences in their level of performance or process satisfaction. It was felt (and validated in a pretest) that the academic experience of these college students afforded them with the skills necessary to develop the assigned, reasonably complex, software design and coding task within a reasonable amount of time. The students were randomly assigned to the treatments.

## Experimental Task

THE EXPERIMENTAL TASK REQUIRED THE DEVELOPMENT of two software subsystems, with one subsystem assigned to each of the project team members. Task duration for each team did not exceed a total of six hours (three-hour sessions on two consecutive days). A pilot study demonstrated that it was possible for a two-person team to complete the programming task within this six-hour time period. The development effort required the execution of detailed design (define required variables and pseudocode), coding (in C programming language), module integration and testing tasks. These activities are typical of software design and coding activities conducted within organizations and exhibit the same form of team collaboration, communication, and decision-making requirements (such as, data requirements identification, shared data among modules, printed report layout, algorithms), but on a smaller scale. Task complexity was controlled across team members by ensuring equality in assigned function points for the modules to be completed by team members. Func tion points were assessed by assigning weights to and summing the count of outputs from the modules, inputs to and outputs from user queries, inputs to the modules, file data items, and data items sent to, shared with, or received from other modules [11]. The total function point count was 51 across all groups (both high and low interdependence).

## Experimental Manipulations

## Task Interdependence

THE DEGREE OF TASK INTERDEPENDENCE was varied through the extent to which the distribution of assigned modules across team members required collaboration and negotiation by the team members in order to refine shared data file contents and format and shared function interfaces (that is, function name and parameters). In the low interdependence condition, the team members were assigned modules with little or no shared interface requirements or shared data files. Specifically, each team member was assigned the design and coding of modules that did not share access to its data files, variables, and functions. Under this condition, no collaboration or negotiation was required. In the high interdependence condition, the project team members were assigned modules with common interfaces (that is, shared data file accesses and function calls). Here, team member collaboration and negotiation for the iterative refinement of the shared data file contents and shared interfaces were required. A number of field studies have noted that file/database and interface design are activities typica to software projects and contribute greatly to communication requirements among project team members [8, 25, 35].

## Coordination Strategies

As discussed earlier, coordination strategies vary along three dimensions [30]: for mality, localization, and cooperativeness. Informal, decentralized, and cooperative strategies are characterized as organic coordination strategies, whereas formal, cen tralized, and controlling strategies are mechanistic strategies [4]. For this study, the formality and cooperativeness dimensions were utilized. The high formality manipulation was implemented by: (1) spatial separation of the team members, (2) limiting information exchange through memos that were transferred through the project manager, and (3) by restricting team member face-to-face communication to formally scheduled design meetings. Low formality was implemented by allowing the team members to work together under face-to-face conditions. Cooperativeness was varied by the extent to which the team members were able to engage in decision-making activities together as a group throughout the task duration. Consequently, in the organic coordination condition, team members were in close proximity where information exchange and collective decision-making could occur informally at any time. The mechanistic coordination condition was characterized by spatial separation of the team members (except at brief design meetings) and the use of formal memo exchanges between the team members. Information exchange between the two experimental sessions was controlled by collection of all materials at the end of the first session and by instructing the team members to limit information exchange to the experimental sessions. ## Goal Conflict

In the cooperatively linked goal condition, both team members were given the same goal of either product quality or timely completion. In the competitively linked goal condition, each team member was given a divergent goal—specifically, product quality versus timely completion. A team member, given the quality objective, was instructed to develop a product that has an extensive set of clear instructions, accepts and recovers easily from erroneous user input responses, and has functionality that exceeds minimum requirements. A team member assigned the speedy completion time objec tive was instructed to complete the task as quickly as possible at the expense of qual ity while maintaining adequate functionality as outlined in the requirements specification and top-level design. Team members operating under competitively linked goals (timeliness versus quality) were aware of the other team member’s divergen goal. In the sample, working under cooperatively linked goals, half of the teams had both team members assigned the timely completion goal and the other half had both team members assigned time quality goal. There were no significant differences in outcomes between instances where both team members were assigned the timely completion goal and where both team members were assigned the quality goal. Fi nally, to induce motivation for individual goal attainment, adherence to the individual’s goal assignment was assessed as “genuineness of participation,” which was required to qualify for a perfect score on any assigned class programming assignment project and individual participation in the monetary award lottery.

“Genuineness of participation” was assessed through a combination of self-report responses from the participants and experimenter observation. Previous studies have noted that “team climate” can dictate the effectiveness and innovativeness with which the day-to-day tasks and activities of a work group are carried out [3]. One of the factors in Anderson and West’s [3] team climate inventory, task orientation, elicits self-report assessments of individual accountability, task commitment, and a concern to maximize the quality of task performance. In assessing genuine participation, we utilized three items that tap into these aspects of task orientation. The subjects were asked to indicate the extent of their input to the solution, commitment to the team solution, and contribution to the correctness of the team solution. The subjects were also directly observed for diligence in constructing the required task deliverables— data definitions, pseudocode, and completed program code. Data from two teams were dropped for nonconformance to experimental procedures and data from two teams were dropped due to “no-shows” for the second session.

## Dependent Variables and Their Measurement

THE GROUP PROCESS SATISFACTION SCALE developed by Green and Taber [16] wa used to measure software development process satisfaction. The scale items are presented in Appendix B. Reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) for the process satisfaction scale was 0.85. Team member responses were averaged to compute a group-level score. The ratio of total-function-points delivered by both team members to project man-hours (that is, project duration) was utilized as a measure of project team productivity [11].

Given that team productivity was a dependent variable, it was decided to use programming ability and grade point average as covariates. Programming ability was measured utilizing each subject’s grade in a C or C++ programming course taken within a year-to-date. Academic grades have been suggested to be an indicator of native intellectual ability of programmers [3, 38]. Statistical analysis was performed utilizing ANCOVA models, with programming ability and grade point average serving as covariates. A separate analysis utilizing non-orthogonal contrasts was used to examine the multivariate fit between task interdependence, goal conflict, and coordination strategy. Non-orthogonal contrasts are used when complex comparisons be tween two or more “mean of means” (that is, combining more than two means) are of interest. We were specifically interested in comparing the performance of the unconflicted contingencies with consistent designs combined with the partially con flicted contingencies against the performance of the unconflicted contingencies with inconsistent designs combined with the conflicted contingencies designs. Contrast codes that combine the appropriate means into two groups were derived (that is, nonconstraining versus constraining conditions).

## Experimental Procedures

THE EXPERIMENTAL GROUPS executed the entire set of experimental tasks (that is, detailed-design, coding, testing, and integration) in a microcomputer-based lab with workstations supporting the C programming language along with appropriate system and programming language documentation. Subjects were instructed to limit their interactions regarding the experiment to the two experimental sessions. In addition, to limit task execution to the experimental sessions, all materials were picked up at the end of the first session. Each experimental session was executed in three major steps: preexperimental activities, the two task execution sessions, and a ten-minute debriefing session. Two topics were covered during the preexperimental session to maintain a minimum level of task understanding across the subjects. First, a general discussion regarding the specification of function interfaces and global variables was provided to the subjects. Then, an overview of the software subsystem to be developed was provided along with a presentation of a top-level design. Samples of the required design deliverables were provided as handouts. During debriefing, subjec reaction to the experimental session was elicited and discussed. Afterward, the subjects were informed of the study’s purpose and were instructed to avoid any discus sions about the experimental task until the study’s completion.

## Discussion of Findings

THE PURPOSE OF THIS STUDY was to examine and document ideal design configurations that would produce optimal performance and to show that deviations from theoretically prescribed designs (that is, misfit of contingencies or inconsistent coordination) will produce suboptimal performance. Four of the seven hypothesized relationships proved significant (H1, H2, H4, H7), and sixty-two percent of the variance in productivity was explained by the research model. Each of these outcomes is now examined in more depth.

As posited, low task interdependence was observed to produce greater team productivity. This finding is consistent with previous research where tasks partitioned with minimally required interactions, on average, exhibited greater productivity. In such conditions, there was potential for greater task focus and excessive time was not consumed in information processing typically needed for role clarification, negotiation, and development of task-integration strategies among interdependent team members. The level of task interdependence did not differentiate perceptions of process satisfaction (efficiency, task allocation fairness, or fulfillment). This could be attributed to the fact that subjects were exposed to either low- or high-task interdependence, but not both, thereby limiting the use of a reference. In addition, the use of student subjects may have limited the range of experience with which to make a critical assessment of ideal versus nonideal working conditions without an apparen reference.

As predicted, an organic coordination strategy was also observed to produce greater team productivity. Likely, the organic coordination strategy’s facilitation of open and spontaneous communication enables the knowledge acquisition, sharing and integration needed to complete assigned software design and coding tasks. Such an outcome lends considerable support to the view that software development is an informationintensive activity benefiting greatly from organizational structures and processes that facilitate team members’ access to needed design, administrative, and problem-solv ing information [54]. Process satisfaction was significantly higher for organic coordination than it was for mechanistic coordination. The subjects were apparently frustrated with the lack of immediate access to problem-solving and task-sequencing information associated with mechanistic coordination.

The study’s significant result for goal conflict was inconsistent with our prediction (competitively linked goals, rather than cooperatively linked goals, were seen to produce higher levels of software development process satisfaction). It was posited that the competitively linked goals would result in negative performance outcomes, reflected in interference behaviors as team members each sought to produce a software design reflecting their own distinct assigned design goal. In this study, it is possible that the realization of competitively linked goals (that is, software quality versus timeliness of completion) could have increased perceptions of task execution difficulty, resulting in greater mutual intellective analysis of the conflict and, hence, greater immersion into and satisfaction with the software development process. Another possible explanation is that the task duration of this study did not allow an adequate transition from mutual intellective analysis of the conflict to observance of self-serving behavior aimed at attaining conflicting personal goals. Although nonsignificant, adherence to competitively linked goals appeared to generate incompatible task behaviors, which diminished productivity.

The significant interaction effect between task interdependence and coordination strategy for productivity is consistent with prior research. Organic coordination, in general, afforded greater productivity, but the extent of its superiority over mechanistic coordination was greater for high task interdependence. The overhead associated with high task interdependence was mitigated by organic coordination because group members were able to pool member knowledge and skills and collectively discover effective task strategies. This confirms the need for informal and open communication channels in order to facilitate role clarification and development of task integration strategies among interdependent team members. In addition, decisional autonomy can evidently facilitate timely decision-making and reduce the need for time-consuming negotiation activities. Also, organic coordination’s general superiority suggests that a surplus of information processing capacity does not necessarily overload low task interdependence conditions. Consequently, this design deviation is non-constraining and can yield suboptimal performance.

The hypothesized goal conflict/coordination and goal conflict/task interdependence interactions were not supported. We suggested that diminished productivity and process satisfaction from adherence to conflicted task goals (that is, quality versus timeliness) would be exacerbated under high task interdependent conditions. As with goal conflict in general, task duration may offer an explanation for no interaction. It is likely that greater task duration may be needed for goal conflict to generate negative effect, negative motivational mechanisms (that is, less effort), and task integration difficulties. These problems must arise for coordination mechanisms applied to task interdependence to significantly differentiate productivity and satisfaction.

Multiple contingency research [17] suggests that the analysis of the interactions among multiple contingencies warrant the use of non-orthogonal contrasts. Gresov [17] observed that, for some configurations of contingencies, deviation in design could yield suboptimal performance as opposed to minimal performance. For pro ductivity, the results of this study support Gresov’s [17] previous findings. Groups in unconflicted contexts utilizing consistent coordination mechanisms combined with groups in partially conflicted contexts (that is, one or more, but not all, contingencies consistently addressed) performed 55 percent better than the combined unconflicted contexts with inconsistent designs and conflicted contexts. In this study, deviation to low task interdependence exhibited minimal trade-offs in productivity (0.031) as com pared to deviation to high task interdependence (0.072). The minimal trade-offs afforded by low task interdependence conditions offer latitude in addressing either low goal conflict (organic coordination) or high goal conflict (mechanistic coordination). In contrast, design deviation to high task interdependence was more detrimental. The trade-offs in design deviation for low goal conflict (0.06) and high goal conflict (0.04) were relatively equal. This suggests that, in this study, task interdependence was the more dominant contingency of the two [17], in that deviation from theoretically prescribed designs was associated with either suboptimal or minimal performance. De sign deviation resulted in greater variance. In the conflicted context (that is, high task interdependence/high goal conflict) suboptimal performance was not possible. Mini mal performance resulted regardless of coordination mechanisms utilized. Apparently, adherence to the quality goal delayed the imposed integration of code developed by subjects under the timeliness goal. When applying mechanistic coordination, it is likely that performance was further delayed by insufficient interactions and lack of immediate access to problem solving and task sequencing information. Group member actions presumably became poorly orchestrated and misdirected effort conceivably occurred. Similarly, increased interactions (that is, organic coordination) could have resulted in time wasted in excessive dialogue aimed at resolving goal conflicts Clearly, when contrasting both unconflicted and partially conflicted contingencie against conflicted contingencies and inconsistent design use cases, the results reveal a relationship between task interdependence, goal conflict, and coordination that is not observed in orthogonal contrasts.

## The Study’s Limitations

FIRST, LABORATORY EXPERIMENTATION, although it allows one to isolate treatment effects and to gain control over confounding variables, raises threats to external validity. The use of student subjects performing a scaled-down programming task presents limits on the generalizability of results to a real-world context. Second, although the subjects were advised to limit discussion of the experimental task to the sessions, we cannot be certain that team members did not discuss the task in between the two sessions. However, to limit between-session dialogue, all completed work was picked up at the end of the first session and returned at the start of the second session. Third, student subject experience at working in teams may be limited as compared to stable and longer tenured groups that may exhibit higher levels of involvement in their work. The assessment of subject “genuine participation” was performed using sub ject self-reports. However, method bias (that is, potential motivation to overrate individual contribution) from this self-report measure was minimized by direct observation of the subjects for continued task diligence. Groups that did not exhibit observable continued task involvement were dropped from the analysis. Finally, the limited sample size resulted in low observed power tests for some of the statistical tests. Small sample sizes can lead to the conclusion that there are no differences between the treatment groups when in fact differences are present. A larger sample size better facilitates the detection of the smallest meaningful difference between treatment groups by overcoming threats to conclusion validity, such as scales with low reliability or ineffective treatment implementation.

## Implications of Findings and Conclusion

THIS STUDY’S FINDINGS have a number of implications for both practice and research. These are now discussed and are then followed by a conclusion.

## Implications for Practice

Four major implications for practice can be drawn from the study’s findings. First, the findings for task interdependence strongly reinforce the desirability of producing very modularized software designs that allow project team members to be assigned largely independent development tasks. Second, these findings indicate that, in general, productivity is enhanced when implementing organic coordination within a software development team. Furthermore, the observation that an organic coordination strategy was associated with greater software development process satisfaction provides further evidence of the information-intensive nature of this work activity, and, very possibly, characterizes the preferred work environment of programmers (that is, self- or teamcontrol). In addition, in discussions held during debriefing sessions, subjects complained about the lack of immediate access to other team members (that is, mechanistic coordination). Other complaints included waiting for team members to complete needed code (that is, task interdependence) and team member displeasure at revising completed code to facilitate overall code integration (that is, task interdepen dence). Third, software project managers are advised to select their tactics regarding coordinating strategies only after they have determined the nature of the task assignments (that is, low versus high task interdependence) involved with a given software project or even with the specific phase of a software project. Finally, software managers should note that when simultaneously addressing multiple contingencies, design deviations could yield varying degrees of suboptimal performance. In addition, attempts should be made to identify the contingency where design deviation would be associated with the most detrimental effect on performance (that is, the dominan contingency).

## Implications for Research

Three main implications for further research examining managerial strategies regard ing software development similarly follow from the study’s findings. First, this study has upheld, within the software development context, a set of relationships found in other similar as well as dissimilar work contexts, the existence of contingent relationship between task interdependence and coordination strategy with regard to task. It is expected that the confirmation of such a relationship could have a major influence on the nature of future research models explaining the desirability of specific managerial and organizational tactics for software development projects. Second, the inconclusive, and somewhat contradictory, results observed regarding competitively linked goals underscores the need for future research, which investigates this phenomenon as it relates to software development projects. Prior research in organization behavior has demonstrated, rather convincingly, that goal linkages are important to task accomplishment. Stressing distinctive goals and establishing varying performance levels for these goals is bound to have an effect on the effort exerted by team members as well as the achievement of specific task goals. But, what happens when a software development team is, explicitly or implicitly, given mixed messages about a project’s goals. Would performance be improved or hurt? Would team member satisfaction be higher or lower? It has been noted that personnel attracted to the computer field have a high need for growth, low need for social interaction, and an inherent need for performance mastery [13, 38]. This suggests that, when faced with goal-conflict conditions, programmers may have an inherent tendency to accept the challenges and find satisfaction in engaging in behaviors that would move toward increased performance. This could explain why results from the current study observed greater team member satisfaction with the development process under the competitively linked goals situation. To what extent is such an observation valid, and to what extent is it related to the personality of software professionals or to task duration? Clearly, fur ther research on this topic is needed.

Finally, our findings emphasize an important role of non-orthorgonal contrasts in uncovering complex phenomena that may not be revealed in classical orthorgona contrasts. When multiple contingencies can assume partially conflicted configurations, orthorgonal contrasts may not reveal multiple interactions. Clearly, these results reveal a relationship between task interdependence, goal conflict, and coordination that is not observed in orthogonal contrasts. Assessments of multiple contingencies should ad dress design deviations (that is, partially conflicted), as prescribed by theory, which yield suboptimal performance (such as, organic coordination applied to low task interdependence). This approach provides a more refined specification of inconsistent patterns of contingencies and enables us to make better predictions regarding misfits between context and design [17]. Further research on a mix of other contingencie faced by software project managers is warranted.

## Conclusion

THIS STUDY’S PRIMARY CONTRIBUTION lies with its finding that, within a tightly controlled software development context, task interdependence and goal conflict can assume configurations that are either unconflicted, partially conflicted, or unconflicted. Furthermore, this multiple contingency perspective reveals contingency design latitude where either optimal, suboptimal, or minimal performance would result depending on coordination mechanisms applied. Software managers are, thus, strongly advised to consider the joint effects of these two variables when crafting (and revising over a project’s life cycle) their management tactics for a given software project. At minimum, when facing multiple contingencies, project managers could benefit by identifying and designing according to the contingency where design deviation would be associated with lowest performance (that is, the dominant contingency). A second contribution is the confirmation of existing theoretical models and empirical findings positing the contingent influence of task interdependence and coordination strategy on task outcomes. Classical software project management has often relied on the use of predefined development methodologies, scheduled periodic design meetings, and formal documentation of system design and change activities. The findings of this study suggest that, as software systems grow in functional complexity (such as, globally interconnected online analytical processing systems), software project managers must deviate from classical practice that force systems analysts and programmers into restrictive and unproductive task behaviors. Under such conditions, project managers should opt for allowing self-managed control (that is, organic coordination), which would empower an individual to formulate an appropriate task strategy that would facilitate accurate and timely completion.
