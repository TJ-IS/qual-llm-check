# Performance-Centered Design of Knowledge-Intensive Processes

ANNE P. MASSEY, MITZI M. MONTOYA-WEISS, AND TONY M. O’DRISCOLL

ABSTRACT: We develop and illustrate a performance-centered design (PCD) methodology for structuring knowledge-intensive, ill-defined processes. PCD provides a holistic view of a performance environment by considering the complex interdepen dencies between the organizational context, business processes, and individual performers. The context for our theoretical exposition is the fuzzy front-end of the new product development (NPD) process. Despite the fact that front-end concept definition and selection is central to a firm’s innovation capability, these activities are illstructured and typically the most poorly managed in the entire innovation process. Through a case study, we illustrate the proposed PCD methodology as applied to the fuzzy front-end and additionally illustrate how electronic performance support technology can be utilized to support the fuzzy front-end process. Although specifically applied within the context of one firm, we contend that the PCD methodology is applicable to other knowledge-intensive and relatively unstructured processes.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: electronic performance support, fuzzy front-end, new product development, performance-centered design, problem-solving theory, prob lem structure, systems theory.

GLOBAL COMPETITIVE ENVIRONMENTS and technological factors are increasingly volatile and evolving rapidly. In response, organizations are examining how they can better leverage knowledge assets for value creation. A firm’s long-term viability depends on the successful expansion and exploitation of its knowledge assets by identifying key leverage points for achieving business results [2, 3, 18, 19, 53]. These leverage points often reside in core business processes, such as, new product development (NPD) or customer relationship management, that may be reengineered to achieve dynamic improvements in performance by capitalizing on or expanding the organization’s knowledge resources and capabilities [33].

Business processes are knowledge-intensive, reflecting what employees know abou customers, products, past successes and failures, and the processes themselves [43, 68]. Knowledge is both a product of and an input to processes and work [22, 45]. Individuals working in the context of a business process are essentially engaged in problem-solving activities as they attempt to make sense of some phenomena [30]. Given that the business process is where work is accomplished, it is the fundamenta link between organizational and individual performance. Generating performance improvements requires deep understanding of how work is (or could be) organized, how knowledge is exploited, and how these factors relate to an organization’s competitive strategy [62]. Providing performance support to knowledge workers and seamlessly integrating the management of knowledge into business processes is particularly challenging when processes and work are ill-structured or poorly defined [3, 20].

The purpose of this paper is to develop and illustrate a methodology for finding and lending structure to knowledge-intensive, ill-structured processes. The context for our theoretical exposition is the fuzzy front-end of the NPD process. Performancecentered design (PCD) provides a holistic view of a knowledge worker’s performance environment by considering the complex interdependencies between the organizational context, business processes, and individual performers [61, 62, 67]. Our central thesis is that PCD provides a powerful approach for understanding and accurately characterizing performance problems and identifying the most appropriate interventions to alleviate performance discrepancies. PCD offers a systematic way to analyze performance problems and their causes, and to develop and implement interventions to address problems and improve performance in knowledge-intensive environments [13, 28, 58]. By addressing “what to do,” PCD facilitates the reengineering of knowledge-intensive business processes. By considering “how to do it,” PCD informs the development and implementation of performance-centered support systems.

In the sections that follow, we first describe the fuzzy front-end of NPD in terms of its problem-solving activities. Next, we develop the PCD methodology. Through a three-year longitudinal case study, we illustrate the PCD methodology as applied to the fuzzy front-end of a firm’s NPD process and briefly describe how the firm imple mented the newly designed process through an electronic performance support system (EPSS). EPSS represents a new paradigm integrating principles found in artificial intelligence, human performance technology, computer-based training, information systems, and user-centered design [37]. Importantly, however, our case illustrates that before an organization should touch the issue of technology, it must first isolate a business need, identify where the ability to capture and reuse knowledge will be most profitable, and figure out what types of knowledge are needed and what knowledge needs to be shared. Although specifically applied within the context of NPD at one firm, we contend that the PCD methodology is applicable to other complex and relatively unstructured knowledge-intensive processes. Finally, we discuss the implications for theory and practice and we identify avenues for future research.

## The “Fuzzy” Front-End from a Problem-Solving Perspective

INCREASINGLY, COMPETITIVE SUPERIORITY is a function of an organization’s capacity to leverage its knowledge base to continuously conceive and develop new products [24, 34, 35, 44, 52]. The front-end concept definition and selection process of NPD is central to a firm’s innovation capability because it is the fundamental driver of the entire NPD process. Because the front-end NPD activities are often ill-structured and ad hoc, concept definition and selection are often referred to as the “fuzzy front-end” of the NPD process [15, 16]. Limited research indicates that the majority of front-end decisions are made without any systematic methods, which consequently contributes to new product failure [15, 17]. This suggests that even marginal improvements to the front-end NPD process would be of significant value to firms given that R&D resources are limited and new product failure is costly.

The front-end NPD process is difficult to map and (re)design since potentially every new product idea is unique. Front-end NPD activities are dynamic and complex, and the boundaries of the front-end process are subjective and somewhat unclear. This suggests that innovation is best understood as a process of problem definition followed by development and application of knowledge to solve a problem and create a business opportunity. Although some past research has considered the NPD process from a problem-solving perspective [11, 12, 24, 36, 51, 74], there has been very little systematic research focused on methodologies for developing and implementing the NPD process from this perspective. Before turning our attention to the PCD methodology, we first examine the fuzzy front-end of NPD process from a problem-solving perspective.

Problem solving is about understanding [30, 55]. Essentially, individuals engaged in the front-end of NPD are attempting to understand a set of phenomena, or solve a problem such that a course of action can be recommended. The front-end NPD problem-solving process involves the deployment or creation of new knowledge for the purpose of enhancing organizational performance. Whereas a number of conceptualizations exist, the problem-solving process is typically described as requiring definition, analysis and alternatives generation, evaluation and selection, and implementation [25, 63]. Past research has generally emphasized the more actionoriented activities of solution finding and implementation, rather than the preliminary problem-defining activities [65]. Problem definition entails the collection of relevant variables and the identification and exploration of the relationships between the variables in order to define the problem [1, 54, 73, 75].

Within the fuzzy front-end of NPD, the development of an idea into a sufficiently well-defined concept that allows for specific action depends on problem-definition activities. In essence, concept definition is about accurately understanding the concept, rather than immediately making a decision about a concept that is not really well understood. Concept definition involves transforming a raw idea into a robust concept through careful delineation of the underlying technologies, identification of expected customer benefits, and an assessment of the market opportunity. A robust concept is one that allows decision-makers to effectively sense whether the newly defined opportunity is worth committing resources to for further exploration.

As the number of variables increases and the specificity and measurability of their efficacy relationships diminishes, a problem is characterized as being more complex and ill-structured [73]. The concept of structure applies not only to the problem, but also to the problem-solving process itself. Here, a problem is ill-structured when the problem solver lacks an effective approach to address the problem or decision task [71]. Smith [65] suggests that before the problem itself can be addressed, the would-be solver must solve the second-order problem of determining how to proceed. If there is little uncertainty regarding how to proceed, this second order problem is trivial and the original problem would be considered well structured. Conversely, the lack of a program to accomplish the desired outcome makes the original problem ill-structured. Thus, structure can be explained in terms of one’s knowledge of the problem (content-based) and knowledge of how to address it (process-based). Ill-structuredness derives from a mismatch between the knowledge requirements of the problem in either form and the capabilities possessed by the problem solver or decision-maker.

Specifically in regard to the fuzzy front-end of NPD, there are multiple problem solvers involved in the front-end concept development and selection tasks. Concep development is typically the domain of an idea generator (such as, an engineer) whose expertise is usually functional and idea-specific. Concept selection is typically the domain of a manager or executive whose knowledge includes business opportunity analysis and resource availability. These various problem solvers may have varying levels of content-based knowledge and process-based experience. The lack of content knowledge or process structure can impede performance. Thus, structuring the fuzzy front-end of NPD requires a design process that carefully considers the multitude of performers and their needs within the larger organizational system within which the fuzzy front-end operates.

In sum, past research has not considered how to design and implement the frontend of the NPD process in a manner that is both consistent and adaptive in application across time, people, and projects. The need for more research on structuring and supporting front-end activities necessitates the need for more studies on methodolo gies to accomplish this objective [23, 39]. With this in mind, we turn our attention to the PCD methodology.

## Performance-Centered Design

THE ULTIMATE BENEFIT OF ANY SYSTEM designed to support performance is often strongly related to the extent to which it exploits whatever structure already exists or provides additional structure [4]. PCD, with its roots in systems thinking, is a methodology that involves a thorough and systematic gathering of data to (1) define needs and performance goals, (2) analyze performance problems and identify their root causes, and (3) design and implement comprehensive interventions to improve performance and achieve goals [28, 61, 62]. PCD takes a holistic view of the performance environment by encompassing and considering the complex interdependencie between the business context, work processes, and individual performers. Importantly, PCD treats “what to do” as well as “how to do it” as part of the problem [13]. As Figure 1 highlights, business needs and performance goals inform the definition of process needs and goals. Similarly, individual performer needs and goals are defined in regard to process needs and goals. The exploration of these complex relationships within and between levels is the essential first step in PCD.

Given its goal of supporting performance, PCD is closely related to systems thinking as applied to human performance in organizational work settings [5, 8, 9, 10, 41, 59, 65]. PCD is both systematic and systemic as it seeks to describe successful performance outputs at multiple levels, as well as the behaviors of individual performers (covert and overt) that will produce those outputs [27, 28, 67]. The achievement of these outputs clearly must be of value to the individual and the organization. However, individual behavior is solely a means to an end—behavior modification canno be accomplished in isolation from its consequences or from context [29].

As a methodology, PCD shifts the focus from human behavior to human accomplishment, the valued output of behavior. PCD considers all intervention means, methods, and media (including information technology) to enhance performance. However, before performance interventions can be offered, performance problems and their root causes must be defined [64, 65]. A lack of attention to these steps may result in an inappropriate solution, the omittance of relevant phenomenon, or solving the wrong performance problem [48]. Figure 2 presents the key steps in the methodology [29, 46, 58, 59].

## Performance Analysis

Within PCD, the performance analysis framework in Figure 3 is intended to assist in the accurate characterization of performance problem(s) with reference to needs and performance goals. This systems thinking-based framework involves gaining a suffi cient understanding of the components of a particular situation before taking any action to bring about improvement [9, 64, 65, 73]. Analysis begins with the systemic or organizational level of performance, then narrows in focus to the process level of performance, and finally the job/performer level. Although linear in description, PCD analysis is fully iterative in practice.

Performance analysis begins at the business or organizational level. An organization is an adaptive system that takes in various inputs and produces valued outputs for customers. The organization exists in a larger environment that consists of competitors who vie for similar markets and input resources and who are similarly influenced by general environmental factors such as the economy and government regulations [38]. Analysis at this high level involves understanding strategic direction in relation to the external environment and defining any strategic-level performance gaps.

Figure 3. PCD: Analysis Phase

The process level of performance concerns workflows and support infrastructures designed to support business level performance goals. Given that the process level is where work is accomplished, it is the key link between organizational and individual levels of performance. Although the most substantive change in performance can take place here, the process level is often the least understood or managed level of performance [40]. Defining process level performance gaps requires analysis of the current process relative to its ability to achieve defined process goals that are informed by business goals. PCD analysis is driven by the perspective that understand ing business and process level goals and performance gaps is essential to understanding and improving individual (and thus ultimately process and organizational) perfor mance. Individuals do not work within a vacuum; rather, they operate within the context of this interdependent performance system [28, 61, 62]. Individual perfor mance must be understood within this broader context.

Specifically, a performer is required to process a variety of inputs for which there are desired outputs. For every output produced (as well as the action required to produce the output), there is a resultant set of consequences or some event that affects the performer. Because consequences are interpreted as either positive or negative, individual behavior is influenced (or motivated) by consequences. Performers will do things that lead to positive consequences and avoid things that lead to negative consequences. Thus, the last component of the performance system at this level is feedback regarding these consequences [61, 62]. This discussion highlights the fact that individual performance is not simply a function of individual knowledge, skills, or capacity. Rather, there are other factors that can influence individual performance: task interference, performance specifications, consequences, and feedback [61, 62]. Consistent performance is a function of all factors and the cause of poor performance is rarely solely attributable to a lack of knowledge, skills, or capacity. Instruction or training alone cannot single-handedly countervail the predominant forces of task interference, negative consequences, or lack of feedback, which are firmly embedded in the performance system. In sum, the PCD performance analysis framework facilitates an accurate diagnosis of performance problem(s) through an understanding of the multilevel factors that affect performance.

## Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis provides a diagnostic approach for identifying the underlying causes of performance gaps defined during the performance analysis phase [8, 25, 29, 47, 50, 56]. As Figure 3 depicts, root cause analysis is multilevel in nature in order to capture the interdependencies between the business, process, and individual performance gaps. For example, the root cause of business level performance gaps may be process level problems. The cause of process problems may be ill-defined tasks or a lack of content knowledge on the task performers. The goal is to close the gap between the present state of performance and the desired higher state within each level, and to subsequently affect other levels due to interdependencies. As Figure 3 shows, although characteristics of individual performers (such as, a lack of skills or knowledge) may cause performance gaps, other environmental factors (such as, a lack of data, tools, or incentives) may also contribute. Root cause analysis seeks to examine each of these to determine their efficacy and whether they can be manipulated or leveraged to affect performance.

## Intervention Design and Implementation

Once performance gaps and their root causes have been identified, interventions must be designed and implemented to address gaps as effectively and efficiently as possible. Oftentimes, these processes are treated as mutually exclusive and linear [72]. PCD recognizes, however, that various constraints and barriers (or opportunities) will likely affect intervention design and implementation decisions in some iterative fashion. For example, because interventions are intended to influence performance at multilevels, systems theorists argue that interventions must be systemically desirable (such as [9, 10, 60]). They should not be isolated initiatives; rather, once implemented, they should be well integrated into the organization as a whole (such as instituting mechanisms for assessing effectiveness, making sure resources are available) [10, 66]. Moreover, interventions should be culturally feasible to implement as this determines whether or not performance change will occur. As these examples illustrate, the reality of the implementation context should inform intervention design. Advance design information also enables the timely consideration of implementation alternatives and the early involvement of the constituencies most likely to be affected by the intervention. As shown in Figure 4, PCD treats the intervention design and implementation processes as iterative. This implies that these processes should be conducted in parallel rather than linearly.

Figure 4. PCD: Intervention Design and Implementation

Because complex and ill-structured problems tend to be multicausal [64, 65], more than one intervention will often be needed to overcome a performance problem. In terventions represent both responses to identified causes of human performance problems as well as opportunities for improving performance. Any given application of PCD could draw from diverse fields such as human factors, training, personnel management, and information systems to develop interventions that yield high performance valued outcomes [14]. Interventions include solutions concerned with improving the development of individuals or teams (such as training) as well as solutions that center on managing and rewarding the performance of individuals and groups (such as incentives). Other possible interventions include the redesign of processes and tasks and may include providing technology-based performance support applications and tools [14, 28].

In sum, interventions should be comprehensive, solving the whole performance problem, not just part of it. Often, the only way to accomplish this and achieve performance goals is to change tasks, structure, people, and technology simultaneously [42]. Although a number of interventions can be conceptualized, the goal of the intervention design and implementation phases of PCD is to ensure the efficacy of the interventions [67]. Efficacy stems from a comprehensive understanding of the complex interactions between the work environment, the work processes, and the work performer(s).

## An Illustrative Application of PCD to the Fuzzy Front-End

PROPER APPLICATION OF PCD requires a holistic perspective of the problem context—here the fuzzy front-end of NPD. Thus, to provide an illustration, we conducted a longitudinal in-depth case study over a three-year period at a large telecommunica tions company, Nortel Networks. Nortel is one of the world’s leading telecommuni cations equipment manufacturers with 74,000 employees working in over 22 countries.

## Data Collection Methods

In order to fully capture and validate the phenomenological nature of the PCD process, data was obtained from multiple sources over the three-year period: (1) multiple interviews with project team members and the project manager, (2) researcher observations, and (3) project documentation. The use of multiple methods enhances the validity of case study findings because it helps to eliminate biases that might result from relying exclusively on any one data-collection method, source, or analyst [76].

First, a key source of data for this research was project documentation, including: (1) International Standards Organization (ISO) documents, (2) project plans, (3) project reports, (4) project presentations, and (5) internal publications. Since our research interest is studying the PCD methodology as applied to the fuzzy front-end, an interpretational analysis was conducted on this documentation to reveal the analytic, development, and implementation processes. For example, process content, chronology, measurement, visualization, and promotion activities were ascertained from the vari ous project documents. Interpretations of the patterns, themes, and relationships were updated as the project progressed over the three-year period.

Second, multiple interviews were conducted over the three-year period with members of the development team and the project manager. The interviews allowed us to validate and ensure that the research findings provided an accurate account of the PCD process. Observation provided the third data component. One of the authors acted as a member of Nortel’s development team. The researcher attended team meetings, project demonstrations, project meetings and presentations, and review sessions. The researcher was required by the firm to keep a log of his daily activities. These historical observations provide corroborative evidence of the assumptions and find ings drawn from the project documentation and the individual interviews.

## PCD at Nortel: Analysis Phase

Two senior executives at Nortel identified a problem of strategic importance to the firm. The divestiture of AT&T in 1984 and the Telecommunications Reform Act of 1996 spawned intense competition in the telecom industry. This competition yielded an explosion in the development of new and innovative telecommunications technology that, in turn, provides consumers with higher quality and lower-cost telecommunications products and services. The two senior executives perceived, however, that Nortel’s competitive “war chest” of innovative new product and service ideas was empty—the implication being that Nortel had lost its ability to innovate. A full idea “war chest” was needed to ensure that Nortel could distinguish itself from its competitors, win over new customers, and maintain the loyalty of existing customers. The executives viewed differentiation through innovation as the key strategy that would allow Nortel to survive in the increasingly competitive telecommunica tions market. A team was formed to investigate and address how Nortel could achieve this strategic goal.

Preliminary analysis indicated that the original concern regarding the empty “war chest” was merely a symptom of a deeper and much larger problem with the fuzzy front-end of the company’s existing NPD process. By conducting a thorough root cause analysis of the business level performance problem, the team determined that the performance gap (that is, an empty war chest) was caused by a work process obstacle (that is, the front-end of the NPD process) rather than by a lack of innovative ability on the part of Nortel’s employees (that is, the work performers). Internal research verified that the employee base had the skills, knowledge, and capacity to offer new and innovative telecommunications product and service ideas. However, the organization did not provide necessary support (that is, data, resources, tools, processes or incentives) to leverage this innovative capability. Thus, the root cause of the business problem was attributed to the lack of a clearly defined front-end process to identify and capture employee-generated ideas.

Internal analysis revealed that front-end concept definition activities were ill-structured due to the lack of a development process for idea generators. Similarly, the concept selection decisions were ill-structured due to a lack of objective evaluation criteria for the decision-makers. The conclusion was that Nortel needed a formalized, front-end NPD process that would provide a consistent and structured approach for concept definition, comparison, and screening. Finding and lending structure to these front-end activities would simultaneously facilitate individual-level performance and ultimately support the strategic business goal.

## PCD at Nortel: Intervention Design and Implementation Phase

The iterative and multilevel performance and root cause analyses focused on identifying where the most important performance gains could be realized in the front-end concept definition and selection process. Intervention design would focus on developing a conceptual model of process-based intervention to address the fuzzy frontend (and related empty war chest) problem. The conceptual process model would describe the activities that should be in the process in a structure based on logica dependencies. However, as prescribed by the PCD methodology, the conceptual process model must be compared to the realities of the implementation environment. That is, decisions and actions emerge out of interactions between design and implementation activities. The final performance system (that is, front-end NPD process) would have to be organizationally sustainable, systemically desirable, and culturally feasible. Thus, intervention design at Nortel followed an iterative evolutionary path as the design team simultaneously considered the design alternatives and the implementation context.

## Intervention Design: Conceptual Process Model

The intervention design process began with an exhaustive external analysis of best practices NPD research (such as [7, 15, 16, 31, 49, 70]) and intensive study of leading innovative firms (such as, 3M, Hewlett-Packard). This external analysis served as a benchmark for designing a fuzzy front-end process that reflected Nortel’s interna objectives and constraints. The guideline for the design process was the articulated business need to develop a superior front-end concept definition and selection process that captures the innovative product and service ideas of Nortel’s employees. In identifying intervention requirements, components, and specifications, the team relied on external secondary research, parallel work from expert cross-functional teams, and an internal process validation test bed to design a customized, process-based solution.

The intervention design process defined a structure for the fuzzy front-end that enables consistent and comprehensive analysis of new product and service concepts. The design consists of a standard process and metrics for concept development, evaluation, and selection. The result was a multiphased process that incorporates the sequences, relationships, and interrelationships of the work activities in the fuzzy front-end. As shown in Figure 5, the process consists of four phases: (1) preliminary idea qualification, (2) idea-to-concept development or concept definition, (3) concept rating, and (4) concept assessment. This process lends structure to individual performers by providing standard, predefined sets of criteria and rating mechanisms. The idea qualification phase provides a quick mechanism whereby many ideas can be narrowed to a few viable ones for concept development. This phase offers a set of questions used to prescreen a new product or service idea at a very high level analysis of market and technological readiness. From past research, the team concluded that each qualified idea needed to be developed into a robust concept and assessed according to marketing, technology, business, and human-factors criteria. Developing concepts using standardized criteria and formats allows concept evaluation by decision-makers to be more formal, consistent, and comprehensive. As a result, each product or service concept can be assessed and directly compared to any or all others that have been through the process.

## Intervention Implementation: Constraints and Alternatives

The process implementation phase is often the point where the organization begins to resist the proposed process change—particularly if the change threatens established norms and work practices within the organization [69]. At Nortel, in-depth research revealed two important implementation challenges. First, since the fuzzy front-end had been paid very little attention in the past, senior management was relatively unwilling to dedicate substantial resources to implement any new process. Second, the performance and cause analyses had revealed that the existing customer-driven NPD process was already providing a demand for extension products that far outstripped the company’s ability to supply them. Therefore, any resources that might be added to the company’s NPD pool would most likely be dedicated to current customer demands rather than developing a new front-end NPD process. These two factors represented significant organizational constraints that were explicitly considered in the iterative intervention design and implementation phase.

Given the organizational constraints, the development team considered implementa tion alternatives that would not compromise the design of the front-end process. Whereas concept evaluation activities are in the domain of decision-makers with the authority to approve further research and development, how to implement the first three process phases in Figure 5 was not a foregone conclusion. One that was identified would entai the use of expert cross-functional teams to qualify and develop every submitted idea. This was deemed to be an extremely costly alternative in terms of time commitmen and opportunity of pulling individuals away from their jobs for pre-project analysis. This presented the team with an implementation constraint—shift the burden of concept definition (that is, the first three phases) to the original idea generator.

The performance goal for idea generators would be to translate their embryonic ideas into robust concepts by conducting a thorough and disciplined analysis in a standard fashion. Whereas the notion of developing concepts along marketing, technology, business, and human-factors dimensions lends structure to the fuzzy frontend, idea generators typically do not possess sufficient knowledge in all of these areas to conduct such analyses. Thus, three implementation alternatives were considered. Nortel could train all employees in the areas of marketing, technology, human factors, and business so that they are able to qualify their own ideas and develop them into robust concepts. Nortel could assign internal subject-matter expert mentors in the area of marketing, technology, human factors, and business to each idea generator. Finally, Nortel could capture the expertise of these mentors and incorporate it into an electronic tool to guide and advise the idea generators as they qualify their ideas and then develop them into robust concepts, thus, providing content knowledge to idea generators. Each alternative was evaluated according to its cost-effectiveness and feasibility given Nortel’s current structure, resource availability, and culture. The objective was to identify an implementation solution that best addressed the perfor mance goals and organizational constraints, with minimum organizational and hu man distress, and within time and budget.

Based on this analysis, alternatives that advocated the use of human expertise to implement the process were deemed too costly and difficult to implement effectively at Nortel. An implementation approach that leveraged EPSS technology could create an electronic version of a human subject-matter expert. EPSS technology offered additional benefits in that it could provide efficiencies in process oversight and administration as well as electronic repositories of the intellectual property associated with idea generation. An EPSS may be considered an electronic infrastructure that captures, stores, and distributes individual and corporate knowledge to enable individuals to achieve desired levels of performance in the fastest possible time and with a minimum of support from others [6, 57]. The goal of the software environment is to provide access to integrated information, knowledge, learning experiences, advice, and guidance at “the moment of need” [26, 28]. Whereas all software tools are intended to support human performance in some fashion, an EPSS is distinguished from other approaches (such as, traditional systems development, expert systems development) by its attention to enabling performance in the context of work [37].

## Process Implementation via an EPSS

The iterative and informing relationship between intervention design and implementation context led to the formalization of the designed process in the EPSS, called

Virtual Mentor. Essentially, Virtual Mentor automates each phase of the intervention process and supports the performance of those individuals who are working within the process. A cross-functional team of process and system development experts developed the EPSS. As a result of the intensive information exchange between these experts, the EPSS was developed with an intrinsic awareness of performer needs and workplace realities. The EPSS simultaneously supports the performance of: (1) an idea generator, (2) a decision-maker, and (3) a process owner. Each of these individual performers adds or uses different data, informational, and knowledge content in the EPSS. As such, each requires different kinds of performance support (that is, support that allows them to conduct their work activity more effectively and effi ciently), and they each need unique interfaces. The intervention design process required careful analysis of the divergent needs of the multiple performers in order to integrate the varied requirements into a single, comprehensive tool. Table 1 summarizes how Virtual Mentor is integrated with and supports the performance model of each performer.<sup>1</sup>

Briefly, Virtual Mentor provides knowledge-based support to idea generators so they are not faced with ill-structured tasks attributable to knowledge inadequacies. Similarly, decision-based support is provided to decision-makers enabling them to compare and contrast the various concepts’ salient attributes as they relate to business needs and performance goals. Such support enables decision-makers to make informed funding (go/no go) decisions for new product/service concepts. Finally, productivity-based support is provided to NPD process owners tasked with tracking the progress of idea generators and decision-makers that interface with the system.

In sum, the EPSS was designed to overcome the implementation issues that Nortel faced. The EPSS design requirements were derived to ensure that a lowest-cost and optimal-output implementation solution was developed. The result of this implementation effort was an EPSS that facilitates the collection, development, evaluation, and screening of employees’ innovative new product or service ideas.

## Discussion and Directions

OUR PRIMARY CONTRIBUTION TO RESEARCH is the PCD method that we have proposed as an approach for designing and improving ill-structured, knowledge-intensive processes like the fuzzy front-end of NPD. PCD applies systems thinking and offers a detailed problem-solving approach for finding structure in complex problematic situations defined by multilevel interactions and interdependencies (goals, gaps, and causes). The PCD method leads to purposeful action (that is, design and implementation) aimed at performance improvement. It can be applied to overcome process-based ill-structuredness (how to proceed) as well as content-based illstructuredness. Importantly, PCD is about learning, not optimizing, since it is not possible to optimize messy, ill-structured problem domains. Successful application of PCD requires that it be tailored to the requirements of the highly interactive per formance environment in question.

At Nortel, the PCD method yielded performance results by validating the business need to address the problem, identifying the root causes of the performance problem and articulating the most appropriate implementation solution to address the problem. Nortel believes that the application of PCD to the “empty war chest” problem has created a process and system that provides their organization with strategic competitive advantage in the areas of innovation and market acceptance rate. Through our longitudinal case study, we illustrated how PCD can be applied to find structure for a complex problematic system. A particularly important element of the PCD methodology is the integrated approach to intervention design and implementation. Our case study highlights the important point that all performance interventions should be based on a thorough analysis of both the causes and the performance context of the problem at hand. Without adequate analysis, it is unlikely that any intervention can be effective in addressing the multicausal nature of most business performance problems. And yet our case study also illustrates the importance of not overemphasizing analysis at the expense of design and implementation. Without the selection and design of appropriate interventions, the analysis phase of this work process is of little value. It is important to ensure that interventions are implemented with a minimum of organizational and individual stress and with the maximum output at the lowest cost [66].

Our primary contribution to practice is the illustration of a successful application of a systematic method for structuring and supporting ill-structured processes. In addition, the Nortel case illustrates how electronic performance support technology can be utilized to support the fuzzy front-end process. Advances in information technol ogy (IT) are enabling redesign of core business processes to significantly enhance productivity and performance [32]. IT applications can be designed to provide knowledge-based support, whereas simultaneously overseeing and administering process implementation and capturing the intellectual property of the ideas that the process generates. However, as illustrated in the Nortel case, we contend that the design of supporting IT applications cannot be disentangled from the task or process. Thus, the true potential of technology as an implementation mechanism will only be realized when the underlying tenets of problem solving are intentionally embedded in the tools [21, 30].

At Nortel, the EPSS solution offers several benefits. It provides the ability to provide contextual knowledge-support on demand. An EPSS facilitates the development of repositories of the knowledge created in the concept definition process. For example, an electronic catalog of accepted and rejected concepts creates the opportunity to track and measure the performance of the front-end. It also creates the opportunity to further leverage technology to monitor “idea readiness.” For example, intelligent agents could be employed to scan the catalog of rejected ideas relative to changes in the organizational and environmental constraints that led to rejection. In general, the intranet has tremendous potential as a collaborative vehicle that can facilitate cross-functional, innovative work, thus overcoming the rigidity and gravity of traditionally siloed bureaucratic organizations. In the Nortel case, expert knowledge was elicited from internal business, marketing, technology, and human-factors specialists, and then it was contextualized to the task of defining and selecting concepts.

The EPSS allowed the organization to leverage its knowledge and supply it over a technological medium such that it could be applied to decisions regarding new product opportunities. At Nortel, the PCD method yielded an EPSS solution that simultaneously met the desired performance while being congruent with the organizational context within which the problem was identified. In general, the PCD method as applied at Nortel can serve as an illustration for managers as they diagnose, identify, and implement appropriate interventions for ill-structured problems or processes like the fuzzy front-end of NPD.

There are two important avenues for future research. First, future research is needed to examine whether the PCD method can be applied to (re)design other elements of the NPD process, or possibly even to design products. Second, there is a need for future research that tests the generalizability of PCD method via more case studies and applications. We acknowledge the limits of our case study research approach with regard to generalizability. However, we maintain that the nature of the PCD process requires holistic study of its application. This suggests a need for additional qualita tive case studies conducted in collaboration with companies experimenting with workplace innovations. It is only when a sufficient amount of systematic qualitative case study research has been conducted that the themes and relationships regarding PCD can be validated via quantitative research methods. Within our case study, we found some evidence of internal generalizability of the PCD method. Since the application of PCD to the fuzzy front-end, Nortel has reapplied PCD to define a new business opportunity development process for the New Business Ventures group. PCD is a useful method for structuring the New Business Ventures group core activities that include evaluating technologies and potential business investment opportunities.

## Conclusion

ORGANIZATIONS ARE INCREASINGLY SEEKING WAYS to leverage and manage knowledge-based resources. To accomplish this goal, they must first isolate a business need and find out where the ability to capture and reuse knowledge will prove most valuable. The reality is that organizational outcomes are achieved through processes (such as new product development, production, sales), and in the end, an organization is only as good as its internal processes. Fundamentally, process work is essentially about problem solving, which involves tasks performed and managed by individuals or teams. In fact, the process associated with the generation of new knowledge is often more valuable than the knowledge it produces. For example, as evidenced in our case study, capturing why an NPD decision was made and providing insight into the circumstances surrounding the decision-making process is the context that makes knowledge so valuable. Thus, finding and lending structure to knowledge-intensive processes is essential to capturing and reusing the very knowledge that creates value.

Finding and lending structure in order to exploit knowledge and improve perfor mance will happen only if the whole system is understood and managed. Although IT may ultimately play a key role, the reality is that effectively structuring knowledge intensive processes first requires thinking through what knowledge workers know, why they know it, and what they need to know relative to strategic issues. A tripartite focus on people, process, and technology will enable the planning and implementation of solutions that meet an organization’s business goals. We believe that our PCD methodology provides a systematic way to understand and structure knowledge-intensive, ill-structured processes and to bring about the changes necessary to enhance performance at all levels, that is, organizational, business process, and knowledge worker.
