ABSTRACT: This study investigates the job satisfaction of information technology (IT) professionals in an environment where computer aided software engineering (CASE) tools are used. Although the recent downturn in the economy might have temporarily eased the IT labor shortage, issues of recruitment and retention of qualified personnel are key to the success of IS development projects. This study presents a model of the combination of CASE tool usage and job satisfaction as related to internal career orientation. Two hypotheses based on this model were tested using empirical evidence collected through a survey method. The first examines whether the career orientation of IS personnel influences their job satisfaction. The second incorporates the impact of CASE tool usage on this relationship. The results indicate that in a CASE tool environment, personnel with a predominant technical career orientation have more job satisfaction than those with a predominant managerial orientation. However, there is a significant and positive synergy between the sophistication of the CASE tool used and managerial competence orientation leading to higher job satisfaction These findings indicate that combating the IT personnel shortage through task automation may also increase worker satisfaction, thereby decreasing turnover. Careful selection of the CASE tool for use may result in this win–win situation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: career orientation, CASE, information systems development, information technology workers, job satisfaction.

IT IS WELL KNOWN THAT A SEVERE SHORTAGE OF information technology (IT) professionals exists and is likely to continue in the future [63]. A U.S. Commerce Department report issued in June 1999 indicates that an additional 1.3 million computer programmers, computer scientists, and engineers will be needed by 2006. Recent events have disrupted these expectations of employment and added to the general uncertainty in the economic environment. The dotcom crash led to 74,199 employees losing their positions in Internet-related firms from January to June of 2001 [2]. However, the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, have greatly added to the general demand for those who specialize in technology [39]. As society becomes more reliant on information provided by technology, it is clear that employees possessing such skills will remain valuable in the future. Even in uncertain job markets, the importance of retaining valuable employees remains high [13].

Two approaches are currently taken to combat the shortage of productive IT employees: recruit and retain qualified personnel, and restructure tasks so they require fewer personnel. Attempts to recruit and retain valuable IT workers have included increasing pay [62] and offering perks and nonsalary compensation such as company outings, lunches, out-of-town conferences, and bonuses for performance [38]. Whereas companies expect that these perks increase job satisfaction and reduce employee turnover, the effectiveness of these measures is in question [19]. The second approach of restructuring tasks in an IT environment often employs automation tools such as CASE (computer aided software engineering). CASE tools reportedly aid developers in designing and delivering application software systems in a timely manner, by automating many of the tasks associated with the phases of the traditional systems development life cycle (SDLC). Most IT tasks are included in the SDLC phases of requirements definition, analysis, design, code, test, implement, and support and maintenance. Since CASE tools alter the daily work environment of IT personnel, they may also impact job satisfaction and consequent turnover. In summary, the acute current and projected shortage of IT professionals in an increasingly information-intensive environment creates the impetus for us to better understand antecedents to job satisfaction and consequently reduce turnover.

Previous studies have shown that turnover and absenteeism among IT professionals is reduced when job satisfaction is high [9, 10, 32]. Factors shown to influence job satisfaction include the environment provided by the company such as supervisor support [63] and advancement opportunities [22], and on values that an individual perceives as important to their career [40]. These values are called internal careers [40], career anchors [89], or career orientation [29], and refer to a core set of values, beliefs, and intentions with regard to one’s direction in their professional calling. It is theorized that these career orientations affect occupational choices, employment transitions, promotional opportunities, career changes, and other events pertaining to the work environment [15].

Task restructuring as an attempt to improve software development and maintenance productivity incorporates automation tools, such as CASE. CASE tools have evolved over the past few decades [6] and are now fairly commonplace in new systems development. A Software Engineering Institute survey of companies considered as “high maturity” found that 25 of the 35 firms either used CASE tools as standard practice (N = 13) or as a common practice (N = 12), with Rational Rose (N = 11) mentioned most often [78]. One online list of CASE tools and vendors contains 270 active vendors, of which 83 are headquartered outside of the United States [68]. AComputerWorld survey of hiring IT managers’ lists Oracle Developer 2000 and Visual Studio as skills in demand despite the slow economy [83]. The incorporation of CASE tools into organizations that develop and support software seems inevitable. However, this transition is not without difficulties to individuals and organizations. Successful adoption of automation tools may require a shift in design and development methodology to the one presented by the specific tool [69, 104]. It is unknown how this changing development environment affects individual job satisfaction and thus turnover. Some studies indicate that there is more staff turnover in information systems (IS) departments with automation such as CASE than in those that have not yet adopted these tools [50, 80]. Other studies show that acceptance of an automated environment by system developers depends not just on the tools, but also on individual, organizational, and technological attributes [45, 77]. We specifically explore the environment of systems development and support using CASE tools to address the following two research questions:

1. Are there differences in job satisfaction among IT personnel depending on their internal career orientation?

2. How does the nature of automation tools impact the relationship between career orientation and job satisfaction?

## Dependent Variable—Work Outcomes

Work outcomes are feelings or perceptions that personnel possess related to their work in general, particularly as reflected in their job and career. The outcomes previously studied for IT personnel are career satisfaction [63] and job satisfaction [25, 72, 85]. Job satisfaction refers to the primary affective reactions of individuals to various facets of the job and job experiences [44], for which Hackman and Oldham [47, 48, 49] proposed a conceptual framework called the Job Characteristics Model (JCM). It has been theorized that individuals possessing higher degrees of job satisfaction will have better performance, less turnover, and more organizational commitment than persons with less job satisfaction [37]. Empirical support for the satisfaction–performance relationship can be found in Petty et al. [79], Hummel [54], and Srivastva et al. [97]. Srivastva and his colleagues [97] identified 31 studies, covering a diversity of populations, where a positive relationship existed between job satisfaction and performance. Although there is some disagreement regarding the strength of this relationship, in general, it has been shown to be positive and significant.

Demonstrable evidence also exists supporting an inverse relationship between job satisfaction and turnover [9, 10, 24, 32, 52, 71, 81]. Since turnover is undesirable from the point of view of the organization, studying job satisfaction could be one means of identifying situations where turnover is probable. Corrective measures could be identified and action taken before imminent attrition occurs. This study focuses on job satisfaction of IT personnel in a CASE tool environment. It is expected that the positive relationship between job satisfaction, performance, and turnover exists within this environment as well.

## Impact of Career Orientation

The proposed model indicates that the career orientation of an individual affects the outcomes of their work including performance and satisfaction. Theories of career development indicate that there are two primary types of career foci—internal and external career pathing. External career paths are the opportunities made available by a corporation [42], whereas internal careers focus on the individual’s self-concept and career values. An important aspect of the internal career concept is the career anchor [89, 90] or career orientation [29]. What is now referred to as Schein’s Career Anchor Model, grew out of longitudinal research conducted from 1958 to 1964 and later refined at MIT’s Sloan School of Management. Schein’s original model contained five career anchors: managerial competence, technical competence, security/stability, creativity/entrepreneurship, and autonomy/independence. DeLong [30] subsequently validated the model empirically and identified three additional anchors: identity, service, and variety. Some of these career anchor needs can be met through efforts of the organization. For instance, one strategy for retaining employees is to provide a “mass customization” environment, where organizational policies and culture are tailored to individual needs [13]. This approach seems to address the needs of those with dominant anchors related to the work environment, such as identity, service, variety, and security. However, a developmental environment is generally technical in nature, consisting of technical workers and their managers. These technical managers typically begin their careers as technical workers and have initial interest in a technical field [70]. While recognizing the relevance of other career anchors, we argue that the anchors of managerial and technical competence are particularly important for this study, since they address the nature of the work itself. Interestingly, studies of primarily U.S.-based IS personnel have found these two orientations to be prominent. For instance, a survey of 464 members of the Association for Computing Machinery [57] determined that technical and managerial competence orientations dominated in that group. In a non-U.S. study of IS employees in South Africa, these orientations were not as dominant [59]. Yet another survey of non-IS professionals involved in engineering and R&D reported lower levels of managerial and technical competence [58].

Whereas it is clear that many aspects of a work environment are of concern to technology workers, U.S.-based firms have long recognized the presence of both technical and managerial orientations. When assessing promotion to management, McClelland and Boyatzis [70] found that leadership motive generally predicted promotion to nontechnical management positions and verbal fluency predicted promotion to technical management positions. Efforts to accommodate employees who wish to maintain a technical orientation include a dual-career ladder option. A dual-career ladder provides promotional possibilities in a technical or managerial track [40], satisfying those with technical as well as managerial competence anchors.

We argue that the nature of systems development will remain technical, with value placed on technical competence. A certain level of technical ability and interest must exist to secure even an entry-level position in many technology-based firms. However, innate in the systems development teams are people with varying degrees of technical and managerial competence as their career anchors. These orientations and their relationship to job satisfaction are important to evaluate in the context of an automated CASE environment, where the very nature of work is changing.

## Hypothesis 1: Impact of Career Orientation on Job Satisfaction

Our study examines the relationship between those career orientations that concern the nature of the work and job satisfaction for IT personnel in a CASE tool environment. As the model in Figure 1 shows, the career orientation of a worker affects work outcomes such as job satisfaction and consequent turnover. We argue that in a systems development and maintenance environment, where the work is technical in nature, those with an orientation of technical competence will have higher job satisfaction than those with a managerial competence anchor [57, 70]. Since systems development is considered technical work, comparing technical and managerial orientations of IT workers also begins to address the issue of fit between the person and the tasks to which they are assigned. We propose the following hypotheses:

H1: Job satisfaction will be higher for those with a predominant technical as compared to a managerial career orientation in a CASE tool environment.

H1a: Technical competence orientation is positively correlated with job satisfaction in a CASE tool environment.

H1b: Managerial competence orientation is negatively correlated with job satisfaction in a CASE tool environment.

## Task Automation for IT Professionals

Besides its overall potential to alleviate the applications systems development bottleneck, CASE tools possess many features and functionalities that contribute to improving the performance of systems analysts and designers. Popular features include representing a design in terms of process or flow models, detecting inconsistencies in models, generating screen mock-ups, and allowing concurrent use by several users. With the prevalence of CASE tool use and the variety of features available, we will investigate specifics of CASE environments and the impact that environment has on job satisfaction of IT workers.

Investigation of the extent of the capabilities of the CASE tools used will be conducted based on the Functional CASE Technology Model (FCTM). The FCTM is an empirically derived framework used for classifying CASE vendor products according to their inherent functionalities. It was developed as a result of research conducted at the MIT Sloan School of Management by Henderson and Cooprider [51]. The model was evaluated empirically through both a Q-sort study of IS planners and designers (familiar with CASE technology) and the use of the dimensions to characterize the strengths and weaknesses of commercially available CASE products. The model is composed of two primary dimensions of CASE technology, coupled with their respective functionalities. These two dimensions are referred to as production technology and coordination technology. Production technology affects the planning or design decisions and results in various diagrams such as context diagrams, data flow diagrams, and entity-relationship diagrams [51]. Coordination technology enables or supports the interactions of multiple agents in the execution of a planning or design task [51] and results in integrated data dictionaries and model repositories. Whereas production technology represents the classical labor/capital substitution concept, coordination technology pertains to the accrual of benefits resulting from independent agents collaborating toward a common goal and the resultant synergy.

## De-Skilling and Upgrading

Since CASE tools automate portions of, or all of the phases of, the SDLC, the theoretical underpinnings regarding the use of this technology can be framed in the competing theories of de-skilling [14] and upgrading [4] the quality of work life through automation. On one hand, Braverman [14] notes that those who hold the de-skilling point of view suggest that automation through technology is used to strip relatively skilled jobs of their conceptual content, leaving behind trivial, boring, dehumanizing, unskilled work. The central thesis of Braverman’s argument is that there is a pervasive tendency in contemporary capitalism to reorganize jobs at lower skill levels than previously. Such an arrangement of capitalism and automation will lead to a de-skilling process. Supporters of this theory also view the effects of automation as analogous to the implementation of the “assembly line” process in the automobile industry. These proponents see technology as robotizing employees, decreasing the amount of skill and the number of employees necessary to perform the job [73].

On the other hand, scholars argue that technology upgrades rather than de-skills [1, 4, 86]. Attewell [4] notes that automation is most successful in making machines that mimic our repetitious physical movements and, hence, automation most easily replaces already de-skilled, repetitious labor. In this way, through technology, there is more opportunity to concentrate on higher-level, intellectual, or conceptual tasks, thus resulting in a net upgrading of jobs. Adler [1] suggests additional positive effects of automation for employees: a heightened sense of responsibility; a certain abstractness of tasks, resulting in intellectual mastery as a key performance factor; and the synergy created by the interdependence of tasks. In sum, proponents of this view argue that automation leads to a net upgrading of skill level.

Studies searching for evidence of either phenomenon have shown mixed results. In general, when a workplace is computerized, evidence points to an aggregate upgrading effect, with some de-skilling and skill bifurcation, and overall skill disruption [1, 4, 7, 17]. Manufacturing is one area in which automation has significantly altered the work environment. Within manufacturing, several areas have been studied for de-skilling and upgrading effects, including Advanced Manufacturing Technologies (AMT), Computer-Integrated Manufacturing (CIM), Computer-Aided Design (CAD), and Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM). AMT involves incorporating technology for automation in a variety of ways, and has shown increases in productivity and a reduction in the probability of shop floor failure [20, 33]. In a study of various AMT users, findings indicate that skill upgrading of the workforce resulted [94]. When CIM environments were examined for de-skilling/upgrading, conclusions included that the operator’s functions became more routine, simplified, and contained less variety, whereas the role of the supervisor increased in task complexity and range [3]. It is difficult to conclude that de-skilling of the operators functions resulted, since their tasks were manual in nature prior to the automation. However, the changes in supervisor tasks could be considered an upgrade to their skill requirements [3]. A study of CAD and non-CAD workers showed that those spending the majority of their work time using the CAD system reported lower work complexity, autonomy of work methods, and less job satisfaction than those using the system less or not using it at all [53].

Just as we have not experienced the totally automated shop floor, systems development cannot occur without human expertise. Whereas prior research suggests that there might be an upgrading effect for supervisory positions through automation, and a de-skilling effect for “detail”-oriented workers, the results are not clear as to their implications regarding CASE technology. Further, how a company implements CASE technology and their surrounding human resources policies may affect the perceived de-skilling or upgrading of the work [45, 77].

## Hypothesis 2: Impact of CASE Technology on Job Satisfaction

The model presented in Figure 1 indicates that CASE technology moderates the relationship between career orientation and work outcomes. Since the work of Igbaria and colleagues [57] was conducted without regard to a CASE environment, we look to Orlikowski’s interpretive case studies [77] for direction on CASE incorporation. Investigation of the moderating effect of CASE technology will provide empirical evidence to support (or refute) Orlikowski’s [77] interpretive contention that technically oriented system development personnel feel threatened by CASE automation. The workers in her first company indicated that the CASE tool removed them from the underlying system and reduced their activities to filling out forms. They complained of a sense of lost opportunity in keeping abreast of new technology. This argument is consistent with the thesis of job de-skilling [14]. We theorize that this perceived threat (or absence thereof) could be manifested in perceptions of job satisfaction. Alternatively, the managerially aspiring employees in Orlikowski’s [77] first company welcomed the introduction of CASE. These workers reported that the CASE tools reduced the tedium and complexity associated with the details of large application systems. This is consistent with the thesis of upgrading [4]. Consequently, it is theorized that the degree of automation reflected in CASE technology (referred to as its “sophistication”) will have a moderating impact on the relationship between career orientation and job satisfaction. This impact will be different for each orientation, in the direction of the de-skilling and upgrading thesis.

Collectively, these relationships can be investigated through the following hypotheses:

H2a: There is a negative moderating effect of CASE technology sophistication on the association between technical competence career orientation and job satisfaction.

H2b: There is a positive moderating effect of CASE technology sophistication on the association between managerial competence career orientation and job satisfaction.

## Research Methodology

sampling involves the use of nonprobability or nonscientific sampling, certain situations dictate that this kind of sampling can be as accurate (and less costly) than more traditional kinds of sampling [91]. Judgment samples are often called purposive samples; the sample elements are handpicked since it is expected that they can serve the research purpose and it is believed that they are representative of the population of interest [23]. It was not a goal of the research to evaluate specific products; rather, an overall representation of CASE environments was solicited. Another name given to this form of purposive, or judgmental sampling, is expert choice, where experts pick “typical” or “representative” specimens, units, or portions [66]. In this study, the researchers served as “experts” and selected companies that were known users of CASE to participate. This procedure was especially necessary in light of the fact that we were attempting to locate a special group of users—that is, CASE users. Under a snowball design, after being interviewed, each respondent is asked to identify others who belong to the target population [31]. In the present study, users of CASE were asked to name other known users of the technology. Following, these potential respondents were contacted and invited to participate. Thus, the sample “snowballed” by having CASE users identify others for inclusion into the study. Post and Kagan [82] used this procedure to locate users of one particular CASE tool, Rational Rose, in their study. This technique is also known as “chain referral sampling” in the social sciences literature [11] and can be especially useful when attempting to locate rare (or in our case, exclusive) populations [98]. The use of such “locators” in snowball sampling is akin to the use of significant informants in field studies [11]. Correspondingly, it is applicable to this study because we specifically seek to locate this special population of IS professionals—that is, CASE users. Therefore, it is believed that the sample obtained is representative of the population of interest: users of CASE technology.

Data were collected through the use of a survey questionnaire, administered on-site by the contact representative of the participating company. This method of data collection is referred to as a “group administration technique.” It was chosen for several reasons, chief among them being that collecting data in this manner offers more control to the researcher over the distribution and subsequent collection of the survey instruments. Other advantages of this approach include: generally high cooperation rates; the chance to explain the study and answer questions about the questionnaire; and generally low cost, in contrast with mail surveys [36]. In addition, due to the specialized nature of the targeted sample respondents, the group administration technique was employed to obtain the appropriate personnel needed to represent the sample frame.

The 93-question survey was administered on-site to employees whose supervisors made them available to the project directors for research purposes. In this way, it was expected that the researchers would be able to exert more control over the sample composition and response rate, as opposed to the use of more conventional data collection methods, such as mail surveys and telephone interviews. The instrument was pretested among a group of selected professionals having considerable experience in the IS field.

## Research Variables and Measures

## Job Satisfaction

The dependent variable employed in the research model (shown as Figure 1) is job satisfaction. Job satisfaction has been studied in prior research and has demonstrated strong relationships with turnover intentions, absenteeism, and organizational commitment [8, 56] as well as an intuitively appealing link to performance. Although there has been considerable debate regarding the use of job satisfaction as a global measure, as used in this study, as opposed to summated facet scales [61, 87], these investigations concluded that global measures of job satisfaction may be more truly indicative of the degree of satisfaction obtained than when using composite scales. Ironson et al. [61] noted several reasons that the composite scales (such as, satisfaction with pay, promotions, coworkers, supervision, the work itself) may not be sufficient for estimating general satisfaction. Furthermore, the results of Scarpello and Campbell [87] indicate that defining overall job satisfaction as the sum of the evaluations of the discrete elements of which the job is composed, may lead to the neglect of major determinants of job satisfaction. They argue against the common practice of using the sum of facet satisfaction as the measure of overall job satisfaction. We measured job satisfaction with three scales corresponding to the global job satisfaction component of Hackman and Oldham’s [44] JCM. Responses are elicited through the use of five-point Likert scales, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Table 1 shows the items used for the measurement of job satisfaction. The average of the responses for each subject was calculated for an overall satisfaction rating.

## Career Orientation

Career orientation is the independent variable in this study. We focused on measures of managerial and technical competence scores using a short-form Career Orientations Inventory instrument developed by Igbaria and Baroudi [55]. Table 1 depicts the items used for determining these career orientations. The first three items in each of the managerial and technical competence measures use a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “of no importance” to “centrally important.” The last two items use a five-point Likert scale, with responses anchored from “not at all true” to “completely true.”

## CASE Technology Sophistication

Since CASE tools are software programs themselves, the ranges of activities they support differ by product. CASE tools can be generally categorized as upper-CASE, lower-CASE, or integrated-CASE, depending on which phases of the SDLC they support. Upper-CASE tools support analysis and design activities, whereas lower-CASE support the coding, testing, and maintenance phases. Integrated CASE (or ICASE) is a set of tools sharing a common user interface, data interface, life cycle framework, and repository [34]. In this context, an ICASE environment spans the spectrum of development activities. Moreover, some CASE tools support infrastructure-type activities such as project management, documentation, and configuration management.

## Data Analysis and Results

## Sample Characteristics

THIRTY-TWO COMPANIES PARTICIPATED in the research study, mainly from the insurance, manufacturing, and consulting areas (see Table 3 for a summary of responding industry types). These firms varied widely in terms of size, scope, and usage of CASE technology. Although companies provide the CASE environment, the unit of analysis in this study is the individual not the organization. The 226 respondents were generally well educated and experienced in the IS field, as shown in their demographics (Table 4). Analysis indicates that none of the demographic variables (education, age, years in IT) had a significant correlation with satisfaction (a< 0.05). Earlier empirical studies into CASE use contained respondents with similar demographic profiles and IT backgrounds, including Nelson [73], Finlay and Mitchell [35], and Iivari [60]. Based on these observations, these professionals appear representative of those found in IS organizations and lend credibility to the sample.

## Validity and Reliability of Variables

Prior to the formal testing of hypotheses, constructs were assessed for their reliability and validity. The value of the measure of internal consistency [24] for the job satisfaction variable is 0.74. This is greater than the 0.7 desired minimum proposed by Nunnally and Bernstein [75] and used by many researchers as the primary heuristic when evaluating reliability with alpha [21, 27, 55]. Moreover, it should be noted that Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this construct was 0.77 in the Igbaria et al. [57] study. Reliability for the two-career orientations is in the acceptable range with values of 0.87 for managerial competence orientation items, and 0.78 for technical competence orientation.

Construct validity for the career-orientation measure is determined using inter-item correlations between the composite scales used to measure both orientations. These values indicate that the managerial competence items are highly correlated among themselves although having low or negative correlations with items measuring technical competence. Further, convergent and discriminant validity was established through the use of factor analysis [65]. Results from factor analysis using varimax rotation and eigenvalues >1 results in loadings on two distinct factors, with items from each orientation loading consistently indicating good convergent and discriminant validity (see Table 5).

One measure used for career orientation is a binary measure, based on the “dominant” orientation. Each individual was assigned a career orientation of either managerial or technical competence, depending on which set of questions scored highest on average by the subject. This is the same method employed by Igbaria et al. [57]. Based on this classification scheme, 81 individuals were categorized as having managerial competence orientation and 122 were categorized as having technical competence orientation. These results are not surprising since CASE tools are normally found in fairly sophisticated IS environments and individuals with a technical inclination are needed to support the implementation and operation of such aids. The categorization of subjects according to their dominant career orientation was used to test all of the hypotheses. The career orientations of technical and managerial competence were based on the nature of daily tasks. Whereas other orientations may actually dominate for an individual, the other anchors are associated with lifestyle more than professional tasks.

Correlation and t-test analysis indicates that career orientation did not significantly influence the number of features indicated in the CASE tool (a> 0.05). Those with a managerial competence orientation indicated that the CASE tool in use contained a larger number of features than those with a technical competence orientation, however, the difference is not statistically significant. These results hold even when the features are separated into production technology and coordination technology. This verifies that knowledge of the CASE tool in use in an organization did not significantly depend on the career orientation of the respondent, thereby eliminating the possibility of any confounding mediating effects.

The results of the statistical analysis of the data for the hypotheses are shown in Table 6. Results of t-test analysis supported our original hypothesis (H1) and showed that individuals categorized as having technical competence as their dominant career orientation were, in fact, more satisfied with their jobs than those with predominantly managerial competence $( t = 2 . 5 1 , p = 0 . 0 1 )$ . In addition, job satisfaction correlated positively with technical orientation $( r = 0 . 1 4 , p = 0 . 0 5 )$ , whereas it correlated negatively with managerial orientation $( r = - 0 . 1 6 , p = 0 . 0 2 )$ , in support of H1a and H1b.

H2 investigates the interaction between the selected career orientations and CASE technology sophistication (as measured by the variable SOPH) and its resulting impact on job satisfaction. Table 7 shows the results from the analysis, which indicates that the impact is different depending on career orientation. The relationship between technical orientation and job satisfaction is not affected by an increase in CASE tool sophistication (interaction term is not significant), rejecting H2a. However, the CASE sophistication (SOPH) score positively and significantly moderates the relationship between managerial career orientation and job satisfaction $( p = 0 . 0 1 )$ ). There is more variance (260 percent) explained by the interaction effect indicating a significant change in the managerial orientation/job satisfaction relationship as the number of automated CASE features increases. This is consistent with the findings of Orlikowski [77] and provides support for H2b.

Thus, although the results from H1 and H1b suggest that a technical competence orientation was associated with more job satisfaction in an automated development environment, the results from H2a seem to indicate that these professionals do not derive satisfaction from a higher level of automation with CASE. These individuals were indifferent when considering the impact of the SOPH of CASE tools.

## Discussion

THIS PAPER REPORTED A STUDY OF THE relationship between internal career orientations and work outcomes, as measured by job satisfaction, of IT workers in an automated development and maintenance environment. It also considered the effect of the tool sophistication on the job satisfaction of these workers. The 226 professionals responding to the survey were categorized into managerial competence and technical competence as their dominant career orientation, and their answers to job satisfaction questions were analyzed. The results suggest that job satisfaction of technically oriented specialists is higher when compared to workers with a managerial orientation. Because CASE tools add a layer of technology to an already technical environment, it is not surprising that persons who strive for technical competence find a CASE environment satisfying. CASE tools can be very complex mechanisms. Using them requires an understanding of a myriad of relationships among automated data flow diagrams, data repositories (data dictionaries and project dictionaries), code generators, reverse-engineering tools, and the like. Senn and Wynekoop [92] note that using CASE tools in development initially adds complexity to the development process and its management and, furthermore, that implementing the CASE tool means learning the breadth and depth of the tool’s new features, interfaces, and notations as well as associated rules and procedures. Such relationships may be best understood by those with such a technical inclination and, hence, lend credibility to our findings.

The finding that managerial competence orientation was negatively associated with job satisfaction (H1b) seems to reaffirm the interpretation described above. As indicated by the scales, the managerial competence orientation measures the extent that these IS personnel desire positions of leadership and authority in their jobs, as well as the prestige associated with such occupational roles. Individuals with high managerial competence orientation would obtain satisfaction by delegating authority and responsibility for tasks, and the coordination of effort that results from such assignments. Thus, managerially oriented persons may view the learning process and subsequent use of CASE as technical, impersonal, and a hindrance to their management aspirations.

Although H1a showed a significant, negative correlation between managerial competence orientation and job satisfaction, H2 provides more granular insight into the relationship. The results of regression analysis indicate that personnel with higher technical orientation are indifferent, in terms of their job satisfaction, toward more CASE features, whereas those with higher managerial competence orientation derive more satisfaction using a more sophisticated tool. We may attribute this to the nature of CASE tool development. Basic CASE packages mainly support the technical aspects of system development, such as data flow diagramming and code generators. More advanced CASE tools include additional support for automation of production tasks (that is, test for consistency between a process and a data model) as well as project coordination and management activities [16, 45]. It seems possible that tools scoring high on sophistication include project management support in addition to basic technical support of development. Technically oriented persons viewed that inclusion with indifference, whereas managerially oriented persons viewed it favorably. Without project management support, managerially oriented individuals might have a sense of frustration over a complex, technical tool that they must accommodate, but has limited benefits with respect to their orientation. ## Implications

## Implications for Research

THIS STUDY ADDRESSES THE POTENTIAL CAUSES and possible remedies of the IS personnel shortage that we are experiencing and that is expected to continue by combining the notions of creating a satisfying work environment and reducing the need for personnel through job automation. Previous studies have shown that high job satisfaction results in increased retention among IT professionals [9, 10, 32]. Therefore, one method of retaining valued employees is by facilitating high job satisfaction. Job satisfaction can be facilitated through external referents such as market valuation, or through internal referents such as a better work environment or a satisfactory job structure. Therefore, the research question of how to increase retention has a rich repertoire of variables that can be examined. We focus on examining a personal characteristic (internal career orientation) with job satisfaction.

One method of reducing the need for personnel is automating aspects of the software development and maintenance cycles by incorporating CASE technology. Although this study examines job satisfaction in a CASE tool environment, it sheds limited light on the question of whether CASE use leads to better systems. The results indicate that a technical competence career orientation leads to satisfaction in the software development and maintenance environment and an increase in CASE sophistication does not enhance this. CASE sophistication does, however, have a positive impact on those individuals who consider themselves to have a managerial competence orientation. These findings may be interpreted through prior research on deskilling and upgrading of the workforce as task automation occurs.

## Positioning of Research Findings in Terms of Automation Studies

We would argue that this research has found support for both sides of the historical debate between the competing schools of thought pertaining to the “de-skilling” and “upgrading” effects of technological automation. Attewell and Rule [5] also found support for each side of the controversy in their treatise on this subject. Respondents in our study that seemed to be empowered through the use of CASE appear to support the upgrading thesis. Other participants, such as those who consider themselves to have a more technical career orientation, might suggest a de-skilling perspective.<sup>1</sup> Thus, the following sections outline our findings in terms of each school of thought.

## The De-Skilling Hypothesis

Evidence of support for the de-skilling perspective can be traced as far back as the impact of the printing press and the resulting decline in craft on workers in the printing industry [101]. The data from their study suggest that skill levels in that industry have indeed declined and, moreover, that these declines are largely due to the shift to more capital-intensive printing techniques (that is, automation). We do not find significant support for H2a, that technically oriented personnel, when presented with a more sophisticated CASE tool, have less job satisfaction. However, the direction of support is in the expected direction, suggesting that IS professionals in this study might perceive the advent of CASE in similar fashion to the printers of yesteryear.

Some IS personnel have spent many years sharpening their programming skills, and the imposition of CASE into their environment could be viewed as threatening to their livelihood. Couger et al. [26] identified some reasons why some personnel are reluctant to use CASE tools. Among these explanations was the perceived loss of elegance in developing unique coding logic or algorithms because the CASE tool translates everything into common logic. Another reason offered was housed in a “lack of tradition” perception. In the past, colleagues could go to ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) meetings and spend hours with peers discussing coding approaches and debugging episodes; with CASE tools, personnel feel more like draftsman than engineers. Kwok and Arnett [67] note that some programmers are simply not interested in changing the way they develop software: they are trapped by the common human shortcoming of learning one and only one way to perform a task. Edward Yourdon [105], a noted authority on the application of structured development techniques, found that some personnel worry about the impact of the tool on their status, prestige, and creative freedom more than anything else.

Furthermore, as stated earlier, technical specialists from Orlikowski’s [77] first case study cited similar misgivings regarding CASE. In an earlier study of the social implications of CASE tools for systems developers, Orlikowski [76] reported that frustration, and resistance to its introduction, led to misuse and even sabotage of the CASE tool. Thus, our interpretation of the results from H2a seems congruent with the de-skilling school of thought. Even more sophisticated CASE tools, which purportedly entail greater investment, do not result in reinforcing the relationship between this orientation and job satisfaction.

## The Upgrading Hypothesis

The central theme of the upgrading philosophy is diametrically opposed to the deskilling school of thought. The premise of the upgrading position is that by automating routine, tedious, methodical activities, personnel have more time for intellectually challenging tasks. In this way, technology upgrades their work by removing those parts of the job requiring a minimum level of skill, thus allowing employees the opportunity to maximize their creativity and analytical skills on a variety of other issues. Such a perception seems to be shared by that portion of our respondents who appear to be empowered through the use of CASE. Specifically, our interpretation of the results from H2b supported such a stance, where the sophistication of CASE technology positively moderated the relationship between managerial orientation and job satisfaction. As previously noted, the managerially oriented personnel in Orlikowski’s [77] first case study also lauded the benefits of the technology. These workers viewed their knowledge of CASE as contributing to their skill set, as well as making them more marketable and attractive to potential employers.

In an earlier study [5], evidence supporting both sides of the de-skilling–upgrading dichotomy was reported. A plausible explanation offered by these researchers is not that either group of observers is wrong, but that both processes (de-skilling and upgrading) are occurring within occupations as automation affects information processing jobs. Thus, findings in this study that support either (or both) of these positions is neither problematic nor unexpected.

As previously noted, the theories of work adjustment, job characteristics, and career development provided much of the impetus for this research. Importantly, the relationship between job satisfaction and other internal states such as organizational commitment [95] and turnover propensity [46] is well documented. Intuitively, it would seem that personnel with more job satisfaction would likely perform better than persons lacking such contentment. What is sadly lacking is the identification of interventions that would generate such contentment. For instance, blanket assumptions regarding investment in better CASE tools might have a detrimental impact on job satisfaction, and might require a more carefully thought out automation strategy.

## Future Research

The research model provides a theoretical framework that suggests several different areas of study. First, it identifies what we believe to be the most salient personal trait that has an empirical relationship with job satisfaction. Other personal traits such as security, stability, creativity, entrepreneurship, and independence might be useful to examine in a developmental context, as they manifest in a dominant career orientation. This study only examined orientations of technical and managerial competence, as they seem important given the nature of the work. However, other orientations may dominate in individuals and determining the proper fit between dominant career orientation and job tasks may reveal other ways of increasing job satisfaction. Second, this study suggests that all automation may not be positive, and the tools of automation should be evaluated within the context of the traits prevalent among IT professionals in the developmental environment. The de-skilling and upgrading hypotheses can be used to explain this effect, and provide a rich school of thought for future studies.

This study has laid the foundation for future research into both attitudes and attributes of IS personnel engaged in the use of automation tools in system development environments. Whereas this study took a snapshot approach to measuring job satisfaction, longitudinal studies are necessary to track the career progression of IS personnel and their subsequent job satisfaction. Perhaps such an analysis could be conducted within the confines of the career stage model [28], which describes how each of four successive career stages—apprentice, colleague, mentor, and sponsor— involves different tasks, different types of relationships, and different psychological adjustments. The inclusion of a productivity aid, like an automation tool, could have different effects on satisfaction during each phase.

Another area of potential research might include the level of psychological stress and strain associated with individuals in IS occupations. Such internal feelings could be investigated in terms of matching career orientation to professional responsibilities and to automated tool use. Caplan et al. [18] presents a theoretical framework from which investigation into these relationships could proceed. Nelson and Teng [74] suggest that although CASE tool use does not reduce stress, an increase in methodology usage did seem to reduce stress among systems analysts. Their study attempted to separate out the effects of methodology and CASE usage, although many CASE tools contain methodological restrictions. Further investigations into the causes of job-related stress and possible tool-related stress relief should focus on whether a tool incorporating a defined methodology is preferred for the particular work environment.

## Implications for Practice

The use of CASE tools in systems development will continue, as their benefits are intuitively if not practically compelling. The implications of these findings are that once the decision is made to incorporate CASE tools in a development environment, the selected tool should support the details of software development as well as the supervision of the process. Technically oriented individuals already seem satisfied with the current functionality of CASE tools, in terms of meeting their development needs. Those with a more dominant technical competence orientation indicate that specializing in a skill is more desirable than having the responsibility for supervising others.

Also, those with a managerial inclination have aspirations of influence and control over the work of others. Since CASE tools can facilitate communication of the tasks, it is possible that managerially oriented individuals feel more able to exert influence and control by having access to the work of others. In order to satisfy those types, selection of a CASE tool should emphasize a common repository that is accessible to the group, even if read-only access is given. This will enable technical specialists to focus on their interests while allowing those with managerial inclinations to view the work of others. This facilitation is accomplished with the incorporation of a CASE tool with configuration control, or with the combination of CASE and a configuration control package. This approach is recommended by the Rational Software Corporation’s Web site where they indicate, “By using the modeling environment in conjunction with the development environment using the Unified Modeling Language (UML), Rational Rose and your favorite configuration control solution enable all team members to use one tool and one language to communicate effectively” [84]. Individuals operate in private work spaces that contain a personal view of the entire model, with the ability to modify their own piece of the model until it is ready to be shared. Whether your software team consists of 10 individuals or 500 individuals across 20 different groups, Rational Rose will enable you to develop individually, communicate collaboratively, and deliver better software. As evidenced by the findings of McClelland and Boyatzis [70], communication skill is a predictor of promotion to management positions. CASE tools that facilitate the communication required to coordinate a group, although minimally imposing on those with more technical interests, seem to produce a win–win situation for individuals with dominant technical or managerial career aspirations.

## Limitations

This study focuses on the job satisfaction of technology workers and the impact of CASE tool sophistication on job satisfaction. There is no control for factors within the company, the work environment, or individuals that may also influence job satisfaction. As Orlikowski’s [77] study seems to indicate, the overall environment presented by a company may impact the perceptions of technology workers on productivity tools. Other studies have shown relationships between organizational influences such as politics [103], procedural and distributive justice [88], and job satisfaction. In addition, factors specific to an individual’s work environment, such as job tasks [99], task responsibilities and performance appraisal satisfaction [12], the quality of leadership [41], and friendships at work [102], have been shown to impact job satisfaction. Characteristics of individuals, other than career orientation, such as attitude toward money [100] and the disposition of the individual [64] may also impact job satisfaction. As in all research, we must selectively measure aspects of a situation that are deemed important to the situation. The results of this study are then incorporated into a more generalized model of effects on job satisfaction. Currently, there is no known measure of job satisfaction that incorporates every significant aspect of a job and is able to elicit completely unambiguous perceptions of that job.

Moreover, the measure of sophistication of the CASE tool used was a basic count of the features. This measure does not provide details on the importance of the features and leads us to speculate on the specific impact. Therefore, whereas this study provides a general indication of CASE use, suggesting specific implications, such as which functions CASE tools should accommodate, requires additional research.

## Conclusion

THIS STUDY INVESTIGATES A COMBINATION of two methods of combating the shortage of IT workers: recruit and retain qualified personnel, and restructure tasks so they require fewer personnel. Task restructuring may take the form of automating tasks where possible, and may use a CASE tool. Previous research involving either job satisfaction of or CASE tool use by technology workers resulted in ambiguous findings. This study presents a model of the combination of CASE tool usage and job satisfaction as related to internal career orientation. Two sets of hypotheses based on this model were tested using empirical evidence collected through a survey method. The first examines whether the career orientation of IS personnel influences their job satisfaction. The second incorporates the impact of CASE tool usage on this relationship. The results indicate that in a CASE tool environment, technically oriented personnel have more job satisfaction than those with a managerial orientation. However, we found a significant and positive synergy between the sophistication of the CASE tool used and managerial competence orientation leading to higher job satisfaction. These findings indicate that combating the IT personnel shortage through task automation may also increase worker satisfaction, thereby decreasing turnover. Therefore, selection of a CASE tool that facilitates communication while allowing for technical specialists to contribute easily may result in this win–win situation.

In today’s business environment, automation tools appear to provide solutions to increasingly complex organizational issues. Furthermore, with increasing attempts to downsize, right-size, or reengineer company processes with less emphasis on human components and more on IT, practicing managers can ill afford to ignore the results of this study and others that incorporate the human component into the success of the organization.
