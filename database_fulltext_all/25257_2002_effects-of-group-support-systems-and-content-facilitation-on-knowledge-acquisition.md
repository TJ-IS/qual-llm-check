# Effects of Group Support Systems and Content Facilitation on Knowledge Acquisition

RON CHI-WAI KWOK, JIAN MA, AND DOUGLAS R. VOGEL

ABSTRACT: This paper investigates the effects of group support systems (GSS) and content facilitation on individual knowledge acquisition in general, and on changes in an individual’s knowledge structures in particular as indicated through concept mapping development. We present a model explaining the enabling effects of GSS and content facilitation on group processes (group participation, quality of feedback, domination, and communication barrier), cooperation in learning, and individual knowledge structures (knowledge complexity, integration, and commonality). An experiment that employed a 2X2 factorial design was used to explore the main and interaction effects of GSS and content facilitation on knowledge acquisition. Experimental subjects were randomly assigned to one of the four treatment groups; that is, nonfacilitation and non-GSS, nonfacilitation and GSS, facilitation and non-GSS, facilitation and GSS. Results of the experiment indicated that both GSS and content facilitation positively affect certain aspects of individual knowledge acquisition. Content facilitation particularly enhanced learners’knowledge commonality, whereas GSS enhanced the quality of feedback and cooperation in learning, and reduced domina tion and communication barrier. However, the results also indicated that GSS and content facilitation have crossover interaction effects on group participation and knowl edge commonality. The effects of combining GSS and content facilitation were not additive in this study. Explanations are presented.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: collaborative learning, facilitation, group support system.

RESEARCH ON KNOWLEDGE ACQUISITION has a long history and can be traced back as far as the early Greeks [31]. Knowledge acquisition concerns “learning, filtering new information, and storing it in memory” [75, p. 5]. It is a fundamental and essentia process of individual and organizational learning [31, 58]. There are various models about the way people learn, and a variety of learning approaches are also promoted, including constructivist, which promotes individual development of a mental model [42]. Pask [63], in his conversation theory of learning, suggests that understanding can be built up through conversation with other individuals about the subject. Advances in computer and communication technologies have created new opportunities for use in supporting knowledge acquisition [65]. For example, a group support system (GSS) enables gathering and sharing of knowledge among learners, however, we know little of an individual’s change in knowledge while being part of a GSS-enhanced collaborative environment.

A GSS environment is composed of technology and facilitation components. The success of GSS is not a given, but depends on how group interaction is facilitated and the technology applied in a learning environment [50]. GSS encourages knowledge acquisition through software tools that can provide an effective collaborative learning environment where individuals can communicate, organize, and evaluate knowledge acquired and shared by the group. A facilitator is a person whose role is to maximize knowledge sharing among individuals by structuring group interaction and providing feedback during the meeting.

In today’s schools, the teacher’s role shifts from instructor to facilitator, emphasiz ing andragogy more than pedagogy [1, 68]. The role of facilitator is to guide how the learners interact with each other by orchestrating the process and structure of the learning interaction [1]. GSS research has begun to focus on application in academic organizations [3, 4, 47]. Although previous GSS research findings on learning are encouraging and suggest that GSS may be used to improve learning under certain situations, there are still many questions that need to be answered. How GSS technology and facilitation interact to affect an individual’s change in knowledge is one such question.

As research on applying GSS and collaborative learning in schools grows [3, 39, 47, 52], facilitation style in the presence of GSS takes on increased importance. Facilitation style covers a range of process and content focus that varies with circumstances [22]. Jacques [35] argues that it is difficult for traditional instructors to adopt a facilitation style that involves careful listening and eliciting, rather than just giving of his or her own knowledge. Small group researchers have long investigated whethe group interaction and performance can be enhanced through group facilitation [8, 53, 72]. Facilitation support style has been regarded as a critical dimension in self-directed learning [30] and group learning [68] in both synchronous [69] and distributed [1] environments.

This paper investigates the effects of GSS and content facilitation on individual knowledge acquisition in general, and changes of an individual’s knowledge structures in particular, under experimental conditions within a post-secondary schoo context. The theoretical background and its hypotheses are presented in the next section. Then we discuss the design of the study’s research methodology and its results, followed by the discussion of the experimental results, limitations, implications, and future research directions. The conclusions of this study are presented in the fina section.

## Theoretical Development and Hypotheses

COGNITIVE LEARNING THEORY ASSUMES that learners interact with new information, interpret it, and build personal knowledge representations by relating the new information to their prior knowledge [73]. Learners depend on the existing mental models to interpret new information. The existing mental models are thus necessary and important to the achievement of knowledge acquisition. Information itself is not knowl edge, but rather the stimuli [41]. Knowledge is acquired through the cognitive processing of information, and results in accommodation—a change to the learner’s mental model [78].

According to cognitive learning theory, knowledge acquisition can be composed of two components—personal and social [24]. Knowledge acquisition is personal in that it depends on the ability of the learner to assimilate and interpret new information. The prior knowledge or existing mental model of the learner determines how this new information will be interpreted [37]. It is the way in which prior knowledge is organized rather than merely the amount of knowledge that is important [12]. Knowledge acquisition also has a social component—the conversation theory of learning [63] suggests that understanding can be built up through conversation about the subject with other individuals. Both the learners who ask for help and those who provide help can benefit from peer feedback for testing and refining their mental models [66]. In social conversations, meaning is negotiated and shared. Tan [77] suggests that effective communication in conversation is critical to the development of individual understanding, and that effective communication also enhances shared understanding among individuals.

Leidner and Jarvenpaa [50] propose that the cognitive learning theory can be appropriately applied to guide the study of a GSS-supported learning environment. Based on the cognitive learning theory, Alavi [3] identifies an important attribute of an effective learning environment—cooperation in learning—which can enhance both the personal and social components of knowledge acquisition. The development of understanding requires discussions with others, and the negotiation of a shared meaning comes out as one of the main strategies used to ensure that understanding is thorough. This cooperation in developing understanding reflects Pask’s [63] ideas about the important function of conversations in cognitive activities. Bruner [11] sees this as an essential facet of knowledge to which psychology has given too little emphasis. Cooperation can develop understanding by providing feedback, social support, and reinforcement.

In this research we focus on how an individual’s knowledge changes while being part of a GSS-enhanced collaborative environment. As illustrated in Figure 1, we suggest that knowledge structure is affected by cooperation in learning, which is influenced by group processes. GSS and facilitation enhance group processes by increasing group process gains or reducing group process losses [62]. Some of the effects of GSS and facilitation on group process gains/losses improve the effective learning environment by contributing to cooperation in learning. The enhancement of the effectiveness of the learning environment may, in turn, lead to the acquisition of a refined knowledge structure by the learners. The links in our model of knowledge acquisition are not being tested in the current research.

## Effects of Group Support System

With GSS support, participants can type their questions and feedback simultaneously into a network of computer workstations. GSS immediately makes all these contributions available to be read on their individual screens. This means it is not necessary for participants to take turns contributing ideas (as one example of a communication barrier). Participants can also use GSS anonymously to eliminate fear of reprisal from tutors or peers when contributing unpopular or sensitive ideas. The anonymity helps individuals to focus on the merits of the contribution rather than its source [14]. As it is difficult to preclude others from contributing ideas in an anonymous GSSsupported environment, the domination of the meeting by one or only a few group members can be reduced [19]. Decision-makers are therefore not intimidated, and become less inhibited [25]. A large number of alternatives and comments could be generated (as an example of group participation), whereas the normative influence of the majority or a powerful minority is eliminated [19].

Furthermore, GSS encourages the participants to provide feedback that is useful for generating and reinforcing understanding of the task. This is accomplished through information sharing [38], participation and objective evaluation in catching errors [15], and by reducing meeting time fragmentation and fear of reprisal [19, 25]. GSS may also encourage positive interpersonal communication and relationships [79]. In addition, GSS helps participants better understand and analyze task-related information by structuring the task and using techniques, rules, and models [21]. It enables learners to perform a deeper analysis of the problem, resulting in a better understand ing of the task [47]. GSS also helps reduce incomplete use of shared feedback by providing information and computation infrastructure to the group [62]. As a result, in a GSS-supported learning environment, learners are encouraged to become more active, autonomous, and confident in constructing ideas [47].

## Effects of Facilitation

Facilitation is another important intervention that can affect group process. The essential function of facilitation is to create and protect the group meeting environment so that group process can take place easily [64]. The main belief behind group facilitation is that full cooperation between everyone is both possible and desirable, where values of equality, shared decision-making, equal opportunity, power sharing, and personal responsibility are basic to full cooperation [34].

For more than a decade there has been growing research regarding how to facilitate the work of group members who are using GSS [6, 9, 22, 23, 26, 28, 57, 81]. Although there are no definite facilitation techniques to support group meetings in a GSS environment, researchers have used a number of different types of GSS facilita tion—chauffeured [22], process [26], task and interaction [23], automated system based [51], and content [26, 54].

Built on prior work [46] on the effects of process and content structure of facilitation on GSS-supported collaborative learning, this study examines a content-facili tated learning environment in which the facilitator tries to empower learners to take control and responsibility for their own efforts and achievements. As cognitive learning theory mentions that feedback received through interaction helps learners to accommodate their knowledge, we believe that facilitation could contribute content feedback in the group discussion and enhance the quality of feedback for effective knowledge acquisition. In addition, the general roles played by the facilitator are to meet the needs of a group of learners and to assist that group toward a goal. With the power of status, the facilitator can exert his or her influence to prevent the group from following nonconstructive paths and protect learners from inappropriate actions by the group [28]. In this way, the domination of the meeting by one or only a few learners can be reduced.

During the group meeting, the facilitator can set up a procedure/agenda that supports both the group’s social and cognitive processes, freeing group members to focus their attention on substantive issues [71]. By managing the sequencing, connectedness of the group activities, and contributing relevant content feedback, the facilitator can ease the group’s work of managing its interaction process and help learners focus and analyze task-related information more effectively [5]. The facilita tor also helps learners to perform a deeper analysis of the problem, resulting in a better understanding of the task without inhibiting the creative exploration of issues [27]. Facilitating learner interaction in this way enables members of the group to learn and develop by self-discovery and personal insight [32]. Therefore, learners are encouraged to contribute ideas actively in the meeting.

By managing the socioemotional issue of the meeting, the facilitator keeps learners in a resourceful and positive emotional state [43]. This encourages effective task and relationship behavior, and deals with disruptive influences in the meeting [9]. This nature of content feedback by the facilitator could reduce communication barrier. Learners are then motivated to present their viewpoint during the meeting (group participation) and encouraged to have a less negative reaction to criticism. This may stimulate enhanced objective evaluation and more awareness of errors, leading to a higher quality of feedback from learners.

All of the above effects of content facilitation and GSS may contribute positively to group processes, as well as cooperation in learning [3]. Both content facilitation and GSS intervention encourage learners to contribute feedback in a collaborative learning environment. Preparing to give feedback helps learners reorganize and consoli date their existing mental models [7]. Both interventions help learners to receive and become aware of feedback. The feedback received through interaction helps the learners to validate and accommodate their mental models.

The main prediction of our research model (see Figure 1) is that GSS and conten facilitation enhance the effectiveness of knowledge acquisition by positively affecting group process gains and losses, cooperation in learning, and learners’ knowledge structure. Therefore, our empirical study aims to verify the following hypotheses:

H1: Subjects in a GSS-supported collaborative learning environment will attain a higher level of knowledge acquisition (that is, group process gain and loss, cooperation in learning, and knowledge structure) than subjects in a collaborative learning environment that is not supported by GSS.

H2: Subjects in a content-facilitated collaborative-learning environment will attain a higher level of knowledge acquisition (that is, group process gain and loss, cooperation in learning, and knowledge structure) than subjects in a collaborative learning environment that is not supported by content facilitation.

## Combined Effect of GSS and Facilitation

Based on Adaptive Structuration Theory, Anson et al. [6] pointed out that the structures provided by a facilitator and GSS are expected to complement one another, resulting in additive, supplemental, or enhancement effects on appropriation processes, and hence group outcomes. GSS makes various constructive group process structures available to support and guide their faithful appropriation through automated features, interfaces, and processing routines.

A facilitator sets effective agendas and brings his or her own knowledge to the GSS-supported meeting. The facilitator extracts, chooses, and adjusts group process structures grounded in his or her own expertise. By giving guidelines to encourage faithfulness, as well as encouraging positive attitudes and consensus about the GSSsupported structures’ use, the facilitator can help the group successfully appropriate these structures [6]. Based on the findings of GSS field and experimental studies [17], a constructive interaction could occur when facilitation and GSS are used together, adding their influence to produce better group processes and outcomes. This forms our third hypothesis regarding the additive effects of GSS and content facilitation on knowledge acquisition.

H3: The combined effects of GSS and content facilitation on knowledge acquisi tion (that is, group process gain and loss, cooperation in learning, and knowl edge structure) is additive.

## Subjects

Undergraduate students enrolled in a management information systems course were randomly assigned to 24 four-member groups—six per cell. (Eight additional groups were used in a pilot study to test and fine-tune procedures and instruments.) No significant differences between students existed in the four experimental treatments in terms of their age and gender. No student reported that he or she had prior experience with the GSS tool or concept mapping technique used in the experiment. For the purpose of the experiment, the students were exposed to GSS (students in cells 3 and 4) and concept mapping techniques (all students) in the classes and tutorials for one semester before the experiment. In this study, incentives were provided to subjects in the form of cash prizes for participation (HK\$92) and for the top performing groups (HK\$92), as is typically done at our university.

## The Task and Procedure

The task involved student development of an evaluation scheme for their term projec (see Appendix A) as part of a collaborative assessment method [48] for the course. Each session lasted about 1.5 hours and involved the performance of the collabora tive task. The instructor explained the definition of a list of preset assessment criteria (see Appendix A), the course objectives, the available learning resources, and the assessment guidelines and policy of the university before the commencement of the task. To evaluate students’ initial knowledge structure concerning the evaluation scheme, they were required to draw freehand a concept map (see Figure 3 for an example) of the evaluation scheme (see Appendix A) before the start of the experi ment. The allotted time for this task was ten minutes. After this was completed, the collaborative task was carried out using the following three steps, each having allot ted times based on pilot study data.

1.The students were asked to brainstorm the definition and description of al related aspects of predetermined and spontaneously created criteria with respect to the course objectives, the learning resources, and constraints (12 minutes).

2. The students were then asked to discuss why the criteria were appropriate for assessing the project (16 minutes).

3. Following a two-round voting procedure, the students selected five criteria and assigned weights reflecting the relative importance of the selected criteria (five minutes).

After completing the collaborative task, the students were required to independently draw freehand a second concept map related to the evaluation scheme (10 minutes)

## GSS Treatments

GroupSystems software [62] was used for the nonfacilitation and GSS (Group 3) and facilitation and GSS (Group 4) treatments. Students were seated around a table in a small GSS laboratory (see Figure 2), and in front of each student was a computer workstation, networked to run the software. Students could type in their contribution and GSS would immediately make these contributions available for other students to read on their individual screens. The software supported anonymous brainstorming and voting. In addition, subjects were reminded that they could use both verbal and GSS communication channels to interact with each other. Their contributions were recorded by the GSS log. Also, equivalent to the other treatments, all the students verbal contributions were videotaped. One researcher acted as the instructor for al GSS treatments. His responsibilities included: (1) instructing groups in GSS and the use of specified tools; and (2) starting and stopping tools, and timekeeping during the session. For the nonfacilitation and GSS (Group 3) treatment, the instructor acted as the system operator, helping the group exclusively with technology for brainstorming and voting. For the facilitation and GSS (Group 4) treatment, the instructor not only acted as the system operator, but also as the facilitator providing content facilitation to the group. For the non-GSS treatments (Groups 1 and 2), the instructor coordinated the voting procedure manually using the whiteboard (see Figure 2).

## Facilitation Treatments

The instructor provided feedback in response to the group’s needs, giving expert advice, direction, and counseling. The instructor only made indirect contributions to the discussion through neutral enhancement of the processes of communication and information processing by the group. This role of facilitation enabled students to learn and acquire knowledge through group collaboration that was facilitated by an instruc tor regarding the process, structure, and also the content of the learning interaction.

For the facilitation and non-GSS (Group 2) treatment the instructor acted as the meeting facilitator, helping the group by using the content-facilitation approach. For the facilitation and GSS (Group 4) treatment, the instructor not only acted as content facilitator but also as the system operator, helping the group with the technology. Every contribution made by the instructor was identifiable, which was displayed on the monitor followed by the word “Tutor.”

As the researcher and study facilitator were the same person, there were potential threats to the internal validity of the findings across treatment groups. To minimize these threats, two independent raters were instructed to assess and analyze the facilitator’s performance to assure that facilitator feedback was appropriately distrib uted. Both raters were unaware of the conditions or experimental nature of the cases. Both raters agreed that there was no systematic facilitator feedback bias across the 12 content-facilitated groups.

## Nonfacilitation and Non-GSS Treatment

Like the facilitation and non-GSS (Group 2) treatment, the nonfacilitation and non GSS (Group 1) treatment used traditional face-to-face communication among students of the group throughout the whole task. The instructor’s main role was to monitor the process of the meeting and to record the time used for each procedure. While completing steps 1 and 2, students discussed the assessment criteria with each other verbally without interacting with the instructor. During step 3, the students selected their own assessment criteria using the manual two-round voting procedure, which was coordinated by the instructor.

## Measurement of Dependent Variables

Measurements used to assess the dependent variables included a questionnaire, GSS log, video record, and concept map. Table 1 illustrates each dependent variable and its corresponding measurement(s).

## Measurement of Group Process and Cooperation in Learning

A questionnaire, with three three-item constructs, was developed to measure the student’s perception of GSS and facilitation effects on two group process losses (domination and communication barrier) and cooperation in learning. The questionnaire and definition of the constructs are illustrated in Appendix B. The questionnaire was developed based on Moore and Benbasat’s [55] use of conceptual construct valida tion and was pretested in our pilot study.

At the end of the experiment, the subjects rated themselves on domination, communication barrier, and cooperation in learning. Each item used a seven-point Likerttype scale (strongly agree = 1, quite agree = 2, slightly agree = 3, neutral = 4, slightly disagree = 5, quite disagree = 6, strongly disagree = 7). The related ratings were averaged for the two group process losses and cooperation in learning. Domination, communication barrier, and cooperation in learning were perceptual constructs measured using multiple items. Therefore, they had to be tested for convergent and discriminant validity [29].

Factor analysis using the Principal Component analysis was conducted with VARIMAX rotation to test discriminant reliability. The results (see Table 2) indicated that a three-factor solution was the most likely solution, as three factors had eigenval ues greater than 1.0. The three factors accounted for approximately 67 percent of the variance in the data set. No item loaded highly on more than one factor. In addition, all items loaded on their factors with the lowest loading being 0.497. Comrey [13] indicated that loading higher than 0.45 could be considered fair, greater than 0.55, good; 0.63, very good; and 0.71, excellent. Table 2 showed that most loadings on their factors were in the excellent range, with only one in the “fair” range. More evidence of reliability could be attained from the correlation between all nine items. Each item should correlate more highly with other items measuring the same construct than with other items measuring other constructs [29]. Table 3 indicates that all items, except D3, correlated higher with their related items than with the others.

Quality of feedback for each subject was independently assessed by two raters on a five-point scale based on Dennis et al. [18] and Web [80]. According to our classifica tion (see Appendix D), there are five levels of feedback quality ranging from 1 (very poor quality) to 5 (very good quality). In this study, we considered the mean quality of feedback. It was calculated by averaging the quality scores for all the feedback generated by each subject. This measure rewards subjects that generate high quality ideas and penalizes those that produce less than perfect ideas. Of 1,338 feedbacks coded, there were only 103 differences in the rater’s grades, indicating an initial agreement of 1,235/1,338 = 92.30 percent, an adequate inter-rater reliability. Remaining differences in grading were resolved through a discussion between the two raters.

## Measurement of Knowledge Structure

Based on Khalifa et al. [46], a simple concept mapping method was employed to measure student’s knowledge structure in terms of knowledge complexity (the degree to which the learner picks up relevant and meaningful relationships between the concepts that belong to the same hierarchy) and integration (the degree to which the learner constructs correct relationships between the concepts that belong to differen levels of hierarchies and abstraction), as well as the knowledge commonality (the degree to which the structural knowledge of a learner is similar to that of the other learners of the group). The method used the principle of concept maps [45, 59] and similarity coefficient [40]. A concept map (see Figure 3) is a graphical representation of meaningful relationships between concepts. It is a semantic network describing a cognitive structure: ideas and their interrelationships that can be used to describe a person’s knowledge structure [41]. With concept maps, learners can develop a schematic representation of their mental model regarding a particular knowledge domain through the externalization and visualization of concepts and meaningful relation ships between them [45]. This differs from Ackermann and Eden’s [2] use of concep map as a multidimensional representation of the information for providing groups with a pictorial view of the issue during group discussion.

With concept mapping, each key concept can be symbolized as a hierarchy of concepts going from a higher level of abstraction (general concept) to lower levels (specific concepts, examples, objects, and events) [45]. Direct-links relate concepts that belong to the same hierarchy, whereas cross-links relate concepts from different hierarchies. Cross-links are usually more difficult to learn than direct-links [45].

Novak and Staff [60] proposed a number of methods for students to construct their own concept maps. We adopted a simple method to measure the knowledge structure acquired by the subjects of our empirical study. First, we provided the students with a number of concepts and asked them to draw a concept map illustrating the links between the given concepts. Then, we asked them to label and describe each link in their concept maps.

In addition to the concept map completed at the beginning of the study, each subject drew one at the end of the experiment. Two raters were employed to indepen dently code all the concept maps based on the principle of Concept Propositional Analysis (CPA) [59]. By comparing the two concept maps constructed by the same subject, changes in knowledge complexity, integration, and commonality were derived. Two coders were instructed to rate independently the same student’s concept maps to ensure the reliability of the measurement. The initial levels of agreement on knowledge complexity, integration, and commonality were 97.37 percent, 98.48 percent, and 97.60 percent, respectively, indicating adequate inter-rater reliability. Remaining differences were resolved through a discussion between the two coders.

Measurement of Knowledge Commonality. To determine the level of a subject’s knowl edge commonality in comparison to other members of the group, a similarity coefficient [40] was used (see Appendix E for a detailed description of the measurement of knowledge commonality). In a group of four students, it is necessary to compare the concept map of an individual with those of the other three members of the group. Therefore, three scores of commonality were obtained for each concept map. To measure the level of knowledge commonality of a subject, we averaged the three scores of commonality. A high average level of knowledge commonality of a subject implied that there was a high level of knowledge commonality between the subject and the other members of the group. To determine the change of a subject’s knowledge commonality, we subtracted the subject’s pre-experimental commonality score from his or her post-experimental commonality score.

Measurement of Knowledge Complexity and Integration. The knowledge structure of each subject is measured by its level of complexity and integration as symbolized by the subject’s concept map. Complexity and integration are the main features that distinguish the knowledge structure of the expert from that of the greenhorn [45]. As expertise is achieved through learning, the structure of one’s mental model becomes increasingly complex and interconnected [20, 70]. Based on the findings of Khalifa and Kwok [45], we used the total number of direct-links in the associated concept map as a measure of complexity of a knowledge structure. On the other hand, we used the total number of cross-links to measure integration of the knowledge structure.

To determine the change of a subject’s knowledge complexity and integration, we first located the new links found in the subject’s concept map drawn at the end of the experiment by comparing it with the one drawn at the beginning (see Appendix F for an example of a “before” and an “after” concept map, and the illustration of the way to measure changes of a subject’s knowledge complexity and integration). We then categorized the new links into either direct- or cross-links. Finally, we used the tota number of new direct-links found as a measure of the change of a subject’s knowl edge complexity, and the total number of new cross-links as a measure of the change of knowledge integration.

## Results

IN THIS STUDY, ANALYSIS OF VARIANCE (ANOVA) procedures were used to test hypotheses for each dependent variable with the Tukey follow-up [44] to assess the effects of GSS and content facilitation on group processes (gains and losses), coop eration in learning, and knowledge structure.

Both qualitative and quantitative data were collected in this study to test the hypotheses and to better understand the main (Hypotheses 1 and 2) and interaction (Hypothesis 3) effects of GSS and content facilitation on knowledge acquisition in terms of the two group process gains (group participation and quality of feedback), two group process losses (domination and communication barrier), cooperation in learning, and three knowledge structures (knowledge complexity, integration, and commonality). The main prediction of this study (see Figure 1) is that GSS and content facilitation enhance the effectiveness of knowledge acquisition by positively af fecting group processes, cooperation in learning and learners’ knowledge structure.

Table 4 presents means and standard deviations for each group process, cooperation in learning, and three knowledge structures, and Tables 5 and 6 summarize significant ANOVA, HANOVA [5, 76], and Tukey test results. The HANOVA test was used to detect significant main and interaction effects for each group process, coop eration in learning, and two knowledge structures (knowledge complexity and inte gration), which were individual-level measures. The main benefit of using the HANOVA test for individual-level measures is that it nests individual responses within groups and groups within treatments, hence adjusting for group-level effects [5]. The ANOVA test was used to detect significant main and interaction effects for knowl edge commonality, which was a group-level measure. The results indicate that the main effects of GSS on knowledge acquisition (Hypothesis 1) were supported with the data on quality of feedback, domination, communication barrier, and cooperation in learn ing, whereas the main effects of content facilitation on knowledge acquisition (Hypothesis 2) were supported only with the data on knowledge commonality. However, the interaction effects of GSS and content facilitation on knowledge acquisition (Hy pothesis 3) were not supported.

## Discussion

WHAT CAN WE NOW SAY WITH RESPECT to whether GSS enhances individual knowl edge acquisition in collaborative environments, and whether the impact of GSS will differ when groups are exposed to different modes of facilitation? The messages are mixed.

It would appear that both content facilitation and GSS can positively affect an individual’s development of a concept map but they are not additive. In fact, they result in quite a different effect and seem to cause cognitive dissonance with respec to concept map formation. There is crossover interaction among the treatments. We see that the highest performing groups, according to their knowledge structure scores, were those that had only content facilitation support without GSS (Group 2), or those who used GSS without facilitation support (Group 3), but the results are different.

## Content Facilitation Without GSS

For content facilitation without GSS, there is focus around the product and the results are positive and significant. In fact, it is the only treatment that results in improved concept map commonality. The individuals in the group become more similar as they extend their original concept map. Content facilitation in this study had no positive main effect on reducing group process losses or enhancing process gains. Based on the results (see Table 5), group participation in the facilitation and non-GSS group (Group 2) was significantly worse $( p = 0 . 0 4 5 ^ { * * } )$ than the nonfacilitation and non GSS group (Group 1), which received no special treatment. Content facilitation had no positive impact on cooperation in learning.

Overall, individuals in groups with content facilitation (see Table 6) demonstrated the ability to form more common concept maps $( p = 0 . 0 1 8 ^ { * * } )$ in spite of relatively poor group dynamics. This is definitely useful in some circumstances (such as where a body of well-accepted knowledge is to be learned), but might cause some groups to develop “group-think” [36], as some important input may be suppressed through poo group dynamic in situations where conflicts need to be resolved.

## GSS Without Content Facilitation

For GSS without content facilitation, the focus is on group dynamic. Misbehavior— that is, flaming or buffoonery—was found in previous studies involving high school students and undergraduate business students when they worked anonymously in GSSsupported classroom discussions (for example [67]). However, our results (see Table 5) indicated that cooperation in learning was significantly enhanced $( p = 0 . 0 3 5 ^ { * * } )$ when using GSS. In our study, we found process losses significantly reduced as was consistently found with prior research (for example [62]). Domination $( p = 0 . 0 0 8 ^ { * * } )$ and communication barrier $( p = 0 . 0 0 1 ^ { * * } )$ were both significantly lowered through use of GSS. In our study, we also found some indication of group process gain. Quality of feedback was observed to be significantly better when using GSS $( p = 0 . 0 0 9 ^ { * * } )$ Interestingly, group participationwas not enhanced through the use of GSS (see Table 5). Instead, the two GSS groups scored significantly lower $( p = 0 . 0 2 8 ^ { * * }$ and $p =$ $0 . 0 2 0 ^ { \ast \ast } )$ than the nonfacilitation and non-GSS group (Group 1). The measure of our findings on group participation was based on observation and was more critically focused on convergence and the building effect of participation rather than just the number of unique ideas generated, as was the focus in some prior research (for example [61]).

Although the main effect of GSS on change in knowledge commonality was non significant, overall, individuals in GSS groups tended to develop concept maps of less commonalitywhen their final and initial concept maps were compared (see Tables 4 and 6). This suggests that GSS is useful for creating an environment conducive to raising more divergent issues by reducing process losses (see Table 5) (domination, $p = 0 . 0 0 8 ^ { * * }$ and communication barrier, $p = 0 . 0 0 1 ^ { * * } )$ and enhancing process gain (quality of feedback, $p = 0 . 0 0 9 ^ { * * } )$ , but may not lead to convergence even though there is cooperation in learning $( p = 0 . 0 3 5 ^ { * * } )$ . This is especially useful in complex situations requiring consideration of multiple perspectives or when trained facilita tion presence is not an option. This differentiation and lack of commonality in concept maps may possibly be consistent with numerous studies demonstrating decision-making taking longer when GSS is used, and suggest process structure alternatives [18].

## Nonadditive Nature of GSS and Content Facilitation

Our findings that GSS and content facilitation are not additive command special atten tion and pose a challenge. One would think that content facilitation, with its focus on enhanced commonality, and GSS, with its focus on enhanced cooperation in learning through enhanced process gains and reduced process loss, would be a natural marriage. Based on our findings, this is not entirely the case. In the GSS groups, process losses were indeed reduced and the quality of feedback enhanced. We found only insig nificant improvement in changes to concept map integration.There was sufficient power to find differences. Strikingly, the only other group with a lower level of integration and reduction of commonality of the final concept maps was the nonfacilitation and non-GSS group (Group 1), which had no special support. This dissonance, when the two independently successful approaches are joined, suggests that the balance and interaction between content facilitation’s focus on convergence and GSS’s induced divergence needs to be carefully crafted to achieve an additive effect.

Some evidence of partial success of content facilitation and GSS exists in the literature. For example, Ackermann and Eden [2] have successfully facilitated groups using technology and a content-oriented approach to yield a convergent cognitive map, albeit without extensive individual use of GSS by the group’s members. Miranda and Bostrom [54] report successful integration of process facilitation (without content focus) and GSS use for problem solving, albeit without special atten tion to group members’ individual production of concept maps. Dean et al. [16] combine process facilitation and group-based activity modeling successfully, albeit with special facilitation techniques and software specifically developed to support the nature of the group’s task. This may be the key to success for our more genera case. Both the GSS technology and the facilitation approach need to be sufficiently flexible and blended, as dictated by the group task to be successful, as adaptive structuration theory would suggest [6]. Such was not the situation in our study. Is it the situation that GSS and content facilitation somehow work against each other when it comes to groups integrating knowledge? Perhaps each structure directs group members down diverse paths simultaneously, preventing them from assimilating the information? If so, this would suggest that the two structures be interleaved, rather than provided to a group in tandem. This would suggest further research that looks at the two structures carefully.

Overall, we see that there is an impact of the group on an individual’s concept map formation. This is consistent with the cognitive learning theory, which assumes that learners interact with new information, interpret it, and build new personal knowledge representations by relating the new information to their prior knowledge [73]. Our research demonstrates that interaction in a group provides the opportunity for both personal and social knowledge acquisition through conversation and cooperation as noted by Pask [63] and Entwistle and Entwistle [24]. Assisting group interaction with content facilitation and GSS enhances different aspects of this acquisition process. Content facilitation focuses on relating information, whereas GSS focuses on encouraging information generation and sharing. Both are important and, as we have seen in our research, can independently enhance concept map development. However, we cannot expect the effects to be synergistic and additive without effective integration to avoid difficulties in concept map integration and resultant commonality.

To achieve an additive effect with GSS, we suggest that facilitation focuses more on process and plays a lower content role (perhaps even anonymous), especially in the early phases of the group’s interaction. As the collaborative model advises, learners enjoy communicating when they are given a nonthreatening environment in which to participate [49]. Due to status influence, learners might treat the feedback generated by the content facilitator as expert advice. The facilitator’s feedback through conver sation might influence the learners to validate and accommodate their mental models like facilitator’s without sufficiently exploring alternatives. In later phases, the facilitator could lead the group toward construction of knowledge structure with a high degree of commonality among group members, one weakness of GSS. This is consis tent with Philips and Philips [64] and Miranda and Bostrom [54], but the implication of such a setup for individual change in knowledge remains to be examined carefully.

## Limitations

There are a number of limitations related to the setting of the experiment. The first limitation concerns how facilitation is applied. Most prior GSS and group dynamic experimental studies evaluate a given procedural structure (such as Nominal Group Technique or a specific GSS tool or tool set). The facilitator follows a prewritten script of what to say and how to act when applying the treatment structure. Nevertheless, this fixed or prescripted approach sacrifices external validity regarding facilita tion in real settings [6]. Normally, facilitators act with flexibility to help the group select and adapt procedural structures to meet their specific needs. Hirokawa and Gouran [33] argue that a fixed, scripted approach is inappropriate to study process intervention, given the fluidity and unpredictability of typical group interaction.

This study, based on Hirokawa and Gouran’s suggestion, involved one facilitator (the researcher) who flexibly intervened through neutral enhancement of the group processes based on their judgment of how to best assist the group. However, as the researcher and the facilitator were the same person, there were potential threats to the internal validity of the findings. In order to minimize the threats of the validity, two independent raters were instructed to assess and analyze each facilitator’s feedback to assure that the facilitator actually performed as intended. Although both raters agreed that there was no systematical bias in the facilitator’s feedback across the 12 contentfacilitated groups, the researcher was still able to bias the treatment groups, either consciously or subconsciously.

The second limitation relates to the nature of the task’s rewarding scheme. The students were externally motivated by the cash rewards, which may not be proper motivation for improving participation and learning in some settings and cultures. Although common (and expected) practice in Hong Kong, the results of the experi ment may not have been the same had the reward scheme not been as motivating.

The third limitation concerns the reliability of collected data. Results indicated tha the data on domination did not meet an adequate level of construct validity. Therefore, although the findings for domination were reported, conclusions must remain tentative.

The fourth limitation relates to the experimental setting of the facilitation treatments. Facilitation was treated as an all-or-none condition as opposed to varying amounts of facilitation (for example [54]). What are the relative advantages/disad vantages of the two approaches?

## Implications and Future Research Direction

GSS is not a panacea. For students not familiar with technology, some distractions could occur that might potentially inhibit knowledge acquisition, at least initially. Despite the limitations, the results of this study have implications for both practice and research. They bring direct attention to variables and processes that have a bearing on GSS- and facilitation-supported collaboration applicable to both education and business contexts, particularly while GSS in the classroom is still regarded as one of the unanswered research questions [10]. In addition, the results of this study also provide insights for the design and use of technology in training and education. In practice, schools can add GSS-supported learning to the curriculum to nurture knowl edge acquisition, although much remains to be explored with respect to broader implications on the learning environment [67]. Teachers and students can be trained to use GSS to create an effective collaborative-learning environment. Likewise, in a business context, GSS and facilitation can be used to promote the construction of group knowledge and support the development of a knowledge-sharing culture, contributing in this way to organizational learning.

It is our hope that this research will be helpful in laying the groundwork for a bette understanding of the appropriate and successful implementation of GSS technology and human facilitation within the classroom context. Although former analyses found support for some, though not all, of the hypotheses we have outlined, it is clear that more research is needed to advance our understanding of GSS and facilitation in the classroom, and help answer questions such as: Which types of learning tasks are most appropriate for GSS support and facilitation, and how does GSS and facilitation help? What is the effect of different levels of GSS and facilitation on different types of learners? Which group sizes are most appropriate? What are the factors of success for implementation (such as training for facilitators)?

GSS has expanded from a synchronous (same time/same place) environment to a further three environment modes (same time/different place, different time/same place, and different time/different place), in which meetings and organizational learning can take place. The time has come to move our thinking about GSS and facilitation support in these three modes of learning environments. Facilitator anonymity bears investigation. If the facilitator’s feedback is anonymous, the status influence of the facilitator might be counterbalanced, whereas the quality of student feedback could still be enhanced. The setting of GSS with anonymous content facilitation might be a better environment to enhance knowledge acquisition. The potential of such an envi ronment remains to be investigated.

## Conclusion

THIS STUDY USED AN EXPERIMENTAL APPROACH to investigate an individual’s change in knowledge while being part of a learning environment enhanced with GSS and content facilitation singularly and combined. We proposed and tested the predictions of a theory-based research model explaining the enabling effects of GSS and content facilitation on group processes and cooperation in learning that altogether lead to richer levels of knowledge representation. It would appear that both content facilita tion and GSS can positively affect an individual’s development of a concept map bu they are not additive. In fact, they result in quite a different effect and seem to cause cognitive dissonance with respect to concept map formation.

For content facilitation without GSS, there was focus on information combination that resulted in improved concept map commonality without reducing process losses or enhancing participation or encouraging learning. For GSS without content facilitation, process losses were reduced, whereas feedback and cooperation in learning were enhanced. However, concept map commonality was poor. The combination of GSS with content facilitation did not improve knowledge complexity, integration, or commonality. GSS and content facilitation interventions acted as substitutes or neutraliz ers for one another when combined.

We suggest that GSS is useful, but facilitation with GSS is best accomplished with a more passive process-oriented approach. This is especially true in early phases of group interaction, reserving a more active approach for later phases as the group struggles with information consolidation. By doing this, the benefits of GSS can be preserved and commonality in an individual’s concept maps can be attained.

## Measurement of Knowledge Commonality

To determine the level of a subjects’knowledge commonality, a similarity coefficien [40] was used. The measurement includes the following steps:

Step 1. Identifying propositions of the concept map constructed by each subject. The propositions in each concept map were identified using the method of Concept Propositional Analysis (CPA) [59], and summarized in the form of a table (see Figure A1 and Table A1).

Step 2. Calculating the frequency of each proposition using a quota sampling method.

As shown in Table A2, the presence or absence of each proposition in the concept maps constructed by a group of subjects was described mathematically by a binary number, which assumed value one if the proposition was present in a particular concept map and value zero if the proposition was absent. Similar concept maps had more propositions in common than dissimilar concept maps. If each proposition of the concept maps scored 100 percent response, it meant all the individual concept maps were identical.

Step 3. Calculating the level of commonality between pairs of concept maps using the Jaccard measure.

In order to measure the similarity coefficient of a pair of concept maps, the tota frequencies of matches (1-1 or 0-0 match) and mismatches (1-0 or 0-1 match) of propositions of a pair of concept maps were obtained as illustrated in Figure A2.

We used the Jaccard measure [40] to determine the similarity coefficient of a pair of concept maps. It gave equal weight to matches (1-1) and mismatches (1-0 and 0-1). Matches (0-0) were disregarded. We did not consider the 0-0 match because it was impossible to have a link which did not present in both concept maps. The value of the similarity coefficient fell between 1 and 0 (1 = entirely identical; 0 = entirely different).

According to the Jaccard measure, we computed

Similarity coefficient

$$
\begin{array}{c} S _ {(x, y)} = n \big (\mathbf {C} _ {x} \cap \mathbf {C} _ {y} \big) / n \big (\mathbf {C} _ {x} \cup \mathbf {C} _ {y} \big) \\ = a / (a + b + c). \end{array}
$$

## Step 4. Calculate the level of knowledge commonality of a subject.

In a group of four subjects, it was necessary to compare each concept map with the other three of the group. Therefore, three scores of commonality were obtained for each concept map. To measure the level of knowledge commonality of a subject, we averaged the three scores of commonality. A high average level of knowledge commonality of a subject implied that there was a high level of knowledge commonality between the subject and the other members of the group.

During the experiment, each subject was required to draw a concept map at the beginning of the experiment. Also, he/she had to draw another concept map at the end of the experiment. To determine the change of the subject knowledge commonality, we subtracted his/her pre-experimental commonality score from his/her post-experi mental commonality score.
