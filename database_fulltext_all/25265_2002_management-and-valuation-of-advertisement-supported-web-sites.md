# Management and Valuation of Advertisement-Supported Web Sites

RAJIV M. DEWAN, MARSHALL L. FREIMER, AND JIE ZHANG

RAJIV M. DEWAN is an Associate Professor of Computers and Information Systems at the William E. Simon School of Business Administration at the University of Roches ter. His research interests include management of information systems in organiza tions, markets for information goods and services, and electronic commerce. His research appears in Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Computing, and other journals.

MARSHALL L. FREIMER is a Professor of Management Science and Computers and Information Systems at the University of Rochester. He utilizes applied probability and decision sciences to problems in information management, electronic commerce, and marketing. His papers have appeared in engineering, management, economics, and statistics journals.

JIE (JENNIFER) ZHANG is a doctoral candidate at the William E. Simon School of Business Administration at the University of Rochester. She employs analytical and empirical techniques to examine a number of issues in electronic commerce and management of information systems.

ABSTRACT: A key decision by the manager of an advertisement-supported Web site is the balance between content and advertising. Content is costly but attracts viewers, whereas advertisement generates revenues but repels viewers. The period-by-period balancing decision is further complicated by the growth and diffusion nature of Web site viewership. This decision problem is modeled as a control problem that captures the essence of the business model of such Web sites. Using this model we show that it may be optimal for the Web site to initially have negative cash flows from having fewer advertisements and more content. This is more than compensated for by future profits from the Web site. We use the solution to the control problem to also develop a forward-looking measure of Web traffic called the “discounted total traffic.” We empirically examine this new measure and find that it better predicts market capitali zation than backward-looking measures like page views.

KEYWORDS AND PHRASES: advertising policy, content management, market capitalization, Web traffic.

VALUATION OF INFORMATION AGE ORGANIZATIONS, especially ones directly dealing with information technology, is hard to do as a substantial part of their assets—which include intellectual property, specialized and talented human resource, brand, and goodwill—are intangible. This is reflected in the ratio of market value to book value of a firm. Whereas the market value of the firm would include the value of any intan gible assets, the book value typically does not. This ratio varies by industry with values ranging from 1.2 for the oil and gas industry to 12.0 for the software industry. The manufacturing and farming industry has a ratio of 2.2 [9]. The valuation problem is especially acute for advertisement-supported Web sites as they have an as yet unproven business model. We begin to study this problem by first analytically modeling the management problem for a firm with such a Web site. Next, we empirically vali date this model using audience data<sup>1</sup> and market capitalization.

Advertising-supported Web sites offer a variety of content ranging from search engines to news and entertainment. This content is costly to acquire, format, and publish. Advertisements provide a majority of revenue for these sites. A sample of such firms is shown in Table 1.

Advertisement-supported content is not exclusive to the Web. The New York Times Corporation, which owns a large-circulation U.S. national daily newspaper, received 68 percent of its revenue from advertisements in the year 2000. Radio and television networks in the United States are largely advertisement-supported. These media have been examined extensively in economics. A survey is contained in Owens and Wildman [11]. The focus of this literature has been on the public good aspect of broadcasting in the United States and on the competitive positioning by programming choice. The Web publishing industry presents a different challenge, as the medium is not mature yet. This adds a new dimension of technology adoption and diffusion to that of consumer choice in a more mature medium. In this paper, we present a diffusion type model that incorporates consumer behavior. This model is in the spirit of similar models used in marketing [4].

How do the viewers react to a Web site? The content is what brings the user to the Web site. More valuable, dynamic, and updated content attracts more viewers. Advertising, in contrast to content, makes the Web site less attractive to consumers.<sup>2</sup> The viewers incur a cost from the additional download time, cluttering up of the page, reduced usability, and less valuable content.

The content and advertising balance presents a different picture to the Web site owner. Although it gets revenue from advertisements, it finds the content costly to publish. This presents the Web site owner with a trade-off: increasing advertising may increase the revenue per customer, but fewer customers will find the Web site attractive to visit. This problem of evaluating this trade-off is further complicated by the technology-diffusion nature of Web site viewership: the number of viewers in a period depends on the number of viewers in the previous period, the stickiness, or conversely the attrition, of the Web site, and the general growth in the population of World Wide Web users. These consumer behavior and diffusion aspects are together modeled as a control problem that is exhibited in the next section.

We find that it may be optimal for the firm to have more content and fewer advertisements initially. This may be net value maximizing as the firm invests in building up a viewership. This implies that it may be optimal for the firm to have a negative cash flow at the start and it may only start making profits later. This makes it hard to determine the market value for a firm that has no history of profits. In the next section we use the model to define and construct a forward-looking measure of Web traffic, which we call the discounted total traffic (DTT), to determine the future profit poten tial of the firm. We close the loop between the theoretical model and reality by using this new measure to predict market capitalization.

## Modeling the Web Site Management Problem

IN THIS SECTION WE PRESENT THE Web site management problem that was exhibited in the conference version of this paper [5]. It is included here for convenience and completeness.

Consider a Web site manager who is managing an advertising-supported Web site that offers content of value to visitors. There are many design factors that affect the attractiveness of the page to a potential viewer. More, well organized, and frequently updated content attracts more viewers, but is also expensive to acquire, format, and place on the Web on an ongoing basis. The content cost is modeled as $k q _ { t } ^ { 2 } ,$ , where $q _ { t }$ is the proportion of the Web page devoted to content in period t and k is a constant.<sup>3</sup>

In contrast, advertising reduces the attractiveness of the page—it adds clutter, increases download time, and leaves less room on the page for the content that attracts a visitor. The Web site owner, however, gets revenue from advertisements. In this respect, advertisements are like price. In an earlier version of this paper reported in conference proceedings [5] we explored this aspect further. We examined the advertising policies on Web sites of national and regional news dailies. We found that the Web sites associated with national dailies, which have a more competitive market than regional dailies, devoted a smaller portion of the Web page to advertisements. This corresponds to our expectation of lower prices in more competitive marketplaces. Just as setting the price is an important strategic decision in competitive markets, so is setting the advertising policy when the users can easily switch from one site to another.

Increasing the number and exposure of advertisements results in greater revenue for the Web site owner. The relationship between impressions and revenue depend on the type of contract between the advertiser and the Web site. The simplest kinds of contracts are written on “cost per thousand impressions” or CPM basis. More complex contracts may include click-throughs or actual sales performance. Even these can be simplified to a per impression rate. In this model we assume that the revenue in period t is $c p _ { t } u _ { t }$ , where, at time $t , p _ { t }$ is the proportion of the Web page devoted to advertisements, $u _ { t }$ is the number of viewers, and c is the revenue rate.

Putting the revenue and the cost together, the Web site owner’s profit in period t is $c p _ { t } u _ { t } - k q _ { t } ^ { 2 }$ . In each period the manager has the option of varying the amount of content and advertisements. These are the variables he controls. We take the objective to be to maximize the net present value of the firm as this maximizes shareholder wealth. Firms may have operated with different objectives in the short run, but economics and finance dictate that the long-term objective has to be the maximization of net present value of future cash flow streams. By picking different discount rates, one can easily model managers with different focus on long-term versus short-term performance. Let $\beta$ be the rate of continuous discounting. The manager’s objective function is

$$
\max _ {p _ {t}, q _ {t}} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \left(c p _ {t} u _ {t} - k q _ {t} ^ {2}\right) e ^ {- \beta t} d t.\tag{1}
$$

The formulation of the problem is not yet complete for we have not considered the effect of the manager’s decisions on the potential viewers. Let $u _ { t }$ denote the number of viewers in time t. Starting at time 0 with $u _ { o }$ viewers, we want to estimate the changes in viewership from period to period. For this purpose, we formulate an economy growth type model in which we relate the change in population to Web site characteristics.

Let $\dot { u } _ { t }$ be the change in number of visitors in period t. If the Web site is perfectly sticky, and if we ignore the effect of advertising and content for the moment, then the only growth in viewership will come from growth of the Internet itself. Let a represent this rate. The Web site, however, is not likely to be perfectly sticky. Taking f to be the attrition rate, $f u _ { t }$ is the attrition in viewership. Putting these effects together, we get

$$
\dot {u} _ {t} = a - f u _ {t}.
$$

The effect of content and advertising on viewership depends on the market condi tions for Web-based content—the degree of differentiation of content, the level of competition, the disutility to consumers from advertisements, and other related factors. Overall, the viewership decreases with the portion of the Web page devoted to advertising and increases with the portion of content that is valued by users. Let b and e be the rates of decrease in viewership from advertising and increase with content, respectively. Putting all of these factors together, we posit that

$$
\dot {u} _ {t} = a - b p _ {t} + e q _ {t} - f u _ {t}.\tag{2}
$$

The top left portion of the rendering of the Web page on a viewers’ screen is the most important part as most users view this part. Le $p _ { t }$ and $q _ { t }$ represent the allocation of this valuable space to advertising and content, respectively. Normalizing, we get<sup>4</sup>

$$
p _ {t} + q _ {t} \leq 1.\tag{3}
$$

Combining Constraint (3) together with Objective (1) and Constraint (2), we get the following control problem for the manager:

$$
\max _ {p _ {t}, q _ {t}} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \left(c p _ {t} u _ {t} - k q _ {t} ^ {2}\right) e ^ {- \beta t} d t
$$

subject to:

$$
\dot {u} _ {t} = a - b p _ {t} + e q _ {t} - f u _ {t}
$$

$$
p _ {t} + q _ {t} \leq 1
$$

$$
p _ {t}, q _ {t} \geq 0, u _ {0} \text {   given.   }
$$

This is a control problem with $p _ { t }$ and $q _ { t }$ as controls and $u _ { t }$ as the state variable. In the rest of the paper we consider the case when Constraint (3) is binding.<sup>5</sup> Set $p _ { t } = 1 - q _ { t }$

Dewan et al. [5] show the solution to be:

$$
u _ {t} = u _ {\infty} - \left(u _ {\infty} - u _ {0}\right) e ^ {- r t},\tag{4}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{c} u _ {\infty} = \frac {c (a + e) (b + e) + 2 k (a - b) (f + \beta)}{(2 f + \beta) (c b + c e + f k) + f k \beta} \\ r = \sqrt {(f + \beta / 2) (f + \beta / 2 + c (b + e) / k)} - \beta / 2. \end{array}
$$

Note that $u _ { t }$ moves asymptotically from its initial value $u _ { 0 }$ to its asymptotic value $u _ { \infty }$ as t increases. This is shown in Figure 1 for $\beta = 0 . 0 2 , a = 1 , 0 0 0 , b = 5 , 1 0 0 , c = 3 , e =$ $5 , 0 0 0 , f \mathrm { = } 0 . 0 6 , k \mathrm { = } 1 0 0 , 0 0 0$ , and $u _ { 0 } = 2 0 { , } 0 0 0$ page views.

Using the specific solution and equation, we obtain $q _ { t }$ as

$$
q _ {t} = q _ {\infty} + \frac {r - f}{b + e} \big (u _ {\infty} - u _ {t} \big),\tag{5}
$$

where $q _ { \infty }$ the asymptotic value of $q _ { t } ,$ is

$$
q _ {\infty} = \frac {b - a}{b + e} + \frac {f}{b + e} u _ {\infty}.
$$

Examining Equation (5) we note that $q _ { \infty }$ is a constant and $u _ { \infty } - u _ { t }$ decreases over time if $u _ { 0 } < u _ { \infty }$ Further, from the definition of $r$ in Equation (4) we note that $r > f .$ . Hence, $q _ { t }$ decreases with time if $u _ { 0 } < u _ { \infty }$ . This is illustrated in Figure 2. Using the same parameters as in Figure 1, the proportion of content, $q _ { t } ,$ is plotted. The proportion of advertisements is $1 - q _ { t }$ . We note that the firms make an initial investment in the site by having extra content and fewer advertisements. The proportion of content falls as the Web site matures. Higher content and lower advertising may imply that the net cash flow is negative initially. This is shown in Figure 3 and fits with the notion of invest ing in visitors.

Examining Figures 2 and 3 we see that one can expect the Web site manager to overinvest in content and have few advertisements initially. As time passes, the number of advertisements increases and investment in content decreases. This implies that initially the Web sites may not be profitable and only approach profitability over time. This may be a shareholder value maximizing strategy. What matters is not the current profits but the discounted total profit.

Further examining Figure 2 we see that there are positive network externalities, from a consumer’s perspective, in the Web market for content. Notice that a Web site with fewer visitors at time 0 has less content and more advertisements than a site with more visitors. Consequently, the Web site with more visitors is more attractive to new visitors. This gives rise to portal behavior, where a few Web sites get a disproportionately large share of the visitors—a phenomenon that is also explained by competition among Web sites for visitors [6].

## Market Value of Firms

IN THIS SECTION WE PRESENT some empirical evidence to validate and justify the model. As we do not have time-series data on the proportion of Web pages devoted to advertising and content, a direct test of the model is not possible. However, an indi rect test, of the kind used in marketing, is possible. To make this analogy, first note that the model in this paper is similar to that in adoption and diffusion literature as exemplified by Bass [2], Chatterjee and Eliashberg [4], and Horsky [8]. Second, Bass et al. [3] have shown that it is possible to validate this kind of a model without directly appealing to the underlying decision variables. This is the approach we follow. The shareholder wealth maximizing behavior of the manager, as modeled in Equations (1), (2), and (3), results in a specific form of the viewership curve over time. This allows us to construct a new measure of Web traffic that is forward-looking and depends on the model-predicted viewership curve. The relationship between this measure and market capitalization of firms with advertising-supported Web sites helps us validate the model.

The Discounted Total Traffic at a Site

Define DTT as the area under the $u _ { t }$ curve discounted continuously at rate $\beta { : }$

$$
\mathrm{DTT} = \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} u _ {t} e ^ {- \beta t} d t.
$$

Substituting for $u _ { t }$ from Equation (4), we get:

$$
\mathrm{DTT} = \frac {u _ {\infty}}{\beta} - \frac {u _ {\infty} - u _ {0}}{r + \beta}.
$$

To illustrate the economic significance of DTT, consider the situation in which each visitor generates the same net revenue. In this case, the market value of the firm would be expected to be proportional to DTT. So this forward-looking measure of Web traffic is closely related to the market value for firms. This is a proposition that we next test empirically. We do the empirical validation in five steps:

1. We start with a sample of firms that have been classified into the portal and search category by Wall Street Research Net [14] and ones that are primarily advertisement-supported. This sample is the same as the one used for Dewan et al. [5]. We obtain the estimates of the asymptotic traffic, $u _ { \alpha }$ and the growth rate, r, from Dewan and Freimer [5, table IV]. This estimate is based on audi ence data from February 1999 to April 2001.<sup>6</sup>

2. We use the estimates of $u _ { \infty }$ and $r ,$ along with an appropriate discount rate, to compute the DTT. This is shown in Table 2.

3. For each firm, we use the first earnings announcement on or after May 1, 2001, to get income and balance sheet data. We get the market capitalization for the first business day after the day of the announcement.

4. Get the Web traffic numbers for the month just before the month of the announcement.

5. Examine the association between DTT and market capitalization of the firms and compare it to that with other Web traffic measures.

## Conclusions

A NEW WEB TRAFFIC MEASURE, discounted total traffic, is defined, which is forwardlooking and is significant in explaining the market valuation of a class of e-commerce firms that have been notoriously difficult to valuate. Just as investors in a firm are forward-looking, trying to estimate the future profit potential of the firm, this new measure of traffic is a discounted total of all future traffic. In this sense, it is a better measure of Web traffic than the number of page views or unique visitors in the previous period.

Another reassuring aspect of DTT as a Web traffic measure is that it is a link between the operational decisions of Web site managers, the growth rate of number of visitors to a Web site, and the market capitalization of the firms. This has important implications for the manager of the Web site and investors in evaluating Web site performance.
