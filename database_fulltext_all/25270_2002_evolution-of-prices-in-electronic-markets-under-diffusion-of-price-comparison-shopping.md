# Evolution of Prices in Electronic Markets Under Diffusion of Price-Comparison Shopping

CENK KOCAS

CENK KOCAS is an Assistant Professor of Marketing at the Department of Marketing and Supply Chain Management with a joint appointment with the Information Systems Group at the Eli Broad School of Management of Michigan State University. His research interests focus on stochastic service systems and application of theory from economics, marketing, and operations research in e-commerce and information systems research. He obtained a B.S. in Industrial Engineering and an M.B.A. from Bogazici University, Istanbul, Turkey, and a Ph.D. in Management from the Krannert Graduate School of Management, Purdue University, Indiana. His research was presented at conferences such as the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences and INFORMS.

ABSTRACT: Price-comparison engines allow customers to compare product offerings of online sellers and reveal almost complete information on the alternatives, and hence create erosion in store loyalty. Consequently, the competitive dynamics of online sales are affected in markets where price-comparison shopping is diffusing rapidly. We develop a dynamic competitive pricing model that deals with an asymmetric duopolistic market where the segment sizes are determined through a diffusion process. Our diffusion-of-innovations approach allows us to dynamically capture the proportion of informed and uninformed customers in a homogenous goods market. We use this model to analyze how strategic profit-maximization behavior evolves over time. This analysis shows that the increasing numbers of price-comparison shoppers pull prices down, and the rate at which prices decrease is shaped by the diffusion curve and brand preference. Our analysis shows that stores with loyal customers, or with a pref erence for their brands, can attain higher profits further into the diffusion process. The direct implication is that firms should use their information technology, operations, and marketing capabilities to create, enhance, and cultivate stronger preferences for, and loyalty to, their brand names to survive the inevitable information-rich markets of tomorrow.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: diffusion of innovations, Internet economics, price-comparison engines, price-comparison shopping, price competition.

The explosive growth of the Internet promises a new age of perfectly competi tive market. With perfect information about prices and products at their fingertips, consumers can quickly and easily find the best deals. In this brave new world, retailers’ profit margins will be competed away, as they are all forced to price at cost. [8]

ONE OF THE GREATEST PROMISES of the Internet has been the radical cost reduction of obtaining multiple retailers’ price and product information through Internet tools such as price-comparison sites. Price-comparison sites allow customers to compare price offerings from multiple retailers for any product. Results are listed on a single page, with links direct to the respective stores’ product. Price-comparison shopping has become a simple matter of just a few clicks. Hence, Internet efficiency dictates that the location of the seller is irrelevant and the customer has the power. Armed with price and product-offering information of virtually all available sellers, the customer effortlessly chooses where to shop. This easily collected, processed, and disseminated information is predicted to profoundly change the efficiency and dynamics of markets [2, 9].

Although an efficient market, at least in terms of price transparency, has been the promise of the Internet, recent years have also proved that it does not happen overnight. Online prices have not come down even close to expectations, nor have online seller margins decreased on these sales. Whereas “Will it ever happen?” remains another question we need to answer, we show in this paper how it will happen—if it ever does.

Price-comparison tools, such as pricing agents or price robots, have been at the heart of unleashing price wars. While these tools are readily available and easily accessible, we believe their sluggish adoption has slowed the market transformation expected when price information is widely available and commonly used. An important reason is that customers are not in the habit of using price-comparison tools in regular commerce. There has been no similar way of comparing prices in traditional markets. As stated in the Wall Street Journal [17], shoppers find shopping bots too unfamiliar because they are a difficult concept to explain and there is no offline equiva lent. It is a straightforward observation to say that it will take time for customers to embrace online price-comparison tools.

In this paper, we step back from the predictions of an efficient market where price information is complete. Instead, we start with an inefficient market where customers are uninformed about prices, and price comparison is nonexistent. That is, we assume initially that the market is completely composed of loyal customers. However, we allow the diffusion of price-comparison tools, which convert uninformed customers into informed customers, whom we refer to as switchers. Switchers buy from the lower-priced firm when they have homogenous store preferences. If a switcher buys from a higher-priced firm for price differences less than a certain threshold, we refer to such markets as markets with heterogeneous brand preferences. The research questions we address include: How does price-comparison behavior diffuse? How do prices behave as price-comparison behavior diffuses? How will price dispersion change as more people start using price-comparison tools? and What are the differences between markets with heterogeneous versus homogenous preferences for online sellers as price comparison diffuses?

To answer these questions, we developed a dynamic competitive pricing model. Our model deals with an asymmetric duopolistic market where the segment sizes are determined dynamically as the result of a diffusion process. Our diffusion-of-innovations approach allows us to dynamically capture the proportion of informed and unin formed customers in a homogenous goods market. We use this model to analyze how strategic profit-maximization behavior evolves over time. Therefore, it prescribes the behavior of prices and price characteristics, such as price ranges and variances over time, which is the main contribution of this paper.

We calibrate our model’s diffusion-of-innovations component with an estimation of diffusion parameters based on visitor data to some of the major price-comparison sites. The estimated diffusion parameters are then used to define the competitive environment in which firms behave strategically to capture market segments. In dynamically determining the sizes of these market segments, we assume that the proportion of price-comparison shoppers determines the proportion of informed customers. We assume that any customer is loyal to one firm until the adoption of pricecomparison shopping, and, after adoption, that customer remains a switcher.

We recognize that in real life, it is possible for a customer to use price-comparison tools, yet still retain firm loyalty to a degree when that firm charges higher prices. This type of loyalty, we believe, can be captured with heterogeneity in preferences, because it is not as strong as complete brand loyalty. That is, if the price difference exceeds a certain threshold, the customer can switch to the lower-priced firm. Therefore, in our research, we refer to this type of behavior as brand preference, whereas we reserve the term “loyalty”for those customers who do not use price-comparison agents.

Based on these assumptions, we observe how the diffusion of price-comparison shopping affects price levels and price dispersion. We let prices be determined endog enously in each period to maximize profits given the demand composition in terms of the numbers of switchers and loyal customers. In this analysis we track the lowest price, the highest price, the average price, and dispersion measures such as range of prices and variance in prices. Since our model provides market analysis as the proportion of the switchers approaches 100 percent, the equilibrium market conditions with any percentage of switchers are also readily available for markets in which a certain proportion of the population remain loyal.

We believe that managers of all firm functions need to understand the forces that shape these market prices so they can employ smart strategies and effectively cope. This paper provides a more accurate picture of the electronic markets under increasing information-sharing and different preference structures, and unmistakably leads to deductions on the importance of loyalty-enhancingprograms that sellers can use in online markets. For chief information officers, this paper emphasizes the need for more effective customer interfaces and data mining, and enhanced knowledge management and customization capabilities that will enhance customer loyalty. For marketing managers, this paper demonstrates that, in online markets, a strong brand name that signals trust, convenience, and service can foster loyalty, which is the only way to survive in the inescapable information-rich markets of tomorrow.

This paper also demonstrates the path on which markets will travel with more informed customers, as well as how the proportion of uninformed customers will help companies attain higher margins despite the adoption of price-comparison agents. The negative externality uninformed customers create in terms of higher prices can sustain higher margins despite widespread comparison shopping. In fact, it is this negative externality that increases the importance of loyal customers and a strong brand preference.

## Literature Review

THE LITERATURE ON ELECTRONIC COMMERCE is replete with observations on price levels and price dispersions on the Internet. There are studies that report higher prices on the Internet than corresponding prices in conventional markets [1, 10], and there are studies that report lower prices on the Internet [5]. On the price dispersion front, a similar picture emerges: whereas some studies draw attention to substantial price dispersion in electronic markets [4, 7], others find that online price dispersion levels are similar to those observed in traditional markets [5]. Also, where social economies and their equilibrium characteristics are researched, economists have examined the effects of information and search behavior on prices and price dispersions [6, 15].

Diffusion models that capture the level of innovation spread among a given set of prospective adopters over time are widely accepted and applied in marketing literature. For an extensive survey, see Mahajan et al. [11]. Rogers’s [14] diffusion of innovations theory states that adoption of a certain innovation is influenced by five characteristics: relative advantage, complexity, compatibility, trialability, and observability. In light of this theory, the compatibility of established behavior may possibly play an important role in the adoption of shopping bots, since price-comparison tools do not have an offline equivalent. Similarly, the complexity of the innovation plays an important role in the adoption process, possibly due to shoppers finding shopping bots unfamiliar.

The Bass [3] model, which is the first and most robust model, assumes a constant population size for the prospective adopters, M. If the cumulative number of adopters at time t is represented by S(t), then the number of consumers who adopt at time t, f(t), is given by

$$
\phi (t) = b (M - S (t)) + \frac {q}{M} S (t) (M - S (t)).\tag{1}
$$

Bass [3] refers to b as the “coefficient of innovation,” and the first term in Equation (1) represents adoptions by buyers whose adoption timing is not influenced by the number of people who have already adopted. The constant q, named as the “coefficient of imitation,” is part of the second term in Equation (1), representing adoption by buyers who are influenced by the number of previous adopters.

As mentioned, the model we develop in this paper is a dynamic competitive pricing model. We built this dynamic price competition model to be as simple, and as robust, as possible. Because of its simplicity, cross-sections of the model are also the most straight forward representations of the underlying phenomenon they capture. For example, the diffusion component is based on the simplest and most robust model accepted in the marketing literature by design, which is due to Bass [3]. Similarly, the static pricecompetition component of our dynamic model is the simplest representation of asym metric price competition. This formulation is similar to the model used by Narasimhan [13]. Whereas these two mathematical formulations are similar, the analysis they are used for are entirely different. Narasimhan [13] builds his model to capture promotional competitive strategies focusing on two key comparative statistics—depth of discounts and frequency of deals. He analyzes the cases when either the firm with the larger loyal segment size or the firm with the smaller loyal segment size promotes more frequently, offers larger discounts, or offers the largest possible discount. The deriva tion and use of industry profits, average prices, dispersions in prices, and price variances are unique to our model.

## Model

OUR MODEL DEALS WITH ASYMMETRIC DUOPOLIES. We assume a duopolistic market for a homogenous good. In order to examine firm pricing strategies as they react to the increasing number of price-comparison shoppers, we analyze a series of pricesetting games where the segment sizes are dynamically determined as the result of a diffusion process. For the sake of simplicity, we assume that opponents do not take into account previous or future periods when playing the game.

To capture the diffusion of price-comparison shopping we employ the most widely accepted, simplest, and most robust diffusion model that is due to Bass [3]. We assume that price comparison is a new service, the adoption of which increases over time, affecting the proportion of price-comparison shoppers. We refer to each future period as period t, where $t = 1 , 2 , 3 , . . . , T$ . We also assume that in each period, a new subset of the total buyer population enters the market, and each member of this subset purchases one unit of the homogenous product. Without loss of generality, we set the total number of potential customers, M equal to the total number of periods custom ers enter the market, T. Therefore, the number of customers entering the market per period is normalized to one. In each period, t, any customer belongs to one, and only one, of these groups: those that are loyal to Firm 1, $N _ { 1 t } ;$ ; those that are loyal to Firm 2, $N _ { 2 t } ;$ ; and Switchers, $S _ { t } .$ . By definition, it follows that $N _ { 1 t } + N _ { 2 t } + S _ { t } = M$ . Without loss of generality, we assume that $N _ { 1 t }$ is greater than $N _ { 2 t }$

Naturally, with the diffusion of price-comparison shopping, firms lose customers from their loyal segments; some loyal customers begin comparing prices, which makes us categorize those customers as switchers. We assume that the number of lost loya customers is proportional to the sizes of the loyal customer segments, hence $N _ { 1 t } / N _ { 2 t }$ remains constant for all t. Therefore, the cumulative number of switchers, $S _ { t } ,$ increases by f(t) each period, whereas the total number of loyal customers decreases in the same amount as was presented by Equation (1) throughout the diffusion process.

Each period a group of size one enters the market out of the $N _ { 1 t } + N _ { 2 t } + S _ { t } = M$ customers in the potential customer pool. We denote those customers who enter the market in period t and loyal to Firm 1 and Firm 2 as $n _ { 1 t }$ and $n _ { 2 t } ,$ , respectively. Those customers who enter the market and are not loyal to any firm are denoted as $s _ { t }$ . We assume that these segment sizes are directly proportional to their parent populations; that is, $N _ { 1 t } / S _ { t }$ is equal to $n _ { 1 t } / s _ { t }$ for all $t ,$ and the same is also true for $N _ { 1 t } / N _ { 2 t }$ and $N _ { 2 t } / S _ { t }$ Since $n _ { 1 t } , n _ { 2 t }$ , and $s _ { t }$ are proportional to $N _ { t } , N _ { 2 } .$ , and $S _ { t }$ and $N _ { 1 t } + N _ { 2 t } + S _ { t } = M$ , one can easily verify that $n _ { 1 t } + n _ { 2 t } + s _ { t }$ equals one. Hence, $n _ { 1 t } , \ n _ { 2 t } ,$ and $s _ { t }$ also represent the proportion of customers that are loyal to Firm 1, loyal to Firm 2, and switchers in period t, respectively. These customers buy from either firm only if the given price is less than their reservation price, $r ,$ which we assume to be homogenous over the population. Whereas the sizes of the segments and the reservation price r are common knowledge, firms cannot price discriminate since they cannot identify which customer belongs to which segment. Both firms face cost functions with fixed and marginal costs assumed to be zero without any loss of generality. As in Narasimhan [13], we analyze duopolies under two conditions: (1) homogenous preferences where switchers are indifferent; and price-comparison shoppers buy from the lower-priced firm; and (2) heterogeneous preferences where switchers are not indifferent, and for price differences less than a threshold, price comparison shoppers prefer to buy from the firm with the larger loyal segment.

We analyze both homogenous and heterogeneous preferences for online sellers. The online markets for books, CDs, DVDs, and consumer electronics are good examples. For example, in the online market for books, Amazon.com has an estimated 75 percent market share, whereas Barnes and Noble and Borders each have market shares of around 6 percent. There are also many less well-known players, each with market shares of less than 1 percent. The online market for books displays characteristics such as asymmetric loyal segment sizes and possibly heterogeneity in brand preference for larger firms. Heterogeneity is observable when a customer using a price-comparison agent buys from a higher-priced firm just because that customer prefers that online retailer.

## Homogenous Preferences: Switchers Are Indifferent

In this market structure, switchers buy from the lower-priced firm. That is, they have no preference for either firm with similar prices. We assume that in the event of a tie in prices, the firms in the market serve switchers equally. The profit functions of the firms in period t are given by

$$
\Pi_ {i t} \left(p _ {i t}, p _ {j t}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} p _ {i t} \left(n _ {i t} + s _ {t}\right) & \text { for } \quad p _ {i t} <   p _ {j t} \\ p _ {i t} \left(n _ {i t} + s _ {t} / 2\right) & \text { for } \quad p _ {i t} = p _ {j t}, \\ p _ {i t} n _ {i t} & \text { for } \quad p _ {i t} > p _ {j t} \end{array} \right.\tag{2}
$$

where $p _ { i }$ represents the price quoted by Firm i.

It is straightforward to observe that in this static pricing game, there is no pure strategy Nash equilibrium. Any fixed price chosen with a probability equaling one will be undercut, allowing the undercutting firm to increase its profit by selling to the comparison-shopper segment. However, the lowest prices are not pure strategy choices because any firm can increase its price to the reservation price and increase its profit. Also, as in Narisimhan [13], a mixed-strategy equilibrium exists. In this mixed-strat egy equilibrium, Firm 1 randomizes its price in period t with respect to the probabil ity density function:

$$
f _ {1 t} (p) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & p <   \frac {n _ {1 t} r}{n _ {1 t} + s _ {t}} \\ \frac {(1 - n _ {1 t})   n _ {1 t} r}{(1 - n _ {2 t})   p ^ {2} s _ {t}} & \frac {n _ {1 t} r}{n _ {1 t} + s _ {t}} \leq p \leq r \\ \frac {n _ {1 t} - n _ {2 t}}{n _ {1 t} + s _ {t}} & p = r \\ 0 & p > r \end{array} \right..\tag{3}
$$

Firm 2 quotes prices in period t from the probability density function given by

$$
f _ {2 t} (p) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & p <   \frac {n _ {1 t} r}{n _ {1 t} + s _ {t}} \\ \frac {n _ {1 t} r}{p ^ {2} s _ {t}} & \frac {n _ {1 t} r}{n _ {1 t} + s _ {t}} \leq p \leq r \\ 0 & p > r \end{array} . \right.\tag{4}
$$

These random prices have been suggested to reflect the observed prices we encounter in homogenous goods markets, from the normal price to discounted prices. The resulting profit functions are $\pi _ { 1 t } = n _ { 1 t } r$ and $\pi _ { 2 t } = ( n _ { 2 t } + s _ { t } ) ( n _ { 1 t } r / ( n _ { 1 t } + s _ { t } ) )$ ). Note that $p _ { i t } =$ $( n _ { i t } r ) / ( n _ { i t } + s _ { t } )$ is the lowest price that Firm i will charge, and in this game, the lowest price is determined by $\mu _ { 1 t } .$ . Under this asymmetric duopoly, we can see that prices are random variables chosen from the support $[ ( n _ { 1 t } r ) / ( n _ { 1 t } + s _ { t } )$ , r], and hence both average prices and price dispersions will be functions of the sizes of both the loyal and switcher segments. Since the total market size is standardized to one for each period, and we assumed zero marginal cost, average price paid in this market can be calculated by dividing the industry profit per period by one, which will yield: $P _ { \mathrm { } a \nu g , t } = ( 1 + s _ { t } ) ( n _ { 1 t } r / ( n _ { 1 t }$ $+ s _ { t } ) )$ ). We determine the price range of possible prices as the difference between the lowest and highest prices that have a positive probability of being chosen by either firm, which can be calculated as $D = r - \underline { { p } } _ { 1 t } = r - ( n _ { 1 t } r ) / ( n _ { 1 t } + s )$ ). We also calculate the variances in each of the firm’s prices as variances of the price distribution functions.

## Heterogeneous Preferences: Switchers Are Not Indifferent

This industry is similar to the previous one. The only difference is that for price differences less than a threshold, d, customers prefer to buy from Firm 1. That is, Firm 1’s brand reputation affects even switchers for small price differences.

The profit functions for Firm 1 and Firm 2 are given by

$$
\Pi_ {1 t} \left(p _ {1 t}, p _ {2 t}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c c} p _ {1 t} \left(n _ {1 t} + s _ {t}\right) & \text { for } & p _ {1 t} <   p _ {2 t} + d \\ p _ {1 t} n _ {1 t} & \text { for } & p _ {1 t} \geq p _ {2 t} + d \end{array} \right.\tag{5}
$$

$$
\Pi_ {2 t} \left(p _ {1 t}, p _ {2 t}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c c} p _ {2 t} \left(n _ {2 t} + s _ {t}\right) & \text { for } & p _ {2 t} <   p _ {1 t} - d \\ p _ {2 t} n _ {2 t} & \text { for } & p _ {2 t} \geq p _ {1 t} - d \end{array} \right..\tag{6}
$$

As in Narasimhan [13], we can show that when $d > \underline { { p } } _ { 1 t } - \underline { { p } } _ { 2 t }$ , where $\underline { { p } } _ { i t } = ( n _ { i t } r ) / ( n _ { i t } + s _ { t } )$ is the lowest price that Firm i will charge, then a mixed-strategy equilibrium exists in which Firm 1 randomizes over the range $( \underline { { { p } } } _ { 2 t } + d , r )$ and Firm 2 randomizes over the range $( \boldsymbol { \underline { { p } } } _ { 2 t } , \boldsymbol { r } - \boldsymbol { d } )$ and r. Firm 1 randomizes its price in period t with respect to the probability density function:

$$
f _ {1 t} (p) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & p <   \frac {n _ {2 t} r}{n _ {2 t} + s _ {t}} + d \\ \frac {n _ {2 t} r}{(d - p) ^ {2} s _ {t}} & \frac {n _ {2 t} r}{n _ {2 t} + s _ {t}} + d \leq p \leq r \\ \frac {d n _ {2 t}}{(r - d) s _ {t}} & p = r \\ 0 & p > r \end{array} . \right.\tag{7}
$$

Firm 2 quotes prices in period t from the probability density function given by

$$
f _ {2 t} (p) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & p <   \frac {n _ {2 t} r}{n _ {2 t} + s _ {t}} \\ \frac {d + \frac {n _ {2 t} r (n _ {1 t} + s _ {t})}{n _ {2 t} + s _ {t}}}{(d + p) ^ {2}} & \frac {n _ {2 t} r}{n _ {2 t} + s _ {t}} \leq p \leq r - d \\ \frac {(n _ {1 t} + s _ {t}) (\frac {n _ {2 t} r}{n _ {2 t} + s _ {t}} + d)}{r s _ {t}} - \frac {n _ {1 t}}{s _ {t}} & p = r \\ 0 & p > r \end{array} . \right.\tag{8}
$$

The profit Firm 1 realizes in period t will be $\pi _ { 1 t } = ( n _ { 1 t } + s _ { t } ) ( \underline { { { p _ { 2 t } } } } + d )$ ). That is, Firm 1’s profits are higher than they would be solely from its loyal segment, and Firm 2’s profits are the same as from its own loyal segment, $\pi _ { 2 t } = n _ { 2 t } r$ . Again, the average price paid in this market can be calculated by dividing the industry profit by one, which yields: $P _ { _ { a v g , t } } = ( n _ { 1 t } + s _ { t } ) ( \underline { { { p _ { 2 t } } } } + d ) + n _ { 2 t } r$ . The dispersion in prices, as well as price variances, are also calculated as they were for the case of homogenous preferences. However, note that in the heterogeneous preferences case, the lowest price available is determined by the lowest price Firm 2 is willing to offer compared to the lowest price Firm 1 is willing to offer that we use for calculating the lowest available price under homogenous preferences.

## Data

WE GATHERED DATA ON THE NUMBER OF unique users of major price-comparison sites. The leading price-comparison sites are MySimon.com, DealTime.com, PriceGrabber.com, PriceWatch.com, and PriceScan.com. Although we needed the number of unique users who visit at least one price-comparison service to examine the diffusion of price-comparison behavior, the data we had access to was the number of unique users for each price-comparison site. The problem with this type of data is that the summation of unique users across all sites leads to multiple counting of indi viduals who use more than one price-comparison site. Moreover, the greater the number of sites included in this tally, the higher the inaccuracy due to multiple counting.

Fortunately, the two leading sites, MySimon.com and DealTime.com, have a combined market share of more than 80 percent among these sites. The third largest has a market share of around 5 percent. This suggests that we can minimize the multiple counting errors by summing the unique users of only the two leading sites, while stil capturing an almost complete picture of the market. Therefore, we combined the total number of unique users from these two leading sites to form the data that we use in this analysis. We hope that the number of unaccounted-for users from other sites (the number that we fail to include in our count) approximately cancels the number of users counted more than once in our data set.

The data on the number of unique users that visit one of these two price-comparison sites are compiled from company Web sites, reports, and press releases of the market research firms Media Metrix [12] and Nielsen Net Ratings, as well as the Wall Street Journal [17]. According to the market research firms, when compared on a year-overyear traffic growth percentage in the United States, these two leading price-compari son sites also rank among the top ten gaining retail sites for the 2000 holiday shopping season. MySimon.com, which is the top-ranked comparison-shopping site in terms of traffic, page views, and time spent on a site, is ranked second, and DealTime.com is ranked fifth. These reports also support the fact that the diffusion of price-comparison shopping is continuing, and that the rate of diffusion is relatively strong.

We calculate the number of consumers who adopt at time t, f(t), as the sum of the nonnegative change in the total number of unique users of these two sites from time period t – 1. The data set of the total number of adopters at time t, S(t), is presented in Figure 1 alongside the reported number of visitors to DealTime.com and MySimon.com.

## Analysis

WE USE THE ORDINARY LEAST SQUARES (OLS) PROCEDURE, which involves estimation of the diffusion model parameters by taking the discrete analog of the differential equation formulation of the Bass [3] model; that is, Equation (1). We estimate the values of the parameters b, q, and M. We estimate the value of the coefficient of innovation parameter, b, as 0.001949, the value of the coefficient of imitation, q, as 0.1669, and the static total market size as 34.2 million customers.

One shortcoming of the OLS procedure is that it does not directly provide standard errors for estimated parameters, so we cannot assess the statistical significance of these parameters. However, we turn to other resources that provide approximations. Jupiter Research, a market research firm, estimates that there were 35 million U.S. online buyers during the 2000 holiday season. Media Metrix Online Shopping Index, which measures the total number of online shoppers, also reports that there were 35.2 million shoppers online by November 30, 2000. Both of these measures seem to support the static total market size of 34.2 million customers that our diffusion model yields. We present our estimated diffusion curve in Figure 2. Note that our data set spans from July 1999 to January 2001, the first 19 months in Figure 2, whereas the remaining are projections of the model. Also, the values of the parameters b and q compare consistently with values reported in previous meta-analysis [16], which provide b and q values of 0.03 and 0.38, respectively. The values of b and q also suggest that imitation is a much stronger influence than innovation in diffusion of price-comparison shopping.

To observe the sensitivity of the results to the relative loyal segment sizes of the two firms, we examine two cases: one in which loyal segment sizes of the firms are almost equal, and one in which Firm 1’s loyal segment size is ten times greater than that of Firm 2’s; that is, $n _ { { \scriptscriptstyle 1 t } } = 1 0 n _ { { \scriptscriptstyle 2 t } }$ . ## Heterogeneous Preferences: Switchers Are Not Indifferent

As mentioned, we believe that it is possible for a customer to use price-comparison tools, yet still remain loyal to a firm, to a degree, when that firm charges higher prices. That is, if the price difference is more than a certain threshold, the customer can switch to the lower-priced firm, but may as well prefer to buy from the higher-priced firm if the difference in prices is negligible. We refer to this type of firm preference as a heterogeneous preference.

In a market with heterogeneous preferences, as the upper panels of Figures 6 and 7 show, the prices and price ranges are almost identical to those with homogenous preferences, if we assume that the two firms have almost equal loyal segment sizes. ## Conclusion

IN THIS PAPER, WE DEVELOPED a dynamic competitive pricing model to analyze price paths as the diffusion of price-comparison site usage increases. Our model deals with an asymmetric duopolistic market where the segment sizes are determined dynami cally as the result of a diffusion process. We use this model to analyze how strategic profit-maximization behavior evolves over time. Hence, the model prescribes the behavior of prices and price characteristics, such as dispersions and variances over time, which is the main contribution of this paper.

Our model findings demonstrate the importance of loyalty and preference enhancement for online sellers. Information technology (IT) professionals and marketing managers have the tools to resist the forces of information sharing in online markets. For example, retailers may use IT to create buyer lock-in (one-click shopping, purchase history), or use marketing to create buyer preference (loyalty benefits, preferred customer status), further reducing price sensitivity in the shopper’s decision.

One of the unique contributions of this paper is the finding that average prices in the markets will have a tendency to remain close to the highest possible price for a rather long period of time, even though the number of comparison shoppers increases continuously and at an increasing rate. This happens because the firms will prefer to serve their loyal segments with higher prices rather than lowering their prices to capture price-comparison shoppers. Effectively, the largest loyal segment will create nega tive network externalities on the market that will result in higher prices. This finding partially explains why, despite the expectancy of price wars and reduced prices for homogenous goods, we still observe rather high prices online.

Another important finding of our model is that after the period of relatively high prices, if price-comparison tool adoption continues, prices will decline sharply during a relatively short period of time. During this short period, the accelerated growth of the comparison-shoppers segment will both reduce the number of the firms’ loya customers and increase their reward for serving these comparison shoppers. Therefore, assuming that customers will adopt Internet tools such as price-comparison sites in the future, the erosion of loyal segment sizes and loss of high-profit customers may arrive all too suddenly. Consequently, online retailers are likely to benefit from loy alty that is based on the value customers derive from the relationship, rather than loyalty that is due to a lack of knowledge of alternatives. Hence, implementing customer relationship management programs that enhance loyalty is essential.

An additional crucial finding of the paper is that, when a firm with a relatively large loyal segment dominates the online market, and when even the switchers prefer that firm despite marginally higher prices, then the minimum available prices will decline much faster and to a much lower level. Again consider a market for a specialty book in which only Amazon.com, for example, and a smaller retailer operate. In such a market, for example, if we assume that Amazon.com is preferred by comparison shop pers for price differences less than a certain threshold, then the lowest available price will be much lower as the no-name competitors will have to deal deeper to overcome d. Therefore, price dispersion will be higher in this market with a heterogeneous preference for Amazon.com. Similarly, the average price paid in this market will be a little lower, but the average price paid by Amazon.com’s customers will be higher. This will naturally result in higher profits for Amazon.com. Also notice that in such a market, those customers who have adopted price-comparison tools will find much lower prices and enjoy the benefits of online information sharing to the fullest degree. We believe this finding consistently explains observations made regarding the online market for books. It also helps us understand how preferable and sustainable, say, Amazon.com’s position is. Whereas having loyal customers is great, having a name that even switchers prefer makes the firm much stronger and able to withstand the changing currents of the competitive markets of tomorrow.

One final finding of our model is that diffusion of price-comparison tool usage will most likely increase the dispersion in prices. Whereas the range of possible prices will widen continuously, being heavily shaped by the diffusion curve, the variance in prices may peak, and even decline, if the adoption process continues after a certain point. However, note that although the range of possible prices just gives us an idea about the lowest possible price that can be found, the variance will be a more accurate measure because it also shows the likely prices that can be observed within that range of possible prices. Hence, as diffusion continues, more varied prices will be available. However, that variety may vanish if the diffusion continues after a certain point. Nevertheless, after this point, the less varied prices will be much closer to the margina cost, which we assume to be zero.

To conclude, we note that the findings presented in this paper are unique contributions in that they reveal the nonlinear behavior of prices and price characteristics, such as range of possible prices and variances in prices, as firms strategically react to changing market conditions and each other.

## Limitations

ONE LIMITATION OF OUR MODEL is that we ignore strategies that firms may develop for repeated games. In such multiperiod games there would be many equilibria; the equi librium that we sketch in the paper being just one of them. Whereas cooperation, learning, reputation, and punishment may be issues that shape strategies in games played repeatedly, we believe the costs of including these complicated strategies would out weigh the benefits. As stated before, our objective is to shed light on the future of prices and price dispersions in online markets as they are affected by competition in duopolies with asymmetric loyalties. Hence, we use the series of one-period simulta neous move games as a lens through which we see the price-dispersion effects of competition under asymmetry, and sketch the result of these consecutive observations.

Whereas we know that online markets for homogenous goods are served by at leas three firms most of the time, we examine a case of duopoly. We acknowledge this limitation but emphasize that in order to capture the effects of competition in an asymmetric market, a duopoly is the least complex and most robust market structure to work with.

We also assume that a firm’s pricing strategy has no impact on consumers’adoption of price-comparison tools. This may not be completely true. Given a market where high prices prevail, customers may overcome hurdles to adopt price-comparison tools. However, we ignore these aspects in this paper and hope to include such dependen cies in future research.

We also recognize some inherent complexities of price-comparison shopping. For example, visiting shop bots may not necessarily mean that they are being used effectively; that is, they are “really” adopted. Similarly, price search is usually conducted on only one item at a time. However, most homogenous good purchases involve multiple items. Prices may or may not include taxes, shipping, and handling. There are tools to support “basket price” or “final price” comparison, and we hope to incorpo rate these considerations into our future research.
