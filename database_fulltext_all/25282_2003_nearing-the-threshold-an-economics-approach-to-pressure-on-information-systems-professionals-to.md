# Nearing the Threshold: An Economics Approach to Pressure on Information Systems Professionals to Separate from Their Employer

ABSTRACT: Organizational approaches to managing information systems (IS) professionals have been making headlines as technology-intensive businesses search for ways to cope with an ever-changing economic landscape. Consequently, understanding and predicting employee quitting or separation behavior is crucial. We incorporate human capital theory from economics to form an alternative theoretical perspective for understanding IS professionals’ separation and retention. This shift allows us to focus on precursors to observed separation, rather than attitudinal precursors of intentions. We introduce three new constructs: pressure to separate, retention frontiers, and separation thresholds. These constructs provide a basis for identifying when an employee is close to leaving the firm and for a new approach to analyze the potential effectiveness of action taken by a firm to change separation behavior: pre-implementation retention intervention assessment (PRI-assessment). We illustrate the application of the new approach using data on the observed separation behavior of 661 IS professionals at a large multidivision firm.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: binomial logit model, economic analysis, information systems professionals, information technology human capital, personnel separation, workforce retention.

EMPLOYMENT OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS (IS) professionals in American industry continues to make headlines as managers shift from one challenge to another. Not long ago, the struggle was to attract and retain [18]. More recently, the trick has been to retain and motivate the best while trimming staffs during an economic downturn. Now, if recent surveys are correct, managers could be faced with another round of pressure to attract and retain. In May 2002, the Information Technology Association of America (ITAA), a trade group that follows information technology (IT) human capital and employment issues, released results of a major study revealing that hightech hiring managers estimate they will attempt to fill 1.1 million positions in the following 12 months, up 27 percent over the level measured the prior year [19]. Of this total, almost 600,000 will go unfilled due to a lack of qualified workers. Regardless of the economic climate, the ability to retain talented individuals is critical. Ed Michaels, a McKinsey & Co. director, says it this way: “Capital is accessible, and smart strategies can simply be copied. . . . For many companies today, talented people are the prime source of competitive advantage” [8, p. 108].

Despite how managers sometimes feel about it, turnover, in and of itself, is neither good nor bad. The concerns that most senior managers in IS organizations have are with unexpected departures of critical talent and their disruptive effects. As a result, solving the “IT human capital problem” is an issue that ranks very high in CIO surveys of key IT problems for their firms. Furthermore, IS and human resource policies related to the IS workforce and employee-specific interventions appear to be far less effective than is desirable. Consequently, increasing our understanding and developing the ability to predict the impact of IS workforce-related policies and retention interventions is crucial. Doing so can enable senior managers to deal with the complex “internal economics” of IS human resource management within the firm [1], and increase their likelihood of success.

In this article, we propose a new evaluative approach that is intended to reduce the level of uncertainty that managers have with respect to IT professionals. It facilitates prediction, and thus enables interventions that can head off unexpected separation behavior.

The reader who is familiar with the IS literature related to turnover will immediately notice at least two important ways in which the approach we take is different:

 First, we view the issue primarily from a human capital perspective. This approach is consistent with 40 years of empirical work in labor economics in which human capital has been the dominant force behind explanations of individual behavior [25] as individuals actively manage their investment in knowledge and abilities [40]. This body of knowledge offers an interesting complementary theoretical perspective to the behavioral and attitudinal focus that is most frequently seen in the IS literature on turnover.

 Second, we work with data on observed separations from the firm rather than intention to leave. Whereas the use of intentional data has a substantial history, we are able to execute this work using actual behavior. As a result of these two differences, the explanatory factors and the theoretical model are different from what we are accustomed to seeing in the IS and behavioral literature. We view this as a strength of this research because it offers an alternative way of thinking about the subject.

Our application of these new constructs and methods involves data on the observed separation behavior among 661 IS professionals at a large multidivision firm. We will use a binomial logit model for modeling separation decision-making because it handles the “separate or stay” binary dependent variable appropriately. We also will show that our pre-intervention assessment method provides a useful basis for estimating separation thresholds and retention frontiers and for performing intervention assessment.

## Setting the Stage: Theoretical Perspectives

MUCH OF THE TURNOVER LITERATURE FOCUSES ON identifying potential explanatory factors that correlate with turnover. Economics provides an important theoretical perspective based on the concept of human capital and related variables for explaining observed separation behavior (e.g., [42]). IS and organizational research generally provides insight into attitudes and intentions. Regardless of the perspective, the likely independent variables include various characteristics of the individual, job, employing organization, industry, and labor market.

## The Theory of Human Capital—An Economics Perspective

Becker [5] was first to lay out a formal theoretical basis for the study of human capital as an economic asset. This definition of human capital is as follows:

Definition 1: “Human capital” is the productive capacity embedded in a single individual that results from natural capability, education, training, and experience.

Consequently, investments in skills and training of workforce participants—an investment in human capital—build an asset that is owned by the individual, not the firm [6]. These investments contribute to either general human capital or firm-specific human capital. These terms are defined as follows:

Definition 2: “General human capital” can be employed in more than one firm.

Definition 3: “Firm-specific human capital” is employable in only a single firm.

General human capital includes general management capabilities, communication and writing abilities, problem-solving skills, and knowledge of industry standard software and hardware tools in IS. It is mobile without significant loss of value. Firmspecific human capital is immobile. It has no value-producing capacity outside a single employer. In the IS field, firm-specific human capital may include knowledge of a specific firm’s unique inventory control process, or the object-oriented model of a specific bank’s collateral tracking business, or a development methodology not used in other organizations.

In the economics literature, theories that explain both turnover and the absence of turnover rely upon the concept of human capital. One of the explanations for quitting behavior is that quits are the result of “shopping” for employment that maximizes job returns given an individual’s human capital [21, 29]. Another explanation focuses on the quality of the match between an individual’s human capital and requirements of the job [22], suggesting that poor matches are more likely to result in a quit. Wage ladders [39] provide an explanation for the absence of turnover, primarily among older blue-collar workers, in which workers benefit from an increasing wage scale. Whereas the accumulation of firm-specific human capital tends to reduce quits provided that employees share in benefits attributed to firm-specific human capital [38].

## The IS and Organizational Perspectives

We note three primary theoretical models to explain turnover in the IS and organizational literature. The Mobley model [30, 32] describes a progression of conditions, attitudes, and behaviors that begins with job dissatisfaction and ends with intention to quit followed by an actual quit. The Steers and Mowday model [41] begins with unmet expectations that lead to negative affective responses, intention to quit, and then actual quitting behavior. The more recent Lee and Mitchell model [27] suggests that quitting is the result of shocks (i.e., triggering events) and image violations (i.e., mismatches between the values and goals of the individual and those of the organization), either of which may also result in dissatisfaction.

The related literature reports on a vast number of potentially relevant variables. For dependent variables, Mobley [31] discusses the use of observed separation from the firm, and the extent to which intention to separate can be considered a useful proxy. With respect to independent variables, Cotton and Tuttle’s [9] meta-analysis of research within the industrial/organizational psychology literatures on employee turnover reports that almost all of 26 commonly used variables have some relationship to turnover. Many more variables appear in the underlying literatures but not with sufficient consistency to allow inclusion in the meta-analysis. Still other correlates of turnover are discussed in the IS literature (e.g., [2, 4, 16]). These literatures provide information about the direction and magnitude to which factors like these are related to individual intention to leave an employer.

Lee and Mitchell’s unfolding model [27] is relatively new and considers “push and pull theories.” Push theories are those that focus on job-related perceptions and attitudes. Pull theories focus on factors external to the employee, such as supply and demand for labor, which are typically concerns for macro-labor economists and industrial relations researchers. The unfolding model presents four decision paths along which an individual may travel from being employed to actually quitting. Lee et al. [26, 28] have examined its explanatory power. The research designs involved interviewing individuals who quit to see if the stories of their personal paths to quitting fit with one of the four paths portrayed by the model. Although the authors found a considerable fit, no individuals who actually stayed in their jobs were interviewed, so the conclusions are based on incomplete data. The work presented in our paper is compatible with the unfolding model in that the source of perceived pressure to separate may come from the shocks, image violations, or other factors identified in this model.

## Construct Development

WE NOW TURN TO A DISCUSSION OF two existing constructs, separation and turnover, and some other new constructs that we present here for the first time.

## Existing Constructs: Separation and Turnover

Our first concern is to analyze effectively the mechanics underlying actual separation from an employer. This focus on observed quits is consistent with the approach used by researchers in empirical labor economics in which separation is a binary outcome: an employee either stays or leaves in an actual event. In contrast, most of the literature in the IS field focuses on another variable: intention to separate [13, 15, 17]. Intention to separate and its correlates have been relatively more accessible to researchers than actual separation has been and intention to separate was thought to exhibit a high correlation with separation [31]. However, longitudinal studies find that individual level of intent does not predict individual level actual turnover behavior [10, 20, 24]. Clearly, there is more to understanding what is going on with separation than the theory in prior literature suggests.

In each literature, there are a variety of factors that are postulated to influence separation, turnover, and other related constructs; so many that Mobley [31] suggests that it may not be possible to test a fully specified model. With that in mind, we will emphasize a subset of factors that are typically readily available to managers and those that can be identified as actionable within a policy-making context. We use these factors primarily to facilitate illustration of a mechanism by which to understand separation behavior at two levels: at the individual level and at the firm level.

## New Constructs: Pressure to Separate and Retention Frontier

We expect to observe natural variation in the values of the separation correlates across a firm’s workforce. Similarly, we expect that different people will judge the utility (or disutility) of remaining with the firm in a somewhat different manner and will be differentially sensitive to observed separation correlates. However, as pressure and intention to separate change over their continuous values, actual separation will occur only when an employee’s utility of separation reaches some critical separation threshold.<sup>2</sup> Employees who ultimately decide to separate behave as if their decision to leave is based on their proximity to this threshold. Over time, fluctuations in the values taken by the turnover correlates will increase and decrease the intention to separate. These are increases and decreases in the utility of separation, a result of pressure to separate.

Definition 3: “Pressure to separate” is an unobservable force that, when sufficiently great, will cause an individual to leave their current employer.

Definition 4: “Separation threshold” is an unobservable measure of pressure to separate beyond which a separation is observed to occur.

Our pressure to separate concept is independent of the underlying theory of turnover. The pressure may have its origins in Mobley’s dissatisfaction, Steers and Mowday’s unmet expectations, Lee and Mitchell’s shocks or image violations, Johnson or McCall’s need to shop for jobs or occupations, or Jovanovic’s poor match between human capital and job requirements. Our contention is that some set of factors and circumstances contributes to an unseen pressure to separate. Then, when this pressure is sufficient to exceed the individual’s unobservable separation threshold, we will observe a quit.

One challenge associated with individual separation thresholds is that they are unknown until an individual separates. By then, it is too late for proactive managerial attention with respect to that employee. This is an issue with other theory and explanatory factors too. For example, we are aware of no theoretical guidance or empirical evidence that points to the amount of dissatisfaction, unmet expectations, shocks or image violations, need to shop for jobs or occupations, or poor quality of match necessary to produce a separation. However, we believe that if in the aggregate individuals exhibit some central tendency, then we should be able to estimate parameters related to the aggregate. So, the challenge is to find some way to leverage the information managers have about employees who are still with the firm, as well as the information they have about those who have already separated. The methodology presented in this paper provides a way to do that.

We suggest a retention frontier concept. It provides a way to think about separation thresholds in the aggregate because, as a frontier, it represents the boundary beyond which individuals are generally likely to separate. From the perspective of the firm, aggregation of individual separation thresholds forms what we call the retention frontier, which we define more formally as follows:

Definition 5: A “retention frontier” is a firm level measure of the central tendency of its employees’ individual separation thresholds in the aggregate.

Since we expect individual employees will have different separation thresholds, we expect the employees of a firm to exhibit a distribution of thresholds in the aggregate. The retention frontier is intended to represent this distribution. Although we expect variation, we also expect that the majority of the workforce will be consistent in terms of their response to separation correlates and separation thresholds. Therefore, the aggregation of thresholds results in what will turn out to be a useful probability density. This aggregation parallels the aggregation of separation and hiring at the individual level to obtain turnover at the firm level.

To illustrate these ideas, Figure 1 shows a scatter plot of possible pressure to separate values for employees for an IT organization with 100 employees. The issue for the manager and employer is to determine at what level of pressure the individuals are likely to separate. In practice, since individuals do not leave an organization at a single point in time, we must be flexible and estimate the threshold level over a period that is long enough to provide data on multiple quits, yet short enough that the threshold is not likely to have moved significantly. We propose that with knowledge of the retention frontier and pressure to separate, we can predict, with some degree of accuracy, whether an individual will stay with the firm or will leave.

We develop these constructs with greater mathematical formality in subsequent sections of the paper to create the basis for a new approach for evaluating retention interventions before the interventions are implemented. We call this approach preimplementation retention intervention assessment (PRI assessment). With the term “pre-implementation,” we emphasize that the intent is to assess what effect a proposed retention intervention action will have prior to the time that the intervention actually occurs. The method then becomes a useful approach for evaluating strategies for workforce management.

Figure 2 illustrates fluctuation in pressure to separate and the impact of a deliberately executed IT human resource policy action—a retention intervention—intended to influence separation behavior. The aim of the intervention is to enhance the likelihood of retention by adjusting employee utility, the economic decision-making construct that incorporates individual-level pressure to separate.

With these concepts and theory in mind, let us now more carefully consider how to model an employee’s decision to separate from the firm.

## Modeling Separation

WE NEXT DISCUSS STATISTICAL MODELS USED to study turnover and our explanatory model’s formulation.

## Suitability of Statistical Models for Research on Observed Separation

Turnover-related research in the IS literature typically employs path analysis or structural equation modeling to analyze cross-sectional survey data on attitudes and intentions (e.g., [2, 4, 16, 33]). However, researchers studying observed separation typically use other methods. This is important because the empirical model should be consistent with both the theory and the nature of the observations. For example, Morita et al. [34] present an introduction to survival analysis using data that were previously analyzed in Mowday and Lee [36]. The paper’s primary contribution is to introduce the basic elements of survival analysis to organizational researchers. This paper focuses on group differences in turnover using what the authors referred to in their earlier paper as an “analysis of variance (ANOVA)-analog” to survival analysis [35].

In another paper, Morita et al. [35] provide a comparison of results using the stratified Cox proportional hazards model, continuous covariates, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, and logistic regression. The authors make the point that OLS regression is never appropriate to use because the outcome variables are binary (e.g., one for quit and zero for stay); OLS allows fitted values outside that range and is in fact likely to produce such values. They state that logistic regression should be used when “the researcher’s goal is simply to predict the occurrence of an event like turnover, regardless of when it occurs. . . . Moreover, logistics regression analysis should be used when data concerning the timing of an event during the study are unavailable.” They also comment that survival analysis should be used “when the timing of the event is theoretically meaningful and . . . of substantive interest to the researcher” [35, pp. 1457–1458].

There are two reasons that make it appropriate to use a logit model and singleperiod data for our study. First, the conceptual model does not include time as an explanatory variable. Second, we want to leverage cross-sectional data to understand where the retention frontier is at a point in time across the relevant group(s) of employees. In sum, we conclude that our use of logistic regression for this study is appropriate. The data that we employ are amenable to binomial logit analysis and time is neither a theoretically meaningful explanatory factor nor a control variable. Since the binomial logit model is appropriate, the issue then becomes one of finding a way to estimate the separation threshold. We will demonstrate how we can obtain a sense of the average individual’s separation threshold using cross-sectional data on individuals who have left and those who are still with the firm.

## The Binomial Logit Model and Its Properties

To effectively model separation behavior, we use a variant of the binomial logit model, which is appropriate because it estimates the probability of observed separation as a binary dependent variable:

$$
P \big (S e p a r a t i o n = 1 \big) = \frac {e ^ {\alpha + X \beta + D \gamma}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha + X \beta + D \gamma}}.\tag{1}
$$

The dependent variable, P(Separation = 1), in Equation (1) is the probability of observed separation. The reader should recognize that it is also possible to state this relationship in terms of the probability that the employee remains with the firm, which is $P ( S e p a r a t i o n = 0 )$ . The independent variables include X, a vector of continuous explanatory variables, and D, a vector of discrete or binary explanatory variables. In both cases, the actual instantiation of these variables is motivated by human capital and other relevant theories of employee decision-making. In addition, a, $\beta ,$ and g are the estimated coefficients, and $e ^ { ( \bullet ) }$ indicates the exponential $( e = 2 . 7 1 2 . . . )$ raised to the () power, which implements the logit functional form.

## Functional Form

Functional form is an important consideration in this research, so our choices require some explanation so the reader can appreciate how our model will work. First, simple linear regression is inappropriate. Why? Because the estimation of a linear regression model, even though it is intuitively appealing, will result in non-normally distributed error terms, heteroskedascticity (or nonconstant error variance), and parameter estimates that will yield fitted probabilities greater than one or less than zero [23]. Sec ond, one may consider using a probit model to circumvent problems associated with the dependent variable. However, the application of a probit model is limited by its assumption of distributional normality when categorizing a specific observation as zero or one. Intuitively, we know this is probably not the case for observed workforce separations. Indeed, it is likely that the true distribution (were we able to observe one) will not be normal. Fortunately, an additive logit model allows us to overcome these limitations [11]. Moreover, since each independent variable enters as a main effect, interpretation of coefficients and the mechanics of separation will be more intuitive for managerial users.

## R<sup>2</sup> and the Deviance-c<sup>2</sup> Statistic

Despite these attractive features of the binary logit model for our application, there are countervailing disadvantages. For example, analyzing the error terms is more difficult in a logit model than it is in an ordinary least squares (OLS) model. As Neter et al. remind us, “the residuals will not be normally distributed and, indeed, their distribution under the assumption that the fitted model is correct is unknown” [37, p. 595]. Consequently, the binary logit model does not support the use of the $R ^ { 2 }$ statistic as a means to assess model fit, as is normally the case. So we ask the reader to recognize this difference, but understand that there are remedies nonetheless. One effective alternative that is frequently used with logit models is called the deviance-c<sup>2</sup> statistic. The deviance-c<sup>2</sup> statistic indicates the percent of uncertainty in the data under the null hypothesis that the model has a good fit [12]. Sufficiently large values of the statistic indicate insufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis. Furthermore, the changes in deviance-c<sup>2</sup> that result from dropping variables from the model indicate whether we can achieve the advantages of a more parsimonious model without impairing goodness of fit [37]. Thus, the reader should also recognize that in spite of the difference in analytical approach that is applied with OLS regression, we still have a strong basis for estimating and interpreting observed separation behavior.

## Concordant and Discordant Pairs Analysis

Another benefit of the binary logit model comes from its ability to analyze concordant and discordant pairs. Concordant pairs are the primary means to assess the quality of a logit model as a tool for predicting behavior. A concordant pair results when the probability predicted by the parameterized model is consistent with observed behavior. Discordant pairs result when the predicted probability implies a behavior that is the opposite of the observed behavior. Ties result when the predicted probability implies that either outcome is equally likely.

To make the idea of concordant and discordant pairs more concrete for the reader, we offer the following explanation. For some applicable data set, we can define $s _ { 1 }$ and $s _ { 2 }$ as the fitted probabilities of separation that result from the estimated logit model. We can further define $R F ^ { * }$ to be some critical probability above which the model predicts that separation will occur. Now, suppose the person associated with $s _ { 1 }$ actually separated and the person associated with $s _ { 2 }$ did not. Suppose further that $s _ { 1 } > R F ^ { * }$ and $s _ { 2 } < R F ^ { * }$ so the predicted outcomes are separation and retention, respectively. This is a concordant pair: the model’s prediction is consistent with the employee’s observed behavior. If the predicted outcomes were not consistent, this would be a discordant pair. When the predicted and observed outcomes are the same, we say the model produced a concordant result. However, when the predicted and observed outcomes are different, the prediction is discordant. Finally, when $s _ { I } = R F ^ { * }$ or $s _ { 2 } = R F ^ { * }$ a tie occurs for that observation because the model is unable to discern an appropriate prediction. As is usually the case with predictions that are based on statistical distributions of data, there is only so much power to predict outcomes.

In this way, the concordant pair analysis allows us to move from the probability of an outcome, as determined by fitting the model, toward a prediction of an outcome. This type of analysis is very useful where the outcome is a binary choice. These include: Shall I separate or stay (as in the present context)? Shall I buy or not buy (as in the marketing research context)? It is not applicable when the outcome is a matter of degrees. Such questions would include: How strong is my intent to leave? Or, how much am I willing to pay to purchase a given item?

## Analyzing Probability of Separation

Another advantage of the binary logit model is the ability it offers to predict the probability of separation. This is important in this research, because it enables close alignment of our economic utility-based theory of separation decision-making with the associated interpretation of the strength of the factors that drive such separation. As in OLS regression, the first partial derivative of an estimated logit model with respect to an independent variable indicates the change in fitted value of the dependent variable given a unit change in the independent variable’s value. Sensitivity analysis enables an analyst to understand how employees are likely to respond to interventions that are aimed at increasing their utility from remaining with the firm (e.g., increasing compensation, providing job training, offering promotion or job enrichment, etc.). If such an intervention is to have the desired effect, it must change the individual’s position relative to the separation threshold, and thus her utility for the employment relationship.

## Assessing Marginal Effects with Binomial Logit Derivatives

Thus, the derivative analysis for a binomial logit model seems to be a useful basis from which to develop an assessment method. From a usability standpoint, this is certainly the case for typical linear models and OLS regression, where sensitivity analysis and the assessment of marginal impacts are commonplace. However, when compared to OLS regression, the derivative analysis for the binomial logit model is more complicated to implement. The functional form of the logit model is such that the first partial derivative with respect to any single independent variable depends upon the “specific values” of the “other” independent variables. Thus, to obtain useful insights using this method, it is necessary to perform a derivative analysis with many different values of the other independent variables to examine the comparative statics properties of the model.

Consider the binary logit model with two independent variables, $x _ { 1 }$ and $x _ { 2 } ,$ , shown below:<sup>3</sup>

$$
P \left(S e p a r a t i o n = 1\right) = \frac {e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}.\tag{2}
$$

The first partial derivatives of the logit model, $\partial _ { x 1 } P$ and $\partial _ { x 2 } P _ { ; }$ , are shown in Equations (3) and (4):

$$
\partial_ {x _ {1}} P = - \frac {e ^ {2 \alpha + 2 \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + 2 \beta_ {2} x _ {2}} \beta_ {1}}{\left(1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}\right) ^ {2}} + \frac {e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}} \beta_ {1}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}\tag{3}
$$

and

$$
\partial_ {x _ {2}} P = - \frac {e ^ {2 \alpha + 2 \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + 2 \beta_ {2} x _ {2}} \beta_ {2}}{\left(1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}\right) ^ {2}} + \frac {e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}} \beta_ {2}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}.\tag{4}
$$

It turns out that the best we can do with these Equations (3) and (4) is level set analysis. This involves setting values for the other independent variables, and then assessing the impact for unit changes in $x _ { 1 }$ or $x _ { 2 } ,$ , one-by-one. Fortunately, there is an analysis approach that offers a powerful tool to develop insights called the “odds ratio.”

## The Odds Ratio

The odds ratio provides an appropriate and more readily interpretable alternative [37] in terms of the odds of an outcome (like separation):

Definition 6: The “odds of an outcome” are equal to the probability that the outcome will occur divided by the probability that it will not occur.

Definition 7: The “odds ratio” is the ratio of the odds given a one-unit change in a variable while all the other variables remain constant.

There are two reasons for using the odds ratio rather than using the first derivative. First, the odds ratio provides a constant and meaningful measure of a variable’s impact on the probability of an outcome in the context of a binomial logit model. Second, and more important, the ratio does not depend upon the specific values taken by other independent variables, once model parameter estimates have been obtained. (For additional details, see the Data Analysis Appendix.)

## Organizational Context and Human Capital Type

To isolate organization-level factors that may affect individual-level separation behavior, we include a set of binary variables to represent Divisions 1–4, one for each relatively autonomous part of the large, decentralized organization under study.<sup>4</sup>

The use of human capital type as an explanatory variable is one of the ways in which the human capital perspective enters our empirical work. We initially operationalized skill sets as human capital type (general or specific), a decision that was directly motivated by Becker’s theory of human capital. We mapped individuals in our study sample to human capital type based on the dominant type of human capital required for the type of work they performed. Based on our efforts with two versions of the data set that we used in this research, we found that we could increase the level of granularity to obtain information about different general human capital types: technical management, architecture, and applications.

Under the right circumstances, it may be possible to construct even more finegrained measures of the “amount” of each type of human capital that an individual holds. Potential measures that come to mind involve self-reporting of skill levels relative to others in the IS profession; peer or management assessment of relative skill levels (again, relative to others in the IS profession); or testing of IS personnel to determine skill levels. In fact, when we first began working with Diversified on this research project, the firm had already made substantial investments in an initiative to capture this type of detailed information about their workforce. Had that effort been on schedule, it might have given us finer-grained and possibly continuous measures of human capital levels. However, two and a half years after that initiative began, even the small pilot group had not completed data collection. We opted to alter the study design and leverage accessible data.

## Compensation Equity and Relationship Equity

We argue that an equitable employment relationship will provide rewards to both the employer and employee that are consistent with their contributions to the relationship. These rewards may be tangible, like physical work product and compensation, or intangible. Relationship equity describes the nature of the balance between contributions and rewards. (See Figure 3.)

Definition 8: “Relationship equity” is a measure of the balance between contributions made to and rewards received from participation in an employment relationship.

Since relationship equity is not observable, we use a proxy variable. Specifically, we take information that is related to both compensation (annual salary) and performance (performance rating from personnel reviews) to form a proxy we call compensation equity. Although proxies never capture the entirety of the related concepts, they can provide useful approximations.

Definition 9: “Compensation equity” is a proxy for relationship equity that is constructed using data about salaries and performance.

Although compensation and performance are discussed separately in various places in the literature, it is more meaningful to standardize and cluster these explanatory variables. Compensation equity implies compensation at the midpoint of the market salary range for individuals delivering midpoint performance. Similarly, in an equitable relationship, performance above or below the midpoint will result in compensation similarly above or below the midpoint. In terms of the variables that we use in this study, if actual annual salary is sufficiently lower than performance-adjusted salary, then the resulting relationship inequity will appear in the model as the binary variable, underpaid = 1, creating pressure to separate for the employee. Two other binary variables are used: overpaid represents inequity in the other direction, and equitypaid represents payment that is in line with performance. The percent salary gap measures the extent to which annual salary differs from performance-adjusted salary. Using a percentage facilitates comparison of relative differences rather than absolute differences; that is important given the range of compensation levels in an organization.<sup>5</sup> More detailed information on these variables appears in Table 1 and in the Methods Appendix. The relationship between the relationship equity constructs and their empirical proxies appears in Methods Appendix Figure 1A.

## Expanded Definitions for Econometric Estimation of Separation Probabilities

The relevant boundary to this problem is the retention frontier of Diversified’s workforce. Recall our initial definitions of a separation threshold for an individual and a retention frontier for the firm. We now define these terms more carefully relative to economic theory as follows:

Definition 10: An individual employee’s “separation threshold” is defined in terms of that person’s utility to separate from the firm: when utility to separate reaches a “critical” threshold, a person who is considering leaving the firm actually makes a decision to leave.

Definition 11: A firm’s “retention frontier” is a probability density function that is anchored on the mean value of a firm’s employees’ separation thresholds in aggregate over time.

As pressure and intention to separate change over their continuous values, actual separation will occur only when an employee’s utility of separation reaches some critical threshold. Although the separation threshold cannot be observed in practice, we argue that employees who separate from the firm behave as if their decision to leave is based on the threshold location. In practice, we would expect to observe that an employee will show evidence of having reached her separation threshold by taking an observable action: resigning from employment at the firm. If no action on the part of the employee has been observed, then the person’s separation threshold has not been reached. Although individuals will vary in the level of pressure at their separa tion threshold, it is reasonable to assert that there is some central tendency. Figure 4 presents a view of the retention frontier that aggregates individual separation thresholds, in which the frontier is clearly seen as a boundary between separation and retention for an entire workforce.

The frontier is constructed by plotting the occurrence of employees (on the y-axis) for whom the separation threshold is greater than or equal to the pressure to separate (on the x-axis). It is essentially a histogram of employees for whom the pressure to separate is less than the separation threshold at each point along the x-axis. The firm may retain any number of employees within the retention frontier (the region labeled Retention in the diagram) but cannot retain the number beyond the frontier (the region labeled Separation in the diagram). The frontier represents the outer limit of retention at each level of separation pressure.

## Estimating the Retention Frontier and Implementing the PRI Assessment

OUR GOAL IN ESTIMATING THE LOCATION OF the retention frontier is to maximize the accuracy of separation and retention predictions. Clearly, achieving 100 percent accuracy with respect to either predicted separations or predicted retentions is trivial; we merely predict that all employees will stay or that all employees will leave. On the other hand, achieving 100 percent accuracy with respect to both separations and retentions is not a reasonable goal. Instead, one must balance the relative importance of correctly predicting one type of outcome while simultaneously avoiding incorrect predictions of the other outcome.

In this section, we propose an approach for arriving at a practical estimate of the location of the retention frontier. Our approach requires use of a new metric, the preimplementation assessment metric (PRIAM).

We employ a four-step process. First, we obtain estimates of individual probabilities of separation, $s _ { i } ,$ for individuals subscripted by i. Second, we classify these individuals based on their estimated probability of separation and a range of candidate retention frontiers, $R F _ { c }$ for candidates subscripted with c. The first two steps are straightforward. Third, we evaluate model predictions in light of observed behavior. Fourth, we then select the retention frontier that best balances correct and incorrect predictions. The third and fourth steps are achieved by maximizing PRIAM, as we explain and illustrate next. (See the Methods Appendix.)

PRIAM provides a reasonable means for measuring the relative performance of candidate frontier locations, $R F _ { c } ,$ and for arriving at an estimate of the retention frontier location, $R F ^ { * }$ . The metric is:<sup>8</sup>

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { PRIAM } = \theta \left[ \frac {\text { Number   of   correct   quit   predictions }}{\text { Maximum   possible   number   of   correct   quit   predictions }} \right] \\ + (1 - \theta) \left[ \frac {\text { Number   of   correct   retention   predictions }}{\text { Maximum   possible   number   of   correct   retention   predictions }} \right]. \end{array}
$$

The ratios measure the percentage of correctly predicted separations or retentions, while q represents the relative importance of correct predictions of each type along the [0,1] interval. Note that $\theta = 1$ indicates that correct separation predictions important while correct retention predictions are not important. Also, $\theta = 0$ has the opposite interpretation and $\theta = 0 . 5$ places equal weight on each. Since the prediction ratios are bounded by [0,1] and their coefficients sum to one, the value of the metric is restricted to the [0,1] interval. This allows the intuitive interpretation of PRIAM as a measure of the predictive capability of the estimated model as an importance-weighted percentage.9

## Searching for q

For a given data set, we use numeric search to find the approximate value of $R F ^ { * }$ . The search is an iterative process of comparisons and calculations. The process begins with a comparison of fitted probabilities of separation to values drawn from the set of possible values of $R F _ { c }$ . If the fitted value exceeds the proposed value of $R F _ { c } ,$ , the prediction is separation. Otherwise, the prediction is retention. Comparing the predicted outcome with the observed outcome for each observation yields the values of the number of correct quit predictions and the number of incorrect retention predictions.Application of the balancing factor, q, yields the value of PRIAM for each value of $R F _ { c }$ . Successive iterations of this process will yield values of PRIAM that, when maximized, will give the value of $R F ^ { * }$

## Conclusions

ANALYZING SEPARATION AND RETENTION-RELATED ISSUES from the perspective of economic theory is a new approach among the many papers in the IS literature that aim to improve our understanding of how to manage a workforce of systems professionals. In this paper we present a set of constructs and a method for understanding and predicting behavior of IS professionals in employment relationships. Taken together, the pressure to separate, separation threshold, and retention frontier constructs provide the conceptual basis for the PRIAM that we illustrate using data from a large employer of IS professionals. This illustration relies on data typically available to mangers at the individual level and demonstrates a reasonably high degree of accuracy in predicting behavior.

We make the following contributions. First, we present an alternative theoretical perspective from which to model IS professionals’ decision-making relative to observed separation. This perspective incorporates human capital theory from economics. Analyzing separation and retention-related issues with economic theory is a new approach in the IS literature that aims to improve our understanding of how to man age a workforce of systems professionals. This provides a complementary theoretical perspective that goes beyond theory aimed at understanding attitudinal precursors of intentions.

Second, we propose a set of new constructs, including pressure to separate, separation threshold, and retention frontier. Pressure to separate provides a way to bridge the gap between the influence of a myriad of seen and unseen separation-inducing factors and the observed act of separation. The level of pressure may rise and fall over time as turnover-inducing factors drive the pressure up and down. One may think that there is a relationship between pressure to separate and intention to leave, however, pressure to separate is not the same as the intention nor is it an attitude. One of the principal challenges of using this metric is that we cannot directly observe the level of pressure that an individual is experiencing.

The separation threshold is the individual’s point of no return. Once pressure to separate exceeds an individual’s separation threshold, separation is observed. The threshold itself is unobservable and its level varies from individual to individual. There is some parallel between this idea of a threshold and that proposed in the unfolding model in which a shock, if large enough, will cause an individual to execute a script that results in quitting. The parallel is that something (pressure or a shock) must be big enough to cause the individual to act. However, while the unfolding model does not currently attempt to specify the magnitude of shock necessary to cause execution of an action, we show that the separation threshold provides a ready path for estimating its location (at least in the aggregate).

The retention frontier is a firm-level measure of separation thresholds in the aggregate. It is based on the idea that we would expect individual separation thresholds to exhibit some central tendency around which employees generally seem to quit. We would expect some variation in the location of the frontier from time to time and from company to company just as we expect variation among individuals.

Third, we propose the use of a new method and metric, the pre-implementation assessment metric (PRIAM), and the related procedure that leads to the classification of individuals relative to pressure to separate. This approach leverages the new concepts and provides a new way of thinking about employee separation and retention problems. It also enables us to explore ways to conceptualize how IT managers and human resource professionals may craft and test interventions intended to help the firm to retain its most important assets: highly valued members of the IS workforce. The approach also offers a specific way of identifying when an employee is near to her threshold of separation from the firm.

Fourth, we contribute new ideas for practice, by illustrating how a real organization may employ this set of concepts and procedures to influence the behavior of its own IT professionals. To facilitate and bring realism to the illustration, we use data collected from archival records in consultation with IS and human resource professionals of a sponsoring firm about hundreds of the firm’s IS employees.

Since it is a new approach, we have endeavored throughout the paper to carefully discuss choices we made with respect to the research context, the coding of variables, and the statistical methods employed. Perhaps future research will benefit from making other choices. These may include incorporating IS professionals from a variety of work settings, such as manufacturing and government; using sophisticated multiperiod models to assess the impact of time-varying covariates; or perhaps employing even finer-grained measures of human capital when these are available.

Perhaps even more importantly for practice, throughout this work, we have chosen to ensure that the focus is on factors that are actionable for senior management and those concerned with employment policy and practices within the firm. Using the theoretical and statistical elements that we propose, researchers and managers will be in a position to evaluate formally the potential behavioral impact of changes in employment policy and interventions before putting them into action.

## How Odds Ratio Analysis Works for a Binomial Logit Model

FOR THE READER INTERESTED IN THE ANALYTICAL DETAILS, we will now illustrate how the odds ratio is a straightforward means to assess the effect of a one-unit change in the value of some independent variable. Let us begin by defining $P _ { ( x 1 = a ) }$ as $P ( S e p a r a t i o n = 1 \mid x _ { 1 } = a )$ and $P _ { ( x 1 = b ) }$ as $P ( S e p a r a t i o n = 1 \mid x _ { 1 } = b )$ , where $b = a + 1$ . $$
P _ {(x _ {1} = a)} = \frac {e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} a + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} a + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}\tag{A1}
$$

$$
P _ {(x _ {1} = b)} = \frac {e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} b + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} b + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}.\tag{A2}
$$

Using the general definition of the odds ratio, $P / ( 1 - P )$ , we determine the odds ratios for $x _ { 1 }$ set to a and b, as follows in Equations (A3) and (A4):

$$
o d d s _ {(x _ {1} = a)} \frac {P _ {x _ {1}}}{1 - P _ {x _ {1}}} = e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} a + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}} / \left(\left(1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} a + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}\right) \left(\frac {1 - e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} a + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} a + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}\right)\right)\tag{A3}
$$

$$
o d d s _ {(x _ {1} = b)} \frac {P _ {x _ {1}}}{1 - P _ {x _ {1}}} = e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} b + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}} / \left(\left(1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} b + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}\right) \left(\frac {1 - e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} b + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}{1 + e ^ {\alpha + \beta_ {1} b + \beta_ {2} x _ {2}}}\right)\right).\tag{A4}
$$

## A Four-Step Pre-Implementation Assessment for the Retention Frontier

THIS METHOD IS INTENDED TO PROVIDE maximum predictive capability for future “stay” and “separate” decisions that are made by an organization’s employees. There are four steps:

1. Obtain estimated probabilities of separation, $s _ { i ^ { \prime } }$ for each member of the IS staff by fitting the estimated binomial model of separation behavior with observed values of the independent variables.

2. Establish a pool of candidate retention frontiers and their associated predictions. This is an iterative process. First, select a probability that will be used to categorize individuals as either those who will separate or those who will be retained. The selected probability becomes a candidate value to indicate the retention frontier location, $R F _ { c }$ . Second, categorize individuals whose $s _ { i }$ values exceed $R F _ { c }$ as those who will separate and the rest as those who will be retained. Repeat this step over a range of probabilities to establish the pool of candidate retention frontiers.

3. Evaluate performance of the retention frontier candidate values, $R F _ { c }$ , with respect to observed separation and retention.

4. Select the retention frontier candidate value, $R F ^ { * }$ , that achieves the best balance between correct and incorrect predictions, q, by finding the value that maximizes PRIAM.
