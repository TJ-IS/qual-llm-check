# Information Technology Investments and Firms’ Performance—A Duopoly Perspective

JING “JIM” QUAN, QING HU, AND PAUL J. HART

ABSTRACT: Over the past two decades, numerous empirical studies have been conducted on the contribution of information technology (IT) to productivity and other measures of firm performance. However, few theoretical studies have attempted to explain the contingencies under which IT investments may or may not be valuable to a firm in a competitive market. This research proposes a duopoly competition model to study the impacts of IT investments on firm performance and productivity. We show that the extent to which a profit-maximizing firm benefits from IT investments is a function of, among other things, market sensitivities to the price and quality of the products and services offered by the firm and its competitor. We demonstrate that, under duopolistic competition, the effects of IT investments are not as deterministic as under monopolistic competition. We further show that the effect of IT investments on productivity, in a duopoly market, are contingent on market sensitivities to changes in the price and quality of products and services offered by the firm and its competitor, as well as on fixed and overhead costs being sufficiently large in relation to market size—an important condition in a monopoly market. Especially, the price sensitivity has a positive effect on the impact of IT investments on productivity and quality sensitivity has a negative effect. We submit that firms are better off making efficiency-enhancing IT investments if the market in which they operate is more price sensitive than quality sensitive.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: competitive strategies, duopoly, game theory, information technology impacts, information technology investments, information technology productivity paradox.

IS THERE AN INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) PRODUCTIVITY PARADOX? There are many empirical studies that argue for (e.g., [4, 24, 26, 35]) and against (e.g. [1, 6, 11, 22, 38]) its existence. There are also studies that suggest the answer is contingent on firm characteristics and types of IT investments [16, 38, 39]. Mixed empirical results are an invitation to develop better theories. Soh and Markus [36] suggest that a useful approach would be to focus less on the question of whether IT creates value, but more on how, when, and why benefits occur or fail to do so. Thus, there is a need for more rigorous theoretical studies that analyze the impact of IT investments on productivity and other performance measures in the context of complex interactions between internal and external forces that all firms face in competitive market environments.

Two theoretical studies are particularly salient in this investigation of the role of competitive market conditions. The first, by Barua et al. [2], proposes a duopoly model to study the impact of IT investments, where one firm is more IT efficient than the other in reducing IT costs for a given level of service offered to customers. Their analysis focuses on a specific sector of the economy in which services are not priced initially (e.g., the financial services sector) and the benefits come indirectly (e.g., in the form of interest earned on consumer deposits or floats on checking accounts). The second study, by Thatcher and Oliver [37], addresses the IT productivity paradox in a more direct fashion. They suggest that “one reason for [the] mixed empirical findings is that studies have not effectively differentiated among (and often confuse) the goals of increasing production efficiency, improving product quality and increasing productivity” [37, p. 18]. They propose a closed-form analytical model to demonstrate the interactions among production efficiency, product quality, and productivity under a single-product monopolist market condition. They found that although various efficiency-enhancing IT investments have unambiguously positive effects on a range of performance measures, including revenue and profits, the impact on productivity is contingent on the firm’s fixed costs and market size.

This study extends both the work of Thatcher and Oliver [37] by introducing a duopoly market model and the work of Barua et al. [2] by including both price and product quality components. We analyze the impact of IT investments on a firm’s performance measures including revenue, profit, and productivity and draw comparisons between the outcomes under duopolistic and monopolistic market conditions. It is our belief that a duopoly is more reflective of market conditions than a monopoly and that firms compete on the basis of product price and quality in most industries. For example, AOL can attract more customers by either lowering its monthly subscription fee or enhancing its content and improving the speed of access. We also argue that the effect of IT investments on firm performance is influenced to a significant extent by the interactions between firms and market characteristics. In a monopoly market, the benefits of IT investments depend solely upon the firm’s own production efficiency. However, in a duopoly market, such benefits also depend upon how the competition reacts to the investment decision made by a given firm, because firms will respond and attempt to neutralize the competitive advantage of the competitors’ uses of IT by copying and possibly improving the technology. Further, we demonstrate that in a duopolistic market the effects of IT investments on productivity and the other performance measures of pricing power, product quality, production costs, and profits are not as deterministic as in a monopolistic market.

We introduce two types of market conditions in the proposed duopolistic model. Under one condition, price is a critical factor in customers’ purchasing decisions. The demand for one firm’s products or services should be influenced by the competitor’s pricing challenge. This market is often referred to as commodity market [10]. Under another condition, quality is a critical factor in customers’ purchasing decisions. The demand for one firm’s products or services should be influenced by the competitor’s quality challenge. This is most likely the case where firms use quality as a differentiating factor or enjoy distinct name brand recognitions. We demonstrate that the extent to which a profit-maximizing firm benefits from various efficiency-enhancing IT investments is a function of, among other things, market sensitivities to the price and quality of the products and services it offers. We show that the impact of IT investments moves in the same direction as market sensitivity to price, but moves in the opposite direction of the market sensitivity to quality. Therefore, firms are better off by making efficiency-enhancing IT investments if they operate in price-sensitive markets than if they operate in quality sensitive markets.

## Research Background

OVER THE PAST TWO DECADES, numerous empirical studies have been conducted on the relationship between investments in IT and their effect on firm performance. The results of these studies have been mixed, at best, especially with respect to the performance measured in terms of productivity. The findings of these latter studies have led to an important debate on the “IT productivity paradox.” Since Brynjolfsson [5] summarized this debate in 1993, there have been a number of studies supporting the value of IT and providing various explanations of the paradox. Yorukoglu [40] suggested that the paradox may be an artifact of the estimation procedures used, which ignore the vintage features of capital. Barua and Lee [1] reanalyzed the data set used by Loveman [24] and found that the negative results in Loveman’s study could be attributed to the deflator used in IT capital. After examining IT and productivity data of 367 large firms, Brynjolfsson and Hitt [6] concluded that the productivity paradox had disappeared by 1991, at least in the large firms studied. Dewan and Kraemer [11] supported the notion by showing that IT investments worldwide have significantly higher returns (70.6 percent) on productivity than non-IT investment using gross domestic product (GDP) and IT capital data of 17 developed countries. A comprehensive review of the IT productivity paradox is provided by Brynjolfsson and Yang [8].

Although most of the published empirical studies (e.g., [1, 3, 6, 7, 11, 17, 21, 29]) have found significant positive relationships between IT variables (e.g., capital, labor, and investment) and productivity and performance measures (e.g., labor productivity, production cost, revenue, and profitability), some studies have reported otherwise. Berndt and Morrison [4] examined the relationship between industry performance and IT capital for manufacturing industries based on the two-digit SIC codes. They found that industries with a higher proportion of high-tech capital had better economic performance. However, industry analysis demonstrated that increases in the share of high-tech capital to total capital did not improve economic performance of the firms examined. A recent study by the McKinsey Global Institute [26] concluded that the relationship between IT and productivity growth between 1995 and 2000, a period of phenomenal economic growth in the history of the United States, was murky at best. Nearly all of the post-1995 productivity growth acceleration could be explained by the performance of only six economic sectors. While the remainder of the economic sectors contributed 62 percent to the acceleration in IT investment in the United States, many of these experienced deceleration of productivity growth. As a whole, these sectors experienced almost no productivity growth during that period of time. A similar study by Gordon [16] suggested that the post-1995 productivity growth revival was narrowly based on the production and use of computers without any sign of fundamental transformation of the U.S. economy. Based on the multifactor productivity (MFP) statistics in the 1995 to 2002 period, he concluded that computer capital did not have any kind of magical or extraordinary effect—it earned the same rate of return as any other type of capital.

The debate over the IT productivity paradox will most likely continue despite the overwhelmingly support for the positive impact of IT on productivity in academic research. Many researchers have attempted to put forward explanations as to why the paradox exists. Brynjofsson summarized four likely explanations: measurement errors in input and output variables used in the different studies, the lagged effect of IT due to learning and adjustment, redistribution and dissipation of profits, and mismanagement of IT [5]. A number of studies have also discussed the issue of correlation versus causality [7, 17, 18, 20, 30]. The argument is that most of the IT impact studies have relied on regression statistics to discern the relationship between IT variables and productivity and performance variables. Since regression techniques have no inherent directional implications, a significant relationship does not automatically imply causality. Hence, a positive correlation between productivity or performance measures and IT investment measure should not automatically be taken as evidence of the IT impact on productivity or performance as, explicitly or implicitly, implied in many studies.

Given the mixed results found in empirical research, it is logical, and perhaps even more desirable, to focus on developing rigorous theories that identify where, how, and why IT creates value for firms and organizations [36]. More specifically, it is important to establish theoretical frameworks incorporating market conditions that explain the contributions IT investments make to productivity and firm performance. From this perspective, two studies are particularly salient. The first is the work of Barua et al. [2] on the strategic impacts of IT investment in a duopoly market. They propose a formal economic duopoly model in which one firm is more IT efficient than the other in terms of IT costs for a given level of service provided to customers. Their study focuses on quality competition and consumer welfare implications for simultaneous and sequential IT investments. They show that both firms in a duopoly prefer sequential to simultaneous investments, even though both have the required technology. Moreover, the IT-efficient firm has conditional leadership incentives and the IT-inefficient firm has unconditional follower-ship incentives. The conditions are the difference in IT cost structures and the degree of substitutability between the services of the two firms. Consumer-switching cost is used as a proxy for price. The model suggests that switching costs have negative impacts on both consumer welfare and total industry profits. But the negative effect on existing consumers is reduced in a dynamic market with new consumers when the IT-efficient firms exercise leadership.

In the second study, Thatcher and Oliver [37] use a closed-form analytical model to demonstrate the interactions among efficiency, quality, and productivity under a singleproduct monopolist market condition. They classified the efficiency-enhancing IT investments into three categories: those that reduce fixed and overhead costs, those that reduce product design and development costs, and those that reduce operational (manufacturing, distribution, and services, etc.) variable costs. Their analyses show that IT investments that reduced fixed and overhead costs do not affect product quality or pricing decisions, but they do increase profits and improve productivity. IT investments that reduce product design and development costs and that reduce operational variable costs improve product quality and the pricing power of the firm, but do not always improve productivity. The study concludes that the effect of IT investments on productivity depends on the relationship between a firm’s fixed costs and its market size.

Based on the framework developed in these earlier studies, we propose a closedform duopoly market model to analyze the impacts of IT investments on a number of productivity and performance measures. In this duopoly model, two firms compete in the same market for one product and interact with each other’s decisions on price and quality. By introducing a duopoly market condition model with both price and quality considerations, we hope to derive more generic and practical inferences on how IT investments can be used more effectively to the benefit of firms in a competitive market environment. We demonstrate that the extent to which a firm benefits from the various efficiency-enhancing IT investments is a function of the firm’s behavior toward the competition and the market conditions under which it operates. We also demonstrate that, under duopolistic competition, the effects of IT investments on pricing power, product quality, production costs, and profit are not as deterministic as under monopolistic competition illustrated in Thatcher and Oliver [37]. In terms of the impact on productivity, we show that in addition to the condition in a monopoly where fixed cost has to be sufficiently large in relation to market size, market sensitivities to product price and quality are also important factors in a duopoly. Price sensitivity has a positive impact on the effect of IT investments on productivity and quality sensitivity has a negative impact. We submit that firms are better off making efficiency-enhancing IT investments if the market in which they operate is price-sensitive than if the market is quality-sensitive.

## Theoretical Foundations

SINCE FIRMS INTERACT WITH EACH OTHER under competitive market conditions, such interactions must be taken into consideration when studying the impact of IT investments on firm performance and productivity. For instance, in a competitive market, a firm may invest in IT just as a response to the competitor’s investment decision, regardless of whether such an investment is in the strategic interest of the firm following, as Powell and Dent-Micallef put it, “the law of strategic necessity” [34]. The dot-com bubble in the late 1990s provides historical evidence of this kind of behavior. The benefits of IT investments made by a firm depend upon how the competition reacts to such an investment decision. The benefits would be either enhanced or reduced compared to what they would be in a monopolistic market. A good case study for this is the battle between FedEx and UPS concerning the overnight delivery service. In November 1994, FedEx was the first to use World Wide Web to offer an online package tracking service. Six months later, UPS introduced the same service. Then, in March 1996, UPS commenced its complete Web-shipping service. However, in less than a year, a similar FedEx service was introduced.

In a competitive market, a firm can adopt either of the two basic competitive strategies to gain competitive advantages: compete through cost leadership or compete by differentiation [33]. The same principle applies to a duopoly market where a firm can theoretically engage in two types of competition and responses: pricing (cost leadership) and quality (differentiation). When one firm cuts the price of its product, the other firm can either cut the price to match or keep its price intact but improve quality. When one firm improves the quality of its product, the other firm can either match the quality or keep its current quality intact but lower its price. When product quality is included as a differentiating factor, the products offered by the two firms are not perfect substitutes of each other, and the market determines the effects of one firm’s pricing or quality changes on the demand for the product of the other firm.

In a market where price is the sole critical factor in the customer’s buying decision, the demand for one firm’s products would more likely be influenced by the competition’s pricing challenge. Products in this type of market are often regarded as commodities, such as airline tickets, consumer $\mathrm { P C s , }$ and long distance telephone services. We characterize this type of market as price sensitive. On the other hand, in a market where quality is also a critical factor in addition to price in the customer’s buying decision, the demand for one firm’s product is influenced by the competition’s quality challenge. The service industry often exhibits such market behavior where service providers use quality as a differentiating factor or enjoy brand name advantages. For example, although AOL charged a higher monthly fee than most of the other Internet service providers (ISPs) in the industry, the number of its subscribers was still growing faster than others for an extended period of time, due to its brand recognition, rich content, and user-friendly interface, among other factors. Another example is the loaded mutual funds industry where quality of service is highly valued even though service fees are charged. We characterize this type of market as qualitysensitive.

Formally, two firms, i and $j ,$ in a duopoly, compete in the same market with one similar product and have the opportunity to improve the quality of their product or adjust the price of the product [31]. The quality can be defined as a numeric level that reflects a set of attributes perceived by consumers [14]. It is a composite measure of some vector of attributes, such as performance, reliability and responsiveness, convenience, variety, and customer support. Assume that firm i’s product is a substitute, but not a perfect substitute, for firm $j ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ product. The market demand for firm $i \mathrm { ^ { \circ } s }$ product is a function of its own price and quality determined by the firm and of the substitute’s price and quality determined by firm $j .$ If firm i lowers the price or improves the quality of the product, demand for its product increases. If firm j lowers the price or improves the quality of its product, because of the substitution effect, the demand for firm i’s product decreases. Specifically, by modifying the demand functions specified by Barua et al. [2] of Thatcher and Oliver [37], the demand $( D _ { i } )$ function for firm i’s product under duopoly market can be written as

$$
D _ {i} = a - b \left(P _ {i} - \lambda P _ {j}\right) + c \left(Q _ {i} - \theta Q _ {j}\right),\tag{1}
$$

where a, $b , c > 0$ are parameters. $( P _ { i } , P _ { j } )$ and $( Q _ { i } , Q _ { j } )$ are the combinations of the prices and quality levels of the products of firms i and $j ,$ respectively. λ and $\theta , \lambda < 1 , 0 < \theta$ are the cross-elasticity of substitution of price and quality, respectively.

The reason for introducing θ, λ is that consumers are not homogenous in terms of their preferences and the appearances of θ and λ “soften” the cross-impacts of competitors’ price and quality changes [2]. More specifically, λ is a factor that determines the positive substitution of an increase in $j ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ price on i’s demand. The higher λ is, the more strongly the demand for firm $i \gamma _ { \mathrm { s } }$ product is positively affected by its competitor’s price change. On the other hand, θ is a factor that determines the negative substitution of an increase in $j ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ quality on i’s demand. The higher θ is, the more strongly the demand for firm $i \mathrm { \ ' } _ { \mathrm { s } }$ product is negatively affected by its competitor’s quality improvement. The functional form (1) is a combination of the one used by McGuire and Staelin [25], in which only the price factor is considered and the one used by Barua et al. [2], in which only the quality levels are considered.

In terms of cost, the firms have several components, such as fixed and overhead cost, the product design and development cost, and the variable cost of actual manufacturing and distributing the products. Following Thatcher and Oliver’s formulation [37], the cost function for firm i at a given output level $D _ { i }$ and a specified quality level $Q _ { i }$ can be defined as

$$
C _ {i} = F _ {i} + f Q _ {i} ^ {2} + e Q _ {i} D _ {i},\tag{2}
$$

where $f , e > 0$ are parameters that characterize the firm’s production capabilities and $F _ { i }$ $> 0$ represents the amortized fixed costs associated with running the business. Similar formulas can be found in IT investment related research, such as Barua et al. [2], Gal-Or [13], and Moorthy and P’ng [28].

To improve efficiency, management can direct IT investments toward any one, or a combination, of the three components of the total costs represented by Equation (2). In this paper, we focus on the individual effect of IT investments on each component. For example, investments in office automation software and enterprise resource planning (ERP) software may help reduce the fixed and overhead cost F. Technologies such as integrated computer-aided software engineering (CASE) and computer-aided design (CAD) tools and more accurate usability testing and evaluation systems may reduce the cost component of designing and developing the product. This can be represented by a decrease in the coefficient $f .$ Programmable automation (PA), computerized numerically controlled (CNC) tool, electronic data interface (EDI), B2B, and B2C applications may reduce the variable cost of the manufacturing and distribution processes of the product. The impacts of the investment in such technologies can be characterized by a decrease in the coefficient e. It is also worthwhile to note that the efficiency-enhancing technology investments not only expand the firm’s production capabilities but also increase the firm’s ability to improve quality and pricing power as a result of the efficiency enhancement.

Next, we introduce economic efficiency measurements including revenues, profits, and productivity into our model. Before proceeding to formalize the functions for these terms, we rewrite Equation (1) as

$$
D _ {i} = a + b \lambda P _ {j} - c \theta Q _ {j} - b P _ {i} + c Q _ {i},\tag{3}
$$

or

$$
D _ {i} = A - b P _ {i} + c Q _ {i},\tag{4}
$$

where $A = a + b \lambda P _ { j } - c \Theta Q _ { j } > 0$ can be defined as the market size for firm i. Notice that this market size is a function of the price and quality choices $P _ { j }$ and $Q _ { j }$ made by firm j. It follows that the revenue of firm $i , R _ { i } ,$ , can be defined as

$$
R _ {i} = P _ {i} D _ {i} = P _ {i} \left(A - b P _ {i} + c Q _ {i}\right),\tag{5}
$$

profit $\pi _ { i } \mathbf { : }$

$$
\pi_ {i} = R _ {i} - C _ {i} = (P _ {i} - e Q _ {i}) (A - b P _ {i} + c Q _ {i}) - F _ {i} - f Q _ {i} ^ {2},\tag{6}
$$

and productivity, $\rho _ { i } ,$ is defined as the ratio of revenue to the total cost:

$$
\rho_ {i} = \frac {R _ {i}}{C _ {i}}.\tag{7}
$$

Equation (7) provides a best-case scenario for observing productivity gains from IT investments, under the assumptions of instantaneous impacts of IT investments and costless technology [37]. It could be argued that ρ, as defined in Equation (7), does not meet the conventional definition of productivity (e.g., [27]) since the productivity of a firm is endogenous to its internal processes and should be independent of the external market conditions that often determine revenue. However, if we consider the market equilibrium and assume that firms operating in a market will behave rationally, that ${ \mathrm { i s } } ,$ they will not intentionally produce more than market demand, then $\rho ,$ in its current form, can be considered as a proxy of the real productivity of firms in our duopoly model.

Based on the theoretical framework established above, we can now obtain the equilibrium values for price, quality, demand, cost, revenue, profit, and productivity. Given that firms choose the appropriate levels of price and quality to maximize the profit, we take the partial derivatives of Equation (6) with respect to $P _ { i }$ and $Q _ { i } ,$ respectively, and set them to zero (the first-order condition for equilibrium). Solving the two simultaneous equations for $P _ { i }$ and $Q _ { i } .$ , we obtain the equilibrium levels of price and quality:

$$

$$

$$
Q _ {i} = \frac {A (c - b e)}{\Delta},\tag{9}
$$

where $\Delta = 4 b f - ( c - b e ) ^ { 2 } > 0$ is the determinant of the Hessian matrix for the secondorder condition. To ensure that the firm charges a positive price and chooses a positive quality level for the product, the assumption that $c >$ be has to be made [37].

$$

$$

$$

$$

Thus, in the remainder of the paper, we will focus only on the analysis of firm i. The results are equally applicable to firm j.

Solving Equations (8) through (11) for $P _ { i } , Q _ { i } , P _ { j } ,$ and $Q _ { j } .$ , we get the equilibriums for $P _ { i }$ and $Q _ { i }$ :

where

$$

and the conditions $Z > 0$ and $c - b e > 0$ have to be imposed to guarantee positive price and quality levels.

## Analyses and Implications

IN ORDER TO IMPROVE EFFICIENCY, firms can make IT investments in any one of the three elements of the total costs represented in Equation (2), namely, fixed and overhead costs, product design and development costs, and variable costs related to production, distribution, and services. However, the impacts of the IT investments are affected by the interactions between competitors and the characteristics of the market, as we have explained above. In this section, we formally analyze the effect of IT investments made to reduce each of the cost components under the duopoly market conditions using market equilibrium conditions.

## Impact of IT Investments on Profit and Revenue

First, we examine the effects of market conditions when IT investments are intended to reduce product design and development costs. The effect of such investments can be represented by a decrease in the parameter f in the cost function (2). A decrease in f would allow the firm to charge a higher price, other things being constant:

$$
\frac {\partial P _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f} = \frac {- 2 a (c - b e) (b e + c (1 - \theta))}{Z ^ {2}} <   0,\tag{19}
$$

which is the same result as in a monopoly as shown by Thatcher and Oliver [37]. However, in a duopoly market, the IT investment impact, as shown in Equation (19), is a function of the market sensitivities to the price and quality of the competitor’s product:

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} P _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f \partial \lambda} = \frac {- 4 a b (c - b e) (b e + c (1 - \theta)) (2 f + e (c - b e))}{Z ^ {3}} <   0.\tag{20}
$$

Two implications can be drawn from Equation (20). First, the increased pricing power of the firm in a monopoly market after the IT investment was made as shown in Equation (19), is further enhanced in a price-sensitive duopoly market as shown in Equation (20). Second, in a duopoly, the pricing power increases with market sensitivity to changes in the competitor’s product price. Furthermore,

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} P _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f \partial \theta} = \frac {2 a b (c - b e) [ 2 b f (2 - \lambda) + (c - b e) ((1 - \theta) c + (3 - \lambda) b e) ]}{Z ^ {3}} > 0.\tag{21}
$$

Equation (21) indicates that the increased pricing power in a monopoly as shown in Equation (19) is weakened in a quality-sensitive duopoly market. And, pricing power decreases as market sensitivity to changes in the competitor’s product quality increases.

A drop in f will encourage the firm to improve product quality, other things being constant:

$$
\frac {\partial Q _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f} = \frac {- 2 a b (c - b e) (2 - \lambda)}{Z ^ {2}} <   0.\tag{22}
$$

This result is, again, the same as in a monopoly market [37]. However, this effect of IT investments is dependent on the market sensitivities to price and quality competition in a duopoly. The increased incentives in the monopoly market after the IT investment as shown in Equation (22) was made, are strengthened in a price-sensitive duopoly market and weakened in a quality-sensitive duopoly market. In addition, the incentives increase as market sensitivity to changes in the competitor’s product price increases and decrease as the market sensitivity to changes in the competitor’s product quality increases, as demonstrated by the following partial derivatives:

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} Q _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f \partial \lambda} = \frac {- 2 a b (c - b e) [ 2 b f (2 - \lambda) + (c - b e) ((1 - \theta) c + (3 - \lambda) b e) ]}{Z ^ {3}} <   0\tag{23}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} Q _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f \partial \theta} = \frac {4 a b c (c - b e) ^ {2} (2 - \lambda)}{Z ^ {3}} > 0.\tag{24}
$$

The effect of f on market demand for the product of firm i is, however, not deterministic in the duopoly market:

$$
\frac {\partial D _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f} = \frac {- 2 a b (c - b e) ((1 - \theta) c - (1 - \lambda) b e)}{Z ^ {2}}.\tag{25}
$$

The sign of Equation (25) is ambiguous, which is different from the monopoly model where a drop in f will always increase demand, following Thatcher and Oliver [37]. We believe that this result is realistic because, in a competitive market, demand for a firm’s product depends not only on price and quality levels but also on market sensitivities. A numeric simulation clearly demonstrates this point. The results, shown in Table A1, demonstrate that when λ is small and θ is large, which represents a qualitysensitive market, the f-reducing IT investment has a small, or even negative, impact on the demand for the product of firm i. On the other hand, when λ is large and θ is small, which represents a price-sensitive market, the f-reducing IT investment has a strong positive impact on demand for the product of firm i. Moreover, the IT impact increases with market sensitivity to price and decreases with market sensitivity to quality.

In a similar fashion, the effect of market characteristics on the impact of IT investments can be analyzed. For instance, the impact of f-reducing IT investments on the revenue, total cost, and profit of firm i can be written as

$$
\frac {\partial R _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f} = - 2 a ^ {2} b \frac {e (c - b e) (2 b f (2 - \lambda) + X) + 4 f X}{Z ^ {3}}\tag{26}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial C _ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f} = - a ^ {2} \left(c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2}\right) \frac {2 b f (2 - \lambda) + X}{Z ^ {3}}\tag{27}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial \pi_ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f} = \frac {- 8 a ^ {2} b f X + (c - b e) ^ {2} (2 b f (2 - \lambda) + X)}{Z ^ {3}}.\tag{28}
$$

where $X = ( c - b e ) ( ( 1 - \Theta ) c - ( 1 - \lambda ) b e )$

The main factors that affect the impact of IT investment are market sensitivities to changes of price and quality levels of the competitor. The effectiveness of IT investments under the monopolistic assumption is strengthened if the firm’s market demand is sensitive to changes in the product price and is weakened if the market demand is sensitive to changes in the product quality. Proposition 1a. In a duopoly market, for IT investments that reduce product design and development cost, the impact on revenue and profit is not as deterministic as in a monopoly market.

## Impact of IT Investments on Firm Productivity

We now focus on the relationships among IT investments, market conditions, and firm productivity. Given the definition of productivity in Equation (18), the impact of IT investments that reduce fixed and overhead cost, F, on productivity is straightforward:

$$
\frac {\partial \rho_ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial F} = \frac {- 2 a ^ {2} b f Z ^ {2} [ 2 f + e (c - b e) ]}{[ F Z ^ {2} + a ^ {2} f (c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2}) ] ^ {2}}.\tag{36}
$$

Equation (33) shows an unconditional productivity gain from these investments, which is the same as in a monopoly market. Furthermore, the effects of market sensitivities to a competitor’s price and quality changes on the productivity gain under monopoly when F is reduced are

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} \rho_ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial F \partial \lambda} = - 4 a ^ {2} b (2 b e - c) f Z [ e (c - b e) + 2 f ] \frac {F Z ^ {2} - a ^ {2} f (c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2})}{[ F Z ^ {2} + a ^ {2} f (c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2}) ] ^ {3}}\tag{37}
$$

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} \rho_ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial F \partial \theta} = - 4 a ^ {2} b ^ {2} c f Z [ e (c - b e) + 2 f ] \frac {F Z ^ {2} - a ^ {2} f (c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2})}{[ F Z ^ {2} + a ^ {2} f (c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2}) ] ^ {3}}.\tag{38}
$$

To demonstrate the interactions among market characteristics, changes in $F ,$ and productivity, numeric simulations based on Equation (33) were conducted and the results are shown in Table C1. The results reveal that IT investments that reduce fixed and overhead costs have a positive effect on firm productivity in a duopoly market, which is the same as in a monopoly market. However, such an effect is strengthened in a price-sensitive duopoly market and weakened in a quality-sensitive duopoly market. This leads to our fourth set of propositions:

Proposition 4a. In a duopoly market, as in a monopoly market, IT investments that reduce fixed and overhead costs have a positive impact on firm productivity, other factors being equal.

Proposition 4b. In a duopoly market, price sensitivity has a positive effect on the impact of IT investments that reduce fixed and overhead costs on productivity, whereas quality sensitivity has a negative effect.

The case of the impact of IT investments that reduce product design and development cost, reflected by a deduction in f in Equation (2), on productivity, is more complex. Such an impact can be defined by the following partial derivative:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial \rho_ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial f} = \\ - 2 a ^ {2} b \frac {e Z F X (2 b f (2 - \lambda) + (c - b e)) + 2 f [ 2 Z F X - a ^ {2} f (c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2}) ]}{[ Z ^ {2} F + a ^ {2} f (c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2}) ] ^ {2}} \end{array}\tag{39}
$$

recall $X = ( c - b e ) ( ( 1 - \Theta ) c - ( 1 - \lambda ) b e )$

The sign of Equation (36) is ambiguous. The conditions for a negative sign are

$$
F > \frac {a ^ {2} f (c + b e)}{\left[ 2 b f e (2 - \lambda) + (e (c - b e) + 4 f) ((1 - \theta) c - (1 - \lambda) b e) \right] Z}\tag{40}
$$

and

$$
\left(\left(1 - \theta\right) c - (1 - \lambda) b e\right) > 0.\tag{41}
$$

The impact of IT investments that reduce the production and operation cost, reflected by a reduction in e in Equation (2), on productivity, has similar characteristics, as shown in the following:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial \rho_ {i} ^ {*}}{\partial e} = \\ 2 a ^ {2} b f \frac {a ^ {2} f M + Z F \left[ (c - 2 b e) (2 b f \lambda - (c - b e) (1 - \lambda) b e) - [ 4 b f + (c - b e) c ] (1 - \theta) c ] \right.}{\left[ Z ^ {2} F + a ^ {2} f (c ^ {2} - b ^ {2} e ^ {2}) \right] ^ {2}} \end{array}\tag{42}
$$

where $M = c ( c - b e ) ^ { 2 } + 4 b ^ { 2 } e f .$

$$

$$

and

$$
(c - 2 b e) (2 b f \lambda - (c - b e) (1 - \lambda) b e) - [ 4 b f + (c - b e) c ] (1 - \theta) c <   0.\tag{44}
$$

Conditions (37) and (40) specify that, for productivity to improve as a result of IT investments that reduce the product design and development cost (reduction in $f )$ or production and operation cost (reduction in e), the firm must have a sufficiently large fixed cost (F) relative to the market size of the firm. If this is not true, then the productivity will be adversely affected by such investments. This conclusion is the same as that in the monopoly case. In a duopoly market, however, the market sensitivities are also important in determining the IT investment effects, characterized by conditions (38) and (41).

Numeric simulations based on Equations (36) and (38) are presented in Tables C2 through C5. These results partially confirm what Thatcher and Oliver [37] discovered when studying the impact of IT investments in a monopoly market. That is, IT investments that reduce product design and development cost as well as variable production and operational cost should have a positive effect on productivity subject to the condition that the firm’s fixed and overhead cost is sufficiently large in comparison to its market size. Otherwise, the effect is negative. But in a duopolistic model, market sensitivities also play a key role in determining whether productivity improves from IT investments. What we have shown here is that the effect, when positive (large F), is strengthened in a price-sensitive duopoly market environment; but it is weakened in a quality-sensitive duopoly market. The effect, when negative (small F), is weakened further in both price-sensitive and quality-sensitive duopoly markets. This leads to our final set of propositions:

Proposition 5a. In a duopoly market, unlike in a monopoly market, not only fixed and overhead costs but also market sensitivities affect the impact of IT on productivity.

Proposition 5b. In a duopoly market, if fixed and overhead costs are sufficiently large, price sensitivity has a positive effect on the impact of IT investments on productivity, whereas quality sensitivity has a negative effect.

This set of propositions may not be as easily interpreted as the others, and they may be controversial. If an IT investment reduces variable production and operations cost, why should its impact on productivity be negative? Thatcher and Oliver [37] provided some explanation by arguing that a decrease in either e or f leads to increases in both revenue and total cost. Because productivity is defined as the ratio between revenue and total cost, the impact on productivity depends on the percentage of increase in each element. When the fixed and overhead cost (F) is large, it tends to reduce the percentage of the increase in cost, thus increasing productivity, and vice versa. In a duopoly market, the price and quality sensitivities of the market simply amplify this effect, rather than diminish it.

It remains to be seen whether this contributes to the IT productivity paradox, as suggested by Thatcher and Oliver [37]. Overall, there are two main concerns with the Propositions 5a and 5b. The first is the definition of productivity. If productivity is defined in other ways, such as employee productivity, which is defined as total output per employee per hour, the result might be different. We used the definition proposed by Thatcher and Oliver [37] for consistency and, more importantly, for comparability between their monopoly model and our duopoly model. Second, there is no empirical evidence as yet to substantiate the claims of Propositions 5a and 5b. However, the proposed duopoly model comes closer to replicating existing market conditions and thus suggests an easier path for empirically testing.

## Discussion and Conclusion

IN THIS PAPER, WE EMPLOY A DUOPOLY MODEL to study the effect of competitive market forces on the contributions of IT investments to firm performance and productivity. We draw comparison between our results and those under the monopolistic assumption made by Thatcher and Oliver [37]. Under the duopolistic model, the benefits from various efficiency-enhancing IT investments are not as unambiguously deterministic as under the monopolistic model. Further, we submit that the impact of IT investments on firm performance depends on market sensitivities to changes in the competitor’s price and quality levels. We demonstrate that, in a market environment, where products and services are commodities, the contribution of IT investments is usually enhanced. On the other hand, in a market environment, where perceived quality of products and services is the decisive factor in consumer purchasing decisions, the contribution of IT investments is usually diminished.

## Managerial Implications

By establishing a quantitative model of the relationships among costs, price, quality, profits, and productivity under the equilibrium conditions in a duopoly market environment, we aim to expand the understanding of the role of IT investments in improving firm performance and productivity and come closer to reflecting the complex processes in the real world than what has been outlined in prior literature. Incorporating competition into analytical models, even at the crudest level, such as the assumptions of duopoly, enables us to analyze a much richer set of real-world scenarios than what would be possible under the restrictions of monopoly models. As a result, our findings in this study have broader practical implications for both academics and practitioners in the information systems field. We showed that, under duopolistic conditions, just investing in IT is not enough to have a significant impact on firm performance or productivity, contrary to the case in most of monopolistic situations [37]. Managers must first decide the market characteristics of their products and services and then decide where the IT investments should be directed. For instance, a discount retail chain that competes mostly on price would be much better off to invest in efficiency enhancing IT systems (such as supply-chain management software, automated warehouse and distribution systems, real-time inventory control systems, etc.) than quality enhancing IT systems (such as customer relationship management systems, demand forecasting systems, etc.). On the other hand, a brand name retailer, which competes mostly on product quality and brand image, would be much better off to invest in quality enhancing IT systems than efficiency enhancing IT systems. A recent study by Farrell et al. [12] compared the IT investment strategies among Wal-Mart, Neiman Marcus, and Saks Fifth Avenue, and provided clear support for this basic principle.

The same principle applies to firms in manufacturing, financial services, health care, and many other industry segments. In the widely cited study of valve manufacturers, Weill [39] reported that transactional IT investments aimed at enhancing operational efficiency had a strong positive effect on profitability and productivity of the firms; while informational IT investments aimed at managerial control had no such impact. Moreover, strategic IT investments aimed at market expansion had a strong negative impact on profitability and productivity. Given the commodity nature of industrial valves, these firms most likely were competing on the basis of price than quality. Our model provides a more plausible explanation to these findings than the one provided by the monopoly model [37].

## Limitations

As with many analytical models, ours inevitably has limitations. First, from the operational and managerial point of view, it is hard to quantify the economic and business meaning of some of the complicated mathematical relationships. This makes the quantitative empirical verification of relationships derived from the model somewhat difficult. However, empirical studies that test the basic principles derived from our model are quite practical, and it is our intention to do so in future studies.

Second, the model specifications could be more generalized to include oligopoly and multiple products. When there are more than two players with many products in the market interacting with each other, the model can become overly complex. Game theory may shed some light on this area of future research.

Third, to be able to derive analytical solutions and to make some of the results clear, we imposed several assumptions, including the firm’s ability to adjust quality and price simultaneously and to make IT investments for specific purposes; that is, to reduce operational cost or to improve quality. We readily admit that these are ideal conditions that may be difficult for managers to implement in the real-world environment. For instance, it usually takes time to improve quality, so studies on multi-period IT investments may be warranted. Furthermore, whereas some IT investments can be clearly classified as cost-reducing or quality-enhancing, many others may have dual or multiple effects, such as customer relationship management (CRM) and computeraided manufacturing (CAM). In an effort to derive parsimonious and analytical solutions to the model, certain degrees of generality may have been lost.

Finally, the applicability scope of our results is limited to the linear form of demand function. We adopted the linear demand function from Barua et al. [2] and Thatcher and Oliver [37] for comparability and continuity of the research stream on this important topic. This type of linear demand function has been widely used in economics, marketing, and management information systems (MIS) literature (e.g., [2, 19, 23]). Naturally, other types of nonlinear demand function forms could be investigated. However, as we demonstrate in Appendix D, some of these nonlinear forms can be transformed into linear forms. Those that cannot be transformed have a level of complexity that dramatically reduces their falsifiability, which is an important attribute that separates scientific from nonscientific statements [32]. Falsifiable statements are those that can be disproved by empirical testing. In this sense, a parsimonious model has an advantage over a complex analytical model. However, simplicity does not always provide a guarantee of truth. In this spirit, we would like to invite other researchers to empirically test our model and elaborate on our theoretical research that extends our current model into other types of market conditions.

## Appendix D. Other Type of Demand Functions

BROADLY SPEAKING, THERE ARE TWO TYPES of demand functions: additive and multiplicative. The additive function form is specified as

$$
D = a + \Sigma c _ {i} X _ {i}\tag{D1}
$$

and the multiplicative form is specified as

$$
D = a ^ {*} \Pi c _ {i} X _ {i}.\tag{D2}
$$

If logarithm is taken on the both sides of Equation (D2), it is reduced to the additive in terms of log(D) and log(X ).

It is possible that an inverse term can be added to Equation (D1), so that

$$
D = a + \Sigma c _ {i} X _ {i} + \Sigma d _ {j} / X _ {j}.\tag{D3}
$$

Based on the above and Case [9], we specify an alternative demand function as

$$
D = a - b \frac {P _ {i}}{\lambda P _ {j}} + c \frac {Q _ {i}}{\theta Q _ {j}}.\tag{D4}
$$

Following the profit-maximizing rule and solving for $P$ and $Q ,$ we have

$$
P _ {i} = \frac {q \lambda}{2 b} P _ {j} + \frac {\left(\lambda c P _ {j} + b e \theta Q _ {j}\right) \left(a \lambda \theta (b e \theta Q _ {j} - c \lambda P _ {j})\right) P _ {j} Q _ {j}}{2 b \theta Q _ {j} \left[ (c \lambda P _ {j} - b e \theta Q _ {j}) ^ {2} - 4 \lambda b f P _ {j} (\theta Q _ {j}) ^ {2} \right]}\tag{D5}
$$

$$
Q _ {i} = \frac {a \lambda \theta (b e \theta Q _ {j} - c \lambda P _ {j}) P _ {j} Q _ {j}}{(c \lambda P _ {j} - b e \theta Q _ {j}) ^ {2} - 4 \lambda b f P _ {j} (\theta Q _ {j}) ^ {2}}.\tag{D6}
$$

The falsifiability of Equations (D5) and (D6) is virtually invisible because of its mathematical complexity.
