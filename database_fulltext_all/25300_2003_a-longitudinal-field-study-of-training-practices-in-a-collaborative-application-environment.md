# A Longitudinal Field Study of Training Practices in a Collaborative Application Environment

DAVID KANG AND RADHIKA SANTHANAM

DAVID KANG is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems in the College of Business Administration, University of Cincinnati. He received a Ph.D. from University of Southern California. His research interests are in areas of IT and organizational change, organizational learning, training, and IT infrastructure management. Dr. Kang also has several years of consulting experience in the banking industry. He was the director of training and technical support for a large-scale commercial banking application using Lotus Notes.

RADHIKA SANTHANAM is a Gatton Endowed Research Professor in the Decision Sciences and Information Systems area at the University of Kentucky. Her research interests focus on issues relating to human-computer interaction, management support systems, and information system investments. Using a variety of research methods, she has investigated ways by which training and system design can improve user’s understanding and utilization of information systems. From a macro perspective, her research has proposed methods by which organizations can select and monitor their investments in information systems. Her research findings have been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Decision Support Systems, Database, Information and Organization, Information Technology and Management, European Journal of Information Systems, European Journal of Operational Research, Computer and Operations Research, Omega, Information and Management, and other journals.

ABSTRACT: Researchers have emphasized that existing training strategies must be modified in order to adequately prepare users to employ collaborative applications. We utilize findings from the vast amount of training research conducted thus far and point to some problems that might occur when existing strategies are applied to train users of collaborative applications. We test our ideas by conducting a longitudinal field study of a collaborative work flow application. As proposed in a recent knowledge-level framework, our findings indicate that training programs must not solely focus on developing users’ system proficiency skills but must also educate users about the business processes that the collaborative application will support. This additional knowledge will enable users to deal with technology-induced changes in the business processes due to the deployment of the collaborative application. Furthermore, we find that training programs should sensitize users to the interdependencies that exist among their tasks and make them aware of the collective consequences of their individual actions. We also found that users have to engage in collective problem solving efforts and continuously learn new knowledge during the process of appropriation of the collaborative application. We propose a training framework that integrates these ideas to prepare users to make effective use of collaborative applications. The proposed framework calls for trainers to be continuously engaged with users and help refine their knowledge during the process of appropriation. We suggest that theoretical foundations rooted in collective learning be adopted to guide training research in collaborative applications.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: collaborative applications, end-user computing, end-user training.

COLLABORATIVE APPLICATIONS DEVELOPED USING groupware platforms, such as Lotus Notes and Microsoft Exchange, are being implemented in organizations at a rapid pace [13, 15, 24, 54]. Because many field observations indicate that users are unable to utilize its full potential as a collaborative tool [5, 18, 20, 27, 50], researchers suggest that training programs be modified to prepare users appropriately [38, 48]. For example, Orlikowski [38] found that users of a collaborative application in a consulting organization used it primarily as a communication tool rather than as a tool to collaborate and improve business processes. In an insurance corporation, Vandenbosch and Ginzberg [48] empirically determined that even after several months of use of a collaborative application, the level of collaboration among users had not increased. Organizations can realize the full benefits of deploying collaborative applications only if training strategies are redesigned and users understand that the fundamental functionality of a collaborative application is markedly different from that of individual productivity applications, such as spreadsheets and e-mail [20, 30, 38, 40, 48]. Vandenbosch and Ginzberg explicitly state “Without training aimed specifically at developing a new framework for understanding the technology and its potential, it is unlikely that the full benefit can ever be reached” [48, p. 80, emphasis added].

Compared to the significant amount of progress achieved in developing training models for individual productivity applications, such as word processing, spreadsheet, and electronic mail (e.g., [3, 8, 17, 23, 29, 45, 51, 52, 55]), far less attention has been paid to training programs for using collaborative applications. Olfman and Mandviwalla [37] investigated different types of information provided in an instructional manual for group software. They suggest that conceptual and usage information along with step-by-step procedures to use the software be provided. Some studies have found that well-trained human facilitators enhance the successful interaction in a group system environment [2, 25, 35]. These and other research studies [27, 30, 38] provide some guidelines to facilitate the successful use of collaborative applications. However, there has not been a detailed assessment of the effectiveness of existing training strategies being used in collaborative application environments. For example, we do not have a good knowledge of the types of problems users encounter when using collaborative applications. Further, as pointed out by several researchers, if current training strategies are inadequate, what alternative strategies might be appropriate?

The objective of this study is to address these issues by longitudinally examining the effects of a Lotus Notes training program in real-time use in an organization. The results of the study indicate a need to change the focus of current training strategies on developing users’ system proficiency skills and individual problem solving abilities to also include the development of users’ business contextual knowledge and collaborative problem solving abilities. Based on these findings, we propose a new training framework and suggest alternative theoretical foundations to address training issues in collaborative applications.

## Research Framework

Hence, collaborative applications have to deal with interdependencies among users’ tasks, enhance user’s collaborative efforts, and improve the effectiveness of collective user activities. As indicated in Table 1, the development of good practices will not likely be the same for individual and collaborative applications.

As an illustration, consider the task of evaluating the creditworthiness of a client in order to approve a loan. A user of a spreadsheet might adopt a longer time period for conducting credit analysis or might modify the formula for evaluating creditworthiness and analyze the results. The individual productivity application (i.e., the spreadsheet) is a resource for effective task execution rather than a way to enforce work regulations. Hence, the user may experiment and individually decide on new ways to use the system. But, if a collaborative application is employed for this task, it will typically support an integrated set of tasks that includes credit-related data collection, data compilation, credit analysis, decisions regarding credit worthiness, and loan documentation activities. Because different users will perform these related tasks using a variety of online documents, standardization of tasks becomes necessary to ensure accountability and enforcement of business rules. Individual users may still experiment and identify new methods of using an application; however, since the targeted task requires coordination among many users, the adoption of new ways of doing work will necessitate a collective dialogue to reconcile individual decisions, collective order, and organizational enforcement of business rules.

The differences highlighted in Table 1 suggest that users of individual and collaborative applications face different issues and challenges. Researchers have not paid enough attention to these differences, and therefore training programs have not prepared users of collaborative applications adequately [38, 40, 48]. This study will draw upon the vast amount of training research conducted thus far and build upon it to develop a framework that can better prepare users of collaborative applications. The existing assumptions in training research are examined using three categories: task environment, task interdependence, and task procedures. This analysis is linked to findings from organizational learning research to understand its implications for training programs.

## Task Environment

Because existing training research and practices were primarily developed for individual productivity applications with an emphasis on individual productivity, training research has focused on identifying methods that can improve an individual user’s system proficiency skills [8, 12, 29, 36, 43, 45, 49, 52, 55]. For example, Santhanam and Sein [43] state that the goal of a training program is to help users achieve a high level of system proficiency, that is, help them develop a deep understanding of the system that enables them to complete tasks successfully. They determine that a user’s system proficiency skills can be substantially improved if the training material provides conceptual information that describe the components of the system rather than a training script that describes a step-by-step approach to completing tasks. Olfman and Mandviwalla [36] find that, for systems with graphical user interfaces, a user’s system proficiency skills are improved if training models provide both conceptual as well as procedural information. Compeau and Higgins [8] found that a behavior modeling training approach, where users learn to use a system by observing another person, was most effective in improving each user’s system proficiency skills on spreadsheet software. All these research studies proposing methods for improving system proficiency skills are clearly helpful in training users to make effective use of information systems. However, recent research by Sein et al. [44] indicates that this focus on system proficiency skills is too narrow. Effective use of newer types of business-process–oriented systems requires users to obtain a good knowledge about the business tasks to which these systems are applied [44]. Other researchers [38, 48] have also found that when users understand only the mechanics of using a collaborative application (possess system proficiency skills), rather than understanding the business tasks to which it is applied, they tend not to use the tool to share ideas and collaborate in conducting a business process. As shown in Table 1, because the pri mary function of a collaborative application is to help users collaborate and complete a business process, these research findings have significant implications. Existing training strategies that focus on system proficiency may have to be modified to additionally deliver knowledge about the task environment and business processes for which the system is utilized.

## Task Interdependence

Training research has not paid sufficient attention to educating users about interdependencies among their tasks and their effects upon using a collaborative application. Orlikowski and Gash [40] provide evidence to suggest that users have to be sensitized to task interdependencies and develop a shared common understanding of how the collaborative application is to be used. Based on a field study, Nelson et al. [34] also recommend that training content and training programs can be effective only if they address the user’s, the group’s, and the organization’s requirements. Theoretical findings from organizational learning research further support the idea of recognizing task interdependencies. In executing a single task within a business process, each individual user’s action is guided by organizational routines, that is, learned collective sequences of procedures executed by participants in a business process [6, 7, 16, 42]. Cohen and Bacadayan [7] empirically demonstrate a strong link between a user’s knowledge of how to execute an individual task and the collective sequences by which organizational routines are completed. Therefore, when organizational routines are changed (such as, by reengineering a business process with a collaborative application), individual users have to be sensitized to changes in the collective patterns of work, and to their implications on individual procedural knowledge of how to execute a task. Sein et al. [44] articulate similar ideas by emphasizing that business-process–oriented application enables transactional consistency across many organizational entities, and, therefore, users have to understand the role of other users of the tool. Training must be redesigned such that users are made aware of interdependencies among their tasks and understand how other users employ the collaborative application.

## Task Procedures

Training research assumes that task procedures are static, and that users can be fully trained on appropriate work procedures prior to real use of an application. However, research findings from organizational learning cast doubts on the validity of these assumptions, reporting that much learning takes place during work when new work practices/procedures are invented [4]. Referred to as a “situated learning” perspective, these findings suggest that users engage with other members of their “community of practice” to improvise, innovate, and continuously learn how to conduct new work practices [10, 19, 28, 53].

Research on information technology (IT) adaptation lends further support for the necessity of continuous learning by finding that a process of mutual adaptation between IT and its targeted work practices will follow the deployment of a businessprocess–oriented technology [1, 20, 39]. This adaptation process in a collaborative application is characterized by many exception-handling situations in which users institute new procedures, and develop “good practices” that neither system designers nor management anticipated [20, 39]. For example, in the customer service division of a software company, work practices had been redesigned by users so that resolution of incoming calls became the shared responsibility of a junior and a senior specialist. Prior to the implementation of the collaborative application, resolution of incoming calls had been solely the responsibility of the specialist who received the call [39]. Conceptualizing these changes as an appropriation process in adaptive structuration theory (AST), DeSanctis and Poole [14] recognize that there are structures embedded in both technology design and in group action that impacts the use of technology. The resulting usage patterns of a collaborative application will emerge in the appropriation process during which the group interweaves the two types of structures. These findings suggest that training programs must recognize that work procedures will undergo changes and prepare users accordingly.

## Initial Framework for Collaborative Application Training

Based on the above, it is evident that existing training strategies useful for preparing users for individual productivity applications may not be suitable in developing user skills for a collaborative application. Further, the lack of recognition of task interdependence issues has meant that training research relies solely on individual learning theories. For example, assimilation theory, used in several training studies [36, 43, 45], describes the process by which individuals can meaningfully learn new information. Research utilizing social cognitive theory and codiscovery models of learning also focuses on individual learning behaviors (e.g., [8, 23, 29, 55]). Training outcomes are also measured in terms of individual user competence [31].

This emphasis on the individual user suggests that training programs for collaborative applications should not be completely guided by theoretical frameworks that focus solely on individual learning and the development of system proficiency skills. The most critical problem in using such frameworks in the context of a collaborative application is their separation of learning from working and the disconnection between individual learning and collective actions. Therefore, it might be necessary to adopt different theoretical frameworks and to develop new training strategies, a sentiment echoed by several researchers [20, 40, 48]. In order to investigate and obtain empirical evidence for these ideas, we first need analytical tools to identify the outcomes of using existing training frameworks. Insights from this exercise can be used to guide the development of new frameworks.

Although not specifically developed for collaborative applications, a recently proposed knowledge-level framework [44] presented in Table 2 could serve as the tool. This framework was developed to mitigate the criticism that existing training strategies tend to focus solely on system proficiency and skill-based outcomes. As indicated in Table 2, this framework posits that a training program should help users acquire a continuum of knowledge that range from the technical to the organizational. At the elementary level, users should acquire command-based knowledge, that is, commands/keystrokes, in order to execute operations, such as formatting and spacing a paragraph in a word processing software. A user must also gain tool-procedural knowledge to be able to combine multiple commands and complete a generic task, such as editing a piece of text. A user must also know how to apply the above levels of knowledge to execute a specific business task, such as creating a credit approval letter. This level of business-related knowledge is labeled business-procedural. Furthermore, a user must acquire knowledge at the tool-conceptual level in order to understand the bigger picture of what to do with a tool. For example, an enterprise resource planning (ERP) system can function as a work flow tool. The user also needs to acquire knowledge at the business-motivational level in order to appreciate what the tool can do for his or her job and the tool’s role in the organization. It is business-motivational knowledge that enables the user to recognize the appropriate use of the tool and maintain transactional consistency in executing an organizational process. Finally, knowledge at the metacognition level enables users to transfer their knowledge to other software environments.

By mapping our discussion of training assumptions on Sein et al.’s [44] framework, it is evident that current training strategies (focused on developing individual system proficiency skills) can be expected to provide the command-based and tool-procedural knowledge levels described in the framework. These levels of knowledge deal with basic technical knowledge to use the application. Our discussion of training assumptions regarding the task environment and task procedures indicate that existing training programs will be specifically deficient in providing the business-procedural knowledge required to use a collaborative application. Training programs tend not to educate users about the task environment nor do they indicate how the system proficiency skills could be applied to support the business process. Training programs also assume that task procedures are static while organizational learning research has clearly indicated that task procedures change with the deployment of a collaborative application. Consequently, sufficient and standardized business-procedural knowledge cannot be completely provided during initial training. Therefore, when a new collaborative application is deployed, even after attending a training program, the lack of adequate business- procedural knowledge may create problems for users.

Similarly, the assumption that task interdependencies do not exist implies that training programs cannot provide adequate business-motivational knowledge to use a collaborative application. Users may not have a clear idea of the role played by other users of the system, what the appropriate use of the system must be so that it can support transactional consistency in the business process. They may lack an understanding of how their individual actions in changing task procedures may disrupt the consistency of operations maintained in the organization. The lack of such knowledge may create user conflicts and problems after training. We propose to answer these research questions relating to training outcomes:

1. After undergoing a training program, what types of problems do users encounter when using a collaborative application?

2. Are current training strategies developed for individual productivity applications adequate for developing user skills for collaborative applications?

Although these questions help to focus on specific problems faced by users, it is important to understand how users apply the knowledge acquired during training and how they resolve problems. Hence, it is important to also collect qualitative data to gain a deeper understanding of the problems faced by users.

## Research Method

## Method

TO ADDRESS THE RESEARCH QUESTIONS, it is important to analyze real-time problems over a period of time and evaluate the long-term effects of training in the workplace [9, 20, 39]. A longitudinal quasi-experimental field study by which we can analyze the outcomes of a training program in an organization would be the most appropriate research method [11]. A work flow application has become a highly researched representative genre of collaborative applications [5, 20, 39, 48]. It facilitates the sharing of information among users through an electronic space that serves as a common repository for users to post their documents [48]. It can be viewed as a document creator, an indexer, a database, and a messaging platform. It provides a rich setting to test our ideas and we therefore tested the training effects among users of a work flow system in a commercial bank.

## Research Site

The research site, Bank X, is a large commercial bank on the West Coast with an asset level exceeding \$30 billion in 1999. In making a commercial loan, bankers with different functions had to closely collaborate with each other. The primary responsibility of the portfolio managers was to generate and pursue new business leads and to maintain a good relationship with existing borrowers. To support the portfolio managers, the account executives’ job was to provide a high-quality credit analysis with the help of staff analysts and to compose loan packages that contained credit analysis and other related documents. Once a loan package was analyzed, a team of portfolio managers, account executives, and staff analysts submitted it to a credit administrator for approval.

Before the collaborative application was deployed, the bank supported the collaboration among geographically dispersed portfolio managers and centrally located staff with an infrastructure consisting of filing cabinets, interoffice mail, faxes, and file servers. Document exchange took days and there was no spontaneous access to documents in preparation, leading to delays and problems in loan approval. To address these problems, the bank deployed a collaborative work flow application, developed using Lotus Notes, which allowed users to create, store, and route major types of credit documents online. Any user could open, print, and edit any document at any time in any of the bank’s offices. The bank decided to implement an extensive training program for approximately 250 bankers to facilitate the implementation and use of this new application. One of the two researchers was allowed to observe the training program and collect data on user problems reported to the help desk.

## Training Procedures and Assumptions

In line with current training research prescriptions, the training program conducted by the bank could be categorized as “application-based” training [12]. The training script described the work flow application and the hierarchy of storing documents in folders belonging to a specific loan/customer. The training script also listed step-bystep procedures to execute tasks, such as for editing, formatting, creating, and routing a loan package. Groups of users with similar job responsibilities went through a twophase training session. The first phase was an hour-long group session during which the trainer explained the functionality of the system with the help of the training script. The second phase consisted of a 90-minute individual hands-on training session during which the trainer guided the user to execute operations on the system. The trainer concluded a training session when users could complete the assigned exercises smoothly.

## Data Collection

Observations during the three weeks prior to the training and deployment of the new work flow application indicated that lending activities were being conducted in a smooth fashion. Interviews with employees revealed that their main complaint was the difficulty of collaborating with geographically dispersed team members under the existing infrastructure.

After training was completed, management set up a help desk facility where users could obtain help in resolving problems that occurred while using the new application. When a user called the help desk for assistance, it was labeled as a help event. Information relating to the help events provided data on user problems. The researcher wrote a detailed description of the help event and how it was resolved. The data collection effort concluded after six months because the bank had completed an entire lending season. During the six months of observation, the researcher noted descriptions of 600 help events that came to the attention of the help desk. In addition, problem solving activities surrounding the issues that emerged from the help events and deemed important by users were tracked and described as help scenarios. During and after data collection, the researcher also conducted unstructured interviews with users of the system including managers, analysts, and staff.

## Data Coding and Analysis

Based on the specific usage problem described in a help event, the two authors independently coded the help events into one of the five knowledge categories in the Sein et al. [44] framework.<sup>1</sup> The coding indicated the specific level of knowledge, as described in the knowledge-level model, was deficient and hence caused the problem. For example, if the help event indicated that a user called the help desk because he or she did not know how to save a file in a folder, the event was coded as “commandbased knowledge.” The coding was based on the logic that the user could not independently resolve the problem because he or she did not have the requisite command-based level knowledge. Similarly, a help event was coded as business motivational if it indicated a problem caused by a user whose individual actions created inconsistencies in work procedures. The two authors independently coded all the help events. When the codes were compared, it was found that the reliability index was 0.95 [33]. The 5 percent of cases in which coding did not agree were reconciled through discussion. It is important to note that a few help events indicated that users lacked knowledge at more than one level; these events were therefore coded under more than one knowledge-level category. For this reason, the total number of knowledge-type codes was slightly higher than the number of help events.

## Results

THREE TYPES OF DATA, INCLUDING HELP EVENTS, help scenarios, and interviews with users were analyzed.

## Help Events

The results of the coding and analysis are shown in Table 3 as a frequency summary of help events. Figure 1 provides a pictorial representation. An analysis of the codes indicates that the maximum frequency of user problems occurred in the business-procedural knowledge category (33.72 percent), followed by problems in the business-motivational knowledge category (28.62 percent). Problems under commandbased and tool-procedural knowledge level categories were not dominant problems, as demonstrated by their relatively lower frequency of occurrence. We also found that user problems persisted during the 20-week period after training. Thus, the answer to the first research question is that the lack of business-procedural and business-motivational levels of knowledge created the most frequent problems and further, these problems persisted for some time. Qualitative descriptions, provided later, elaborate on some representative problems.

The frequency distribution of help events indicates an increase in workload and help calls in the first two weeks. Between the second and eighth week, businessprocedural, tool-conceptual, and business-motivational knowledge level problems increased together with heavier usage of the application. Another minor surge in the number of help events occurred around week 13 and 14. This was a peak period for commercial lending activities and as the application was used intensively, many novel issues relating to processing of loans emerged. After this period, the frequency of help requests resumed its generally downward trend with some fluctuations. This frequency pattern in the help events supports the situated-learning perspective because it suggests that users had more questions and seemed to learn more about the system when the work activity level was relatively high [4]. Note that the workload of a certain week reflected the number of initiated rather than finished loans during that week. Therefore, an increase in the frequency of help events sometimes lagged behind a surge of workload. Further, help events did not always indicate a repetition of the same problem. For example, some of the early problems categorized under business-procedural knowledge level were questions about who should create the different types of individual credit documents within a loan package. After these problems were resolved, other problems, such as who should organize and submit an entire loan package for approval, continued to emerge under the business-procedural knowledge level. ## Representative Help Scenarios

The analysis of help events showed that users engaged in collective problem solving efforts to resolve problems. These solutions resulted in changes in knowledge at each level and indicated the presence of interaction among them. Descriptions of representative help scenarios provide qualitative evidence for these observations.

## Scenario 1: When to Insert a Credit Document in a Loan Package?

Users were trained on how to create a credit analysis document using WordPerfect and then insert it into the work flow application as an object linked and embedded (OLE) item. Users had little difficulty in executing such an operation after training; however, when they actually had to utilize this operation in work, they soon realized that they did not have the business-procedural knowledge to decide when to put a credit analysis document into the work flow application. They could either draft such a document as a separate word processing file and later embed it into the work flow application or, on the other hand, initiate such a document within the work flow application. Some users preferred and adopted the former approach citing the difficulty of putting a “dirty” document online; however, others chose the latter strategy because it made the document available to collaborating partners as soon as possible.

In terms of Sein et al.’s [44] framework, the trainers conveyed the tool-procedural knowledge but not the business-procedural knowledge. Further, users were not aware of procedures followed by other users and the ramifications of adopting differing work procedures. Initially, users were not sensitive to the idea that they must follow standard procedures in order to maintain consistency in operations. This showed a lack of consideration of the collaborative nature of their tasks and a lack of appropriate business-motivational knowledge. These differing procedures made it very difficult for the organization to enforce standard and consistent work procedures. When users realized the consequences of individual experimentation and adoption of new procedures, they agreed to establish uniform “good practices” at the business-procedural and business-motivational knowledge level, because they had to collaborate with several partners for each loan package and often switched partners for different loan packages. After a collective discussion, users finally agreed upon the procedure of initiating credit analysis documents in the work flow application. From an organization learning perspective, as suggested by Cohen and Bacadaya [7], this example illustrates the interlocking of individual and collective actions. This event also illustrates how learning may occur during work as users collaborate and learn to execute mutually agreed upon procedures [4].

## Scenario 2: How to Share Nonstandard Document Templates?

The bank designed the work flow application to process, route, and store only 27 standard types of formatted official documents, implemented as standard forms in the Notes document database. Several weeks after the system was put to use, two account executives working in different geographic regions experimented with embedding nonstandard document templates as OLE objects in these standard forms. One user dropped a template as an OLE object in a “shell” form and the other user opened it at a remote site. With copies of the same template on their screens, the users talked about potential changes over the phone. Both users were pleased with the efficiency of this particular way of sharing templates and decided to keep this template online, for future access. Soon other users heard about this procedure of sharing templates and started to adopt the same practice of creating and storing templates. But, as described in Table 4, the proliferation of many nonstandard document templates online created problems at different levels of knowledge. It was clear that the problem could not be resolved by any individual user but had to be solved collectively. While individually experimenting with this new practice, users did not realize that the creation of nonstandard templates would result in these consequences. Similar to the previous event, this event once again revealed that users lacked a consideration for the collaborative nature of their tasks and lacked sufficient business-motivational knowledge.

As can be seen in both examples, different levels of knowledge adapted and interacted in the process of development of good practices of using the collaborative application. The example dealing with nonstandard template sharing indicates that no special facilities were designed to handle nonstandard document template sharing at the command-based and tool-procedural knowledge level because, at the tool-conceptual knowledge level, the application was conceived primarily as a work flow platform for commercial lending. The experiment of sharing nonstandard document templates sparked a debate at the tool-conceptual knowledge level on whether the new application should serve as a repository for nonstandard documents. The first question raised by sharing ad hoc document templates was where to put them in the work flow application. Standard types of online documents were organized in folders that were designed for specific purposes and no folder was designed to hold nonstandard types of documents. A nonstandard document could be put into a folder that was created for a loan package but could not stay in it since the rest of the documents in the folder were associated with a specific loan. A new type of folder— Miscellaneous—was added to the application as a temporary solution for nonstandard document template sharing. But this new solution soon appeared to be problematic since the application hierarchy enforced the rule that every folder had to belong to a borrower and nonstandard document templates did not belong to any specific borrower.

The new practice of sharing nonstandard document templates also posed challenges for users to carry out collaborative tasks. Should the sharing be regulated by individual users, teams, or departments? If it was treated as individual activity, how do individual users control the quality of the templates, avoid duplications, and “publish” them for peers? If it should be regulated at the team and/or department level, then what rules should be enforced? For example, should a description of a template be provided in the subject line of the “shell” form? Who would perform the duties of checking quality, consolidating contributions from different users, and deleting duplicates?

The practice of sharing nonstandard document templates also stirred up a debate among the users and the management about the role of the work flow application. The application could be conceived to (1) process only standard types of documents that are related to the commercial lending work flow, (2) support all types of collaboration, or (3) be used for training of new employees. While not mutually exclusive, these roles did conflict because each needed a different set of governing rules. For example, the first role favored a strict document organization hierarchy of Borrower-Folders-Documents. This hierarchy made the sharing of nonstandard document templates—an important part of the second role—difficult to implement.

While users appreciated the potential to facilitate knowledge sharing, different users had different concerns about sharing nonstandard document templates online. The managerial users worried about quality, duplication, and ease of access, and the impact of “faked” folders on managerial reporting. The senior account executives (AE) and staff analysts (SA) were concerned that the proliferation of document templates would “pollute” the document hierarchy. Since all users put templates online in “fake” packages under the borrowers they were working with, it was difficult for others to find the templates without knowing their authors and the authors’ client list. Furthermore, if a user made “improvements” to a template authored by another user, should he or she save the changed template as a replacement of the original template, as a new copy, or as a new copy with links to the original? As illustrated in Figure 2, in these and other real-world problem solving scenarios, we observed an association between the scope of user involvement and the order in which problems emerged. Problems at the lower level of knowledge surfaced first among a pair of users who wanted to test a new usage of the application. At this point, some temporary procedures to solve the problem were offered. Then, as more users adopted the new procedures, it created conflicts and inconsistencies in task procedures so that the problem escalated to a more formal level of task execution. Since task redesign could not be done at the individual user(s) or team level, it frequently required a rethinking at the organizational level about the role of the tool and the rules and policies that must govern its usage. Due to the collaborative nature of the tool, this rethinking had to occur as a result of a collective problem solving effort among all users who had different organizational roles, responsibilities, and preferences in using the tool. Therefore, business-motivational knowledge among different users was dependent, rather than independent, and users had to understand the collaborative nature of their tasks.

## Effectiveness of Training

Based on open-ended interviews with more than 30 users, it appeared that they were dissatisfied with the training program. Their comments suggested that the training program was not as effective in preparing users to deal with the more complex problems of processing a loan when each loan was different and required the collaboration of different users with different responsibilities. For example, when one user was asked about whether he felt that the trainers had taught him how to embed a WordPerfect document in the loan package, he replied: “The training scenarios were real and useful but simple. I know it is difficult to use complex scenarios for training, but the job we do is much more than composing and sending documents. I would really like some guidance on how to use the application more effectively for my job.”

Another set of users said: “The training was helpful to get me started. I am still checking the training script for details from time to time. Once I started to use the application heavily on a daily basis, many surprises popped up. The application really reshapes my work on many aspects and I have to invent solutions while moving along with work changes. . . . The technical part of the system is really not that hard to figure out. I did not use the system for several weeks after training but could pick it up easily on my own. What I am still working on, even today (after a couple of months of usage), is to figure out an effective way of collaborating with my partners on the application.”

## Summary of Results

Our results indicate that the lack of business-procedural and business-motivational knowledge create the most user problems, supporting Sein et al.’s [44] argument that users must possess adequate business contextual knowledge to make effective use of business-process–oriented systems. We also found that the different levels of knowledge described by Sein et al.’s framework interacted in users’ problem solving activities. This interaction happened in collective, rather than individual, problem solving activities. New knowledge was generated as part of this collective problem solving effort and much learning occurred during work. The analysis of help events, help scenarios, and interviews indicate that the answer to the second research question is: Current training strategies developed for individual productivity applications are not adequate to develop user skills for a collaborative application.

## Discussion

AFTER IMPLEMENTING A COMPREHENSIVE application-based training in accordance with current training research prescriptions, Bank X expected a relatively smooth and effective use of the new collaborative application. However, the high volume of help calls indicating user difficulties and their frustrations surprised the bank’s management. Thus, this study’s findings suggest that training strategies should be modified in order to better prepare users for a collaborative application. From a content perspective, our results confirmed Sein et al.’s [44] proposal that training programs must move beyond emphasizing system proficiency issues and also provide business contextual knowledge. Further, when we applied Sein et al.’s knowledge-level framework, we observed that a user’s knowledge on how to use a collaborative application underwent changes. These changes occurred in the form of interaction among different levels of knowledge and involved the users in collaborative problem solving efforts. These observations strongly suggest that training strategies should be modified from an implementation perspective as well. We propose that, rather than viewing training as an activity confined to coaching users before the deployment of a system, training activity has to extend to coaching users for a period of time during the actual implementation of an application. In the following discussion, we elaborate on these issues and present a new training framework.

## A Revised Framework for Collaborative Applications

To focus training efforts on both the individual and the group, we adapt and build upon the knowledge-level framework proposed by Sein et al. [44] to propose a new training framework for collaborative applications. The proposed framework is presented in Figure 3. The left box in the framework outlines three categories of knowledge, namely, application, business contextual, and collaborative task knowledge that training programs must deliver prior to the deployment of a collaborative application. In this proposed framework, application knowledge is preserved in accordance with theoretically justified empirical findings that indicate users must develop a good mental model of the system. Hence, they must have a good understanding of system components and know basic procedures to operate the system [36, 43]. This type of knowledge corresponds to the command-level, tool-procedural, and tool-conceptual knowledge in Sein et al.’s framework [44]. Our study found that users were able to utilize the application knowledge acquired during training and had relatively fewer problems in terms of executing system operations. In the proposed framework, we label the second category of knowledge business contextual knowledge to include the business-procedural and business-motivational knowledge proposed by Sein et al. [44]. Our study findings confirm the importance of business contextual knowledge. The importance of this knowledge is also theoretically supported by Orlikowski and Robey [41], who find that, when a collaborative application is deployed as the primary platform for a business process, it often has built-in mechanisms to enforce standard work practices. Hence, understanding the business context in which the collaborative application is deployed serves as a reference framework from which to view the changes imposed by the collaborative application and it also facilitates the innovation of new business practices. Orlikowski and Gash [40] address this need by stating that there must be a congruently shared understanding of the role of the tool in supporting organizational processes.

In our framework, the inclusion of the third category of knowledge, labeled collaborative task knowledge, has been motivated by our findings, and also theoretically addressed by Cohen and Bacadayan [7], who state that individual actions are necessarily “interlocked” with collective actions. Our findings indicate that the first two categories of knowledge are strongly conditioned by the interdependency of interconnected tasks performed by multiple users. Hence, prior to deploying the collaborative application, users must understand the interdependent relationships among all users’ work procedures, and how they relate to the interfacing functionalities built into the collaborative application. This type of knowledge should enable users to assess the collective consequences of their individual ways of using a collaborative application in the context of a business process. Note that, in our framework, we also include knowledge on how to collaboratively problem-solve. We found that this knowledge will be important in enabling users to collectively solve emerging problems in use.

Theoretically supported by the situated learning perspective, our framework adds and builds upon the prior framework by emphasizing the dynamic nature of knowledge. The double arrow between the left and middle box in Figure 3 indicates that initial knowledge obtained during training provides initial guidance, but will be supplemented, modified, and renewed during the period of actual use. The users in this study were generally domain experts and hence had some degree of business contextual knowledge, but they lacked knowledge of the possible ways in which the collaborative application would transform their existing business contextual knowledge as they knew it. While the actual changes in business practices and corresponding changes in knowledge content cannot be predicted, we suggest that training programs better prepare and sensitize users to possible changes.

The framework also directs attention to the appropriation process in the time period immediately following the deployment of a collaborative application. During this time, users execute real tasks, but are still exploring rather than settled on the best way to use the application. Researchers have stated that deployment of IT will be followed by this appropriation process and that the resulting usage patterns can be described as the emergent outcomes of this process, rather than as the result of careful prior design and planning [1, 14, 20, 32, 39, 41]. Only as the result of this appropriation process will a robust set of good practices emerge as indicated in Figure 3 by the arrow linking the middle and the right box. Hence, by including both the appropriation process and the resulting good practices in the framework, we propose that training not only deliver initial knowledge but also support the continuous process of knowledge refinement and the development of good practices. During this important period of technology appropriation, trainers can act as facilitators to help users integrate multiple perspectives and to assess the collective consequences of alternative work practices discussed.

Traditionally, deployment of a collaborative application would mark the end of training and the beginning of system support activities, viewed as troubleshooting activity performed by technical staff. Hence, users are left without much guidance during the critical period in which they appropriate the initial knowledge acquired from training and develop good practices in using the application. The framework proposed by this study posits that the ending point of training should not be the completion of initial knowledge delivery but the establishment of good practices in system usage. This new view of training suggests a training-implementation strategy that integrates training and system support activity by expanding the role of the latter to include continuous nurturing and further development of users’ knowledge.

Theoretical support for this expanded perspective on training and the expanded role of trainers is obtained from organizational learning research, which emphasizes that a substantial amount of learning is situated in work [4]. Therefore, it is vital that organizational mechanisms to support this learning activity be put in place. Researchers are now finding that system support from help centers can play a very influential role in a user’s learning process. Haggerty and Compeau [21] propose a framework based on social cognitive theory to demonstrate that the traditional “IT support” or “help center” plays a much more significant role in users’ learning activities than is previously acknowledged. The expanded role of trainers as facilitators is also supported by empirical findings that show mediators (also referred to as facilitators) can significantly enhance the interaction among group members and influence the use of group support systems [2, 35]. Sein et al.’s [44] framework encompasses this notion of continuous user learning as “metacognition” (learning to “learn”). They define it as the highest level of knowledge delivered during training. While embracing the centrality of “metacognition” in training, we suggest that, rather than terming it as a level of knowledge, it should be viewed as a capability that is acquired through continuous action. Based on the situated-learning perspective discussed earlier, we propose that such action is most likely to happen during appropriation and use of a collaborative application. Developing this capability is central for users to function effectively in an environment in which knowledge is continuously refined.

## Study Limitations

Our findings were limited to data from one site and hence cannot be easily generalized. However, we observed a pattern of events similar to that documented by other researchers, suggesting that our observations were not unique to this specific site. Although only those problems submitted by users to the help desk were noted, because we were interested in identifying only serious problems that users could not resolve, this is not a serious drawback. A researcher unaware of the coding schemes documented the events as they occurred. Both authors independently coded the events.

This method of coding over 600 events reduces the possibility of systematic biases in the analysis.

## Implications for Future Research and Practice

In future research, the proposed training framework can be used as the basis for developing testable hypotheses in the context of collaborative applications. Research could examine methods (training scripts, exercises, and tasks) to develop collaborative problem solving skills. Research could examine whether “collaborative learning” methods, where small groups of three to four people interact collectively to complete a task, will be effective to train users of collaborative applications [46].

The importance of the appropriation process highlighted by our findings suggests that future training research must investigate training as a continuous rather than a one-time engagement with users. With this perspective, it might be useful to reexamine the role of end-user help desk facilities to determine how it can support user learning and go beyond providing technical help as suggested in recent research [22]. Research should examine how to integrate training with system support activities to form methods that can continuously improve users’ ability to use technology.

The findings of this study highlight the need for examining alternative theories to guide training research. For example, because organizational learning research addresses learning by a collection of users, future research could examine whether theoretical frameworks from organizational learning can serve as a foundation for training research. Several researchers have identified a link between individual and organizational learning processes [7, 16, 26], and perhaps individual learning can be viewed as part of organizational learning. This study has demonstrated that adopting organizational learning perspectives of “learning in working” and “organizational routines” was helpful in interpreting our findings. An integration of individual and organizational learning theories could provide a better understanding of how to develop knowledge during training and facilitate the transfer and refinement of this knowledge in the workplace.

From a training practice perspective, the findings suggest that a training program must inform users about possible changes in work practices and the need to continuously learn. A trainer can discuss the perceived role of an application in the context of business process and then discuss the implications in areas such as work changes and standardization of procedures. Second, training should be conducted in a real work environment using real tasks. Training should not stop after the general deployment of an application, but continuous and adapted retraining should be provided along with traditional system support operations. Trainers should also disseminate the results as good practices emerge and changes occur at different levels of knowledge. Finally, because the effective use of a collaborative application relies on a good collective order, trainers should develop and discuss with users general guidelines for collective problem solving. Surveys indicate that organizational investments in training programs for groupware and other technology will continue to grow at a rapid pace [47]. This study finding provides one way to improve training outcomes and enhance the benefits of investing in training programs.
