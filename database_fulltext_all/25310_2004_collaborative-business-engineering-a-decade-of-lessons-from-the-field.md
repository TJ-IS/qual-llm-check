# Collaborative Business Engineering: A Decade of Lessons from the Field

MARIËLLE DEN HENGST AND GERT-JAN DE VREEDE

MARIËLLE DEN HENGST is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Systems Engineering of the Faculty of Technology, Policy and Management at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands. In 2002 she was a visiting professor at the Department of Management Information Systems at the University of Arizona. She obtained her Ph.D. from Delft University of Technology on the subject of “Interorganizational Coordination in Container Transport: A Chain Management Design” in 1999. Currently, her research interests include the development of collaboration processes, the application of collaboration technologies, and the facilitation of group meetings. She has presented at a number of national and international conferences and has published several articles of her work, including a paper in International Journal of Electronic Commerce.

GERT-JAN DE VREEDE is a Professor at the Department of Information Systems & Quantitative Analysis at the University of Nebraska at Omaha where he is Director of the Peter Kiewit Institute’s Program on E-Collaboration. He is also affiliated with the Faculty of Technology, Policy and Management of Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands from where he received his Ph.D. His research focuses on the application, adoption, and diffusion of collaboration technology in organizations, the development of repeatable collaboration processes, facilitation of group meetings, and the application of collaboration technology in different sociocultural environments. His articles have appeared in various journals, including Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Decision Systems, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Creativity and Innovation Management, DataBase, Group Decision and Negotiation, Simulation, and Journal of Simulation Practice and Theory.

ABSTRACT: Business process reengineering (BPR) projects have been carried out for many years, with varying degrees of success. Two key reasons for failure are distinguished from the literature: insufficient stakeholder involvement and poor analyses of the business processes. The purpose of this paper is to present a decade of field experiences with collaborative BPR. Nine BPR projects were executed and analyzed in detail, leading to the identification of 87 themes regarding the efficiency and effectiveness of the project. These themes were organized into 12 categories of lessons learned that provide insight into the “best practices” and together informed the evolution of the collaborative business engineering (CBE) approach to BPR. The CBE approach combines a BPR process with collaboration and simulation modeling support to address the above-mentioned reasons for failure in BPR. The field experiences show that the CBE approach can be successfully applied for BPR projects in real life.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: action research, business process reengineering, collaborative business engineering, group support systems, groupware, participative design, simulation.

ALTHOUGH ORGANIZATIONS HAVE LONG FACED varying degrees of uncertainty, complexity, novelty, and change, scholars agree the degree of these in today’s environment is unprecedented. In the information economy, businesses are changing more often and more rapidly than ever before [14]. To stay efficient and effective in a changing environment, organizations must continuously innovate to align their structure, processes, and technologies [8, 9, 18, 28, 54, 58, 68]. Innovation is key to the survival of organizations.

Several approaches emerged in the 1980s and 1990s to support organizations in improving, or even innovating, their structure, processes, and technologies. A classical approach is the problem solving process proposed by Simon [60]. Departing from incremental and piecemeal approaches such as the problem solving process, business process reengineering (BPR) emerged as a radical innovation and change program with a multidisciplinary nature [9, 27]. It provides the opportunity to reengineer the processes with the help of information technology. Yet, despite the potential BPR has to improve business performance, many BPR projects turn out to be unsuccessful for a variety of reasons: human resistance may be too great, the philosophy may not be followed, the wrong people may be involved, risk factors may not be appreciated enough, existing and new business processes may be poorly analyzed, and very often organizations are not willing to invest a large amount of money in information technology and call off the reengineering plans [15, 30, 40]. In this paper, we focus on the following two reasons for failure of BPR projects: (1) insufficient stakeholder involvement and (2) poor analyses of the business processes.

The purpose of this paper is to present the collaborative business engineering (CBE) approach that evolved over ten years of BPR experiences. In particular, the paper shows how field experiences informed the incorporation of collaboration support and simulation modeling support into a BPR approach to address the above two failure reasons. We identify a number of key lessons learned with using collaboration and simulation support in nine BPR case studies that were executed between 1992 and 2003. These lessons learned inspired and shaped the way of thinking, the way of working, the way of modeling, the way of controlling, and the way of supporting of the CBE approach.

## Background

## Business Process Reengineering

BPR PROJECTS HAVE BEEN CARRIED OUT for many years, and many different (variations of) approaches have been used. We start with describing three important BPR principles: radical change, the clean slate approach, and top-down participation. Finally, we elaborate on the causes for failure as mentioned in literature.

The first BPR principle concerns radical change. The traditional BPR approach focused on large dramatic improvements of organizational systems [27, 28]. Radical change is preferred over incremental improvements. Incremental improvements can lock organizations into the old processes, and marginal improvements generally make things worse by complicating simple processes [28]. Recent empirical results show that incremental implementation is possible as long as the design effort is radical [32]. Dennis et al. [14] present empirical evidence that radical and incremental techniques can be used concurrently.

Another key BPR principle concerns the clean slate approach. The clean slate approach assumes to ignore the current situation [27, 28]. Looking at the current situation would only make it difficult, or even impossible, to move away from the old organizational processes. Creativity would be hindered by paying too much attention to the current situation [51]. To avoid anchoring on current processes instead of freely designing the ideal situation, the clean slate principle is advocated. Opponents of the clean slate principle argue that process modeling creates a shared understanding of the current situation, enables to identify and keep the best parts, and creates a fact-based baseline against which to compare the new processes. Following Mitroff and Featheringhouse [42], one may add that building a model for understanding reduces the risk of solving the wrong problem. And, since people just want to talk about their problems in the current situation at some point in the BPR project, it is better to put this on the agenda at the beginning of the project [14]. Recent research suggests that detailed analysis of current processes contributes to the success of the BPR project [5, 14, 25, 64].

The final key BPR principle concerns top-down participation. Traditionally, participation in BPR projects was exclusively for top managers [27, 28]. According to traditional approaches, middle managers are to be avoided because they lack the vision and the authority to implement BPR. A few outsiders are to be invited to bring objectivity and a different view [28]. However, there is evidence that it is important to include knowledge and build support from the bottom as well, by inviting the middle managers [5, 10, 14]. Top managers are often isolated from daily practice and, therefore, do not understand the business and real issues to the extent middle managers do. Top managers are needed as the “catalysts” of the BPR project, whereas middle managers form the core of the project [14]. Dennis et al. [14], furthermore, show that top managers should be involved early on in the process to avoid failure in the end. And, besides middle managers and top managers, the participation of another group of stakeholders is argued for [14]: clients and suppliers because they will be affected by the changes that result from the BPR project. More recent BPR approaches align with these notions by formulating different teams to carry out different activities; you could have a consulting team (outsiders), a steering team (top managers), a core design team (middle managers), and a stakeholder team [51].

Despite the potential BPR has to improve business performance, many BPR projects turned out to be unsuccessful [28]. Hammer and Champy [28] noted failure rates as high as 70 percent. Several reasons for this high failure rate are addressed in literature, including resistance to change, not attuning the BPR philosophy to the situation at hand, poor stakeholder involvement, poor risk assessment, poor analysis of current and future business processes, and organizational reluctance to invest in large reengineering projects [15, 30, 40]. Of these reasons for failure, this paper focuses in particular on two: poor stakeholder involvement and poor business process analysis.

Poor stakeholder involvement often manifests itself in insufficient involvement of top and middle management [7, 10, 30, 31]: the wrong people are involved at the wrong moments in time and for the wrong reasons. This can lead to incomplete information, human resistance, and outdated information [75]. There are several closely related reasons. First, many different stakeholders have to be involved to complete the picture of the business processes. Second, the stakeholders should contribute their knowledge about the business processes and should come to agreements about the evolving analysis of the business process, which can be a time-consuming activity. Third, the lack of quality and creativity of ideas for redesigning the business processes is also frequently mentioned [7, 30, 47]. The stakeholders involved are usually not creative, but tools could be used to support people in their creativity [53, 55]. Finally, the difficulty in capturing processes is mentioned as a reason for failure [12, 30]. Different stakeholders have different disciplines and often have difficulties in describing the processes in a consistent way that can be seen by the multidisciplinary team members.

Several authors point out that BPR projects fail because of difficulties with respect to capturing and analyzing business processes. The use of modeling tools in BPR projects to support in describing the processes is very low [26]. Even when it is possible to capture processes, this does not automatically mean that the outcome of radical changes can be predicted [30, 65]. Without the use of supportive tools, it is hard to predict the outcome of changes, and, without an indication for the outcome, it is difficult to decide on it [46]. Given the above, BPR projects could greatly benefit from the use of appropriate supportive tools [65]. The subsections below describe the added value of groupware and simulation as tools to support BPR.

## Groupware

Groupware is a collective term for technologies that support collaborative work [20]. Technologies range from electronic mail, to video conferencing, and workflow management systems. Group support systems (GSS) are a special type of groupware. GSS are designed to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of meetings by offering a variety of tools to assist the group in the structuring of activities, generating ideas, and improving group communications [44, 45, 66]. The success of GSS meetings is often attributed to specific GSS characteristics: anonymity, parallel input, and group memory [22].

GSS can reduce the time required for projects, and, as such, tackle the problem of prolonged analysis. Previous studies on GSS in general have reported labor cost reductions averaging 50 percent and reductions of project calendar days averaging 90 percent [24, 50, 74]. Time and cost reductions are also reported for applications in the BPR field [12, 16, 44]. GSS have the potential to increase the productivity of brainstorming teams: more ideas and better ideas are generated [67]. GSS can also effectively facilitate the involvement of large groups in meeting processes, enabling stakeholders at all levels to be involved [13, 45]. Finally, GSS can be used to capture processes graphically [12, 14, 70]. However, it must be noted that collaborative tools to support this are hardly available, and those that are available are lacking both in quality and functionality [14, 70].

## Simulation

Simulation modeling supports the creation of dynamic models of organizational processes and information systems [29, 70]. Simulation models describe processes in the organizational system with graphical symbols, as conceptual models do, and provide quantitative information about these processes. Simulation modeling tools provide a structural environment in which one can understand, analyze, and improve business processes [49]. The visual interactive features of many simulation packages allow multidisciplinary team members to understand the model and communicate about it [48, 72]; this might even reduce the time required for analysis. Besides capturing the processes, simulation also supports the quantitative evaluation of different alternatives before implementation. Quantitative process metrics that can be addressed by simulation include, but are not limited to, costs, cycle time, serviceability, and resource utilization [49, 59]. These metrics also form the basis for evaluating alternatives in BPR [23, 36]. Although the benefits of using simulation for business process modeling are numerous [30, 48, 52], many BPR projects still rely on the intuitive judgment of experts; even the use of conceptual models in BPR is very limited [26, 34, 36].

## Research Method

## Action Research

THIS PAPER REPORTS ON LESSONS LEARNED between 1992 and 2003 regarding the combination of BPR, groupware, and simulation. During this period, a number of studies were carried out following an action research approach. Action research has the dual intention of improving the practice and contributing to theory and knowledge [2, 6]. Action research was employed as our research approach for several reasons. First, the phenomena at hand were considered too complex to be studied in a constructed setting. Second, action research is especially appropriate to address “howto” research questions. Our studies were intended to explore and develop ways in which BPR could be supported by simulation modeling and could be carried out collaboratively. Third, action research involves the actual, that is, practical, application of tools and methods. We wanted to evaluate the combination of BPR, GSS, and simulation modeling. This combination hardly ever occurs “naturally,” which makes a more passive approach, such as case studies, impossible. Finally, as far as we knew at the initiation of the projects, very little work had been conducted on the combined application of GSS and simulation modeling in the context of BPR.

We employed the model proposed by Zuber-Skerritt [77] (see Figure 1), which states that an action research study may consist of four activities that can be carried out over several iterations. “Plan” concerns exploration of the research site and the preparation of the intervention. In this activity, the researchers define the problem to be addressed, both practically and scientifically. The practical problem in the cases is described in the next section. The scientific problem has been addressed above. “Act” refers to the actual intervention made by the researcher. This is based on the planned intervention, which may also be informed by starting theories. Our starting theory concerned the “dynamic modeling” approach to BPR, which is described in more detail below. “Observe” concerns the collection of data during and after the actual intervention to enable evaluation. Finally, the “reflect” activity analyzes the collected data and infers conclusions that may feed into the “plan” activity of a new iteration. The lessons learned are described in the fourth section. The fifth section describes the integration of the lessons learned from the cases in the form of the CBE approach that was developed based on all the case experiences.

## Selection of Cases

Between 1992 and 2003, we were actively involved in 31 BPR cases. Although all focusing on BPR, the cases slightly differ in the approach and the emphasis they put on certain elements in the approach. As part of our research, we selected cases that met the following criteria:

• The number of participants actively involved in all the steps of the project should be more than one. Those cases are left out in which the participation was limited to only a few phases, such as the data gathering phase and the presentation of the results.

• The simulation models in the project should be developed with the intention to support the activities of the participants. Those cases are left out in which models were used for purposes of the researcher only and not for the support of the activities of the participants.

• The client organization should allow us to use and report on the information and experiences gained in the case. Some organizations insisted on remaining anonymous, but allowed us to include the results in this study.

Based on these criteria, nine cases were selected (see Table 1 for a short description of each case’s goal). Three cases were carried out for police forces: two for the Amsterdam Municipal Police Force and one for the South African Police Force. Another three cases concentrated on the airport industry: two on the Amsterdam Airport Schiphol and one on a Dutch airline. Two cases were carried out for an insurance company in the Netherlands. One case concerned an American software development organization. More background information on the CS, LE, CIP, and IC cases can be found in Maghnouji et al. [38].

## BPR Approach

The “plan” phase of each action research case had a common base in terms of the general BPR approach followed. This approach was based on the dynamic modeling approach, which has been developed based on the problem solving literature [1, 60] and used successfully in a substantial number of projects (for overviews, see [41, 73]). The dynamic modeling approach considers executing a BPR project as solving an ill-structured problem. There are many possible courses of action and many variables, while options for organizational change are often dangerous or impossible to test in reality causing the effects of these changes not being known in advance. The dynamic modeling process, which is the foundation for our BPR interventions, is depicted in Figure 2. The process consists of several design activities that are usually carried out in an iterative fashion. These activities show many similarities with traditional BPR approaches (see, e.g., [39]). First, the problem situation in an organization is conceptualized in order to structure the problem situation. Second, using empirical data, a descriptive and validated empirical model is built that can be used to analyze and diagnose the problem situation. Third, the problem diagnosis informs the identification of alternative solutions that are analyzed using prescriptive empirical (simulation) models. Fourth, based on the analysis of alternative solutions, a particular (set of) solution(s) is selected. Finally, the selected (set of) solution(s) is implemented.

## Tools Used

During the BPR projects, the conceptual models were built using simple, single-user diagramming tools, such as Microsoft Visio. GSS were used, for example, to capture process definition information, such as descriptions of process activities and descriptions of problems related to these activities, to identify ways to redesign the processes, and to collect feedback on proposed redesigns. The GSS used in all case studies was GroupSystems<sup>TM</sup>. Different versions have been used over time, including GroupSystems V, GroupSystems for Windows, and GroupSystems for Workgroups. Although somewhat different in user interface, the overall functionality that we used was comparable. In two cases, CID and ESDO, collaborative diagramming tools were used: TeamGraphics and GroupModeler. In TeamGraphics and GroupModeler, changes made by one user are immediately visible on the screens of other users. To build and use simulation models, we made use of two single-user simulation packages: Arena and Simple++. Both simulation packages have similar possibilities for visualizing (animating) the simulation model and for analyzing the results.

## Data Collection and Analysis

During the cases, we collected data from both quantitative and qualitative sources:

• Direct observation. The researchers participated and observed the different phases of the BPR project. They kept notes of incidents, remarks, and events that conveyed critical information.

• Interviews. The researchers used both open-ended and closed interviews with the members of the BPR project teams, both during the project and afterward.

• Questionnaires. Each of the participants in a group session was asked to fill out a questionnaire afterward in order to measure their perception on a number of issues related to the session.

• Session data. The results of each group session were stored electronically. These files were used to trace the flow of information exchange during the sessions, typical meeting behavior, and other events.

This variety of data collection instruments gave a rich representation and enabled comparison and contrast of the collected data. Given the exploratory nature of the study, we analyzed the collected data through an iterative process in which we identified recurring themes in the data. This way, from the 9 cases, we identified a total of 87 themes. We then identified 12 categories that appeared to capture a number of themes. In a few iterations, we grouped similar themes into these categories, sometimes redefining the category. In the end, each category represented a (number of) lessons learned from the cases.

It should be noted that the main purpose of the questionnaires was to complement the interpretation of observations and interview feedback. The “statistics” themselves were not a key result. In addition, due to the explorative nature of the study, there was no overall consistent questionnaire used in every case. Rather, the questionnaire used evolved with each project.

## Role of Researcher

The role of the researchers in the cases was that of observer and participant at the same time. Researcher participation was twofold. First, the researchers facilitated the process. This included scheduling and documenting the BPR project and group meetings. The researchers also helped to prepare an agenda for each meeting and moderate the meetings. Second, the researchers constructed simulation models to analyze the current and future situations.

A dual role as participant and observer enables researchers to study unique situations, but also creates the potential for bias because the researchers can become advocates for the groups or phenomena under study [76]. Interventions by the researchers during the project were only aimed at supporting the organization in achieving its goals in the BPR project. The researchers had no personal stake in any of the projects, neither with the problem situation nor with the solutions that were to be explored. The primary motivation for the client organizations to involve the researchers stems from the absence of BPR, GSS, or simulation expertise within the organizations.

## Results

AS STATED ABOVE, WE IDENTIFIED a number of (categories of) lessons learned from the cases that appeared to impact the execution and results of the BPR project. The categories of lessons learned are presented in Figure 3 and are described in more detail below. The fact that some categories appeared to impact other categories is depicted by an arrow (see Figure 3). We distinguish between categories that focus on efficiency and categories that focus on effectiveness.

## Efficiency

Efficiency concerns the amount of effort and resources that are required to complete a task. Attention is an organization’s scarcest resources [62]. Therefore, BPR projects should take as little time as possible. The longer the BPR project lasts, the longer stakeholders have to divide their attention over their daily work and the BPR project. The longer the project lasts, the more difficult it is to keep the momentum in the project and to keep the stakeholders’ attention. Yet, even in short projects, it might be difficult to keep the stakeholders’ attention. The perceived speed of the project is vital for the stakeholders. Even small projects may be perceived as long-winded, resulting in little or no attention for the BPR project.

## Modeling Strategy

The lessons regarding the modeling strategy became apparent on two different levels. First, we address the overall project strategy, including all steps of the project. Second, we look at the strategy for each individual step.

The overall project strategy mainly pays attention to which steps are carried out when. The dynamic modeling approach presented in the “BPR Approach” subsection was the point of departure for each case. It starts with building a model of the current situation. Most cases followed this approach for various reasons as they were mentioned in the second section: create a shared understanding, create a fact-based baseline against which to compare the new processes, reduce the risk of solving the wrong problem, identify the best parts, and give people an opportunity to talk about their daily business. The ESDO case showed two other important reasons. First, it was very difficult to convince people to put a lot of effort into reengineering before seeing the problems they faced. Second, through models of the business processes problems may become apparent that otherwise would have remained unknown. Three cases (IC, CS, and LE) used a slightly different approach. These cases started off with brainstorming alternative solutions to make the design questions really clear. The advantage of this strategy is that it facilitates setting the project and model boundaries.

There are three possible strategies for each individual step. First, the analyst can carry out each step individually, possibly based on feedback from interviews with a selection of stakeholders. Second, the stakeholders can carry out each step. They would have to construct models, identify problems, and design solutions—all by themselves, from scratch. The final strategy lies in between these two extremes. It has more of a prototyping flavor: the analyst prepares the desired output for the step after which stakeholders are invited to refine the output. Several iterations could be needed to reach an acceptable level of output. In the cases, all three strategies were used for all steps throughout the project. Those cases in which the analyst carried out the step were identified as time-consuming (ACOST). Cases in which the stakeholders had to start from scratch were evaluated negatively as well, since it appeared to be very hard for participants to start off with nothing (CID, IIS, and SAPS). The best results were reported in those cases where the prototyping-oriented strategy was used (CID, IIS, ESDO, IC, CS, LE, and CIP). In two cases, CIP and LE, the prototype models consisted partly of reused old models. This accelerated building a first prototype model, but appeared to increase the time for refining the model with the stakeholders. The old models were based on assumptions unknown to the stakeholders or the analyst. Therefore, fine-tuning the models took more time.

## Project Team Definition

The case experiences showed that it is crucial to clearly define the project team in terms of the roles and responsibilities during the BPR project. We distinguish between the analysts’ and stakeholders’ roles and responsibilities. The analysts provide procedural knowledge, whereas the stakeholders provide content knowledge regarding the BPR project.

It turned out that the analysts perform two important roles in a BPR project. The first role is that of the facilitator of the process. The facilitator makes a planning for the BPR project, and prepares and moderates meetings. The second role is that of modeler. The modeler builds conceptual and simulation models. In most cases, two different people carried out the roles of facilitator and modeler (ACOST, CID, ESDO, CS, LE, CIP, and ESDO). In the SAPS and IC cases, one person performed both roles. This turned out to be problematic: the high cognitive load of both tasks made it difficult and very time-consuming to carry them out at the same time. Also, performing both roles implies that the modeling and facilitation tasks have to be carried out sequentially. Having separate people for each task allows them to be carried out in parallel. Experience showed that this not only saved time but also resulted in comparatively higher quality results.

The stakeholders perform different roles in a BPR project as well. Four different roles are distinguished. First, some stakeholders are decision-makers (or the top managers, as they were called in the second section). Second, there are two types of middle managers: process owners and data experts. Process owners carry out the work. Process owners know about the procedures and have qualitative process information. Data experts have quantitative process information. In practice, the roles of process owner and data expert are sometimes combined in one person. Finally, the fourth stakeholder role is that of clients and suppliers who will be affected by the changes resulting from the BPR project. None of the cases analyzed in this study incorporated the clients and suppliers as stakeholders in the BPR project. All cases involved top or middle manager. Their involvement was evaluated positively and considered a critical success factor for the project in most cases (LE, IC, CS, CID, ACOST, SAPS, and IIS).

## Extent of Participation (Time/Completeness)

The previous lesson highlighted that stakeholders have to be involved in the project in different roles. Lessons can also be drawn regarding the extent of their participation, both in terms of the number of people involved and the moment of involvement.

Different stakeholders are involved in a BPR project to get as rich and complete a picture of the processes as possible. In this sense, it might be best to involve as many people as possible to ensure information completeness. However, this was not pursued in any of the cases, as it was considered too time-consuming.

During the LE, IC, and CS cases, different stakeholders were involved in different phases of the BPR project. On the one hand, this is positive, as it stimulated the pace of the project since stakeholders were only involved when really necessary. On the other hand, it turned out to have some negative aspects as well: stakeholders that joined the project in a later stage wanted to discuss things that were already decided upon in previous stages. This especially was true for accepting the model that has already been built and validated in previous stages. Stakeholders that were not involved earlier were very reluctant to accept the model.

During the IIS, CID, SAPS, and ACOST cases, different stakeholders were involved simultaneously. Although this was comparatively more time-consuming and more challenging to coordinate in terms of agenda, it was considered to be very fruitful. Middle managers provided detailed process knowledge, whereas the top managers were present to make immediate decisions based on the generated insights. The stakeholders involved liked this way of working very much, especially in terms of the pace it created during the workshops.

## Tool/Technology Complexity

The complexity of the tools and technologies used during the BPR project appeared to influence the time needed to complete it. In the context of tool/technology complexity we distinguish between stakeholder usability and task usability.

Stakeholder usability addresses how easily a stakeholder can learn to use the tool. The more complex the tool, the more time the stakeholders need to get acquainted and use it. In the cases, GroupSystems appeared to be easy to learn and use. The collaborative modeling tools (TeamGraphics and GroupModeler) used in the CID and ESDO cases appeared to have a high learning curve. Stakeholders did not use the simulation packages themselves; only the analysts operated them.

Task usability addresses how well the tools support the tasks at hand. GroupSystems, TeamGraphics, and GroupModeler appeared to suit the tasks very well. The simulation tools used supported the analysts’ modeling tasks very well, although they remained very time-consuming. However, the simulation tools showed limitations in the collaborative setting. For example, it appeared to be too cumbersome to modify the models during the sessions. Also, the simulation run time was often too long to wait for the results during a session. To overcome such problems, three alternative ways of using the models were explored. In the IC case, long waiting times were made more acceptable to the stakeholders by showing an animation of the simulation model. In the CIP case, the simulation model could be kept very simple and small. This enabled easy modification and fast running during the session. In the other cases, the simulation model was executed off-line after the session. The results were presented in a next session.

## Tool/Method Understanding and Acceptance

The complexities of the tools and methods used appeared to influence the ease with which the participants understood them. When participants had difficulties understanding and accepting the project’s tools and methods, it slowed down the project. Too much discussion is focused on the tools and methods instead of on the content of the project. A poor understanding and acceptance of the tools and methods also affected the effectiveness of the BPR project; this will be discussed in the “Effectiveness” section below.

## Integration of Tools

A BPR project consists of a number of steps. Usually, the output of step n is used as input for step n + 1. For example, the first step in a BPR project could be a brainstorming session in which the stakeholders describe the current situation. The second step could be to translate this information into a conceptual model. In the third step, this model could be presented to the stakeholders who suggest empirical data about; for example, process times and number of resources. The fourth step could entail the construction of a simulation model. For each of the steps, different tools are used: an electronic meeting system for brainstorming, a drawing tool for the conceptual model, and a simulation tool for the simulation model. In our cases, these tools were not integrated. This made it labor-intensive and time-consuming for the researchers to transfer the results from one step to the next. This lack of tool integration was particularly felt in the CID, SAPS, IIS, ESDO, and ACOST cases.

## Effectiveness

Effectiveness concerns the extent to which a task is completed successfully; it is the extent to which the goals of the task have been realized. Stakeholders have certain goals with regard to the BPR project. The project will be considered effective if these goals are met. Hence, an important indication for the effectiveness of a BPR project is the stakeholders’ acceptance of the models and final outcome. Often, quality is defined as another dimension of effectiveness. However, although they may be related, quality is not a sufficient indicator of effectiveness. The IIS and ACOST cases illustrated that low-quality outcomes were still accepted by the stakeholders who considered the projects effective. Moreover, the LE case showed that stakeholders may reject a high-quality outcome, making the project highly ineffective. Therefore, the effectiveness of the BPR efforts in the nine cases is associated with the stakeholders’ acceptance of the outcomes. Yet there are important lessons learned with respect to model quality, which we discuss below. Other lessons concern encouraging involvement, understanding through models, and convergence among stakeholders.

## Model Quality

The quality of a model, be it a list of problem perceptions or a detailed simulation model, can be described by its completeness (does it depict all relevant aspects of the business situation?) and correctness (does it accurately depict these aspects?). Model quality may also depend on the correct use of modeling techniques. However, this turned out not to be an issue in our cases.

The cases showed that stakeholders perceived models that were more detailed and correct to be of higher quality. The amount of detail that could be incorporated into a model depended on the extent of the participation: the more stakeholders that were involved, the easier it was to end up with complete and correct models. This was explicitly noted in the CID and SAPS cases.

It is interesting to note that, in the IIS and ACOST cases, the project was considered effective, though the completeness of the models left something to be desired. We feel, however, that this was an exception. In most projects, the outcomes would not be accepted when the model quality is low, because the stakeholders’ understanding would not be supported limiting the extent to which they converged on key issues. This, for example, happened in the CS case.

## Encouraging Involvement

Stakeholder involvement is key to ensure that the stakeholders’ interests are accommodated and, therefore, the goals of the BPR project are met. Based on the experience in the cases, we distinguish between two kinds of involvement: passive versus active involvement. Passive involvement concerns stakeholders behaving as bystanders, watching the project going on. They only provide input when explicitly asked. When stakeholders behave passively, they expect others to do most of the work. Active involvement is when the stakeholders take on responsibilities and keenly work for a good project result. From a number of cases (e.g., CID and SAPS), we conclude that active involvement results in a greater effectiveness of the project.

The degree of involvement appears to be influenced by the level of understanding and acceptance of the tools and methods used. The more the stakeholders understand and accept the tools and methods, the more they get actively involved in the project. This was observed and explicitly mentioned in the CID, IC, CIP, LE, CS, and SAPS cases. Using GSS as a low intimidation communication medium together with simulation models to clearly visualize business processes appears to stimulate stakeholders to actively participate.

## Understanding Through Models

A key reason for using models during a BPR project is to facilitate the creation of shared understanding among the stakeholders. Most stakeholders’ knowledge and understanding is limited to their own activities and processes. They lack a comprehensive complete picture of the organization. With models, it becomes easier to understand the problems in the organization and to understand the effect of certain solutions. Without such understanding, it would be very challenging to achieve agreement on and gain acceptance of proposed changes. In other words, without such understanding, the BPR project would not be effective.

The case experiences showed that understanding through models is influenced by at least two factors. The first factor concerns the stakeholders’ level of understanding through models. There was a difference in the level of understanding through conceptual or simulation models. Conceptual models were noted to provide a certain level of understanding, but were considered insufficient to help the stakeholders move beyond understanding and see the effects of change alternatives (e.g., ACOST, CID). Simulation models facilitate both understanding and insight (e.g., CID, SAPS, ESDO, CS, IC, LE). Apart from the statistical model output, the visualization (animation) of the simulation models appeared to be instrumental in enhancing stakeholders’ insight into their problem situation (e.g., SAPS, CID, IC, CS, CIP).

The second factor concerns the representation of stakeholders’ mental models. The IIS and LE cases showed that informal visual models were used more often than formalized techniques. The visual models appeared to correspond better to the mental models of the stakeholders. The CID, IC, and ESDO cases showed that different stakeholders have different mental models and therefore individual “model views” should be supported: several visualizations of the same model were needed to match the mental models of different stakeholders. Animated simulation models supported this by showing different visualizations, each hiding or highlighting particular details of the problem situation.

## Convergence Among Stakeholders

Convergence among stakeholders concerns the level of shared focus and shared understanding during the BPR project. This is an important notion regarding the effectiveness of the BPR project. When stakeholders have a shared understanding, it is more likely that they will accept the outcome of the model. The cases showed that the level of convergence is influenced by at least the extent of participation and the understanding through models.

The extent of participation describes when and how many stakeholders are involved in the project. Especially the moment when the stakeholders are involved in the project appears to influence the convergence among stakeholders. Several cases (e.g., CID, CS, and LE) illustrate that stakeholders’ opinions do not, or only with great difficulty, converge when they are sequentially involved in the project. At the same time, the CID case illustrates that convergence could be reached much more easily when all stakeholders were involved simultaneously. In order to achieve shared understanding, stakeholders have to exchange information. This is much easier synchronously than asynchronously.

As illustrated in the IC, CS, and CIP cases, stakeholder convergence also appears to be influenced by the level of understanding of models. When the models are understood, the discussion and information sharing among the stakeholders is much easier and more focused. This, in turn, appeared to result in higher perceived levels of convergence.

## Model/Outcome Acceptance

The ultimate goal of a BPR project is to improve organizational performance through solutions that are accepted by all stakeholders. As stated earlier, the acceptance of the model and outcomes is the most important indicator for the effectiveness of a BPR project. The previously mentioned lessons learned all related in some way to the acceptance of the model and outcomes. When stakeholders’ ideas and opinions are in line, acceptance of project results will be easier than in conflict situations. Convergence among stakeholders depends on the timing of stakeholder involvement and model understanding. The extent of participation also influences the acceptance of project outcomes. Although the cases do not offer empirical evidence to this effect, literature shows that involvement creates buy-in [6, 11, 16, 75]. Broader participation is expected to yield broader acceptance of the outcomes.

## Collaborative Business Engineering

THE CASES PROVIDED INSIGHT into the “best practices” for carrying out BPR projects using collaboration and simulation support. The case experiences and lessons learned together provide the foundation for the CBE approach. This approach was developed over the course of the case studies. It is grounded in both the literature on BPR, simulation modeling, and GSS, as well as in the cases that we analyzed. We describe the CBE approach in this section according to the framework proposed by Seligmann et al. [57]. This framework (Figure 4) contains five components that each address particular aspects of a design method. Together, these components, or “ways,” offer a comprehensive description of a method or approach. The way of thinking reflects the underlying philosophy, or “Weltanschauung,” of the method. It delineates how we observe the application domain of the design method. The way of working describes the activities to be carried out in order to design the artifact under consideration (e.g., a business process or information system). The way of modeling describes the techniques used to build and portray representations of relevant aspects of the design artifact. The way of controlling describes the project management activities and techniques in the design method. Finally, the way of supporting describes the tools used to support the way of working, modeling, and controlling.

## Way of Thinking

The CBE approach adopts a systems perspective on organizations and organizational processes. An organization is seen as a system—a set of related objects [1, 6, 35]. Organizational processes are seen as structured sets of subsequent transformations to achieve an organizational goal [35]. These transformations are carried out by a set of related actors (objects) that have their own goals and their own subjective perception of their working environment [35].

BPR is seen as (re)designing organizational systems. Following Sol [63], we adopt a problem solving perspective on the process of designing organizational systems. Given its complexity, the BPR process can be seen as solving an ill-structured problem. There are different ways to handle ill-structured problems. Simon [61] distinguishes between substantive rationality and procedural rationality. Substantive rationality assumes perfect conditions for the problem solving process so that, eventually, the optimal solution is identified. However, in ill-structured real-life problem situations, these perfect conditions hardly ever exist. In these situations, the focus shifts from finding the optimal solution to finding a process that will yield a satisfying solution. The CBE approach adopts this perspective, referred to as procedural rationality. The focus of the CBE approach is not on finding the single optimal new organizational design but on facilitating a diagnosis and design process that will yield a satisfying and acceptable solution.

Finally, the CBE approach assumes that actors in an organizational process hold essential information that is required for the effective completion of the redesign effort. Therefore, these actors (stakeholders) need to be involved in the redesign activities. Stakeholders involvement is also required to recognize and accommodate their goals.

## Way of Working

The way of working describes the steps that need to be taken to design business processes. In other words, the way of working defines the design activities in the CBE approach. The CBE approach follows the dynamic modeling process as described in the “BPR Approach” subsection and depicted in Figure 2. This process outlines five major design activities that are executed: (A) conceptualize problem; (B) create and validate empirical model, diagnose problem; (C) construct alternative models and conduct experiments; (D) choose most preferred solution; and (E) implement solution. These five activities are not to be followed rigidly. The process is not linear in nature. Depending on the problem situation at hand, the CBE approach allows for iteration and incrementation. The case studies showed that analysts and stakeholders often iterated back and forth between conceptual and empirical modeling, building and extending the relevant models.

The sub-activities, too, were executed in an iterative and incremental fashion. The cases showed that a “prototyping” approach to building models was more effective and efficient than trying to build models from scratch together with the stakeholders or presenting the stakeholders a comprehensive “all-but-final” model.

In conclusion, it should be noted that the CBE approach cannot, and should not, be regarded as a cookbook. Rather, it should be seen as a set of design steps. In our experience, the order in which, and extensiveness of, these design steps depends on the type of problem, the bandwidth of the problem, the scope of the problem, and the existing amount of insight in the problem.

## Way of Modeling

The way of modeling describes the different types of models that are constructed during a CBE project. A distinction is made between conceptual models, which define the structure of a problem situation, and empirical models, which represent a further specification of the problem situation and facilitate analysis and diagnosis of the problem or possible solutions.

The CBE approach employs different conceptual modeling techniques. A single best modeling technique does not exist; it should be chosen according to the situation at hand. In choosing a modeling technique, several aspects should be taken into consideration based on the lessons learned in the cases (see Figure 3). First, the modeling technique should be relatively easy to understand and accept by the stakeholders. Second, the modeling technique should enable the construction of a comprehensive model of the problem situation. Following the way of thinking, the modeling technique should, therefore, address three components [71]: active objects, actions, and passive objects. First, active objects perform actions and can be both human and nonhuman, such as machines. Second, both tasks and decisions should be available as different forms of action. Decisions constitute the choice between two or more courses of action. Third, passive objects should model “things” that do not carry out actions but that contain information to trigger actions of active objects. These requirements are met by the following conceptual modeling techniques: interaction model, process model, and actor model (see de Vreede and van Eijck [71] for more details).

For the empirical model, all cases employed discrete event simulation models. The success of simulation models depends on the extent to which they meet a number of requirements that can be deduced from the causal diagram in Figure 3. Simulation models, and especially the visual interactive features on top of simulation models, have the potential to closely resemble the stakeholders’ mental models: the visual interactive features graphically portray the dynamic behavior of a process by showing icons that resemble the objects from the real world; moving around a computer screen; and changing aspects, such as shape and color [37, 49, 72]. This affects the understanding and acceptance of the model, the involvement of stakeholders, and the understanding through models. Although simulation models have a positive influence on the effectiveness of CBE, building them is very time-consuming.

## Way of Controlling

The way of controlling determines how design activities and techniques are managed. It includes planning, and feedback, and determines in which ways various persons and groups should interact. Although formalized project management methods are important for the way of controlling, more important is the general need to gather excellent professionals who can contribute relevant information effectively [17, 56]. Two extreme strategies can be thought of: an expert strategy and a participative strategy. With an expert strategy, a technical expert (analyst) is responsible for actual problem solving and reports to a decision-maker, preferably the most important stakeholder of the organization. Although this strategy can be very efficient, results are often difficult to implement because of the “not invented here syndrome.” A participative strategy was first fully addressed in socio-technical literature [21, 43]. With this strategy, the actual problem solving is done by the different stakeholders of the organization. Design decisions are made on a basis of consensus between the different stakeholders involved. Such a strategy can ease implementation, but can be very inefficient as it asks a lot of effort from all the parties involved [19].

The CBE approach follows a strategy that combines the best of both worlds: the adaptive strategy [33, 41]. Stakeholders often have much (tacit) knowledge of organizational business processes, bottlenecks in current functioning, and possible improvements. Outside analysts (consultants or researchers) bring in knowledge about design methodologies, techniques, and tools and can suggest possible overlooked alternatives, both organizational and technical. Stakeholders can learn from analysts and vice versa. For effective learning, a close corporation between stakeholders and analysts is needed in all phases of the approach.

Although it might seem best to invite as many stakeholders as possible to ensure that all available process information is covered and that outcomes are accepted, this is too time-consuming and was not pursued in any of the cases analyzed. At least one, but preferably more, representative of different groups of stakeholders, such as the top manager and the middle managers for each department in the organization, should participate in the process. Simultaneous involvement of all participants contributes to the effectiveness and efficiency of the CBE approach. This, however, may be threatened by time constraints, making convergence among stakeholders challenging.

## Way of Supporting

The way of supporting describes the tools used to support the way of working, modeling, and controlling. In the CBE approach, two types of tools are distinguished, those to support (simulation) modeling and those to support collaboration. The collaboration tools used in the cases (GroupSystems and TeamGraphics) were very good in supporting the structuring of activities and group communication, and had a small learning curve. The modeling tools used in the cases (Arena and Simple++) were single-user-based and hard to use in a collaborative setting. Furthermore, most modeling tools support experienced users, but leave inexperienced users at the sideline. Visualizations of the models had to be used to facilitate communication about the models with stakeholders.

Although specific tools were used in the reported cases, we feel that they can be replaced by other tools with similar functionalities provided they meet a number of requirements, which can be deduced from Figure 3. First, stakeholder usability should be high to increase the understanding and acceptance of the tools. Second, the tools should have a short learning curve. Third, the tools should be integrated as much as possible to enable efficient transfer of information from one tool to the other. For example, for a visual representation in a simulation or collaborative modeling tools, there should be a corresponding textual representation in a GSS so that stakeholder feedback can be collect easily.

## Conclusions and Future Research

THIS PAPER ILLUSTRATES HOW A PROGRAM of research can result in a design approach for complex systems, such as business processes. We employed collaboration and simulation modeling support within a BPR context in nine cases. For each case, we analyzed the design approach used and, from each case, we identified a number of themes related to the effectiveness and efficiency of the BPR project. In total, 87 themes were identified and organized into 12 categories of lessons learned. The cases provided us with insights into the “best practices” for combining BPR, simulation, and collaboration support. This resulted in the definition of the CBE approach.

The CBE approach aims to address two key reasons that have been reported in the literature for the high failure rate of BPR projects: insufficient stakeholder involvement and poor analysis of the business processes. Further, the CBE approach deviates in three ways from the key BPR principles. First, it allows for both radical and incremental change. Second, the current situation is analyzed in great detail; that is, the clean slate approach is avoided. Simulation techniques are used to gain dynamic insight in existing and future business processes and their quantitative metrics. Third, the active involvement of different stakeholders instead of just top managers is facilitated through the use of group support systems. GSS enable broader, more productive, and more satisfying participation in BPR workshops through parallel and anonymous communication support and automatic record keeping. Deviating from the main BPR principles and introducing GSS and simulation into the approach appeared to be quite fruitful. Of the nine cases, only two turned out to be a failure (LE and CS), representing a failure rate of 22 percent. This compares favorably to the failure rate of 70 percent, with traditional BPR reported by Hammer and Champy [28].

## Limitations

The research described in this paper suffers from similar limitations as other action research or case studies. The findings are based on nine cases that are different in nature, employed different (versions of) technologies, took place in different sectors of industry and government, and are based on subjective interpretations of feedback and observations. In this sense, caution is required when attempting to generalize the experiences beyond the context in which they were collected. Yet we feel that the number and variety of case situations allowed us to get a rich understanding of the challenges and “best” or “worst” practices to cope with them. It should also be noted that a design approach, such as CBE, is not static or final. Further field experiences will provide refinement and elaboration of the approach. Below, we present some of the areas in which these improvements and elaborations are sought.

## Directions for Future Research

There are a number of directions for further research into the CBE approach. To conclude this paper, we highlight what we consider are the key ones. First, CBE’s way of working prescribes several sub-activities, the order of which needs to be adjusted to the situation at hand. It would be useful to develop and evaluate further guidelines that support the effective execution of the approach in different situations. Second, research efforts are currently under way to accelerate the simulation modeling process by using building blocks. Building blocks abstract more complex recurring parts of a modeled situation. For example, a building block may represent a complete airport gate. Using building blocks allows a modeler to “click” a model together in a dramatic short time frame [69]. However, it remains to be seen if, and to what extent, building blocks may thwart stakeholder buy-in: if stakeholders do not accept the building blocks they may put the model on the agenda for discussion, rather than the decision they should make based on the model’s information.

Third, the CBE approach includes only general guidelines as to who should participate, to what extent, and when in the BPR project. Further research should elaborate on these guidelines, especially with respect to the political, cultural, and social issues that influence the extent and nature of stakeholder involvement. To this end, the field of process management appears to be fruitful. Process management recognizes that besides rational considerations other issues are present when groups work together to solve problems and make decisions [4]. Stakeholders establish relations with other stakeholders to achieve their goals and serve their interests. The considerations regarding content may come second to strategic and power-political considerations.

Finally, future research will address the collaboration processes that were designed and used in each of the case workshops with the stakeholders. For each case study, a specific collaboration process was designed from scratch. Recent research in the area of collaboration engineering open possibilities to capture the experiences with the design of a collaboration process into a repeatable process using thinkLets [3]. This is expected to facilitate training other analysts to run CBE workshops with organizational stakeholders using collaboration tools.
