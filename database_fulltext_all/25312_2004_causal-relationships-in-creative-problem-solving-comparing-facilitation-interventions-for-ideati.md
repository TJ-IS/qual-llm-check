# Causal Relationships in Creative Problem Solving: Comparing Facilitation Interventions for Ideation

ERIC L. SANTANEN, ROBERT O. BRIGGS, AND GERT-JAN DE VREEDE

ERIC L. SANTANEN is an Assistant Professor of Management at Bucknell University. He earned his B.S. and M.S. in Computer Science at the New Jersey Institute of Technology and his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems at the University of Arizona. His primary research interest deals with developing techniques and technology that can help individuals and groups generate creative solutions to complex problems. This work is based upon foundations of human cognition, problem solving, group support systems, and creativity and has been presented before numerous international audiences. Other research interests include human-computer interfaces and technology-supported learning.

ROBERT O. BRIGGS is Research Coordinator at the Center for the Management of Information at the University of Arizona and Associate Professor of Collaboration Engineering at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands. He is also Director of Research and Development for GroupSystems.com. As a researcher, he has published more than 60 scholarly works on the theoretical foundations of collaboration, and he applies his findings to the development and deployment of collaborative technology to enhance team productivity, team creativity, and team satisfaction. His work on organizational transition of collaborative technology led to new insights about how to conceive of and deploy group support systems so as to create self-sustaining and growing communities of users. He is also affiliated with the Faculty of Technology, Policy and Management of Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands from where he received his Ph.D. His research focuses on the application, adoption, and diffusion of collaboration technology in organizations, the development of repeatable collaboration processes, facilitation of group meetings, and the application of collaboration technology in different sociocultural environments. ABSTRACT: Organizations must be creative continuously to survive and thrive in today’s highly competitive, rapidly changing environment. A century of creativity research has produced several descriptive models creativity, and hundreds of prescriptions for interventions that demonstrably improve creativity. This paper presents the cognitive network model (CNM) as a causal model of the cognitive mechanisms that give rise to creative solutions in the human mind. The model may explain why creativity prescriptions work as they do. The model may also provide a basis for deriving new techniques to further enhance creativity. The paper tests the model in an experiment where 61 four-person groups used either free-brainstorming or one of three variations on directed-brainstorming to generate solutions for one of two unstructured tasks. In both tasks, people using directed-brainstorming produced more solutions with high creativity ratings, produced solutions with higher average creativity ratings, and produced higher concentrations of creative solutions than did people using free-brainstorming. Significant differences in creativity were also found among the three variations on directed-brainstorming. The findings were consistent with the CNM.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: brainstorming, cognitive models, creativity, facilitation, group problem solving, group support systems, idea generation, ideation, thinkLets.

ORGANIZATIONS MUST BE CREATIVE continuously in order to survive and thrive. Globalization, changes in the workforce, increasing competition, and better informed customers drive the need for continuous changes in products, services, and strategies. Government regulations, environmental concerns, economic trends, and a renewed focus on privacy and ethical practices create further need for creative change. The organization that is unable to generate creative solutions to address these and other dynamic pressures may eventually face extinction.

Ideation activities are fundamental to the creativity process. During an ideation session, one or more people work to generate as many solutions to the problem at hand as they can in hopes of finding solutions that might otherwise go undiscovered. For this paper, a solution is deemed to be creative to the extent that it is feasible to implement, and to the extent that it falls outside the set of known solutions.

This paper surveys the existing creativity literature and then offers the cognitive network model (CNM) as an explanation of the mechanisms that give rise to creative solutions in the human mind. It then reports the results of an experiment that compares the degree to which several ideation protocols give rise to creative ideas. It concludes by discussing the implications of the findings with respect to CNM and with respect to creativity practice and research.

## Research in Creativity and Group Support Systems

A VARIETY OF APPROACHES TO CREATIVITY have emerged from more than a century of creativity research. The Four P’s of Creativity, suggested by Rhodes [57], are a useful way to organize and characterize the perspectives taken in the existing creativity literature. Rhodes notes that various authors have framed creativity as an attribute of the:

• product—some ideas, solutions, and designs are more creative than others;

• person—some individuals are more creative than others;

• press—some physical and social environments produce more creativity than others;

• process—some thinking techniques produce more creativity than others.

The product perspective of the Four P’s frames creativity as a property of the outcomes produced by creative endeavor. Brogden and Sprecher [9] define a product as a physical object, a theoretical system, an equation, or a technique. Studies based on this perspective seek to explain the differences between creative and mundane products. Amabile [2] defines a product as creative to the extent that is both novel and appropriate to an open-ended task. MacKinnon [39] indicates that a creative product is novel and adaptive to reality. Jackson and Messick [36] define the creative product as one that is unusual when compared to other products in the same class, is appropriate to the demands of the situation, has the power to transform the constraints and boundaries of the situation, and has high condensation power. An idea is deemed to have condensation power to the extent that it unifies the concepts of simplicity and complexity such that the product, which at first glance appears simple, turns out to possess only apparent simplicity upon closer inspection.

A variety of studies have examined a variety of approaches for increasing the creativity of products, and have used a diversity approaches to measure the creativity of products [29, 61, 62]. However, studies of creativity as an attribute of products leave unanswered the question of why certain techniques result in more creative products than others, and why certain people seem to be more creative than others. A causal model of creativity may help address these unanswered questions.

The person perspective of the Four P’s frames creativity as a property of people and studies the causes of differences in creativity among populations. A variety of theories, collectively referred to as trait theories, have predicted that the creative person is one endowed with certain mental abilities and attitudes. Guilford [33] defines a trait as any relatively enduring way in which persons differ from one another. One device for identifying creative persons is the adjective checklist (ACL) [32], which contains a checklist of 300 adjectives that describe an array of personal traits. Using the ACL with a sample of 1,700 architects, mathematicians, and graduate students, Gough and Heilbrun [32] found that adjectives such as capable, clever, confident, egotistical, humorous, individualistic, informal, insightful, intelligent, inventive, original, reflective, resourceful, self-confident, sexy, snobbish, and unconventional correlate positively with creative people, whereas adjectives that correlate negatively with creative people include affected, cautious, commonplace, conservative, conventional, dissatisfied, honest, mannerly, sincere, submissive, and suspicious. In a study of 132 male research personnel employed by major pharmaceutical and manufacturing organizations, Buel [11] found that more creative men tend to have a positive self-image, a need for personal independence in work and social environments, wide interests, and a tendency to become over-involved. Creative men also tend to react positively to a challenge, seek unstructured work situations, and desire contemplative pursuits.

Trait theories have been tested by a variety of survey and self-report mechanisms; however, they are frequently accompanied by conceptual difficulties. Correlational models that enumerate “contributing factors” and “categories of characteristics” can rapidly become too complex to sustain scientific investigation because each factor can be infinitely subdivided. For example, a trait model might include childhood experiences, attitudes, and personal preferences; childhood experiences might include social, emotional, economic, physical, and other factors, and so on. Studies of traits also leave unanswered the questions of why certain traits seem to correlate with creativity, whether creativity causes the traits, whether the traits cause creativity, or whether something else causes both the traits and the creativity. This approach also leaves unanswered the question of how a given person can become more creative. A causal model of creativity may help address these questions.

The press perspective of the Four P’s frames creativity as an interaction between people and their environments. Studies from this perspective investigate how a person reacts to a particular environment to become more or less creative. Some environmental elements found to lead to creativity include social interaction, intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, the presence of sufficient challenge, freedom and autonomy, access to required resources, and organizational support [3]. Other factors found to have a positive effect on creativity in the workplace are management styles that encourage risk-taking and provide support and encouragement when failures occur [27], as well as those that encourage goal-setting [64] and motivation [59]. Conversely, threats of evaluation, surveillance, competition, and time pressure have been shown to adversely affect creativity in children [2, 4]. These studies demonstrate that creativity may be the outcome of certain environmental forces playing upon certain individuals as they grow and mature. As such, they provide the basis for useful prescriptions for how to create environments conducive to creativity. However, they leave unanswered the question of why people might be more creative in one environment than another, and why creativity can vary within a given environment. A causal model of creativity may help address these questions.

The process perspective of the Four P’s frames creativity as a way of thinking and studies the stages of the creative thinking process. Stage models for creative problem solving aim to enhance human problem solving performance by formalizing a protocol for creative problem solving efforts. Hundreds of techniques that provide step-bystep sequences of actions or instructions have been proposed [16, 52, 58, 70]. Many authors offer creativity prescriptions that produce significant and measurable improvements in creativity of both people and products [34, 40]. A number of studies have explored ways to enhance the production of more and more creative solutions to problems. The brainstorming protocol developed by Osborn [52] was designed to improve the breadth and creativity of solutions to a particular problem [52]. Osborn originally predicted [52, p. 229] that a verbally interacting group could produce at least twice as many unique ideas as an equivalent number of people working individually if a specific set of conventions and constraints were followed.

An early evaluation of the brainstorming protocol by Parnes and Meadows [53] compared the performance between groups that received formal brainstorming instructions and those that did not. Results suggested that groups following Osborn’s protocol produced a significantly greater quantity of “good” solutions than groups that did not use the protocol. Subsequent studies, however, did not bear out these findings. Many studies of the protocol found that the number of unique ideas decreased as group size increased (e.g., [22, 68]).

Later research identified production blocking and evaluation apprehension as constructs that interfered with the production of unique ideas during brainstorming [22, 23]. Production blocking results occurs because only one person at a time may use an oral communication channel. While waiting for a turn to speak, some participants may forget ideas. Others may choose not to contribute if the subject changes before they have a chance to contribute. Evaluation apprehension occurs when people withhold a potentially unpopular or partially formed idea for fear of suffering negative reactions from peers or superiors.

Group support systems (GSS) are collaborative software systems that provide means to overcome production blocking and evaluation apprehension. All users of a GSS can contribute simultaneously, so there is no competition for airtime. Potential solutions can be contributed anonymously, which mitigates evaluation apprehension [20, 21]. Early research with GSS demonstrated that anonymous brainstorming teams produced substantially more unique ideas than did identified teams (e.g., [14]). Dennis et al. [19] found that the number of unique ideas produced increased with group size for teams that used the parallel-input capability of a GSS. Further analysis in both studies revealed that average per person contribution remained relatively constant across various group sizes. Thus, processes based on technology have shown several significant improvements for interactive groups when compared to similar verbally bases processes.

Yet, despite the proliferation of these stage models, research findings indicate that creative problem solving still proves to be exceptionally difficult for people to perform. For example, imagine that, for any given problem, a hierarchical structure can be created that “maps” the potential solution space (the set of all possible solutions) to that problem. The primary level (or branch level) of the map categorizes all of the different types or families of possible solutions, whereas the secondary level (or leaf level) contains all of the specific instances of solutions for each solution type. A rich literature indicates that people facing large, complex problems tend to think within a bounded, familiar, and narrow subset of the potential solution space rather than to think creatively (for seminal work in this area, see [13, 43, 69]). In complex problem solving, subjects routinely overlook between 70 and 80 percent of the potential solution space (primary branches of the hierarchy), and are even unaware that they are doing so [15, 28].

The continuing success of prescriptive models of creativity implies that principles of cause-and-effect must be at work, but, to date, no causal model has emerged to explain why these prescriptions work. A causal model of creativity—a model that explains how and why creative solutions form in the human mind—might help us understand how to make people more creative, and might help us sort out which parts of the various creativity prescriptions are effective, and which are superstitions. With a simple, clear understanding of that which causes creativity, we may be able to deliberately design processes and environments that could make people and the products they produce even more creative than was possible in the past. This paper offers a cognitive model of the mechanisms that cause creative solutions to emerge from the human mind.

Building on the logic of the model, this paper proposes several techniques for leveraging the cognitive mechanisms of creativity to help groups of people generate creative solutions to their problems. The remainder of this paper reviews several existing process models of creativity, arguing why a new model is necessary, and then derives a causal model of creative solution generation. The results of an experiment that compares several brainstorming techniques is described, offering some empirical support for the new model.

## Cognitive Accounts of the Creative Process

The process models of creativity introduced above aim to enhance human problem solving performance by formalizing a particular protocol that can be easily used and reused. Hundreds of stage models have been proposed (e.g., [1, 16, 31, 52, 58, 70, 72]). Process models of creativity have given rise to a list of useful techniques, but they tend to treat creativity as a “black box” process, offering no cognitive explanation of why they work.

Cognitive models of creativity attempt to open this black box by offering insights into actual cognitive processes that underlie creative thinking. A variety of cognitive models of the creative process have been proposed in the past. Mednick [42] defines creativity as the process of forming mental elements into new combinations that either meet specified requirements or are in some way useful, further indicating that the more mutually remote the elements of the new combination, the more creative the process or product. In order to measure one’s ability to generate associations between disparate items, Mednick and Mednick [43] developed the Remote Associations Test (RAT). The foundation of this test is what Mednick calls the associative hierarchy; a cognitive structure that indicates the number of associations (or links) an individual is likely to have to any given element in memory. People with steep associative hierarchies have very strong associations from some initial stimulus to a relatively small set of other elements and thus have the ability rapidly follow only a few associations to a specific stimulus. As a result, processes that operate on steep associative hierarchies are predicted to lead to relatively lower levels of creativity. Conversely, people with flat associative hierarchies have relatively weaker associations with a relatively larger set of other elements, and have the ability to slowly and steadily produce a great many associations to a specific stimulus. Therefore, processes that operate on flat associative hierarchies are predicted to lead to relatively higher levels of creativity as a result of the greater number of potential associations that can be explored in conjunction with any given stimulus. Although the RAT was extensively used in creativity research, some have suggested that Mednick and Mednick’s theoretical model is not well operationalized by the RAT [35]. For example, Mednick and Mednick define creativity as the ability to form new and useful combinations of previously dissociated elements, but the RAT does not test the subject’s ability to form any combinations. Rather, subjects are presented with the combination and merely requested to find a mediator [51].

Similarly, Koesteler’s [37] bisociation process suggests that creativity flows from the association of two typically incompatible frames of reference within some new context. Thus, the creative product is characterized as one that is both unexpected and perfectly logical within each frame of reference. What distinguishes routine thinking from creative thinking, then, is how the frames of reference are used for problem solving. Whereas routine thinking takes place on a single plane (a typical or common frame of reference), creative thinking results from the intersection to two more planes within the context of the problem (defined by the specific point of intersection). Koesteler found support for the bisociation process with a study of eminent achievement among artists, writers, physical scientists, and social scientists. In this study, the subjects indicate that new ideas or new understandings often derive from the spontaneous fusion of two or more frames of reference. This model does not suggest, however, how one might actively use bisociation to improve creative output.

A model that illustrates the impact of social and cognitive factors upon creativity in verbally interactive, idea-generating groups is proposed by Paulus [54]. He posits that factors such as production blocking, task-irrelevant behavior, and cognitive load will lead to lower creativity for interacting groups. Conversely, cognitive factors such as novel associations, priming, attention to the ideas of others, presence of conflict, heterogeneity of participants, divergent style, and incubation of ideas lead to increased creativity for interactive groups. Although this model suggests elements that are posited to impact creativity, it does not depict a causal mechanism capable of explaining how and why these factors have the impact that they do.

Nagasundaram and Dennis [47] present another cognitive approach to solution generation and creative problem solving. They argue that brainstorming is best viewed as an individual, cognitive phenomenon (rather than a social phenomenon) and is therefore subject to the limitation of the human information processing system. This model of cognitive stimulation maps various stimulus attributes (diversity, cardinality, and temporal distance) of previously generated ideas that are presented to individuals participating in electronic brainstorming to response attributes (innovativeness and productivity) of new ideas generated by the participants as a result of exposure to the previous ideas. In the context of this model, stimulus diversity is viewed in terms of the idea that triggers the subsequent response generated by the participant. Diversity can vary along several dimensions, such as the semantic domain of the idea, the contributor of the idea, the idea itself which stimulated the generation of the new idea, and the number of characters contained in the previous idea. According to this model, then, diversity in the stimuli that brainstorming participants receive will increase both the innovativeness and the productivity (quantity) of responses produced. Analysis of the linkages within the model, however, reveals that it is primarily focused on predicting productivity as opposed to predicting creativity.

The various stage models presented above help to conceptualize the creative process. These models illustrate that creative solutions do not automatically occur whenever problem solvers are faced with a difficult problem. Rather, creative solutions are the result of sustained and complex mental effort over time. These models have helped us learn about the creative process and even begin to hint at the possibility of a discrete cognitive mechanism associated with the creative process. However, there is still no formal specification of how such a mechanism may work, leaving us with many unanswered questions. For example, why do these techniques represented in stage models sometimes work? What are the limitations of a cognitive mechanism of the creative process? How can the creative process best be leveraged while engaged in creative problem solving?

## Using Group Support Systems to Enhance Creativity

Since GSSs have been demonstrated to overcome a number of difficulties that typically befall verbally interactive groups, a number of studies have begun to more precisely probe the impacts of using group support technology in creativity-oriented problem solving tasks. For example, in an investigation that compared several types of software specifically designed to help improve creative performance, Massetti [40] found that levels of creativity were higher for subjects using creativity support systems than for subjects that used pen and paper, although the findings did not lend any insights as to exactly how the software enhanced creative performance.

Subsequent studies have helped to provide new insights to the manner in which information systems may impact the creative process. Satzinger et al. [63] performed a set of experiments designed to investigate the effects of presenting various categories of stimuli to subjects as they worked on a creative problems solving task. As subjects worked on a task in the context of what they believed to be a group brainstorming exercise, the role of other group members was fulfilled by software that periodically inserted specific comments into the brainstorming tool. Thus, subjects worked alone and were presented with stimuli that had been previously categorized as “paradigm preserving” or “paradigm modifying.” Paradigm-preserving stimuli are those that retain the current paradigm presented in the business problem, whereas paradigm-modifying stimuli reflect changes in the underlying paradigm of the existing problem. Results indicated that participants exposed to paradigm-preserving stimuli as they worked tended to generate paradigm-preserving ideas, whereas those exposed to paradigm-modifying stimuli tended to generate paradigm-modifying ideas. One difficulty that the authors pointed out, however, is that it is unclear whether the primary influence in these findings was the result of social forces (such as those highlighted by Lamm and Trommsdorff [38]) or a result of a manipulation of cognitive processing.

Recently, Hender et al. [34] investigated creative problem solving using a cognitive approach. They constructed several experiments to explore the impacts of stimuli diversity upon the resulting creativity level of the solutions to a problem solving task. Their investigation involved the comparison of three creative solution generation techniques: brainstorming, analogies, and assumptions reversals. In brainstorming, the only source of stimuli is the emerging pool contributed by the problem solvers as they work. Therefore, it is also the most basic of the three techniques. The assumption reversals takes a more structured approach to solution generation than does brainstorming. In this technique, people state their assumptions about the problem and then brainstorm ideas that would be appropriate if the assumption were reversed. This reversal introduces nontraditional, problem-related assumptions that are hypothesized to increase creativity. Finally, using analogies provides stimuli that is unrelated to the problem and asks the participants to create relationships between this new stimuli and the problem. Results indicated that solutions generated using the analogies technique were more creative (due to the use of unrelated stimuli) than solutions generated with either the assumptions reversals (which uses related stimuli) or more traditional brainstorming techniques (which uses no external stimuli).

Thus, a number of recent studies employing GSSs have helped to contribute additional insights to the creative process. Specifically, these studies highlight the central role played by cognitive processes that may be related to the formation of creative solutions.

## Deriving the Cognitive Network Model of Creativity

THIS SECTION REVIEWS LITERATURE THAT PROVIDES a foundation for one potential cognitive model, the CNM of creativity, which attempts to answer the research question “What is a basic cognitive mechanism responsible for producing creative solutions to a problem?” This model derives from a synthesis of concepts from three bodies of research: organization of memory and knowledge, the role of cognition and knowledge in problem solving, and creativity.

## Memory

Humans have at least two types of memory, each of which serves a different purpose. Long-term memory is the storage area for an individual’s lifetime accumulation of knowledge, whereas working memory is the smaller, temporary workspace for items that are under active consideration at the moment [6].

## Organization of Knowledge

Much research has posited that human knowledge is organized into bundles of related items. These items have alternatively been named concepts [41], categories [26], schemata [8], scripts, and frames [45]. Each of these theoretical structures proposes that knowledge is organized into “bundles” that contain related knowledge (for consistency, the term frame will be used throughout this paper). Thus, when thinking of a frame, one has ready access to all the items bundled into the frame with virtually no additional cognitive effort [45]. For example, the items outdoors, eating, cloth, basket, and sunshine may be bundled into a frame called picnic. Furthermore, these bundles exhibit properties that suggest they are highly interconnected with one another, forming vast networks representing knowledge and personal experiences [48]. Each concept within a frame serves as a variable strength link to, or association with, other frames that contain the same concept [13]. For example, the frame tree may contain the item forest, which acts as a strong link to frames for lumber, shade, and plants. Tree may be linked to fire and fairy tales through forest. Thus, human knowledge is represented in a network of bundles (frames). The strength of the link may be due to frequency of its traversal, or the relatedness among the frames that it connects [13].

## Activation of Frames

External stimuli received through our five senses cause one or more frames from the cognitive network to become activated, or transferred from long-term to working memory [5]. One has little or no control of which frame is initially activated by an external stimulus [55]. Furthermore, when any particular frame becomes activated (for example, thinking about cats or dogs), subsequent activation spreads to other frames that are closely related to the originally activated frame (for example, thinking about “cat” may lead someone to think about their pet). The spreading activation model [13] asserts that activation of one node (or frame) activates the next most strongly associated node, which in turn activates the next most strongly associated node to that one. As activation spreads out in this fashion, the relative strength of activation for each successive frame decreases. A person may move through a chain of associations from one frame to another that is many nodes away on the knowledge network [50]. For example, upon seeing a tree, one might move through a sequence like tree, wood, wooden house, boyhood home, mother, mom’s spaghetti, and so blurt out for no obvious reason, “Let’s eat Italian tonight.” One person might see the same tree and think wood, while another might think biological specimen. The frame that is initially activated bounds the set of frames that may be activated next. Moreover, patterns of activation among associated frames involve two components [55]. The first is an automatic spreading activation that is fast-acting and occurs without intention or conscious awareness, whereas the second involves a limited-capacity processing mechanism that cannot operate without intention and conscious processing.

## Creative Problem Solving

Research has shown that creative solutions typically result from discovering new associations among frames from previously disparate areas of knowledge networks within the context of the problem at hand [42, 46]. The creative process, however, is not simply driven by random combinations of frames. Rather, creativity is often characterized as the result of effortful application of knowledge to the domain of interest [67]. Thus, creativity emerges when two or more knowledge frames not previously associated with one another are activated together in the context of some new problem. This is perhaps the most operationally important proposition for CNM; however, this all-important combination process is subject to several constraints, indicated below.

## Limitations of Working Memory

The pool of processing resources available to manipulate activated frames (and thus explore new associations) is finite and subject to two major constraints: capacity and persistence. In terms of capacity, working memory is only able to hold five to nine items (or frames) at any one given time [44]. Further, once processing resources are shifted away from the contents of working memory, these contents decay and are typically irretrievable within 30 seconds [10]. To some small extent, the capacity limits of working memory may be relaxed through chunking. Chunking is a form of cognitive economy that allows multiple frames, either by their similarity or frequency of association, to be combined (or chunked) into a single larger frame [7]. Thus, through chunking, it is possible that additional frames may be simultaneously activated.

## The Need for Facilitation

It is a relatively straightforward matter to account for research findings on creative problem solving in terms of the mechanisms proposed above. First, consider the implications of a network of knowledge in problem solving. This network structure of strongly and weakly linked frames may be one of the sources of functional fixedness (e.g., see [24]) that serves to limit creativity for people attempting to solve new problems. Presentation of a new problem tends to activate frames for similar situations from the past, so people will often try to retrieve old solutions and then adapt them (oftentimes unsuccessfully) to the new set of circumstances, a practice sometimes referred to as satisficing [17]. The end result of following these strong links serves to reinforce known patterns of thinking and can result in the “stuck in a rut” syndrome of not being able to find a creative solution. A second course of action may rely upon traversing weaker associations to search memory for relevant knowledge. In this event, it is likely that activation may spread too thinly across many problem-related frames such that the requisite threshold activation levels for the corresponding frames are not reached. This course of action leaves the problem solver “drawing a blank” and again unable to solve the problem. Next, consider the limitations of working memory upon the problem solving process. Several studies have shown that subjects engaged in problem solving routinely overlook as much as 80 percent of the potential solution space and are even unaware that they are doing so [15, 28]. Given the relatively small capacity of working memory, it is no surprise that subjects often work within narrow and bounded areas of their knowledge networks.

One simple way to overcome these barriers is to deliver external stimuli to the problem solvers as they work. Additional context-relevant stimuli (such as hints provided by a facilitator) may help the problem solver activate frames that may not lie in the direct path of automatic spreading activation and explore vastly different areas of their knowledge network, thereby avoiding the experience of being “stuck in a rut.” Problem solving research suggests that subjects often need to be given covert (and overt!) “instructions” before problem solving can occur [30, 56]. In the absence of such facilitation, it appears that problem solving can be remarkably difficult. The

CNM therefore posits that facilitation is a vital component of generating creative solutions to problems.

## Summary of Cognitive Network Model Assumptions

The CNM of creativity begins with the assumption that knowledge is represented in the mind as complex bundles of information (frames) that are highly associative in nature. The associative links in this network vary in their strength. Stronger links are present between frames that are proximally closer to one another in the knowledge network, and thus are more closely related. Conversely, relatively weak links may exist between frames that are very distant from one another in the knowledge network, and thus less related to one another. The result is a rich and highly interconnected network that encompasses the totality of our knowledge and experience. By traversing the links that connect some activated frame to other frames within our knowledge network, activation of successive frames spreads through memory causing yet other frames to become candidates for subsequent activation.

Creative solutions to problems typically result from the combination of frames from disparate areas of the knowledge network within the context of the problem at hand. Combinations resulting from more distant frames have the potential to yield solutions of greater creativity than those resulting from more proximally located frames. While engaged in problem solving, people may rely heavily upon their past experiences and knowledge. However, due to the potentially limiting effects of reusing routine patterns of activation combined with the limitations of working memory, people in general are often poor at solving problems without some form of assistance.

## Propositions of the Cognitive Network Model of Creativity

Reasoning from the assumptions presented above, this section derives several propositions for the CNM of creativity. Figure 1 illustrates the main propositions of CNM. Each arrow indicates the direction of causation between two constructs. The CNM of creativity posits that creative solutions are caused by the formation of new associations (links) in working memory of previously unrelated frames in the knowledge network [37]. Conditions that increase the likelihood of forming new associations among previously unrelated frames should therefore also increase the production of creative solutions (arrow 1). The likelihood of forming new associations between previously unrelated frames increases as the disparity among the frames in working memory increases (arrow 2; [46]). However, cognitive load interferes with the ability to create new associations among frames currently in working memory (arrow 3; [10]). Cognitive load will increase as the number of stimuli to which problem solvers are exposed to per unit of time increases (arrow 4; [5, 44]) and as the disparity among the currently active frames increases (arrow 5; [5, 44]). However, when frames are simultaneously active in working memory, they may become chunked into larger frames that can be wielded as a single frame. This chunking can serve to reduce cognitive load (arrow 6; [7]). The process of chunking, due to the combination of two or more frames, is also likely to reduce the overall diversity among currently activated frames (arrow 7). Simultaneously active frames that are highly diverse are not as likely to chunk as readily as frames that are more closely related (arrow 8; [7]). Finally, as the diversity of stimuli increases, so too does the disparity among the active frames (arrow 9; [5]). Thus, increasing the stimulus diversity increases the potential of making new associations among active frames, but it also consumes greater amounts of available cognitive resources.

## Evaluating the Cognitive Network Model of Creativity

IF EXTERNAL STIMULI ACTIVATE NEW NODES on the cognitive network, and if creative solutions arise from novel juxtapositions of concepts, then as the rate at which external stimuli are introduced increases, the generation of creative solutions should also increase. However, each new stimulus imposes additional cognitive load to process it. If some cognitive resources are required to generate a creative solution, and if cognitive resources are limited, then as the rate of stimulation increases, at some point the cognitive load of processing each additional stimulus should overwhelm ones ability to create a new solution for the task at hand. After that point, increasing the rate of stimulation should hinder the emergence of creative solutions. Therefore, all other things being equal, the relationship between the rate at which new stimuli are introduced and the production of new solutions should be an inverted “U.” Therefore, if one were to introduce new external stimuli at a constant rate, people who are trying to devise creative solutions in response to external stimuli should be more creative than people who try to devise creative solutions in the absence of external stimuli. People working in response to external stimuli should produce solutions that are, on the average, more creative and more of the solutions they produce should be creative. Therefore,

H1: People who generate solutions in response to external stimuli delivered at a constant rate that does not overwhelm their attention resources should produce solutions with a higher average creativity rating than do people who generate solutions in the absence of external prompts.

H2: People who generate solutions in response to external stimuli delivered at a constant rate that does not overwhelm their attention resources should produce a higher concentration of creative solutions than do people who generate solutions in the absence of external prompts.

There are at least three sources of cognitive load for people generating solutions in response to external stimuli: processing the stimuli, activating new frames, and generating new solutions. If one holds the rate of stimuli constant across several experimental treatments, and if one were to use the same prompts across several treatments, one could control for the cognitive load of processing the stimuli. If one were to measure the concentration of creative solutions, as well as an absolute count of the number of creative solutions, one could control for cognitive load required to generate new solutions. Controlling for those two sources of cognitive load would allow one to explore the cognitive load of activating new frames and the way that cost balances with the production of creative solutions. CNM is not yet sufficiently mature to predict how many changes-of-topic would be required to induce so much cognitive load that creativity decreases, or whether increasing the number of topicchanges among the prompts improve creativity or interfere with. To gain further insight about the impact of topic-switching upon the resulting level of creativity, we investigated the research question:

R1: How do varying patters of topic-switching (manipulated through external stimuli) affect the production of creative solutions?

## Experimental Tasks

To minimize the risk that results could be an artifact of a particular task rather than of the experimental treatments, two problem solving scenarios were used for this experiment: (1) GOMPIN (Government of a Mid-Pacific Island Nation), a crisis response scenario for providing drinkable water to the victims of a typhoon, and (2) School of Business, an organizational improvement scenario for a hypothetical school of business suffering from 19 interrelated symptoms. Both of these tasks present the participants with ill-structured problems [66], which lend themselves well to creative problem solving situations [59].

It is often the case, as it was in this study, that one can know the criteria for judging the utility of solutions before solutions have been generated. Following the advice of Davis [18], the following five criteria for judging the solutions generated during the experiment were derived for each task.

Good solutions for the GOMPIN task are those that:

1. provide sufficient water to residents of the capital city;

2. can be implemented quickly;

3. can be implemented inexpensively;

4. can be easily implemented; and

5. require minimal heavy equipment.

Good solutions for the School of Business are those that:

1. eliminate the symptoms felt by the School of Business without causing new symptoms;

2. can be implemented quickly;

3. can be implemented inexpensively;

4. can be easily implemented; and

5. are acceptable to all success-critical stakeholders in the School of Business.

Next, four analogous prompts were created for each of the five solution criteria, resulting in a series of 20 prompts for each task. The number of solution criteria and the number of prompts were carefully chosen to allow specific comparisons across both treatments and tasks. ## Independent Variable

The independent variable was the number of topic-changes in a set of brainstorming prompts. If only a single prompt were used to initiate a brainstorming session, then there would be no prompt-related changes-of-topic in an ideation session. If each prompt in a set were to use different phrasing to present the same concept, there would likewise be no change-of-topic during the session. It would be unlikely that such prompts would activate nodes that are cognitively distant from one another in the long-term memory network. On the other hand, if each prompt in a set were to present a different concept than the one that preceded it, there would be as many changes-of-topic as there were prompts.

In three treatments in this study, participants received an oral prompt from a moderator every 2 minutes for 40 minutes, for a total of 20 prompts. The prompts covered a total of five topics, with four prompts for each topic. Each prompt suggested a way to think about the solutions to the problem at hand. All treatments within a task used the same 20 prompts (Table 1). We manipulated the number of changes-of-topic during ideation by arranging the prompts in three different orders:

Quartet variation. In the lowest-variety treatment, the “quartet” variation, prompts were arranged in blocks of four; four different prompts from one topic, then four different prompts on another topic, and so on, following the pattern AAAA-BBBB-CCCC-DDDD-EEEE. Thus, topic changes occurred every eight minutes.

Duet variation. In a more moderate-variety treatment, the “duet” variation, prompts were arranged in pairs; two prompts on one solution criteria, then two prompts on another solution criteria, and so on. The pattern of topics repeated twice, like this: AA-BB-CC-DD-EE-AA-BB-CC-DD-EE. Thus, topic changes occurred every four minutes.

Solo variation. In the highest variety treatment, the “solo” variation, the prompts were organized so that each prompt addressed a different topic than the one that preceded it. Each solution criteria recurred five times, following the pattern A-B-C-D-E-A-B-C-D-E-A-B-C-D-E-A-B-C-D-E-A-B-C-D-E. Thus, topic changes occurred every two minutes.

## Subjects and Procedures

Two hundred forty-four upper-division management information systems (MIS) undergraduate students at a large public university participated in 61 four-person brainstorming groups. Because the experiment purports to test cognitive capabilities that are assumed to be present in children and adults across cultures, students were deemed to be appropriate subjects for this study. Each group was randomly assigned to one of the above treatments. All treatments took place in a 28-seat conference room equipped with a computer for each seat. All computers provided access to collaborative brainstorming software using a GSS.<sup>1</sup> Prior to each session, workstations were randomly assigned to groups of four, and a separate collaborative brainstorming activity was started for each group of four workstations. Any contributions made by one user in a group would also appear on the screens of the other three users in the same group, but would not appear on the screens of people who were not in the same group.

At the start of each experimental session, up to 28 participants entered the room and chose their own seats. Participants did not know that choosing a seat would assign them to a group, and did not know the identity of their group members during or after the exercise. Reading from a script, a moderator introduced the participants to the brainstorming software with a warm-up task. Still working from scripts, the moderator then instructed the participants on their experimental task

The control group brainstormed on their own without any form of prompting from a facilitator. The following instructions were provided to the control group:

The process is simple. Imagine each of you started with blank sheet of paper in front of you. When I say go, each of you will write one solution on that piece of paper, and then throw your paper on a pile in the middle of the room. You will then grab another paper from that pile, and read the solution that someone else has already written on it. After reading it, you should respond to that previous solution in one of three ways:

1. expand on the solution, adding details;

2. argue with the solution; or

3. you might be inspired to a completely new solution.

These directions will appear on the large screen in front of the room in case you lose track of them during the task. When you have finished writing your response, you will again toss your paper on the pile, grab one of the other papers, and read the previous solution. We will continue along in this fashion until you run out of solutions.

Now, electronic brainstorming works exactly the same way, except you have an electronic page. When you finish typing in a solution, just press the F9 key to send your solution back to the group. You will get back another page with someone else’s solution on it. Read that new solution and then respond to in one of three ways:

1. expand on the solution, adding details;

2. argue with the solution; or

3. generate a completely new solution.

In either case, when you have finished typing your solution, click the SUBMIT button to send your solution back to the group. You will get back another electronic page.

Read the last solution on the page, respond to it, and exchange your page again.

For each of the treatment groups, one prompt was read aloud and simultaneously displayed on a large screen in the front of the room every two minutes. The following instructions were provided to the treatment groups:

The process is simple. Imagine each of you started with blank sheet of paper in front of you. I will provide you with a specific goal or objective that will also be displayed on the large screen in front of the room. Each of you will then write one solution on that piece of paper, and then throw your paper on a pile in the middle of the room. You will then grab another paper from that pile, and read the solution that has already been written on it.

I will then provide you with another specific goal or objective. Each of you will write down a new solution to this new goal. When you have finished writing your solution, exchange your page for a new one, and then begin reading any previous solutions already on this new page. The goal that I provide for you will always appear on the large screen in front of the room in case you lose track of it. The process will continue in this fashion until we address each of the important issues of this task.

Now, electronic brainstorming works exactly the same way, except you have an electronic page. When you finish typing in a solution, click the SUBMIT button to send your solution back to the group. You will then get back another page with someone else’s solutions on it. You should read these new solutions that are on this page.

After a short period of time, I will provide you with a new goal that will also appear on the large screen in the front of the room. Each of you will then type one solution to this new problem on your electronic page. When you click the SUBMIT button to send your solution to the group, you will get back another electronic page. Be sure to read the responses on this new page. In a short while, I will read aloud a new goal, for which you should type in another solution. Switch your pages again, and then read what is on your new page. We will continue in this fashion until we complete this task.

All subjects brainstormed for a period of 40 minutes [25], after which they were thanked and released.

## Dependent Variables

To evaluate the hypotheses and compare the different treatments, the following data was collected from each group:

• Total number of contributions: the total number of contributions typed into the electronic brainstorming software by each group.

• Number of duplicate solutions: the number of solutions that duplicated the verb– object pair contained in another solution from that same group.

• Number of nonsolutions: on-task comments that do not directly contain a solution; for example, one group member asking another group member for clarification concerning their solution, and so on.

• Number of off-topic comments: comments that are not relevant to the problem solving task; for example, “I am hungry.”

• Number of unique solutions: the number of nonredundant, on-task solutions contributed by each group.

• Elaboration coefficient: elaborations are task-relevant references to previously submitted solutions. A calculated elaboration coefficient of 0.0 means that no contributions elaborated on previous contributions, whereas an elaboration coefficient of 1.0 means that every contribution after the first was an elaboration on that same contribution [71].

• Creativity score for each solution: each solution was individually scored for creativity by several domain experts, the creativity level for any solution is the mean of the four creativity scores for that solution.

• Creative solution concentration: the ratio of the number of solutions whose average creativity score exceeded a particular threshold value to the number of unique solutions for each group.

We approached the measurement of creativity from two different units of analysis: the solution and the group. This helped control for the possibility of instrument bias and helped triangulate on the effects of the experimental treatments. Trained, treatment-blind coders extracted a master set of 1,560 and 1,345 nonredundant actionable solutions from the transcripts of the GOMPIN and School of Business brainstorming activities, respectively. Inter-coder agreement on the unique solution list was higher than 99 percent. Treatment-blind experts in the task domains used the consensual assessment technique [1] to rate each solution on a five-point Likert scale for creativity. Runco and Bahleda [60] argue that creativity ratings from expert raters using implicit theories derived from their experiences have more ecological validity than do ratings derived from specific criteria. Amabile [1] argues that this assessment technique is cognitively identical to real-world evaluations of creative work. Six individuals with disaster relief expertise from the International Red Cross and the United States Military rated the solutions for the GOMPIN task. Four university officials rated the solutions for the School of Business task. The judges for both tasks had an average of more than 15 years of relevant field experience. Three raters evaluated each solution. Inter-rater reliability was 0.834 for the GOMPIN task, and 0.910 for the School of Business task.

The average creativity score for a given brainstorming group was calculated by adding up the creativity scores assigned to all the unique solutions produced by that group, and dividing by the number of unique solutions produced by the group. The concentration of creative solutions for a given brainstorming group was calculated by counting the number solutions proposed by the group whose creativity scores exceeded a threshold level, and dividing the count by the total number of nonredundant, on-topic solutions. Items with a score higher than 3.5, the midpoint of the five-point scale, were counted as creative. Items with a score of 3.5 or below were counted as not creative.

However, the results of the exploratory analysis suggest that directed brainstorming is not an unmixed blessing. It appears to improve creativity when prompt topics change every two minutes (solo treatment) and when topics change every eight minutes (quartet treatment), but it may actually diminish creativity relative to the control group when prompt topics change every four minutes (duet treatment).

The creativity data presented in Tables 2 and 4 also show several clear trends. For both experimental tasks, subjects participating in the quartet treatment outperformed subjects from both the free brainstorming and the duet treatments. Similarly, subjects in the solo treatment outperformed subjects from the free brainstorming and the duet treatment. This result appears to be very robust, as it is insensitive to the particular creativity threshold value that was used to perform the analysis. The assumptions of CNM suggest reasons why these results occur.

In the solo treatment, the topic of the directed brainstorming prompt changed every two minutes. As a result of the constant changing of the topic from which the prompt derived, the working memory of the participants is likely to contain frames that were activated from diverse and noncontiguous regions of the knowledge network, thereby increasing the associative distance among the now salient frames. This increased associative distance directly increases the likelihood or probability that new associations may be discovered among this set of salient frames. When new associations are formed among frames that derived from disparate areas of knowledge, creative solutions are more likely to result. Thus, the rapid switching of topics among the prompts in the solo treatment is likely to have helped the participants form new and creative combinations of familiar concepts which lead to higher levels of creativity, a finding that is consistent with a great deal of prior theorizing and research [12, 15, 43, 47].

The CNM and the principles of spreading activation also explain why the groups participating in the quartet treatment were so creative. In the quartet treatment, four consecutive prompts were delivered to the subjects from each solution criteria before the solution criteria changed. This specific pattern of prompting kept the subjects focused on a specific topic for a period of eight minutes, allowing a sufficient quantity of time for the subjects to traverse extended “paths” of frame activation through their knowledge networks. By staying on topic for longer periods of time, the participants were able to more thoroughly explore potential combinations among a given set of activated frames and their associated paths of spreading activation. By allowing more time to explore any given set of salient frames, the subjects had a relatively higher probability of discovering some new association, eventually leading to a greater concentration of creative solutions. Thus, with less frequent topic switches, subjects were able to “move beyond” the most obvious solutions that immediately come to mind and explore new alternatives in greater depth.

Given the explanations of the relatively high concentration of creative solutions that resulted from the solo and quartet treatments, the relatively poor creative performance of the subjects in the duet treatment is easily explained. Recall that topic changes occurred every four minutes in the duet treatment. Thus, subjects participating in the duet treatment received precisely half as many topic changes when compared to subjects from the solo treatment. This reduced level of topic changes in the prompting pattern is highly likely to have reduced the overall associative distance that existed among the set of salient frames at any given time. This leads to a reduced likelihood of forming new associations, likely resulting in a comparatively lower concentration of creative solutions. Moreover, since the topic switches occurred twice as fast as they did in the quartet treatment, it is likely that there was not a sufficient amount of time for the subjects to follow patterns of spreading activation beyond the set of obvious solutions that immediately come to mind. Thus, the benefits derived from frequent topic-switching in the solo treatment were absent in the duet treatment. Furthermore, the benefits derived from infrequent topic-switching in the quartet treatment were also absent. In light of this discussion, it seems that the specific pattern of prompting in the duet treatment resulted in task interference, which can account for why the creativity performance of the subjects in the duet treatment was inferior to that of the subjects in the free brainstorming treatment.

## Most Creative Solutions

Efforts to answer the research questions posed at the beginning of this paper lead to an analysis of the top 50 most creative solutions for the School of Business task (Table 6), revealing several interesting trends. First, the subjects that participated in free brainstorming addressed the greatest number of problem symptoms, whereas subjects in the duet treatment addressed the fewest number of symptoms. Second, subjects in the duet treatment had the greatest number of duplicate solutions of any of the treatments. It appears that the duet facilitation pattern caused the unintended effect of having subjects focus on a narrow subset of the entire problem space, as more than half of the 50 most creative solutions addressed only 2 of the symptoms (lowquality teaching and limited budget). Third, and perhaps the most interesting finding of the present analysis, however, is that 12 of the 50 most creative solutions resulting from the solo treatment do not map neatly onto any specific symptom from the School of Business task; these solutions are more broad in the scope of the action they recommend. For example, one solution from the solo treatment was, “Have social events that will allow teachers and students to get to know each other outside of the school environment.” Of all the treatments, subjects that participated in the duet treatment generated the greatest number of duplicate solutions, focused most heavily on fewer of the symptoms, and failed to address the greatest number of symptoms given in the task description. Given this discussion, it is likely that the patterns of spreading activation that resulted from the duet prompting pattern lead to some form of functional fixedness [24] for the problem solvers.

## Productivity and Task Focus

Finally, Tables 7 and 8 highlight some interesting trends that clearly differentiate the effects of the four treatments. First, Tables 1 and 2 show that, although the number of contributions was greatest in the free brainstorming treatment, the number of unique solutions offered by these same groups was also the lowest of all treatments. Thus, free brainstorming resulted in the largest amount discussion of the proposed solutions among the group members as evidenced by the relatively large elaboration coefficient. The significance of this measure is that, as people elaborate on solutions, the solutions accrue meaning and significance among the group members, increasing their usefulness and value. Second, it is interesting that each of the facilitated treatments resulted in much greater task focus than did free brainstorming. A comparison of all four techniques clearly indicates that the greatest number of both nonsolutions (task-relevant responses that did not recommend any specific course of action that would impact the problem) and off-topic comments (those that do not address the problem task at all) occurred in groups using free brainstorming. Alternately, the quantity of unique solutions for all three facilitated treatments was substantially higher than that resulting from free brainstorming. Thus, in comparison with free brainstorming, it appears that the facilitated treatments helped the groups avoid becoming “stuck in a rut” and increased the level of task focus by providing external stimuli to the participants as they worked. This finding is easy to account for using the model.

The design of this study made it possible to compare the effects of switching topics at different rates, but the theory suggests that subsequent studies could produce more powerful effects through certain design changes. If the theory holds, a study that compared 20 prompts on 20 different topics and 20 prompts on the same topic to the control should produce stronger differences in creativity than did this study, which used only 5 different topics. Another study might produce stronger creativity effects by providing prompts every 60 or 90 seconds instead of every 2 minutes. It is not yet clear, however, at what point increasing the prompt rate would overwhelm cognitive resources and begin to harm creativity rather than help it.

This first empirical test of CNM raises a number of questions that subsequent study must address. When using a frequent-switching strategy, what is the optimum rate for delivering prompts? Every 30 seconds? Every 2.5 minutes? Is the optimal prompt rate the same for an infrequent-switching strategy? What is the worst combination of prompt rate and topic switching, and why? Once optimum prompt rates and optimum topic-switching frequencies have been identified, will people be more creative at the frequent-switching optimum or at the infrequent-switching optimum? Are there other optima than those suggested by this study? How could one characterize the differences between solutions produced by free brainstorming, by high-frequency switching directed brainstorming, and by low-frequency switching directed brainstorming? What differences might emerge in the degree to which teams understand and implement the creative solutions developed under different strategies?

## Conclusion

THIS FIRST EMPIRICAL INVESTIGATION OF THE CNM suggests that it may be a useful model for explaining and exploring the emergence of creative solutions in the human mind. The most prominent feature of the solutions that resulted from free brainstorming is the large elaboration coefficient. This finding suggests that free brainstorming may be most appropriate in several situations. For example, free brainstorming seems well suited to circumstances in which the problem is not well defined, it is necessary to explore the problem in greater depth in order to make sure that all participants have the same understanding of the problem at hand, or when it is important for the participants to build consensus concerning their task. The greater level of interaction or conversation among the participants through the electronic brainstorming environment, as indicated by the high elaboration coefficient, would naturally be an asset in any of the above scenarios.

In contrast to free brainstorming, the solutions that resulted from the solo treatment tended to be highly focused upon the specific solution criterion of the prompts. The nature of the responses indicate that the solo prompting pattern may be best suited to circumstances in which it is important to focus the efforts of the problem solvers on very specific areas of the solution space as well as to generate creative solutions. Thus, appropriate circumstances for applying the solo prompting pattern may include situations in which it is important to find creative solutions that do not necessarily need to take into account the goals or desires of a wide variety of constituencies or stakeholders.

The results of the quartet treatment, while leading to solutions of a similar level of creativity as did the solo treatment, took on a very different nature. Solutions from the quartet facilitation pattern tended to be very widely focused, simultaneously addressing many of the problem dimensions as represented by the various solution criteria. Thus, appropriate circumstances for applying the quartet prompting may include situations in which it is important to find creative solutions that do necessarily need to take into account the goals or desires of a wide variety of constituencies or stakeholders.

Finally, since it led to levels of creativity that closely matched that of free brainstorming and resulted in both the greatest quantity of duplicated solutions and the greatest number of neglected problem dimensions, the duet prompting pattern is not recommended until more can be learned about its resulting interaction levels among the participants.

The findings highlighted above were consistent across both problem solving tasks, suggesting that the results do indeed derive from the manipulation caused by the specific facilitation patterns rather than from some other random effect. The remarkable finding in terms of implications for research is that all three seeded treatments utilized precisely the same set of prompts—the only aspect that changed was the order in which the prompts were delivered to the problem solvers.

The results of this study once again highlight the importance of seemingly small details to the design and deployment brainstorming tools and techniques. In this study, two-minute changes in event timing produced a startling difference in results. Creativity researchers and GSS researchers must diligently report even minor details of their treatments. It may be that some of the seemingly conflicting results in the GSS literature were caused by unreported differences in experiment design. GSS researchers must also be careful to refrain from making sweeping generalizations about “the effects of GSS,” and to confine themselves to conclusions about their specific methodology.

This study is, of course, subject to the typical set of limitations that pertains to studies performed with undergraduate students in a laboratory setting. Caution is suggested in extending this research beyond the aforementioned setting; however, given that similar effects were obtained across two problem solving tasks, the findings encourage further experimentation. Specifically, we propose that the CNM of creativity is a useful springboard that serves several important functions. First, perhaps this newfound understanding of the creative problem solving process will enable practitioners to better leverage techniques designed to result in creative solutions. Second, the model should help researchers and practitioners alike to create new and improved methods for generating creative solutions to the problems that they face. Finally, an increased understanding of the creative process through exploration of the model may help to lend additional insights to the remaining perspectives of the Four P’s of Creativity and may help to address the unanswered questions previously highlighted concerning creativity.
