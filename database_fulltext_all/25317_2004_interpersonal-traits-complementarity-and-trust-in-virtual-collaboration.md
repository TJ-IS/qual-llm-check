# Interpersonal Traits, Complementarity, and Trust in Virtual Collaboration

HOUGHTON G. BROWN, MARSHALL SCOTT POOLE, AND THOMAS L. RODGERS

HOUGHTON G. BROWN is a third-year Ph.D. student in the Information and Operations Management Department of the Mays Business School at Texas A&M University. His research focus has been computer-mediated collaboration and telemedicine. Prior to his Ph.D. pursuits, he served as a secondary school teacher and coach. Mr. Brown holds an M.Ed. and M.B.A. from the University of Houston.

MARSHALL SCOTT POOLE is a Professor in the Information and Operations Management and the Communication Departments at Texas A&M University. His research interests include collaboration technologies, implementation of information systems, organizational communication, and group decision-making. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Wisconsin–Madison.

THOMAS L. RODGERS is an Assistant Professor in the Information and Operations Management department of the Mays Business School at Texas A&M University. He has a Ph.D. from the University of Arizona, and has extensive public and private consulting experience. Dr. Rodgers’s research focus is information technology applied to health care, learning, expertise, and creativity in collaborative environments.

ABSTRACT: Trust has been a focus of research on virtual collaboration in distributed teams, e-commerce, e-learning, and telemedicine. Central to several models of trust and virtual collaboration is user’s disposition to trust. This construct, however, has generally been conceptualized in as a stand-alone trait without a substantive theoretical background in personality theory. This paper advances the interpersonal circumplex model (ICM) as a theoretical framework for understanding the role of personal traits in collaboration in virtual contexts. The ICM posits that tendencies in interpersonal interaction stem from personal dispositions that can be understood in terms of dimensions of power and affiliation, fundamental constituents of user’s personality. We develop a model that proposes that interpersonal traits, specifically, personality type as defined by the circumplex, affect the individual’s disposition to trust, perceived trustworthiness, communication, and thereby affects willingness to collaborate and the sustainability and productivity of the collaboration. The model enables us to unpack the black box concepts of disposition to trust, faith in others, and trusting stance that are currently incorporated in theories of trust in information systems. The theory also enables explanation of trust dynamics at the dyadic and group levels. We develop propositions positing that individual’s traits and dyadic complementarity are mediating factors in interpersonal trust and willingness to use new technologies and significantly affect the initiation, duration, and productivity of computer-mediated collaboration.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: collaboration, computer-mediated collaboration, interpersonal traits, technology acceptance, telemedicine, trust, virtual collaboration, virtual teams, virtual trust.

TRUST IS UBIQUITOUS IN HUMAN AFFAIRS. Mutual trust stokes and sustains interpersonal relationships [13, 18, 69, 90] and helps maintain the social fabric [5, 51]. Trust is the lubricant of commerce [15], essential to negotiations [48], and has been related to competitive advantage [4, 14, 78]. It is a factor in leadership [51, 77], effective decision-making [93], innovation [74], and managerial effectiveness [53, 87].

Trust is the ambient current of e-environments. It is a small wonder, then, that trust has been a focus of research involving technology acceptance [66], virtual collaboration in distributed teams [6, 35, 36], e-commerce [4, 8, 27, 28, 58], e-learning [79], and telemedicine [10, 65]. For participants accustomed to face-to-face contact, the uncertainty and ambiguity inherent in virtual relationships is likely to raise doubts that may constrain interactions and transactions—and trust, by definition, mitigates such constraints. Understanding how trust is built and maintained in virtual relationships is important to the design of virtual teams and marketplaces and to the development of processes that enable them to function effectively.

The predominant approach in research on virtual collaboration has been to explain trust as a function of the following: (1) social characteristics of the interactors, such as prior familiarity, reputation, competence, and ability; (2) immediate outcomes of interaction processes, such as judgments of reliability, openness, integrity, trustworthiness, and benevolence; and (3) institutions, such as social norms, legal structures, and privacy policies [27, 36, 52, 65, 94]. Such an approach highlights the immediate context and interchange among participants as the major determinants of trust. It has the virtue of acknowledging that interaction and the information it provides are critical to the development and maintenance of trust. The question arises, however, is “What factors shape participants’ responses to these factors and interpersonal interaction?” The model presented here attempts to address this question by moving a step further back in the causal chain to work out how personality traits of the collaborators affect willingness to adopt information systems (IS) and collaborate with others in virtual contexts.

This paper employs the interpersonal circumplex model (ICM) to develop propositions related to participation in virtual collaborations. The ICM is a well-established model of personality that links personality types to the interpersonal behaviors they are likely to foster [46] (see also [11, 40, 46, 88]). Circumplex theorists contend that the traits defined in the interpersonal circumplex influence trust as well as other cognitive and affective processes that may affect collaborative behavior. Our model posits that interpersonal factors—such as propensity to trust, situational trust, and affect and technology acceptance factors, such as perceived ease-of-use, control over technology, and computer anxiety—are, in part, caused by personality traits identified within the circumplex. These interpersonal and technology acceptance factors, in turn, influence intention to participate and effectiveness of participation in virtual collaboration. While we believe our analysis is generalizable to many types of virtual collaboration, we will illustrate the propositions using telemedicinebased collaboration.

## Trust and Collaboration Via Information Technology

WE WILL ADOPT MAYER ET AL.’S DEFINITION OF TRUST:

[Trust] is the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other party will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party. [52, p. 712; emphasis added]

Although more general definitions of the concept have been offered (e.g., [34]), Mayer et al.’s [52] definition is particularly applicable to situations involving interpersonal trust and, therefore, is well-suited for our purposes. This definition highlights not only the positive aspect of trust as opening oneself to another but the vulnerability the party undertakes when trusting an uncontrollable other. It also focuses on a generalized belief rather than specific judgments of integrity or benevolence of the other. Although specific judgments may contribute to trust, trust itself involves a general intention to open oneself to another [27]. This willingness to be vulnerable has been similarly argued by Mishra to be “based on the belief that the latter party is (a) competent, (b) open, (c) concerned, and (d) reliable” [62, p. 265]. Gefen et al. [27] attempted to distinguish definitions of trust as affect from definitions of trust as generalized belief, but we would argue that affect is a concomitant of this generalized belief. Affect is a dynamic psychological construct that has a reciprocal relationship with trust in interaction [21]. Relatively stable psychological traits, such as those identified by the ICM, influence an individual’s disposition to trust and shape affective responses.

A number of scholars marshal evidence that trust promotes cooperative behavior, a critical component of collaboration [3, 24, 52, 53]. Jones and George [38] discuss seven distinct processes that trust promotes that may increase interpersonal cooperation and teamwork: broad, flexible role definitions, a communal rather than individualist orientation, high levels of confidence in others, help-seeking behavior, free exchange of knowledge and information, subjugation of personal needs and ego, and high levels of involvement in the activities of others. These processes mark trust as an important factor in collaboration.

In virtual interaction, trust is likely to be particularly important, because collaboration can be effective only if both parties enter into it with a willingness to open themselves to one another and cooperate in carrying out a task, solving a problem, and learning [36]. Collaboration requires intensive interaction that creates dependencies parties could exploit if they so desired. Trust is the glue that binds collaborators by fostering faith that both parties will contribute and not behave opportunistically.

Telemedicine is a case in point. Most justifications of telemedicine emphasize the value of services delivered from caregiver to patient via telemedicine. Another potential source of value, however, is the collaborative interaction among caregivers that promotes learning and improves quality of care [65]. First- and second-order learning can create a learning community that distributes and disseminates knowledge across the telemedicine network, and collaborative relationships among community members are essential for such learning to occur [16].

Beyond the need to collaborate to get telemedicine work done and promote learning, trust is vital to the profession of medicine. Health care is predicated on trust: patients must trust their caregivers; physicians must trust their staff, and staff, their physicians; and, patients and caregivers must trust the medical institution and insurance providers. Contemporary medicine operates within an accelerated and uncertain environment characterized by a rapidly changing and ever-expanding knowledge base, increased uncertainty, increasing equivocality, and time compression [65]. Telemedicine adds an additional layer of complexity, since it represents a new mode of practice for most caregivers. In his analytical survey of the telemedicine literature, Miller [61] notes the numerous constraints slowing the adoption of telemedicine: uncertainties arise around issues of cost, quality, reimbursement, and control. Feasibility assessments are undermined by inadequate quantitative information. Qualitative dimensions include the ineffable effects such as touch, tone, and interpersonal treatment. Furthermore, when caregivers are asked to collaborate with others in telemedicine, several vulnerabilities—points of practitioner resistance—are highlighted, including potential loss of patients to other doctors, loss of the connection to the patient afforded by immediate presence or equipment failure, and loss of control over patient care. Specialists are concerned about the consequences of the knowledge-sharing that is facilitated by teleconsultations and the potential diminishing of future referrals: as the general (or nurse-) practitioner teleconsults with the specialist, the practitioner’s know-how appreciates and he or she, thus, gains confidence in his or her ability to make future diagnoses and prescribe remedies—consequently depriving the specialist of future income [56, 61]. These disincentives to participation can be mitigated by trust. Alternatively, this empowerment, vis-à-vis knowledge-sharing, may be promoted by means of partnering affiliative, collaborative specialists with equally affiliative and receptive practitioners. Hence, understanding the factors and processes that create and sustain trust is important to effective collaboration in telemedicine contexts. Just as trust is essential to collaboration in telemedicine, distrust can be a barrier to its effectiveness.

A prerequisite for developing trusting relationships is user acceptance of the technology utilized in telemedicine. Obviously, this is a precondition for all information technology (IT) mediated work, but it has additional implications for trust in working relationships. Trust contributes to effective, rewarding interactions, but it is also a product of them. Zand’s [21] well-known and well-supported model of trust describes a self-reinforcing cycle in which trust leads to positive attributions about others and positive interactions, which in turn increases the level of trust, which increases positive interaction, and so on (the same sort of cycle can also develop and build distrust).

The medium through which parties interact, therefore, has critical impact on the development and maintenance of trust. Switching from more familiar face-to-face and telephone contexts to the more advanced IT involved in applications such as telemedicine has the potential to change the dynamics of trust if participants are not comfortable with the technology and are not open to learning how to effectively use it. Thus, participants’ degree of acceptance of new technology is an additional factor in effective collaboration.

Pavlou [66] proposes an integrative model that incorporates trust and perceived risk as links in the technology acceptance model (TAM). Pavlou provides evidence that trust in an e-commerce site directly affects the consumers’ intention to transact and also affects perceived risk, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use, which in turn also affect intention to transact [66, p. 123]. Pavlou focuses on the user’s current state of trust; we will extend his model to propose that user traits and consequential disposition to trust influence one’s technology acceptance and subsequent participation in virtual collaboration. As McKnight et al. [59] argue, when an individual is unfamiliar with another person or setting [58], it is not the individual’s trust that precipitates his willingness to engage or accept the technology, but rather, it is his disposition to trust. This disposition, we will argue, is a function of one’s personality traits, and one’s traits, in turn, impact the quality and effectiveness of a technologyenabled collaboration. In addition, we will argue that some personality types are more open to innovation and learning new technologies than others.

Our selection of collaboration in telemedicine as our exemplary case goes hand-inhand with two scope conditions for our model. First, the model takes the dyad as its basic unit of analysis. Most telemedicine involves point-to-point connections, therefore dyadic collaboration is primary. Team-based collaboration is also possible in telemedicine, but it is not likely to be the modal approach, and it involves complex relationships that are beyond the scope of the present model. Second, the model focuses on the decision to initiate collaboration and the first stages of use. In telemedicine, the key issue at present is to sustain networks by inducing caregivers to participate. Traits that create a disposition to trust are likely to have their strongest effects in the initial stages of collaboration; in later stages, experience with the other will be far more important than dispositions [52]. Traits set the mold for future collaborations, but the experience of collaborating with the other reforms the original figure into the shape of the long-term collaborative relationship.

## Research on Disposition to Trust

ONE OF THE EARLIEST STUDIES that focused on disposition toward trust was by Morton Deutsch [17, 18], who found that subjects playing an experimental game were either consistently trusting and trustworthy or they were consistently untrusting and untrustworthy as they played various configurations of the game. Subjects who were suspicious of their anonymous counterpart were more likely to exploit their counterpart’s trust when given the same opportunity. Alternately, those students who were trusting and trustworthy indicated that they “also expected trustworthy choices from the other (and) thought the other would be trusting” [18, p. 139]. Deutsch interpreted his results as indicating a projection of self onto another or cognitive consistency: “expect others to do unto you as you would do unto them” [18, p. 139].

Rotter [71, 72] developed a scale for the measurement of interpersonal trust, “an expectancy held by an individual or group that the word, promise, verbal or written statement of another individual or group can be relied upon” [71, p. 651]. Rotter viewed trust as a stable intra-individual characteristic that influenced interpersonal interactions with others. Representative items from this scale included, “in dealing with strangers, one is better off to be cautious until they have provided evidence that they are trustworthy” and “parents can be relied upon to keep their promises.”

Current research in the organizational and IS areas includes several trait-like constructs related to trust. Mayer et al. [52] argue that some parties are more inclined to trust than others. This disposition, which they label propensity to trust, is dependent on the trustor’s cultural background, developmental experiences, and his or her personality traits. In the Mayer et al. model [52], three of the four factors identified as contributing to trust—the trustor’s belief in the trustee’s ability, benevolence, and integrity—are mediated by the trustor’s propensity to trust, which also serves as a direct cause of trust. One’s traits—upon which propensity to trust is partially dependent—are viewed as being stable across situations.

In formulating their organizational trust inventory (OTI), Cummings and Bromiley based their OTI, on the individual(s) and the following definition of trust:

Trust will be defined as an individual’s belief or a common belief among a group of individuals that another individual or group (a) makes good-faith efforts to behave in accordance with any commitments both explicit or implicit, (b) is honest in whatever negotiations preceded such commitments, and (c) does not take excessive advantage of another even when the opportunity is available. [14, p. 303]

Cummings and Bromily’s [14] approach to trust outlines what Deutsch summarized earlier: “Mutual trust is most likely to occur when people are positively oriented to each other’s welfare” [17, p. 278]. Deutsch expressed this good-faith, “common belief among a group of individuals” as the complementarity of intention and expectation [17, p. 273].

McKnight et al. [59] point out a paradox in studies conducted to study the development of trust develops over time (e.g., [7, 43]): the research subjects in their studies, in fact, frequently demonstrated high initial trust. McKnight et al. propose studying initial trust formation, “because the results from such studies require an explanation beyond what calculative-based and knowledge-based trust theories provide” [59, p. 473]. McKnight et al. argue that “[i]nitial trust between parties will not be based on any kind of experience with, or firsthand knowledge of, the other party. Rather, it will be based on an individual’s disposition to trust or on institutional cues that enable one person to trust another without firsthand knowledge” [59, p. 474]. In order to shed light on how initial trust forms, McKnight et al. draw from five different research streams on trust–knowledge-based, calculative-based, personality-based, institution-based, and cognition-based. In the McKnight et al. model, the construct disposition to trust affects trusting intention, trusting beliefs, and one’s institutionbased trust [59, pp. 474–476]. Noting that the personality construct disposition to trust builds on the work of Erikson [20] and Rotter [71, 72, 73], McKnight and Chervany [57, 58], and McKnight et al. [59] further decompose disposition to trust into the factors “trusting stance” and “faith in humanity,” two concepts introduced by Rosenberg [70] and Wrightsman [91], respectively.

Several studies incorporate disposition to trust into models of virtual collaboration. Jarvenpaa et al. [36] found that propensity to trust had a robust impact on trust in virtual teams. Paul [65] bundled traits into his construct affect, which is one facet of trust related to collaboration in telemedicine. In their model of e-commerce, customer relationships trust constructs,” McKnight and Chervany [58] provide a well-reasoned argument linking the two constructs of their disposition to trust concept—faith in humanity and trusting stance—with four belief constructs—competence, benevolence, integrity, and predictability—of trusting beliefs. Trusting beliefs along with trust intentions in turn determine one’s trust-related internet behaviors. The four trusting beliefs are important because they inform one’s willingness to depend on the trustee (anthropomorphic technology can be subjected to trust [8, 92]), “because these attributes address nearly every contingent circumstance in the relationship” [58, p. 49]. For purposes of understanding the utilization and success of IT-enabled interactions (i.e., collaboration, e-commerce), McKnight and Chervany [58] argue that it is important to understand the characteristics of the actors who are exhibiting trust through them.

Constructs, such as propensity to trust, disposition to trust, trusting stance, and faith in humanity, are useful, but they are also quite similar to trust—the construct for which they are utilized to explain. Conceptually, they are, at best, one step removed, taking the concept to be explained and positing a general tendency toward it: a person’s trust is explained as a function of his or her tendency to trust. Although empirical results have shown a relationship between these tendencies and trust in virtual contexts, it is not clear how much these findings add to our understanding of the precursors of trust. Using tendency to trust to explain trust is similar to explaining why something burned by stating that it burned because it was flammable. To understand why something has burned, it is better to look to the materials of which it is constituted. In the instance of trust and disposition to trust, we look to personality.

A useful approach to understanding disposition to trust draws on well-established personality theories that are not conceptually related to trust, per se. One influential theory of personality, the ICM [11, 88], defines broad sets of personality characteristics that have direct implications for behavior, affect, and cognition in general, and for trust and technology use in particular. The ICM provides an explanation for trust that is grounded in a strong theoretical base that has been extensively investigated [26, 30]. Instruments to measure ICM traits have undergone extensive psychometric investigation and have been employed in dozens of studies [26, 29, 41, 54, 55, 63, 75, 81, 82, 83, 84, 88, 89]. Several of these studies provide evidence that ICM personality traits have direct impacts on how people engage in interpersonal interaction. Hence, it has a good fit with existing models of trust in virtual contexts that explain trust as a function of interaction between parties.

The Interpersonal Circumplex Model, Trust, and Virtual Collaboration

THE ICM IS BASED ON THE WORK of psychologist and clinician Harry Stack Sullivan [11]. Sullivan argued that personality arises from and is maintained by interactions that the individual engages in with key others. Although genetics and early experiences set parameters for an individual’s personality, it is created and maintained through interactions with others. Kiesler explains that, “Interactants’ interpersonal needs always seek conjoint expression and reliability; hence, interpersonal behavior can be understood only from a systems perspective. This focus on two-person mutual influence on bi-directional causality represents a fundamental novelty of Sullivan’s formulations” [41, p. 186]. Leary [46], Carson [11], Wiggins [88], and other theorists built on Sullivan’s work.

The circumplex model was first formally developed by Leary [46], later to be famous for his experiments with and advocacy of LSD, and his colleagues at the Kaiser Foundation [25, 44, 45]. A number of other scholars have developed interpersonal circumplex models and measurements following Leary [11, 31, 68, 81, 88]. Some researchers (e.g., [81]) have regarded the circumplex primarily in terms of interpersonal behavior, but most scholars agree that is has both trait and behavioral implications. We will follow this tradition.

The circumplex arrays 16 distinct personality traits in a circle, as shown in Figure 1, according to their relation to the constructs of Control (on a continuum from Dominant (A) to Submissive (I)) and Affiliation (on a continuum of Friendly (M) to Hostile (E)). The circumplex is an interpersonal model because it conceptualizes how individuals interact with the intent of eliciting or “pulling” desired reactions from others. The two dimensions that define the circumplex form four quadrants (I–IV, counterclockwise); 16 interpersonal adjectives are arrayed around the circumplex, according to each trait’s composition of Power and Affiliation. As a result of this arrangement, the eight vector pairings are composed of trait opposites, whereas the two defining dimensions, Control and Affiliation, are considered to be orthogonal. Although there are 16 traits in the model (labeled A through ${ \mathrm { P } } ,$ counterclockwise), the circumplex is more conventionally represented as eight vectors. These eight vectors represent blends of the two underlying dimensions of Control and Affiliation, which constitute personality types. For instance, Exhibitionistic (or Gregarious–Extraverted as described by Wiggins [89]) at 45 degrees on the circle, Quadrant I, is a “blend” of Affiliation (Friendliness, M) and Control (Dominance, A).

For each vector that defines pairs of the 16 traits, the origin represents the population norm and the distance of the individual’s score on a vector indicates the degree to which he or she departs from the average in terms of that trait. Salient to collaboration, especially in the sometimes uncertain and cue-constrained virtual environment, the ICM identifies personality traits—and corresponding interpersonal processes—that encourage the tendency to trust (or distrust). The ICM can be used to identify those who, by nature, extend trust, those whose traits engender trust, and vice versa. The ICM enables us to make predictions about the impact of interpersonal traits on individual participation and collaboration. It also incorporates a theory of complementary traits that enables prediction of collaborative effectiveness at the dyadic level.

## An Illustration: Jarvenpaa et al.’s “Trust in Global Teams” Through the Prism of the Circumplex

FOR PURPOSES OF ILLUSTRATION, we will apply the ICM to Jarvenpaa et al.’s [35, 36] reported research on virtual teams and trust. During the spring semester of 1996, Jarvenpaa et al. [35, 36] conducted one of the most comprehensive tests of trust in virtual collaboration: they linked 350 geographically and culturally different students from around the globe for a six-week, trust-in-collaboration study. Small teams were created of four to six students from different countries. Using two separate measures of trust (these measures were modifications of trust measures used by Schoorman et al. [76] and Pearce et al. [67]), Jarvenpaa et al. averaged individual member scores to compose a team score. These were used to differentiate the high trust teams and from low trust teams. Time 1–Time 2 evaluations of trust were constructed reflecting their consistency, decline, or improvement in trust over time. According to Jarvenpaa et al.’s [35, 36] descriptions, the high-trust and low-trust teams here will be differentiated along the dimension of Affiliation. Jarvenpaa et al. [36] found that propensity to trust (the construct that McKnight and Chervany [57] and McKnight et al. [59] had decomposed into trusting stance and faith in humanity) had a robust impact on trust in virtual teams. Jarvenpaa et al. determined that (1) high-trust teams appeared to enter with higher levels of confidence and optimism, thus seeming to import their own propensity for a positive outlook and projecting it onto the others and that (2) high-trust teams communicated more positively, encouragingly, and forthrightly with their teammates:

From the start, all members expressed excitement about the forthcoming collaboration (“Looking forward very much to working with you all”; “It is exciting to work in a team with so wide a background and I think we can make a good assignment”). . . . This optimism prevailed through out the project (“let’s not give up the fight now. GO-GO-GO!!!” [36, p. 45]).

The team revealed many instances of positive reinforcement such as “great job!” and members would send messages congratulating a member on his or her excellent work. [36, p. 43]

This description fits well with the trait and behavior descriptions of Affiliative types, whose nearest synonymous descriptive terms include nurturing, friendly, cooperative, agreeable, charitable, warm, appreciative, sociable, gregarious, and neighborly [41, 88]. Per the social behavioral inventory (SBI) [63], this quotation aligns well to SBI assessment items for agreeableness, specifically: “I exchanged pleasantries”; “I spoke favorably . . . ”; “I expressed reassurance”; “I complimented or praised another person.” As saliently, along the social dimension, Jarvenpaa and Leidner [35, 36] noted that teams lacking in social, enthusiastic, and optimistic communication reported low initial and low subsequent trust. The low trust teams’ communication was inconsistent, minimalist, critical, or sarcastic. Those descriptions are similar to Leary’s [11, 46] original circumplex descriptions (see Figure 1) for those high in the Dominant–Hostile quadrant (Quadrant I). Similarly, the low-trust teams’ communications and participation are described in terms consistent with SBI items assessing the Hostility–Submissive quadrant (Quadrant II). Adjectives describing the Hostility–Submissive quadrant include unsociable, uncivil, disrespectful, undemonstrative, inconsistent, lazy, impolite, self-doubting, and meek [26, 54].

Along the Control dimension, low-trust teams evidently had followers, but no leaders: team members wished to abdicate decision-making to some other while they awaited instructions (Submissiveness). High-trust team members demonstrated individual initiative (Dominance), combined with emergent and rotating (Moderate Control) leadership. Along the dimension of Affiliation, high-trust team leaders were positive in tone (Friendly). In contrast, the anointed leaders of the low-trust teams were more negative (Hostile) and engaged in more posturing. As this example indicates, correlates of dispositions to trust are clearly evident in measures of circumplex traits.

## Individual-Level Propositions

WHILE THERE SEEMS TO BE GENERAL AGREEMENT in the literature on ICM as to the nature of the relationship between the Affiliation dimension and trust, it is not as clear how the Control dimension affects trust. For example, it is generally agreed that Hostility (the western hemisphere of the circumplex) is associated with distrust, but it is not clear whether distrust is more correlated to Hostility-to-Dominance (the northwest Quadrant I) or to Hostility-to-Submissiveness (the southwest Quadrant II). To sort this out, we will first consider the case of distrust and then turn to trust.

Interpersonal traits that foster a mistrustful attitude are those in Quadrants I and II of Figure 1. These are the traits that sit near the Hostility pole of the Affiliation axis. Individuals low in Affiliation (i.e., high in Hostility) are characterized as competitive, mistrusting, cold, hostile, detached, inhibitive, unassured, and cynical [40]. Two threads of thought have developed regarding how Hostility interacts with the Control dimension to produce distrust. Gurtman [30] asserts that distrust is most likely to be associated with traits in Quadrant I that represent combinations of assertiveness (Dominance) and cold-heartedness (Hostility), as does Kiesler [41] (Figure 1; refer to “Mistrusting” (C) on exterior of Leary’s circle). On the other hand, Leary [46] and Carson [11] believe distrust to be situated in Quadrant II, and associated with traits representing Hostility and Submissiveness (Figure 1; refer to G, Wariness-to-Distrustful).

In a study relating the trust (as measured with the Interpersonal Trust Scale) and Machiavellianism (as measured with the Mach IV Scale), Gurtman concluded that distrust is more closely associated with Dominance with certain shades of distrust inclining toward Hostility: “The Trust Scale appears to measure a kind of dominancedistrust, and the Mach IV Scale, hostility-distrust, the latter related, conceptually, to cynicism and misanthropy” [30, p. 999]. Gurtman also reported distrusting individuals as having problems in competitiveness, envy, resentfulness, vindictiveness, and lack of feelings, with these problems organized around a hostile-dominant intersection [30, p. 999]. Thus, according to Gurtman’s findings, distrust is associated with traits ranging from the northernmost Dominant pole westward toward Hostility.

Distrust has also been associated with individuals whose traits fall in Quadrant II, Submissiveness combined with Hostility. Carson, adapting Leary’s circumplex, summarized the center-most personality traits of this southwest quadrant as being “Rebellious–Distrustful”:

The keynote of this behavior is the expression of hostility and disaffiliation from a stance of relative passivity and powerlessness. It thus includes the congeries of actions whereby the person may indicate passive rejection and distrust of others. . . . This behavior communicates to others the message, “I reject and mistrust you, for you are certain to become, unworthy of my affection and esteem.” [11, p. 110]

Kelly ([39] cited by [26]) also suggests that individuals low in both Control and Affiliation (that is, being Submissive and Hostile) would tend to distrust their relationship partners. Therefore, we derive the following propositions regarding trust and willingness to participate in telemedicine collaborations:

Proposition 1a: Individuals low in Affiliation (i.e., Hostiles) will exhibit low levels of trust toward potential partners in virtual collaboration.

Proposition 2a: Individual low in Affiliation (i.e., Hostiles) will exhibit low levels of intention to initiate and participate in virtual collaboration.

Proposition 3a: Individuals low in Affiliation (i.e., Hostiles) will be ineffective in virtual collaboration.

Traits associated with trust are most likely to reside in the northeastern Quadrant IV of the circumplex, located between the Friendly–Dominant poles. Carson [11] posited that people high in Affiliative–Dominance “would be particularly likely to give relationship partners the benefit of the doubt in potentially problematic situations” (cited by [26, p. 614]). Carson’s description for this “Responsible–Hypernormal” personality is as follows:

Positive affect predominates in this behavior, but the friendliness is colored by a component of dominance. Its prototypic expression is therefore seen when help, support, and sympathy is offered to others. This behavior is also confident and strong in tone, but it takes a more “humanitarian” and personal form. [11, p. 108]

Johnson-George and Swap determined from their literature review that “high trusters emerge as independent and trustworthy (Rotter [72]), honest (Steinke [80]), and open to seeking psychological help (Fisher and Turner [23])” [37, p. 1307], a conclusion that is consistent with the claim that trust is associated with the Quadrant IV of the circumplex. Kiesler [40] characterizes individuals high in Affiliation (i.e., Friendly– Dominant or Friendly–Submissive) as assured, exhibitionistic, sociable, friendly, warm, trusting, open, and humanitarian.

From Kiesler’s widely accepted characterizations of those high in Affiliation, as well as Carson’s characterizations (Friendlies are Affiliative and Humanitarian), we can derive the following three propositions:

Proposition 1b: Individuals high in Affiliation will exhibit high levels of trust toward potential partners in virtual collaboration.

Proposition 2b: Individuals high in Affiliation will exhibit high levels of intention to initiate and participate in virtual collaboration.

Proposition 3b: Individuals high in Affiliation will be highly effective in virtual collaboration.

The northeast Quadrant IV of the circumplex combines traits associated with Affiliation, such as extroversion and nurturance, with the assertiveness of dominance. Given these combinations and Carson’s characterization of these persons as extending the “benefit of the doubt,” we envision an open, extraverted, and asserted “userfriendly” practitioner, who is comfortable taking the initiative with telemedicine and is well-received by his collaborating partner. We, therefore, derive the following propositions to parallel the previous two sets:

Proposition 1c: Individuals in Quadrant IV of the circumplex will exhibit higher levels of trust toward potential partners in virtual collaboration than will individuals in the other three quadrants.

Proposition 2c: Individuals in Quadrant IV of the circumplex will exhibit higher levels of intention to initiate and participate in virtual collaboration than will individuals in the other three quadrants.

Proposition 3c: Individuals in Quadrant IV of the circumplex will be more effective in virtual collaboration than will individuals in the other three quadrants.

It is valuable to be able to explain and predict individual behavior toward virtual collaborations. Collaboration is, however, inherently interactive in nature, so it is also important to be able to predict how pairs of potential collaborators will interact. The ICM enables us to do this.

## Dyadic Level Propositions: Complementarity and Collaboration

The essence of complementarity is that an individual’s interpersonal behavior alters the behavior of his or her interaction partner in predictable ways. [75, p. 81]

CARSON, ONE OF THE FIRST TO ADVOCATE the complementarity principle, summarized it thus: “Generally speaking, complementarity occurs on the basis of reciprocity in respect to the dominance-submissive axis (dominance tends to induce submission, and vice-versa), and on the basis of correspondence in respect to the hate-love axis (hate induces hate, and love induces love)” [11, p. 112]. Leary [46] further elaborated on the notion, suggesting that through acquisition and cultivation of behaviors and techniques, individuals learn to “train” others how to respond to them in the manner they seek [11]. This part of one’s security operations [11, p. 112]. By arraying 16 interpersonal traits or 8 interpersonal vectors around a circle, according to their manifestation of the 2 predominating dimensions of Control and Affiliation, the circumplex presents a geometric representation of traits and behaviors. Depending upon how two individuals’ traits and behaviors’ respectively align around the circumplex, the dyad’s interpersonal compatibility or incompatibility may be predicted. The compatibility or incompatibility of a dyad (or other team) portends its productivity.

According to the ICM [11, 41], dyadic complementarity exists under the following two conditions: (1) the partners exhibit or reply with the similar levels affiliation (either both nurturing or both hostile) and (2) the partner’s demonstrated level of status (along the vertical) elicits or “pulls” the polar opposite (or reciprocal) level of status from the other. Theoretically, then, complementarity exists when one partner is Dominant and the other Submissive [75]. Noncomplementarity exists when dyadic partners are complementary on one dimension, but not complementary on the other. For instance, noncomplementary partners may both be Affiliative, which is complementary, but both occupy the same Control dimension, which creates noncomplementarity. Anticomplementarity exists when actors’ are not complementary on either dimension of Affiliation or Control: their Affiliative traits oppose one another and they are attempting to occupy the same north or south hemisphere of Control. For simplicity, our propositions will use the term noncomplementary to reflect both anticomplementary and noncomplementary.

The ICM propositions concerning compatible and noncompatible dyads have been supported by subsequent research [49, 75, 81, 82]. For example, using confederates exhibiting stimulus behaviors representing various poles of the circumplex, Strong et al. [81] found that the confederate’s behavior systematically biased respondent’s behavior. “For example, the most frequent subject response to the docile stimulus was leading (42.2%) and to the self-effacing stimulus was nurturant (25.7%); the most frequent subject response to the leading stimulus was docile (31.2%) and to the selfenhancing stimulus was cooperative (32.0%)” [81, p. 805]. They also report that critical behavior encouraged distrustful behavior, cooperative behavior encouraged leading behavior, leading behavior encouraged docile behavior, and docile behavior discouraged cooperative behavior [81, p. 807]. Strong et al. concluded that their research supported the validity of the principles of complementarity.

Complementary dyads have been found to produce more satisfactory interactions than noncomplementary dyads [19]. Tracey et al. [83] found complementarity in longterm marriages and close friendships; conversely, they found low complementarity for divorced parents [83]. Conducting a study on dyadic complementarity and productivity, Estroff and Norwicki [21] found that complementary dyads performed better on jigsaw and word-generation tasks than anticomplementary dyads. We therefore derive the following propositions about complementarity, trust, and communication:

Proposition 4a: Complementary dyads will exhibit higher levels of trust than noncomplementary dyads.

Proposition 5a: Complementary dyads will exhibit higher levels of intention to initiate and participate in virtual collaboration than noncomplementary dyads.

Proposition 6a: Complementary dyads will be more effective in virtual collaboration than noncomplementary dyads.

Because the hostile dimension is associated with disaffiliation, aloofness, and distrust [41, 46, 88], we propose the following distinction between the complementary dyads:

Proposition 4b: Complementary Hostile dyads will exhibit lower levels of trust than complementary Affiliative dyads.

Proposition 5b: Complementary Hostile dyads will exhibit lower levels of intention to initiate and participate in virtual collaboration than complementary Affiliative dyads.

Proposition 6b: Complementary Hostile dyads will be less effective in virtual collaboration than complementary Affiliative dyads.

Dyadic or team-level collaboration is influenced by the context in which it occurs. An important aspect of context is the formal authority relationships between the members of the dyad. Moskowitz found that “individuals modify their display of dominance toward others in response to their own position and the other individual’s position in the hierarchy” [48, p. 931]. There will be a tendency to expect those in positions of authority to be more dominant, and if this expectation is fulfilled, the relationship will seem more “normal” and elicit more trust than would a relationship counter to existing power distributions. For example, for a physician and nurse interacting via telemedicine, the physician is generally assumed to have more authority and should be accorded a more dominant position. If the physician exhibits expected dominant characteristics, then both physician and nurse will see this part of the order of things. This situation will elicit trust since it progresses as expected. On the other hand, if the physician does not exhibit dominance, the unusual behavior is likely to be more difficult for the nurse to understand and predict, and the development of trust will be retarded by the ensuing uncertainty. This suggests the following propositions:

Proposition 4c: Affiliative dyads in which the more “senior” level collaborator exhibits Dominance and the subordinate collaborator exhibits Submissiveness will exhibit higher levels of trust toward their partner in virtual collaboration than will other types of dyads.

Proposition 5c: Affiliative dyads in which the more “senior” level collaborator exhibits Dominance and the subordinate collaborator exhibits Submissiveness will exhibit higher levels of intention to participate in virtual collaboration than will other types of dyads.

Proposition 6c: Affiliative dyads in which the more “senior” level collaborator exhibits Dominance and the subordinate collaborator exhibits Submissiveness will be more effective in virtual collaboration than will other types of dyads.

## Traits, Trust, and Technology Use

BASED ON THE ICM, WE CAN ALSO POSIT that an individual’s traits will influence his or her response to new ITs. Since willingness to adopt and use IT is a precursor to any virtual collaboration, this will interact with the propositions developed above.

Melone [60], drawing on Ajzen’s [1] description of attitude, defines user attitude toward IT as “a predisposition to respond favorably or unfavorably to a computer system, application, system staff member, or a process related to the use of that system or application” [60, p. 81]. The technology acceptance model (TAM) proposes that user beliefs and attitudes regarding ease of use and usefulness contribute to acceptance and continued use of an IS [50]. Subjective norms regarding use are also important determinants of intention to use an IS [27, 52, 61]. As noted previously, Pavlou [66] found that trust influenced perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, as well as perceived risk, which influenced intention to transact. In a related study of telemedicine, Chau and Hu [12] found that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness of telemedicine technology affected attitudes toward the technology and perceived control over the technology, which influenced behavioral intention to participate in telemedicine. Perceived usefulness also had a direct effect on intention to participate.

The ICM supports some predictions regarding the attitudes and beliefs various personality types are likely to hold toward new technologies. The interpersonal circumplex posits that Hostiles are characterized as doubting, impatient, and criticizing [81]. Consequently, it stands to reason that Hostiles are inclined to expect lower levels of ease of use and usefulness when encountering a new technology or changes in how they use existing technologies, resulting in lower behavioral intention to use. Similarly, because hostiles are more inclined to rebellion and indifference, hostiles would be less influenced by norms for use. We therefore derive the following propositions:

Proposition 7a: Individuals high in Affiliation (Friendlies) will exhibit higher levels of perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness for new information technologies than will individuals in low in Affiliation (Hostiles).

Proposition 7b: Individuals high in Affiliation (Friendlies) will exhibit higher levels of intention to use new information technologies than will individuals low in Affiliation (Hostiles).

Proposition 7c: Individuals in Quadrant II of the circumplex will exhibit the lowest level of perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness for new information technologies of the four quadrants.

Proposition 7d: Individuals in Quadrant II of the circumplex will exhibit the lowest level of intention to use new information technologies of the four quadrants.

Proposition 7e: Individuals in Quadrant IV of the circumplex will exhibit the highest levels of perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness for new information technologies of the four quadrants.

Proposition 7f: Individuals in Quadrant IV of the circumplex will exhibit the highest level of intention to use new information technologies of the four quadrants.

We can also advance some propositions regarding continuing use of IT based on the ICM. Traits affect one’s affect. Negative affect is manifest in unpleasurable engagement and aversive mood states, which include anger, contempt, distress, and fearful. Positive affect manifests in pleasurable engagement [85, 86]. Manifestations of negative affect are similar to the Hostile–Submissive descriptives (Critical, Distrustful, Self-Effacing) and manifestations of positive affect are similar to Affiliative factors (Extroverted, Friendly, Connected) [81]. From her review of interactional psychology, Nelson finds that “Persons who possess high negative affectivity, for example, tend to accentuate the negative aspects of all situations; thus, we would expect a more difficult adjustment for these individuals [9, 85]” [64, p. 85]. Hence, those high in negative affectivity may respond more negatively toward IS and related surveys than their more positively predisposed counterparts. Therefore, we derive the following propositions concerning reactions to new ITs when used on a continuing basis:

Proposition 8a: Individuals high in Affiliation (Friendlies) will exhibit more positive attitudes toward use of a new IS than individuals low in Affiliation (Hostiles).

Proposition 8b: Individuals in Quadrant II of the circumplex will exhibit the most negative attitudes toward use of a new IS of the four quadrants.

Proposition 8c: Individuals in Quadrant IV of the circumplex will exhibit the most positive attitudes toward use of a new IS of the four quadrants.

The reactions that users have to an IS are likely to influence their intentions to continue to use the new system. Hence,

Proposition 9a: Individuals in low in Affiliation (Hostiles) will report lower level of intention to continue to use a new IS than will individuals high in Affiliation (Friendlies).

Proposition 9b: Individuals in Quadrant II of the circumplex will report a lower level of intention to continue to use a new IS than will individuals in the other three quadrants.

Proposition 9c: Individuals in Quadrant IV of the will report higher intention to continue to use a new IS than will individuals in the other three quadrants.

Degree of control over IT will also affect user reactions and decisions to continue use. Noting that psychologists were “becoming increasingly interested in the role of control over desired behavior,” Ajzen and Madden [2] included perceived behavior control (PBC) to differentiate their theory of planned behavior from Fishbein and Ajzen’s [22] theory of reasoned action. PBC considers the individual’s perception of the presence or absence of the resources necessary to perform or succeed in a certain venture. This inclusion of control is founded on Rotter’s concept of Locus of Control, which developed because “individuals reacted differently to success or failure when the outcome of the task they were to perform was said to be due to either skill or chance (Lefcourt [47])” [32, p. 154]. Further citing Lefcourt [47], Harrington and Beard note that those low in perceived control and status “are found to hold fatalistic, external control beliefs” [32, p. 154]. Ajzen and Madden found

that perception of control, like attitude toward the behavior and subjective norm, can have an important impact on a person’s behavioral motivation. The more that attainment of a behavioral goal is viewed as being under volitional control, the stronger is the person’s intention to try. [2, p. 472; emphasis added]

Harris found that dependent individuals “are more likely to be anxious about using computers than autonomous individuals” and, moreover, “the dependent individuals are more likely to hold negative attitudes towards using computers” [33, p. 121]. Jarvenpaa and colleagues reported that individuals on the low-trust, dysfunctionally participating virtual teams cited technical problems, scheduling conflicts, and other priorities as constraining their individual participation [35, 36]; their excuses indicating a low perception of internal control or PBC. In relation to the interpersonal circumplex, we present the following propositions:

Proposition 10a: Individuals in low in Control (Submissives) will exhibit higher levels of computer anxiety and will perceive that technical problems constrain their ability to participate in IT-mediated collaboration more than will individuals high in Control (Dominants).

Proposition 10b: Individuals in Quadrant II of the circumplex will exhibit higher levels of computer anxiety and will perceive that technical problems constrain their ability to participate in IT-mediated collaboration more than will individuals in the other three quadrants.

Proposition 10c: Individuals in Quadrant IV of the circumplex will exhibit lower levels of computer anxiety and will perceive that technical problems constrain their ability to participate in IT-mediated collaboration less than will individuals in the other three quadrants.

## Discussion

WE HAVE ARGUED THAT TRUST in virtual collaboration is shaped by participants’ personality that influences more proximate “disposition to trust.” We advance the ICM as the most appropriate personality theory for IS research, because it draws direct connections between personality and interpersonal behavior. Based on the ICM, we have developed propositions regarding individual collaboration behavior collaboration within dyads, and acceptance of collaboration technology.

This approach builds on previous theories of trust in IS and organizational research in general, and in virtual collaboration in particular. It attempts to provide a more satisfying explanation by tracing the genesis of trust back to a robust theory of personality. Previous models explain trust as a function of disposition to trust, employing a variable quite similar to trust to explain trust. The ICM moves back in the chain of causality to explain trust as a function of a constellation of attributes grounded in the individual’s core personality: interpersonal traits influence individual interaction styles, which in turn shape the experiences that build or undermine trust and expectations about trust. The ICM enables us to build propositions that are deeper and more fundamental than those in current theories. An additional advantage of the ICM is that exceptional care has been taken to develop and validate measurement instruments for its traits.

The ICM offers an explanation of interpersonal behavior in IS contexts that is both more general and more parsimonious than previous theories. Rather than having to produce a separate disposition for each behavior we are interested in explaining, the ICM offers a single unified framework for understanding the impact of personality on IS behavior. For example, we can use the same theory to explain the impact of individual dispositions on trust, innovation, computer apprehensiveness, and resistance to IS change, rather than having to posit a separate disposition for each behavior, as is currently done in IS research. This promises a more parsimonious theory that mitigates the multiplication of constructs that tends to occur when researchers reason backward from behavior to dispositions. It also promises a more general theory that could account for a wide range of user behavior and reactions within a single framework.

If empirical research bears it out, this analysis also has significant implications for practice. The ICM could be used in staffing decisions to identify those who are more likely to be effective virtual collaborators, as well as those who are likely to undermine collaboration and reject the technology. The ICM also suggests that certain combinations of people are more likely to be effective in collaborating and gives IT managers guidelines for matching people who must collaborate. It suggests that it is not the individual who fuels collaboration, but the interaction—the complementarity— between collaborators.
