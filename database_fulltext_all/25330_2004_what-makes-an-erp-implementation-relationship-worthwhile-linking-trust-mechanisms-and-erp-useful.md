# What Makes an ERP Implementation Relationship Worthwhile: Linking Trust Mechanisms and ERP Usefulness

DAVID GEFEN

DAVID GEFEN is an Associate Professor of MIS at Drexel University in Philadelphia, where he teaches Strategic Management of Information Technology, Database Analysis and Design, and VB.NET. He received his Ph.D. in CIS from Georgia State University and an M.S. in MIS from Tel-Aviv University. His research focuses on psychological and rational processes involved in ERP, computer-mediated communication, and e-commerce implementation management. Dr. Gefen’s interest in IT adoption stems from his 12 years of experience in developing and managing large information systems. He is a Senior Editor for the DATA BASE for Advances in Information Systems. His research findings have been published in the Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, and a number of other journals. Dr. Gefen is also first author of the textbook Advanced VB.NET: Programming Web and Desktop Applications in ADO.NET and ASP.NET.

ABSTRACT: To a large extent, trust determines expected utility derived from business transactions where the trusting party is dependent upon others, but lacks control over them. In many instances, this typifies the relationship between clients and an enterprise resource planning (ERP) customization vendor. This exploratory study examines how trust is built during an ERP implementation, and the relative weight of this trust compared with the perceived qualities of the implemented ERP itself in determining clients’ assessment that the business relationship with the vendor is worthwhile.

The data, collected from companies that were involved in the process of implementing a new ERP with the on-site assistance of a certain customization vendor, show that all three trust antecedents suggested by Zucker’s seminal study of trust— process-based, characteristic-based, and institution-based mechanisms—contribute significantly to client trust. The data also show that client trust in this customization vendor and the perceived usefulness of the ERP both contribute to client assessment that their business relationship with the vendor is worthwhile, showing that both getting the job done and creating a trust-based relationship contribute to this assessment. The implications of the importance of creating trust in ERP implementation and the means of doing how to do so are discussed.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: enterprise resource planning, ERP, ERP implementation, Technology Acceptance Model, trust, trust antecedents.

TRUST IS THE BELIEF THAT OTHERS UPON WHOM ONE DEPENDS, yet has little control over, will not take advantage of the situation by behaving in an opportunistic manner [95] but, rather, will fulfill their expected commitments [4, 18, 27, 41, 45, 59, 61, 80, 84] by behaving ethically [45], dependably [54], and fairly [98], especially under conditions involving risk and potential loss [18, 41]. It is “an expectancy held by an individual or a group that the word, promise, verbal or written statement of another individual or group can be relied on” [80, p. 444], and in proving reliable will substantiate the willingness of the trusting party to depend on the other individual or group [61]. Trust is generally crucial in business and social interactions that are characterized by a dependency on another party combined with a lack of control over that party [23, 45, 61]. In these cases, trust determines, among other things, the expected utility people expect to gain from the business interaction [23, 25].

These preconditions for the need to trust are evident with the process involved in the implementation of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. ERP systems manage a company’s value chain in an integrated manner, handling its inventory, logistics, orders, billing, shipping, sales, customer service, and many other aspects. Standardizing and integrating these activities and incorporating them into the client’s work procedures typically streamlines procurement, increases efficiency, reduces inventory and waste, and improves competitiveness [57]. Successfully implementing an ERP can result in a considerable improvement in company and employee efficiency, with some recent industry reports suggesting as much as a 25 percent increase [10]. Nevertheless, successfully implementing an ERP often requires, in addition to purchasing the software itself, extensive customization to configure it to the specific client and its marketplace [3]. This customization is critical because, when it is not done properly, the result can be an expensive failure that may take a long time to correct [89] and may even entail extensive loss of market share, as happened with Hershey [90].

These clients’ dependence on the customization and their inability to practically monitor the work of the vendor doing so during the implementation phase—that is, prior to the actual deployment of the ERP, when customization errors are more obvious—suggest that client trust in the customization vendor should be a major aspect determining the utility expected by the client from the ERP system. The vast stakes involved for the clients only magnify the importance of this trust. Indeed, in other cases of reliance upon another party for the successful implementation of an information technology (IT), it has been suggested, albeit not empirically examined, that client trust is a major issue determining implementation success [43].

If, indeed, trust is a central aspect leading to clients’ assessment that their relationship with the vendor is worthwhile, then the question arises of how vendors can create this trust. Accordingly, the first objective of this study is to examine trust-building modes that may apply during the implementation of an IT system that requires vendor support, such as an ERP. These trust-building modes are examined within the context of Zucker’s [98] theory that trust in business relationships is created through process-based, institution-based, and characteristic-based mechanisms. The second objective of the study is to assess the relative weight of client trust compared with the operational value of the ERP itself, specifically its perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are the central building blocks of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [16]. The study examines two aspects of the relationship that should increase clients’ assessment that the relationship is worthwhile: that the ERP is useful and easy to use, and that the vendor is creating a relationship based on trust.

The data collected from 133 companies that were in the process of implementing a certain new ERP with the same customization vendor show that clients’ trust was increased by process-based, characteristic-based, and institution-based mechanisms. The data also show that clients’ assessment that their relationship with this vendor is worthwhile was significantly increased by both their trust in the vendor and by the attributes of the software itself, specifically its perceived usefulness.

## Conceptual Background and Research Model

## The Essence of Trust

TRUST CATERS TO PEOPLE’S BASIC NEED to predict, understand, and try to control their social environment and, in doing so, to foresee what other people will do and how their own actions will affect the behavior of others. Understanding the social environment without making some basic assumptions about others, however, is an overwhelming task, because other people, whether as individuals or as members of organizations, are essentially free agents whose behavior cannot be controlled and sometimes is not even based on logic. As a result, when attempting to predict the possible future behaviors of others, people are faced with an overpowering number of possible behaviors that are an inherent part of their interactions with other people. Social uncertainty is typically reduced through the establishment of rules and customs in an attempt to establish enough social order so that social uncertainty will be reduced to a degree where predicting the behavior of others is more viable. When rules and customs are insufficient, people rely on trust as a social uncertainty reduction method. That is, they allow themselves to rule out the possibility that trusted others might behave in undesirable manners [4, 27, 58, 59].

In attempting to define the precise meaning of trust, research has dealt with trust either as a set of specific beliefs dealing with the integrity, ability, and benevolence of the trusted party (e.g., [19, 25, 41, 56]), or as a general belief that the trusted party can be trusted (e.g., [27, 45, 68]), or a mixture of both (e.g., [73, 92]). Some researchers take a third stand, labeling the specific beliefs as trustworthiness and regarding them as antecedents of the general belief in trust (e.g., [6, 47, 61]). Other researchers define trust as a willingness to be vulnerable [61, 65, 96] or as a willingness to depend based on specific beliefs [5, 50]. There is another conceptualization dealing with initial trust, which is trust before any interaction has occurred [66, 80]. This study does not deal with initial trust, because the objective is to assess what builds trust in an actual relationship, rather than what influences it before any interaction has taken place. In keeping with previous empirical research dealing with buyer–seller relationships [19] and relationships between companies [25, 43], this study adopted the first conceptualization of trust as a set of specific beliefs dealing with the integrity, benevolence, and ability of the trusted party. Some researchers (e.g., [67]) add a fourth belief, openness, that includes both openness and integrity, while retaining separate dimensions of the ability, benevolence, and reliability. Combining openness with integrity was also done by Crosby et al. [13]. This belief is included in this study as an aspect of integrity. In choosing this definition, the guideline was to examine as much as possible comparable intercompany scenarios in the literature. These three beliefs have also been identified as core trust beliefs in psychology [41], management [45, 61, 66], marketing [19, 25], and management information systems (MIS) [37, 38, 65]. Reflecting this diversity of meaning, there is no single scale of trust; rather, scales used in research are scenario specific (e.g., [13, 25, 55, 68, 69]). In this study, as in other studies dealing with trust in buyer–seller relationships, the three dimensions are assumed to form one dimension. The logic will be explained below in the instrument creation section.

## The Need for Trust in ERP Implementation

As a social complexity reduction method, trust is an important ingredient in many buyer–seller and service-providing transactions [25, 54, 55, 63], especially long-term and ongoing relationships [15, 24], where it practically defines the nature of business partnerships and people’s assessment of these relationships [23]. Trust in business relationships is especially needed when the trusting party depends upon the other party, yet lacks control over it [23, 43]. Accordingly, trust should also be important for ERP customization clients in determining their assessment of the relationship with the vendor, because the customization of such complex software typically entails vulnerability and dependence on the vendor [33]. As noted by industry reports, not only do clients lack control over the quality of the vendor’s work while it is in progress and the technical ability to monitor it, but, when there are problems with the customization, these problems are often discovered too late [11, 22, 64, 91]. Moreover, much of the customization cannot be properly tested in many cases until the ERP is deployed in real settings, by which time it may be too late to change the customization vendor or seek legal counsel, because the damage has already been done. Such a dependency combined with lack of control is a typical recipe for the need to trust in comparable business scenarios [23, 25, 69].

It is also generally advantageous to both the client and the vendor to develop such trust. Trust is crucial in many long-term and ongoing contrasts, in general, making them more worthwhile and easier to implement because it reduces the need to set up expensive complex legal controls and because it provides a measure of guarantee, albeit subjective, that all parties involved are in the deal for the long term and so will refrain from opportunistic and harmful behavior [23]. These relationships are typically also more open [40] and generally have fewer business conflicts [1, 2]. There is another reason, a long-term one, why a vendor should want to create client trust. Apart from reducing social uncertainty and transaction costs in business interactions [95], trust in business relationships can create a “special” relationship, the kind of relationship that becomes a desirable objective in its own right [4, 23], a relationship based on loyalty that goes beyond the typical business objective of short-term profit [23]. This special relationship is commonly characterized by interorganizational partnerships [42], by cooperative interactions [23, 25], and by reduced monitoring and a lesser reliance on detailed legal contracts [23].

The three beliefs that compose client trust can specifically alleviate the social uncertainty that customization clients face. The customization vendor’s ability is clearly a prerequisite for successfully implementing the ERP and, in doing so, making the business relationship worthwhile. Customizing even only one module of an ERP may require setting hundreds of interrelated parameters [14], as well as adapting the client’s existing work procedures. Vendor integrity is also crucial. Without believing that the vendor is honest and sincere, the client is in an impossible position. The client is dependent upon the vendor doing what the vendor claims to be doing, yet there is little the client can do to monitor the vendor and double-check that the vendor is doing as it says it is before it is too late. Benevolence, that is, caring, is also a valued attribute of the vendor that should contribute to making the relationship more worthwhile, because, otherwise, the client may be exposed unknowingly to honest, yet undesirable, opportunistic behavior on the part of the vendor. Such behavior might include putting the vendor’s own needs first, even when the damage to the client is great. Pulling out key experts at a delicate time so that they can bring more benefit in another project is one example of such behavior. Another example of such behavior is incorporating features that the client did not request, but that increase the marketability and prestige of the vendor. Accordingly, it is hypothesized that the client’s belief in the vendor’s ability, integrity, and benevolence should increase the client’s assessment that their business relationship is worthwhile.

H1: Trust in the customization vendor will increase client assessment that their business relationship is worthwhile.

## Building Trust in ERP Implementation

How then can this trust be built? Zucker [98], examining the American economy between 1840 and 1920, suggested that there are three general modes of trust production in an economic environment: process-based, characteristic-based, and institution-based. The most important of these modes is the process-based mode [45, 98]. In the process-based mode, trust is created as a product of the exchange itself [98] by the creation of a good track record [45]. In this mode, trust is built through the constructive nature of the ongoing relationship, primarily through the investment that the trusted party makes in the relationship [25], which is usually related to previous behavior that was in accordance with the trusting party’s favorable expectation [4, 41, 59, 68], and to instances where the trusted party had the opportunity to take advantage of the situation, but refrained from doing so [21, 53, 54, 63]. Doney et al. [20], referring to this antecedent as a “prediction” process, explain that in this process, knowledge of how the trusted party behaved in the past serves as a basis for predicting future behavior. Indeed, in a business environment where two companies interact, it is often the trust that is placed in the company representative that is a primary determinant of trust in the company itself [19].

Process-based trust is, according to Zucker, the most prominent way of building trust, an assertion supported empirically [19]. Theoretically, this may be because process-based trust is much closer to the objective of the trust, the trusted business partner, than the other two antecedents of trust. Process-based trust is the product of an actual related experience with the specific business partner. The other two modes of trust creation deal with a broader social group, in the case of characteristics-based trust, or with third-party guarantors, both of which are not directly related to the business partner itself and are not directly involved in the actual transaction.

Arguably, the same should apply with the service-providing relationship involved in ERP customization. As in other business engagements [25], when the customization vendor goes out of its way to help the client, this should be an indication that the vendor is honest and caring and is capable of getting the job done. A vendor who lacks the ability or the caring will not waste its time doing so. Consequently, client perceptions that the vendor is investing in the relationship should build client trust. The second mode Zucker [98] suggested was characteristic-based trust. Characteristic-based trust deals with similarity in issues such as culture, gender, and race. Zucker hypothesized that this kind of mode should build trust by creating shared expectations of what is happening and what is expected of all parties involved through a common background of customs and culture. Such a shared understanding should assure each party that the other will engage in what both consider to be appropriate and ethically correct behavior, especially when these differ across cultures, as happened during the great immigration to the United States between 1840 and 1920. Overall, though, this mode of trust creation should be of lesser importance in the broad marketplace than institutional-based trust, because the latter inherently relates to a smaller and more specific group [98]. This mechanism was common in the early days of American banking, when sharing a common custom of what was expected in the business transaction was essential due to a lack of other trust-creating modes because of the extensive cultural mix due to immigration and due to the lack of governing regulations at the time [98]. Other research has also noted how shared values contribute to trust during business transactions (e.g., [23, 35, 95]).

This mode should arguably apply also in the case of ERP customization. Shared cultural characteristics should increase client trust in the vendor by assuring clients that they share the same reference frames as the vendor. Shared cultural characteristics are defined in the context of this study on a personal level as shared values, lifestyle, and appearance with the vendor support personnel. This should reduce cultural misunderstanding between the client and the vendor that could otherwise arise due to different workplace-dependent assumptions, knowledge, and values. Such differences hinder the integration of other IT in organizations [70, 71, 74]. Having the same cultural characteristics should thus provide a level of shared understanding about what constitutes honest, caring, and able behavior in the interaction between client and vendor. Also contributing to possible culture-based misunderstandings is the nature of the different client and vendor marketplace specializations. Given a vendor whose personnel are a priori honest, caring, and able, creating mutual understanding based on shared cultural characteristics should increase client trust by demonstrating to the client that these personnel are behaving as expected. Behaving as expected is generally one of the major ways in which trust is built [4, 59]. Indeed, in buyer–seller relationships, perceived similarity increases trust [19].

## H3: Shared cultural characteristics will increase client trust in the customization vendor.

The third mode Zucker hypothesized is institution-based trust. This mode is tied to “formal societal structures” [98, p. 53], such as certification, regulators, and independent intermediaries. There are two types of institution-based modes that build trust. The first certifies people and institutions based on explicit expectations of what the certification means, such as the state license that medical doctors receive. This thirdparty guarantee reduces social uncertainty by testifying to the ability and character of the trusted party. Certification is very popular in the U.S. economy. Better Business Bureau certifications are widely applied as credible third-party certification to testify to the ability and conduct of merchants and service providers. The second type of institution-based mode creates a third-party guarantor that the transaction will be carried out properly. This happens, for example, with bank-supervised escrow accounts and with insurance policies that guarantee that the trusting party will be compensated should an accident happen. Trust in both cases is not related to the characteristics of the person or to the nature of the interaction but rather to a market mechanism that provides an independent guarantee that either provides a certification, as in the former case, or of a third-party service, as in the latter. This kind of trust production mode is prominent when other modes become disrupted due to major changes in the economy or when there is other uncertainty [98]. It has not been widely accepted by research because of the “obvious question: Who guards the guardians?” [45, p. 389; 88] and because of the inherent imperfect nature of regulations [45]. Nonetheless, according to the Better Business Bureau, it is widely applied in practice regarding other types of service providers (for details, see www.bbb.org/). It is also a major contributor to trust in online marketplaces [74] and should also be in other business-to-business (B2B) relationships [75]. In the case of ERP customization projects, such certification and recorded past history of satisfied clients should imply that the accepted standards of honest, caring, and able conduct also apply to this specific vendor, just as the widely accepted and sought after Better Business Bureau certifications and recommendations build trust among clients of other service providers. In the specific case of this study, the clients had the opportunity to inquire about the vendor with the Better Business Bureau. Accordingly,

H4: Institution-based guarantees will increase client trust in the customization vendor.

## The Role of Perceived Qualities of the Software in ERP Implementation

As important as trust is in the relationship with the customization vendor, the primary objective of this business relationship is getting the ERP to work properly. The benefits from an IT can generally be classified, judging by previous measures of IT success, mostly into two primary categories, namely, benefits as seen by individual users and benefits as seen by senior management [85]. Since this study deals with the implementation phase, where it is too early to assess the types of benefits senior management most cares about, such as return on investment, this study examines individual user benefits, namely, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use.<sup>1</sup> Nonetheless, many senior management benefits, such as moving away from legacy systems [72], enabling electronic data interchange (EDI), and improving productivity [57, 81, 82], should also be reflected by, and consistent with, a useful and easy-to-use IT, which should thereby contribute to ensuring that the relationship with the vendor is perceived as more worthwhile. After all, a major objective of the relationship with the vendor is to make the ERP more useful and easy to use. Specifically, perceived usefulness should contribute to making the relationship more worthwhile because ERPs are designed with the declared objective of helping organizations to manage their operations in a better, more systematic, and more organized manner. The perceptions of the employees in charge of the ERP in these organizations that the ERP improves their work performance should accordingly testify that, at least with regard to the vendor providing a useful ERP, the relationship with the ERP vendor was worthwhile.

H5: The perceived usefulness of the ERP increases client assessment that their business relationship with the customization vendor is worthwhile.

The perceived ease of use of the ERP should also contribute to the organization and hence to making the relationship with the ERP vendor worthwhile. Employee approval of the ERP, arguably increased when it is easier to use, should make the achievement of operational-level benefits more attainable by reducing employee resistance to change. Given that many ERP projects fail because of user resistance [57], increased perceived ease of use should also be of value to the organization. Moreover, an easy-to-use ERP should reduce costly employee errors, reduce organizational dependence on “expert” employees, and shorten employees’ training time. These should additionally increase the benefit from the ERP and, in doing so, make the relationship with the vendor more worthwhile.

H6: The perceived ease of use of the ERP increases client assessment that their business relationship with the customization vendor is worthwhile.

Also, part of TAM is the empirically established relationship between perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. This relationship has been discussed extensively in the past and shown to apply to a wide range of IT [36].

## H7: The perceived ease of use of an ERP will increase its perceived usefulness.

## Trust and the Perceived Qualities of the ERP

An ERP needs to be customized before its deployment. This customization deals with adjusting its parameters to client-specific work processes. If this customization is not done properly, then the ERP will not support the work procedures as well as it could have and, accordingly, will be less useful to its users, the employees. In other words, how useful the ERP is depends on how well it is customized to specific client work procedures. Since it is impractical to monitor every aspect of the customization during the implementation period, the nature of the relationship with the vendor customizing the ERP should contribute to what users expect of it. This is not unique to ERP. It also applies to other complex IT systems that require customization [33]. If users believe that the vendor is able to customize the ERP properly, and is honest about the inevitable problems, and cares about them, then they will rationally expect to get a better-customized—that is, more useful—ERP. Conversely, if the users believe that the vendor is incapable of customizing the ERP properly, or is dishonest, or does not care, then they will rationally have little reason to expect that the customization will be done very well.

At the root of this relationship is a social exchange between the client and the vendor. A social exchange, much like its economic counterpart, is a relationship that is based on an assessment of whether the current costs invested in it will justify the expected benefits, except that in an economic exchange the benefits are in the future, are not guaranteed, and cannot be readily stipulated [51, 52, 93]. In such instances, in the lack of a guarantee or legal safeguard, trust serves as a subjective reassurance that the trusted party will do its share as expected. As a result, trust also determines people’s expectations in ongoing social [4, 52] and business transactions [23]. Social exchange can also explain the relationship between trust in a vendor and the perceived usefulness of an IT [38]. Specifically, in the case of an ERP, the client, lacking a guarantee that the vendor will customize the ERP properly, must trust the vendor to do its work properly in order to gain its expected benefits from the interaction, namely, an ERP made more useful through customization. Previous research supports this connection in the case of ecommerce (e.g., [38, 73]). Accordingly,

## H8: Client trust in the customization vendor increases the perceived usefulness of the ERP.

Good process-based experience with the vendor—such as the knowledge that the vendor will go out of its way to assist the client—should also increase the perceived ease of use of the ERP. This hypothesis is somewhat analogous to the argument made by Gefen et al. [38] that some trust antecedents, such as familiarity and situational normality, should contribute to perceived ease of use through increased understanding of the software and how to operate it. In the case of ERP implementation, vendor support should contribute to increased ease of use of the IT through better user understanding of the IT and how to operate it, achieved through the on-site support and training that the vendor provides. Having on-site technical support that is willing to go out of its way to assist users should contribute to the ease of using any complex system, including ERP.<sup>2</sup> Support for this can be found in previous research that examined the customization process of expert systems, and complex IT systems that require extensive support before they can be used to their fullest extent, finding that users’ ease-of-use perceptions were increased by having such support [33]. Even with microcomputer usage, where the system is much less complex than with ERP, technical support increases ease-of-use perceptions [46].

H9: Good process-based experience with the customization vendor increases ERP perceived ease of use.

## Other Trust-Building Modes Not Included in This Study

The literature suggests that apart from the trust-building modes discussed above, trust is built through personality mechanisms. These mechanisms apply primarily in the early stages of an acquaintance before the trusting party has had the opportunity to get to know the trusted party well enough to form a person-specific assessment. This initial trust is related to the personality of the trusting party, especially its disposition to trust and its belief in humanity. These two dispositions are not specifically related to a given person or organization but, rather, represent a tendency to believe that better outcomes will come about by believing in others and in a general tendency to trust others, respectively. These two dispositions are the product of a lifelong socialization process and are dispositions that people bring into new relationships that enable them to trust even strangers before they get to know them [66, 80]. Given that the study examined current clients who had already worked extensively with the vendor, and given that these dispositions are significantly less important at later stages in a relationship when the parties know each other [66], they were not part of this study. Also excluded from the model, because they are controlled for by virtue of there being only one vendor in the study, are vendor reputation and size, both of which also contribute to trust in e-commerce [47]. ## Research Design

## Instrument Creation

THE ITEMS IN THE CLIENT TRUST SCALE were adapted from existing research dealing with comparable circumstances: (1) trust in buyer–seller and service-providing scenarios [25, 69], (2) trust in a specialist consultant [55, 68], and (3) customer trust in a business agent [13, 26]. This was done with the objective of increasing content validity by using previously validated items. Pooling together items from these scales, this study unintentionally adopted a scale that largely overlaps with that of Doney and Cannon [19]. This overlap is appropriate, because this study also deals with an interaction with a company representative in the context of an interaction between two companies. The integrity items capture the belief that the vendor is reliable, open, and honest. The benevolence item captures the belief that the vendor cares and is willing to help. The ability items capture the belief that the vendor is competent and knowledgeable. These items were incorporated into a single scale, because in the context of the relationship with the customization vendor, the three beliefs overlap, as they do in Doney and Cannon [19].<sup>3</sup> When a vendor is not able to perform the customization (showing low ability), and yet accepts the task, it hardly shows honesty (integrity) or caring (benevolence) about the client. And when the vendor does do the job properly (showing high ability), part of that job is to help the customer (benevolence) while being frank and open about the inevitable problems that will occur (integrity). Moreover, not only do the three beliefs in the specific case of ERP customization reflect overlapping work activities, but it is also hard for the client to tell the difference among the three. When a vendor does not do the customization properly, it is hard for the client to tell whether this is because of lack of ability, lack of caring, lack of honesty through hiding problems, or any combination of the three. And when a vendor does do the job properly, it should be an indication to the client of integrity, benevolence, and ability concurrently.

The perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use items were adapted from TAM [16]. These user-level benefits deal with the support that the ERP gives to ongoing operations. Examining senior management level benefits, while an interesting question in its own right, was beyond the scope of the study. Moreover, senior management level benefits, such as gaining strategic advantage, require that the organization have a unique IT [62]. But once an IT is widely spread in the industry, as is the case with ERP, the benefits organizations derive from it are mainly in the realm of improved operational activities [7, 76], such as allowing employees to perform their tasks in a more effective and efficient manner, paralleling in part the TAM scales. Arguably, it is also a senior management organizational-level benefit to have an IT that is useful and easy for its employees to use. Centering the study on the individual user perspective also enabled a comparison of trust with TAM without modifying the essence of the TAM measures, thus avoiding the question of whether one can really compare trust and TAM if the TAM scales are modified.

The client’s cost-benefit assessment of the relationship with the vendor is captured by the scale titled the business relationship with the vendor is worthwhile. Institutionbased mode, in accordance with Zucker [98], deals with certifications, specifically credentials. There are no third-party guarantors in this market that can compare with the Better Business Bureau or with state agencies that certify software quality. There are, however, more general certification qualifications, such as ISO 9001, and the Better Business Bureau does handle complaints about software companies by providing Member Reliability Reports that include customer experience with complaint listings, albeit dealing with issues of service quality and keeping contracts rather than with software quality. As in other industries, institution-based also includes references from other customers. This item and the previous one deal with two complementary aspects of outsider certification, namely, experts and customers. Accordingly, whereas the two items deal with two different types of outsider certification, they both deal with the assessments of others who should know.

The characteristic-based mode items capture the notion that the vendor has “people like us” on a personal basis, as in Zucker [98]. Specifically, these items deal with appearance, values, and lifestyle. The process-based mode items deal with aspects of the relationship’s positive past experience, the belief that the vendor is going out of its way to help. The items dealing with Zucker’s proposed processes deal with client assessments of vendor behavior, in contrast to the trust items that deal with client beliefs about the vendor. The items dealing with the process-based mode are worded in the past tense to emphasize this distinction. These items deal with perceived responsiveness because, in the preparatory pilot-test interviews with two expert customization managers, it came up as a central aspect of the type of service customers expect of software vendors. Perceived responsiveness is a central aspect of service quality in general [97], and it contributes to successful IT implementation in a variety of environments and packages (e.g., [28, 33, 34]). Perceived responsiveness is among the most important aspects of service quality when it comes to building trust [29, 32].

All the items were measured on a 1 = “strongly agree” to 7 = “strongly disagree” scale, with 4 being “neutral.” All the items were pilot tested with the two expert customization managers prior to the data collection to ensure their content validity. The items are shown in Appendix A. To maintain privacy, the vendor’s name in the Appendix has been replaced with the acronym CV. The ability items are prefixed with AB, the integrity items with IN, and the benevolence items with BEN.

## Population and Sampling

As an exploratory study, the objective of the sampling procedure, in accordance with Cook and Campbell’s guidelines [12], was to increase internal validity by controlling for exogenous validity, even at the cost of reduced external validity. To avoid exogenous variance relating to the different work procedures, ERP types, vendor culture, and legal contracts of different vendors, the clientele of only one customization vendor was examined. The vendor examined was based in the United States and, with approximately 2,500 clients, was one of the smaller companies that specialize in this type of customization. The vendor agreed to take part in the study on the condition that it be the one who mails out the questionnaires and so retains exclusiveness on its customer list. The questionnaires were returned through self-addressed postage-paid envelopes to the university without any involvement of the vendor. This was done to assure the clients of their privacy and the anonymity of their responses. Only aggregate data were provided to the vendor. The questionnaire was sent to contact people on the clientele list by selecting every fifth name on the list in order, using a systematic random sampling method [83]. All the clients were manufacturing companies. There were 133 completed questionnaires returned (25 percent).

The data were collected during the customization and implementation period. During this period, the users, and the contact persons who worked with them and who coordinated between them and the vendor’s technical support, already had experience with the ERP and could therefore assess its usefulness and ease of use. It is the nature of ERP implementation that major changes are discouraged during implementation, and so these contact persons would have had experience with the actual ERP system. This is crucial because, as Karahanna et al. [49] showed, this is a necessary precondition for assessing perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. During this period, the ERP is still being customized and, hence, there still is a need to trust the vendor to make ongoing changes properly. In the specific case of this vendor, the clients had also worked with the vendor in the past, creating an ongoing business relationship that transcended this particular ERP. Marketing research shows that, in general, trust is important during such ongoing business interactions [19, 23, 25].

The contact person in each company to whom the questionnaire was sent was the person in charge of the ERP implementation and the ongoing contact with the vendor. According to the vendor, the contact person in many of the smaller companies was an experienced user, the kind who would coach other users in the system. In the larger companies, the contact person was sometimes a member of the senior management team who was closely engaged in the implementation process and was the go-between with the vendor’s personnel. In both cases, the contact person would have been in a position to judge what the users thought of the ERP. Each contact person also worked with senior management. As such, this person was in a good position to assess both the ERP and the relationship with the vendor.

Among the contact persons who revealed their gender, 90 were men and 34 were women. The contact persons were mostly in their thirties $( n = 4 9 )$ ), forties $( n = 4 8 )$ 2 and fifties (n = 18). As part of the analysis, items were dropped to establish factorial validity—that is, that each item loaded highly only on its assigned construct. Dropping items to achieve this is common in TAM studies [38]. ## Discussion and Implications

## Summary of Results

TRUST SHAPES BUSINESS RELATIONSHIPS. In many ongoing business relationships, trust is an essential building block, determining expectations and assessments [23, 54]. Viewed from the perspective of the contact person, the data tentatively support this view, showing that trust is significantly correlated with the assessment that the business relationship is worthwhile. The data also show that the actual usefulness of the ERP is just as important, capturing two distinct aspects of this assessment. In agreement with previous research [38] and with suggestions from other observations [54], client trust contributes to the assessment that the ERP is useful. This constructive effect of trust on perceived usefulness parallels recent research in the context of e-commerce [38, 73] and freeware [26]. That this relationship between trust in the IT vendor and the perceived usefulness of that vendor’s IT holds, also in the context of ERP implementation, supports the proposition that IT implementation management has distinct, but interrelated, technology aspects and user trust management aspects [34].

The data also support the three modes hypothesized by Zucker [98] to build trust in business transactions. In support of the proposition that trust is built primarily through client perceptions of the vendor’s process-mode behaviors, the data show that this coefficient is more than twice as large as those of other processes. Trust is also correlated, although to a lesser degree, with client perceptions that the vendor has shared social characteristics with them, possibly because, as Zucker [98] suggested, shared characteristics enable a better shared understanding of what both sides expect from the business relationship and from each other. Trust is also correlated with institutionbased mode indicators. The reason for lesser weight of institution-based compared with process-based mode indicators may be that ERP customization is not a standardized procedure; rather, each project might involve a different group of vendor experts and processes. As such, recommendations from previous business contacts should carry less weight once a project is under way, because they apply to the vendor rather than to the specific experts who are actually doing the configuration. It is also perhaps not surprising that certification had only a smaller effect, considering that there is no credible certifier of an outstanding credence, such as the Better Business Bureau, in this market who can testify to the quality of the ERP vendor. Still, because of the lack of such a certification institution, the lesser effect of the institution-based mode should not be viewed as implying that such a mechanism is necessarily always of lesser importance than the process-based mode. Research in related topics, such as online marketplaces where such certifying institutions do exist, shows that such mechanisms can be very strong [74]. It should be clarified that the above deals with a contact person’s perspective of the relationship that the company as a whole has with the vendor. Whether this applies to a more specific user level, as examined by other trust studies (e.g., [38, 73]), requires additional research.

## Limitations and Additional Research

Although the data analysis in PLS shows that each item loaded on its expected factor with a much higher coefficient than it did on other factors, the cross-loadings in the confirmatory factor analysis among the perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and worthwhile items were a bit high. Such degrees of cross-loadings are common among these constructs when analyzed in PLS, where, as shown in “Structural Equation Modeling and Regression: Guidelines for Research Practice” [39], they can be twice as high as in a principal components factor analysis. Previous research with PLS on TAM also reports such high degrees of cross-loadings (e.g., [49]). Also relating to the data analysis, although not uncommon in other recent studies of trust (e.g., [38, 73]), three of the constructs have only two items each, which may reduce the statistical validity of the model. Another possible problem with the data is that the items do not relate explicitly to the new ERP, although it was understood while discussing the questionnaire with the vendor that the context in which the questionnaires were sent out and the installation period they coincided with would indicate that the study is about the current system being implemented. That the items relate explicitly to the ERP being implemented could have been stated better. This caveat should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results. Additional verification with more data may be needed.

The cross-sectional nature of the data should also be considered when reviewing the results. Although the PLS analysis mostly supports the research propositions, the data were collected at one point in time, rather than with a longitudinal study. As such, inferring causation from the significant coefficients should only be done within the context of the theory upon which they are based. That is, the significant coefficients support the propositions of the theory about causation by showing the correlation patterns that would be expected if the causation in the theory is correct. But the coefficients themselves do not necessarily show causation. To show causation, additional longitudinal research, possibly even in a quasi-experimental design, is necessary [12]. In this regard, reverse causality may also kick in. For example, the assessment over time that the business relationship is worthwhile may itself lead to trust rather than be itself the product of trust. The cross-sectional nature of the data precluded examining such effects. Adding such an aspect in future research could shed light on how trust develops in these relationships and on the two-way causation it may have with the nature of the vendor–client relationship and its impact.

Among the many aspects of the process-based mode, this study focused on the vendor’s going out of its way to help the client. Needless to say, there are other aspects of the process-based mode, such as not taking advantage of the situation [21, 53, 54, 63] and providing other aspects of service quality [97]. Expanding the research model to include these also, and, in doing so, to examine additional theoretical aspects of process-based mechanisms, is a promising avenue for additional research. In this regard, it may be that the relatively stronger tone of language applied in the processbased mode items may have also contributed to inflating its coefficients compared with the other two modes.

Furthermore, the assessment that the business relationship is worthwhile captures only part of the story. This assessment dealt with a cost-benefit analysis of working with the customization vendor based on one of the dictionary meanings of cost-benefit. Other aspects of this decision, such as enabling a strategic advantage or providing an otherwise inaccessible service with the ERP, also need to be examined. The same goes for trust. This study defined trust as a combination of integrity, ability, and benevolence, following the common definition of the term in the literature. Additional aspects of trust that have been suggested, but are not commonly applied, include reliability and loyalty [45], kindness and possessing personal attraction [41], and predictability [66]. Additional research could include these aspects. The effect of habit on loyalty also requires additional research [31].

Another possible limitation of the study is nonresponse bias. With a response rate of 25 percent, it is impossible to rule out that those who responded were more positively inclined toward the customization vendor than those who did not reply. Because the vendor adamantly guarded client information, even on the aggregate level, for fear of losing market share, nonresponse bias analysis could not be done to compare the characteristics of companies that did reply with those that did not. This may limit the generality of the results and should be acknowledged when assessing them. The results should also be understood with the caveat that, being an exploratory study, the design deliberately emphasized its internal validity, controlling for exogenous variance even at the cost of a reduced generalization of the results [12]. Additional research is needed to examine the applicability of this exploratory study to other ERP customization vendors.

Another avenue of research would be to expand the perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use constructs to deal with an organizational—that is, senior management—perspective of usefulness and ease of use. It was an implicit assumption in this study that an IT’s being useful and easy for the employees to use—is a benefit to the organization. Arguably, this is the case, as is often assumed (e.g., [17]). Nonetheless, because of possible bias [85], a better picture could be obtained if organizational perspectives of usefulness and ease of use were added. Related to this, the study should be understood with the caveat that it shows the contact person’s take on the usefulness and ease of use of the IT, and on trust in the vendor. This take may at times be secondhand, especially when the contact person is a manager rather than an experienced user.

Moreover, this study examined trust antecedents based on Zucker [98]. Trust may also be increased or ruined through other aspects of the interaction, such as the economic aspects of the relationship. Trust may also be influenced by a variety of other elements that are beyond the relationship itself, such as a trusting disposition and a belief in humanity [66, 80], as shown with regard to novice online users [27]. These aspects were not included because theory [66, 80] suggests that these modes apply primarily before the trusting party has had the chance to establish a specific assessment of the trusted party.

## Implications

Perhaps the most significant implication of the research is the need to recognize that ERP customization projects, and probably also the implementation of other complex IT, should be managed with the dual objectives of creating a useful IT and of building a trusting relationship with the client. Although the explicit essence of the client’s business relationship with the vendor is to improve the usefulness of its ERP, the client’s relationship with the customization vendor, specifically client trust and its antecedents, is also an essential aspect of this business relationship and contributes to its value. This conclusion supports observations in other business relationships where a trusting relationship contributes its additional value by making the relationship even more worthwhile [54, 78]. The data also provide some initial guidelines as to the relative importance of investing in a trusting relationship with the client in comparison with investing in the quality of the IT. Both are necessary, but trust, important as it is, is not more important than providing the client with a useful system. If the results can be generalized to other large IT implementation projects, then creating trust in the vendor, or in the IT implementation management, may help reduce the IT implementation project failure rate, currently estimated at 85 percent [44].

The data also support the propositions from the banking world of approximately 100 years ago on how such trust can be built [98]—specifically, that there are several strategies that vendor managers can implement to increase client trust in them. Convincing the client that the vendor is doing the utmost to help is a central strategy toward achieving this trust. This conclusion is in accordance with the recommendations for the implementation of other complex IT systems that require extensive customization and training, such as expert systems [33]. The data also suggest that convincing the client that the vendor has people with the same characteristics as the client has is a strategy with a significant impact, although of a lesser degree. This conclusion echoes the widely publicized difference between IT and non-IT personnel and its possible problematic impact on the successful implementation of new IT [86, 87] and empirical results showing that reducing these differences can result in a better assessment of the IT [34]

To the extent that the results can be generalized, the data suggest that the institution-based mode of creating trust may also be an important influential antecedent of trust in these cases, although more research is needed to clarify what type of credentials have the most impact. In this context, the data imply that the creation of a certifying institute that will specialize in ERP customization quality control could contribute to the industry through the creation of an institutional trust-building mechanism. Such mechanisms helped the American banking industry in times of uncertainty by creating an alternative to process-mode built trust [98] and currently contribute to the creation of trust in online marketplaces where process-mode mechanisms are not always available [74].

Even if, in the short run, investing in such a strategy may seem too expensive, the benefits, extrapolating from other business relationships (see [23, 45]), can be very valuable in the long run. Relationships based on trust tend to be long term and are characterized by higher degrees of loyalty and reduced negotiation, monitoring, and transaction costs. Developing such a relationship with the clients could thus cement additional projects later on, even when the price is higher than that requested by other vendors, as is the case with other business interaction [77, 78]. The data also suggest that one of the benefits of creating a trusting relationship with the clients is its contribution to the perceived usefulness of the ERP itself, way beyond its direct contribution to the value of the relationship.

## Conclusion

Trust is a central and vital aspect in many long-term business relationships [98, p. 56]. This study shows that the same applies to ERP customization: trust directly affects client assessment that the business relationship is worthwhile even when the qualities of the IT are brought into account. The effects of trust might arguably be even more important than those described in this study, determining a long-term partnership orientation and a lesser emphasis on controls and short-term benefits [23]. Incorporating trust creation into ERP customization projects—and probably the same also applies to other IT implementation projects—should be a prime objective of management, just as increasing the usefulness and ease of use of the IT is.

## NOTES

1. TAM, in theory and as it has been applied in most previous research, deals mainly with perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as antecedents of voluntary use or intended use by individual users of an IT. TAM has been validated across IT types (see Gefen and Straub [36] for a detailed listing and discussion), including those that require extensive technical support to be used effectively [33]. The adoption of an ERP is different. ERP adoption is typically an organizational-level decision that is based on many strategic issues. Within this context, making the IT useful and easy to use to the employees also serves such strategic issues.

2. Some researchers, on the other hand, such as Pavlou [73], claim that trust should directly increase perceived ease of use, at least in the case of e-commerce, by allowing an online buyer who trusts the vendor to gloss over the vendor’s Web site without constantly checking the interface and information. Presumably, by trusting the vendor, the customer will automatically rule out foul play as claimed by Gefen [27]. Avoiding the need to constantly check the interface should, claims Pavlou, make it easier to use an e-commerce Web site. Pavlou [73], however, did not include trust antecedents in his model, whereas Gefen et al. [38] did.

3. Empirically, past studies found that these three beliefs sometimes mix and sometimes do not. In worker appraisal scenarios [60], in online work groups [48], and in some instances of online shopping [30, 65], the three beliefs have been reported to be statistically distinct factors. In other studies, that was not the case. In virtual communities, integrity and benevolence are reported to form one dimension and ability another [79], and in some cases of online shopping, the three beliefs are united [38]. However, in buyer–seller relationships that most closely resemble the scenario of this study, the three beliefs appear as one dimension [19].
