# Stopping Behavior of Systems Analysts During Information Requirements Elicitation

MITZI G. PITTS AND GLENN J. BROWNE

MITZI G. PITTS is an Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at the University of Memphis. She received her Ph.D. from the University of Maryland, Baltimore. Her research focuses on the cognitive and behavioral issues of systems analysts’ performance during the development of information systems. Of particular interest are the communication processes between systems analysts and end users during information requirements determination. Her research benefits from varied industry experience that includes extensive systems analysis and development for both large and small organizations.

GLENN J. BROWNE is the James C. Wetherbe Professor of Information Technology at the Rawls College of Business Administration at Texas Tech University. He received his Ph.D. from the Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota. His research focuses on cognitive and behavioral issues in information requirements determination, systems development, e-business, and managerial decision-making. His articles have appeared in Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, and other journals.

ABSTRACT: Understanding the cognitive activities of analysts during information requirements determination (IRD) has been recognized as a key indicator of IRD success. The research presented here examines one such cognitive activity: analysts determination of the sufficiency of information gathered during the elicitation of requirements. Research in behavioral decision-making has identified various heuristics, or stopping rules, that are used to gauge the sufficiency of the information obtained and to terminate information acquisition. Despite the fact that analysts undoubtedly employ such stopping rules in requirements elicitation, no research has studied this phenomenon. In the present research, we present a classification of stopping rules appropriate for information gathering problems. Stopping-rule use was identified for 54 practicing systems analysts participating in a requirements determination problem in a laboratory setting. Results indicated that analyst experience influences the application of specific cognitive stopping rules, and that the use of these stopping rules has an impact on requirements determination outcomes. In addition, the use of certain stopping rules resulted in greater quantity and completeness of requirements elicited from users. Theoretical implications for the elicitation of information and practical implications for the training of systems analysts are discussed.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: cognitive stopping rules, information gathering, knowledge elicitation, requirements determination, systems analysis.

INFORMATION REQUIREMENTS DETERMINATION (IRD) is the process by which systems analysts acquire an understanding of the problem to be solved and the definition of the users’ needs and expectations for a proposed information system (IS). A significant portion of IS development failures are attributed to incomplete and inaccurate information requirements [7, 14, 21, 67, 75, 81, 82, 83]. Incomplete or inaccurate requirements result in major postimplementation revisions [82] that account for a significant portion of the maintenance costs of IS [38, 51, 62]. The cost of correcting postimplementation problems is considerably higher than that of making changes earlier in the development process [6, 55, 62]. Thus, research that identifies methods for improving the completeness and accuracy of requirements obtained during IRD can provide significant benefits to IS development efforts.

The majority of research on requirements determination has focused on the structure and use of specific methods or tools that analysts use during IRD. The emphasis has been primarily on formal or “hard” approaches (see Potts et al. [49] and Jones [33] for examples). In contrast, much less attention has been given to the cognitive capabilities of the systems analyst, without whom the formal tools are irrelevant. There is evidence that improving the cognitive strategies of a systems analyst can improve the outcome of the IRD task [21, 37, 73, 80, 86]. Past research has examined cognitive differences between high-performing and low-performing analysts [59, 77], cognitive appropriateness of various tools and techniques used for eliciting knowledge [11, 36, 40], and analyst and user communication during the IRD task [7, 41, 73]. However, many aspects of analyst cognition are still unexplored, prompting researchers to pursue a more detailed examination of the IRD process [1].

Both cognitive (e.g., application of heuristics and satisficing) and motivational reasons (e.g., time on task) lead analysts to collect less-than-complete information during IRD [21]. In the current research, we examine one cognitive process that impacts analysts’ ability to obtain complete and accurate requirements: the evaluation of the sufficiency of information obtained during requirements determination. Specifically, we examine the cognitive heuristics (i.e., stopping rules) used by analysts to establish the point at which the requirements elicited are sufficient to continue with the system development effort.<sup>1</sup> Of particular interest are the influence of analyst experience on the application of stopping rules and the effect of stopping-rule use on the requirements elicited. It is widely acknowledged that all requirements for an IS are rarely known before design begins and that requirements continue to emerge throughout system development. However, analysts must gather an initial set of requirements prior to design or prototype development. This study is focused on the analysts’ determination of the sufficiency of this initial set of requirements.

## The Requirements Determination Process

IRD is a complex, interactive process during which a variety of stakeholders attempt to identify and validate the requirements of a proposed system. It is typically the analyst’s responsibility to direct and control the IRD process [69]. To manage this effectively, an analyst must possess critical problem-solving skills [16, 43, 75], which allow him to apply structure to an abstract problem, acquire and organize necessary and diverse information, and transform that information into a complete and accurate set of requirements [77]. Strong inquiry techniques are necessary for directing the IRD process toward issues that are relevant and critical [69]. Furthermore, the assessments of completeness, accuracy, and reliability of the requirements hinge on the analyst’s ability to conduct effective inquiry [79]. These assessments of sufficiency have always been assumed in requirements determination research.

Beyond macro-level attention to interviewing (e.g., structured versus unstructured approaches and interviewing “manners”), there is very little prescriptive guidance available to the practitioner for requirements elicitation. In an ideal world, the analyst would gather requirements indefinitely, thereby ensuring a comprehensive set of requirements. In fact, thorough predecisional information gathering generally reduces the risk of missing information later in the decision process [18]. However, excessive information gathering consumes costly resources. The analyst, therefore, has the conflicting goals of effectiveness (acquiring the best information possible) and efficiency (not wasting resources on costly information acquisition that is unnecessary). Consequently, it is important for the analyst to balance acquisition costs against improved completeness and accuracy of requirements.

Experimental results indicate that humans do not balance information costs and benefits well [17, 18, 48]. Generally, decision-makers fall victim to two types of acquisition errors: overacquiring and underacquiring [18]. In IRD, overacquisition involves the gathering of more information than is needed and results in wasted time and resources in the elicitation and analysis of requirements. Underacquisition, on the other hand, generates an incomplete view of the goals and functionality of the proposed system, leading to potential design problems, iterative redesign, implementation and maintenance difficulties, and possible system failure. The costs associated with the surfacing of such problems later in the systems development life cycle are often significantly higher than those of discovering the problems during requirements determination [6, 55, 62]. Therefore, it is quite likely that the costs of underspecification are much greater than the costs of overspecification when the entire systems development life cycle is considered.

Effective and efficient requirements determination requires the analyst to identify the point at which the requirements are adequate to move to the next phase of system development. To determine adequacy, the analyst invokes a stopping rule to assess the sufficiency of the information obtained. An examination of prior research focusing on information acquisition provides theory and background concerning the application of stopping rules for the assessment of sufficiency of information.

## Stopping Rules in Information Acquisition

IN MOST DECISION-MAKING SITUATIONS, a person gathers information until he believes that information is sufficient, at which point he stops acquiring information and commits to a decision or course of action. Sufficiency is characterized by both completeness and correctness of the information [65]. Physicians, for example, must decide when to stop performing diagnostic testing and administer treatment. Marketing analysts stop collecting focus-group data and begin constructing a new product marketing plan. Systems analysts terminate information gathering from users and prepare system requirements documents. These situations are examples of optional stopping problems, in which a person invokes a stopping rule to decide when to stop acquiring information [53].

The concept of stopping rules has been investigated extensively in decision-making theory and optional stopping contexts. Various normative stopping rules have been identified [13, 48] based on the expected loss from terminating information acquisition [13], the economic value of information [66], and the expected value of additional information [34]. However, these normative models fail to describe the actual behavior of decision-makers. In fact, research focused on cognitive activities of decision-makers provides evidence that people stop information acquisition too soon [4, 47, 52, 60], fail to access relevant information [25, 61], fail to consider all appropriate alternatives [23], and commit other errors of omission [25]. As a result, stopping rules have been identified that reflect actual cognitive processes of people as opposed to the idealized processes suggested in the normative models [3, 28, 45, 52].

Nickles et al. [45] proposed a set of stopping rules that is hypothesized to be descriptive of the type of cognitive activity occurring in IRD. These stopping rules are used in this research as a basis for understanding analysts’ assessments of sufficiency. The distinct psychological processes upon which these rules rely all require the analyst to distinguish between useful and irrelevant information. The specific rules are discussed in subsequent paragraphs and summarized in Table 1.

The magnitude threshold stopping rule assumes that an analyst’s belief concerning the sufficiency of information must reach some predetermined level, or threshold, before he will reach a conclusion [45, 78]. The analyst sets a threshold that acts as the stopping criterion for the cumulative impact of the information acquired. He maintains a mental “running total” of the cumulative impact of the information [27], and when the internal tabulation crosses the intended threshold, information acquisition is terminated.

The difference threshold stopping rule requires that a decision-maker have a preexisting rule (or threshold) for determining when he is not learning anything new. As he gathers information, he assesses the marginal value of each latest piece of information acquired [45]. When the marginal value of new information falls below his preexisting threshold, he stops gathering more requirements. That is, the last piece of additional information must not add substantially to the analyst’s knowledge base for the problem. At that point, he ceases information gathering.

The ability to set thresholds and judge when they have been exceeded is a familiar psychological process that has been investigated in both decision-making (e.g., [12]) and psychophysical research (e.g., [68]). The magnitude threshold and difference threshold rules are both sufficiency threshold models, and we expect them to be descriptive of analysts’ stopping behavior.

The mental list stopping rule involves the use of a person’s belief structures [5, 58] for the construction of mental lists of requirements or criteria sets (see also [57]). A mental list is assumed to be the result of past experience with a particular type of task, and use of the mental-list rule is expected to be related to experience and expertise. As information is obtained, arguments are made for or against using that piece of information to fulfill a requirement on the list. Once the analyst reasons that all of the items on the list have been discovered or verified, the information acquisition ceases.

Representational stability is a stopping rule that involves continuous adaptation of a person’s problem space or representation of a problem situation [45]. First, the analyst develops a mental representation of the situation. A “mental representation” can theoretically take many forms; it may be an organized knowledge structure, it may be a “picture” in one’s mind, or it may take other forms [54]. As requirements are obtained, the analyst’s representation is adjusted to reflect the new evidence. Eventually, the representation stabilizes, and the analyst concludes that he has sufficient knowledge to understand the situation and stops gathering information [85].

Although stopping rules are essential aspects of a person’s thinking, their use has not been studied in IRD. The stopping rules discussed above are arguably more appropriate in information acquisition problems than other stopping rules proposed in the literature because they focus on the sufficiency of information obtained. They are therefore proposed to help understand analysts’ stopping behavior during requirements determination.<sup>2</sup>

## Research Model

FIGURE 1 PRESENTS THE CONCEPTUAL MODEL used to guide this research. In the reality of systems development projects, there are many environmental factors, such as time, budget, and environment, that may also influence the termination of IRD. However, since this research focuses on the analysts’ determination of the sufficiency of the requirements, those factors were held constant. To investigate the use of cognitive stopping rules to gauge sufficiency in requirements determination, we tested the following relationships: the relationship between analyst experience and the requirements determination outcome, the influence of analyst experience on the application of cognitive stopping rules, and the impact of cognitive stopping-rule use on requirements determination outcome.

Past research focusing on the differences between experts and novices suggests that experience may be an indicator of improved performance [21, 59, 71, 74, 76, 80]. However, the application of stopping rules is such a fundamental cognitive activity in the context of information acquisition that it may actually make expert–novice differences insignificant. For example, higher levels of experience may result in a tendency for the analyst to infer requirements rather than explicitly elicit them [42]. Furthermore, while experienced analysts may obtain more information, more does not necessarily result in better performance [10]. In fact, research has shown that too much information can interfere with effectiveness, and that experienced analysts may, in fact, be distracted by an abundance of information [26, 29]. These findings were supported by Marakas and Elam [41], who concluded that low and high experienced analysts are equally likely to elicit incomplete and inaccurate requirements without some structuring of the IRD process. Therefore, in the absence of a priori structuring of an analyst’s requirements determination process, we hypothesize (stated in the alternative form):

## H1: Requirements determination outcomes will be unrelated to analyst experience.

To explain the difference in requirements determination outcomes, we posit that the application of cognitive stopping rules is an intervening variable in the relationship between analyst experience and requirements determination outcome. Therefore, we first examine the influence of analyst experience on the application of cognitive stopping rules. From a theoretical standpoint, it is known that a person’s procedures for performing a task generally change as he gains experience [63]. This is as true for systems development as it is for other domains [59]. Therefore, we expect that the stopping rules used by more experienced analysts will differ from those used by less experienced analysts.

Research on expert and novice analysts indicates that experts have more knowledge about the problem domain, and that novices have difficulty in defining the problem [59]. Development of a useful mental-list rule requires that an analyst have enough experience and a sufficiently substantial level of domain knowledge to facilitate construction of a legitimate list of requirements. Without this domain experience, it is unlikely that an analyst can invoke a meaningful list of required elements. Further, more experienced analysts should have good heuristics for knowing how much information is enough to design a system, and so should have confidence in setting a magnitude threshold for information. Consequently, we expect that the mental-list and magnitude threshold rules will be used by more experienced analysts.

On the other hand, we expect that the difference threshold and representational stability rules will be used by less experienced analysts. The difference threshold stopping rule may require less cognitive effort on the part of the analyst, since there is no need for the analyst to know how much information is enough. The analyst simply stops the information acquisition when he is no longer learning anything new, without regard to volume or quality. Similarly, the use of the representational stability rule may also require a relatively lower level of cognitive effort. The analyst does not have to form a mental list or set a magnitude threshold a priori; rather, he simply continues collecting information until his mental representation of the problem becomes stable.

H2: Analysts with more experience will apply the magnitude threshold and mental-list stopping rules.

Past research has shown that the use of stopping rules can result in premature termination of information acquisition, resulting in underacquisition [4, 23, 24, 25, 47, 61]. Underacquisition during IRD results in incomplete and inaccurate requirements, presenting a potentially significant threat to system success. Therefore, we are interested in examining the extent to which the use of particular stopping rules impacts requirements determination outcomes.

H3: The requirements determination outcome will differ depending on the stopping rule applied by the analyst.

For the purposes of this study, requirements determination outcome is defined as a sufficient set of requirements for subsequent system design. We correlate sufficiency with the quantity and quality of requirements. To evaluate the quantity and quality of requirements elicited, we utilize a taxonomy of requirements proposed by Byrd et al. [14] and later expanded upon by Browne and Rogich [9]. This scheme includes problem domain entities believed to be critical for successful design of an IS [14] and provides for identification of both functional and behavioral requirements. Thus, an ideal set of requirements for most systems would arguably include a significant number of requirements from each of the defined categories. In the taxonomy, requirements are organized into levels: goal, process, task, and information. The goal-level requirements focus on understanding the overall context in which the IS is being developed and the organizational goals for the system. In process-level requirements, emphasis is placed on the analyses of actual business activities. Task-level requirements concentrate on specific steps required to fulfill the business activities and how the environment influences them. Finally, the information-level requirements are based on a complete understanding of the domain’s data needs and their relationships. These universal requirement categories arguably pertain to any problem domain or system development effort [56], making this classification technique useful for capturing requirements elicited in the present study. The taxonomy is presented in detail in the Appendix.

The total number of requirements elicited by an analyst determines the quantity of requirements. Quality is determined by the breadth and depth of requirements. Breadth refers to the number of different requirements categories that are utilized, and depth of requirements refers to the average number of requirements elicited within the requirements categories. A more complete set of requirements would comprise a broad range of requirement categories and explore each of these categories in depth. Breadth and depth measures were used because they represent a type of “quality” measure for the requirements elicited. The true “quality” of requirements elicited cannot, of course, be determined with any degree of certainty until much later in the system development process (e.g., during design or after implementation), if ever. Quality can be managed (although certainly not assured) during analysis with techniques such as client walk-throughs. However, given the controlled nature of the current research, such methods were not feasible. Therefore, in the present study, we utilized the breadth and depth of requirements as a measure of the completeness of the elicitation effort, which we offer as one surrogate for quality. Having divided the requirements determination outcome into quantity, breadth, and depth, we need to partition H1 and H3 to reflect these three variables.

H1a: The quantity of requirements will not be related to analyst experience.

H1b: The breadth of requirements will not be related to analyst experience.

H1c: The depth of requirements will not be related to analyst experience.

H3a: The quantity of requirements will differ depending on the stopping rule applied by the analyst.

H3b: The breadth of requirements will differ depending on the stopping rule applied by the analyst.

H3c: The depth of requirements will differ depending on the stopping rule applied by the analyst.

## Methodology

THE PARTICIPANTS FOR THIS STUDY were 54 practicing systems analysts who were recruited from organizations in the Baltimore metropolitan area. Twelve different organizations participated, representing a variety of industry segments including banking, finance, insurance, construction, manufacturing, aerospace, government, research, and education. Only analysts with at least two years of experience in system development projects were eligible to participate in the study. It is doubtful that less experienced analysts would have participated in enough projects to possess fully developed heuristics for assessing the sufficiency of requirements. Without the existence of consistent and stable stopping heuristics, it would be difficult to identify the stopping behavior of systems analysts.

The experiment employed a case scenario for the development of an online grocery shopping system. The familiarity of the grocery shopping task increased the likelihood of similar domain knowledge across all participants. We expected that this task would adequately stimulate the analysts, ensuring an IRD process as realistic as can be accomplished in a laboratory setting. Furthermore, there is empirical evidence of the effectiveness of this scenario for eliciting rich data from users [56].

Participants were tested individually, and each experimental session was audio recorded. A person unfamiliar with systems development and blind to the research questions in this study assumed the role of grocery store manager (the “user”) in the scenario, and the same user was employed for all participants. This user was thoroughly briefed concerning requirements for the system. During the experimental session, the analyst (participant) made inquiries concerning requirements for the proposed system, and the user responded with a statement of information that directly addressed the participant’s request. The participant was permitted to take notes during the session if he desired. The participant was given no guidance regarding the structuring of the requirements; he was simply asked to continue gathering information from the user until he felt that sufficient information had been obtained to continue with the design of the system.

In addition, each participant was asked to draw a representation of the requirements elicited. The initial instructions for the experimental session informed the analyst that he would be expected to perform this subtask. However, there was no standardization or suggestion as to the type of representation. This was to avoid instilling anxiety in someone without a particular skill set (e.g., data flow diagramming). The generation of representational devices is a natural culmination of the requirements determination process. Asking the participant to create these representations provided him with a specific goal for the IRD process and added another element of reality to the experimental session. However, the resulting representations were not analyzed by the researchers.

## Measurement of Variables

The constructs of interest in this study are analyst experience, stopping-rule utilization, and requirements determination outcome. Analyst experience was assessed at the end of each experimental session and is the self-reported number of years the participant had functioned as a systems analyst. The stopping-rule utilization and requirements determination outcome measures were determined through analysis of the interview protocols and a self-reporting questionnaire.

The tape recordings for each participant’s requirements elicitation session were transcribed and the protocols were then parsed and coded into specific requirements categories. This coding scheme is adapted from Browne and Rogich [9] and was discussed in the previous section. An independent coder unfamiliar with the purposes of the research and blind to its hypotheses was used to code all of the parsed transcriptions. To assess the reliability of the initial coding, a second independent coder was asked to code a random sample of ten participant transcriptions. On average, the coders assigned 82 percent of the requirements to the same categories. To assess the degree of interrater agreement not attributable to chance, Cohen’s kappa was calculated [22, 70]. The kappa coefficient for these data was 0.701, which is considered “substantial” agreement [35]. Considering the number of categories and complexity of the utterances, this level of agreement is considered quite reliable [22]. The results of the coding performed by the primary coder were used in the data analysis.

Stopping-rule use was ascertained by analyzing each participant’s protocol and the self-reporting questionnaire. Each protocol was examined for evidence of stoppingrule use on the part of the participant using a scanning tactic—examination of the protocol for anecdotal information [8]. Key phrases and/or patterns of dialogue were identified and a predominant stopping rule was determined.<sup>3</sup> Since the participants were unaware of the focus of the study, we did not expect clear, explicit references to stopping behavior. Therefore, a post-task questionnaire was administered to supplement the protocol data. The questionnaire consisted of four indirect questions aimed at uncovering the participant’s reasoning when determining sufficiency of the requirements gathered. These questions served as a stimulus for participants to continue saying more about their reasons for ending the requirements sessions, and the responses were treated as an extension of the verbal protocol. Utterances found in the interview protocols and written responses that reflected stopping behavior were analyzed and coded into a stopping-rule category based on criteria determined a priori [70]. As before, two independent coders unfamiliar with the purposes of the research were used to code the stopping rules. A comparison of the coding results indicated that the coders assigned the same stopping rule to 89 percent of the participants. Cohen’s kappa was calculated to assess the degree of interrater agreement not attributable to chance. The kappa coefficient for these data was 0.849, which is considered “nearly perfect” agreement [35]. For instances in which the coders disagreed, the appropriate stopping-rule category to be used was determined by the two coders through discussion. ## Results

ALL SYSTEMS ANALYSTS PARTICIPATING in this study reported at least two years of experience performing requirements determination. The mean number of years of experience gathering requirements was 11.07 years $( \mathrm { s . d . } = 6 . 6 9 )$ . Therefore, it is reasonable to believe that this sample of analysts possessed sufficient maturity to have developed strategies for requirements elicitation. The average time spent on the requirements determination task for the analysts was 48.31 minutes $( \mathrm { s . d . } = 1 3 . 6 9 )$ , with the following means by stopping-rule group: difference threshold = 51.3 minutes, mental list = 52.6 minutes, magnitude threshold = 40.8 minutes, and representational stability = 45.2 minutes. There were no significant differences in time spent on the task between groups $( F _ { ( 3 , 5 0 ) } = 1 . 9 1 ; p = 0 . 1 4 )$ .

To determine support for H1, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to ascertain the correlation between analyst experience and requirements determination outcome. The results of these tests are shown in Table 3. In each case, the correlation coefficient was insignificant (experience/quantity of requirements, $p = 0 . 5 9 1 ;$ experience/breadth of requirements, $p = 0 . 2 6 9 ;$ experience/depth of requirements, $p { = } 0 . 9 0 7 $ . These results support H1 and suggest that analyst experience is not an indicator of requirements determination outcome. That is, quantity, breadth, and depth of requirements elicited were not related to the years of experience of analysts.

To examine the application of stopping rules by the analysts, we first examined the distribution of stopping-rule use among participants. The coding indicated that, of the 54 participants in the study, 22 analysts exhibited use of the difference threshold stopping rule, 13 used the representational stability rule, 10 applied the mental list rule, and 9 used the magnitude threshold rule. Because we had no a priori beliefs concerning the frequencies of participants using each stopping rule, a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test was conducted to see whether stopping-rule use differed from random usage (i.e., that the four stopping rules would be used by an equal number of participants). The test indicated that the numbers of participants using the rules were different from the expected numbers $( \chi _ { ~ ( 3 ) } ^ { 2 } = 7 . 8 ; p = 0 . 0 5 )$ . The Bonferroni multiple comparison procedure for multinomial proportions [2] showed that the difference between the number of participants using the difference threshold rule (22) and the magnitude threshold rule (9) was significant. No other differences between usage numbers were statistically significant.

Table 4 contains the means, standard deviations, and statistics from the tests of H2. For this hypothesis, we tested the mean years as an analyst by stopping-rule use. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) shows that the differences in the means were significant at the $\alpha = 0 . 1 0$ level $( F _ { ( 3 , 5 0 ) } = 2 . 1 8 7 ; p = 0 . 1 0 )$ . The least-significant-differences multiple comparison procedure showed that the years of experience for analysts using the mental-list rule and magnitude threshold rule were both significantly greater than the years of experience for analysts using the representational stability rule at the $\alpha = 0 . 1 0$ level. Given the absolute differences in the means, the statistical differences between the stopping-rule groups are not as significant as might be expected. Nevertheless, these results indicate the mental-list and magnitude threshold rules are more likely to be used by analysts with greater experience, suggesting support for H2.

Support for H3a, H3b, and H3c was determined by comparing the requirements determination outcome between stopping-rule groups. As described previously, outcome is defined as the quantity and quality (breadth and depth) of requirements elicited. The results of these comparisons are shown in Table 5. Due to lack of homogeneous variances, a Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare the means for the quantity of requirements [19]. The analysis revealed that there was a significant difference in the total number of requirements obtained between the stopping-rule groups $( F _ { ( 3 , 5 0 ) } = 2 . 7 9 ; p = 0 . 0 5 )$ , providing support for H3a. Pairwise comparisons revealed that analysts using the mental-list rule elicited significantly more requirements than analysts using the magnitude threshold rule, and that the analysts using the difference threshold rule elicited more requirements than analysts using the magnitude threshold rule. No other differences were significant.

Next, an ANOVA was used to analyze the breadth of requirements elicited, but no significant differences were found $( F _ { ( 3 , 5 0 ) } = 1 . 7 2 3 ; p = 0 . 1 7 4 )$ . All participants, regardless of the stopping rule used, exhibited the same breadth of elicited requirements during the experimental sessions, and therefore H3b is not supported. For the depth of requirements, the mental-list and magnitude threshold stopping-rule groups did not exhibit equality of variance. Therefore, a Kruskal-Wallis test using an ANOVA on the ranked data was performed [19]. The analysis showed a significant difference in depth of requirements between the stopping-rule groups $( F _ { ( 3 , 5 0 ) } = 3 . 4 0 ; p = 0 . 0 2 5 )$ indicating support for H3c. The multiple comparisons procedure showed that analysts using the difference threshold and mental-list stopping rules elicited significantly more depth of requirements than analysts using the representational stability stopping rule and the magnitude threshold rule.

## Discussion

THIS RESEARCH STUDY PROPOSED that the application of cognitive stopping rules by systems analysts during requirements determination has an intervening effect on the relationship between analyst experience and requirements determination outcome (quality and quantity of requirements elicited). Figure 2 summarizes the results of the hypotheses discussed above.

Our findings substantiate prior research indicating that analyst experience alone is not sufficient to explain requirements determination outcomes. Moreover, we have shown that analyst experience influences stopping-rule use and that the application of stopping rules impacts requirements determination outcomes. Specifically, more experienced analysts tended to use the mental-list and magnitude threshold rules, while less experienced analysts utilized the difference threshold and representational stability rules more often. This discovery parallels what is known about the differences in novice and expert systems analysts’ problem-solving abilities [59]. The results are not surprising, given that inexperienced problem solvers are more likely to use problem-solving heuristics that have face validity and are easy to apply. As noted earlier, the difference threshold and representational stability stopping rules are arguably less cognitively demanding and thus applied more easily by less experienced systems analysts.

Furthermore, our findings showed that the use of the mental-list and difference threshold stopping rules resulted in greater quantity of requirements than the magnitude threshold rule and greater depth of requirements than the representational stability and magnitude threshold rules. This is particularly interesting, since one of the more effective stopping rules was more prevalent among experienced analysts (the mental-list rule) and the other was demonstrated more frequently by less experienced analysts (the difference threshold rule). However, when considering characteristics of human problem solving, this result is not unexpected. Factors such as training, preparation, cognitive abilities, and personality traits cause some experienced problem solvers to develop better heuristics for performing tasks than other experienced problem solvers [44, 59, 64]. Similarly, some less experienced problem solvers are able to perform tasks well despite their lack of experience, due to the factors mentioned above and the application of general problem-solving heuristics that work well much of the time [44, 72]. It is possible that the difference threshold rule, used successfully in the current problem-solving task, is a problem-solving heuristic that works well with inexperienced analysts in general.

The magnitude threshold rule, which resulted in lower total requirements and depth of requirements than two of the other rules, may be a rule that is inherently difficult to use, at least in this context. It is arguable that establishing a threshold and keeping a mental running total of information may overwhelm working memory in such a complex environment. Further research is needed to gain a more complete understanding of the use of this rule and the contexts in which it may be used more effectively.

## Implications and Contributions

This research provides several important contributions to the theory and practice of systems development. The most important contribution is to theory supporting requirements determination. Researchers are discovering the importance of cognitive activities in requirements determination [9, 21, 36, 37, 40, 41, 59, 73, 77, 80, 86]. The current study adds to this body of research by providing evidence of the influences and consequences of cognitive stopping-rule application during IRD. This knowledge can help us to understand the behavior of systems analysts as they attempt to balance the goals of effectiveness and efficiency during requirements determination.

A second contribution is the application of these findings to the practice of requirements determination. Our results support the need to inform systems analysts concerning stopping-rule use, since people are generally unaware that they invoke stopping rules when gathering information. Based on our findings, analysts should be encouraged to consciously construct mental lists of necessary requirements prior to IRD and to iteratively assess the amount of new information they are acquiring. Conversely, analysts should be discouraged from relying on their ability to assess the sufficiency of requirements based on the magnitude of information obtained or the stability of their representations. It is likely that the complexity of the requirements necessary for systems development will confound the analyst’s ability to gauge how much information is enough. Similarly, the dynamic systems development environment analysts ordinarily face is likely to make it difficult to establish when a representation is truly stable.

As shown in prior research, providing structure to the requirements determination process results in more accurate and complete outcomes [1, 41]. Explicit training in the application of stopping rules can provide important structuring to the IRD process. For example, the formation of mental lists could be facilitated through the sharing of analyst experience and the use of prescribed frameworks designed to identify critical elements for system design. In addition, the use of the difference threshold stopping rule could be taught to analysts as a simple rule of thumb; that is, “gather information until you are learning nothing new.”<sup>4</sup> Formalizing the use of specific techniques derived from the mental-list and difference threshold rules within system development methodologies should greatly enhance the accuracy and completeness of requirements determination efforts.

The results of this study can also be generalized to other phases of system development. Stopping-rule use in the latter stages of system development has been considered in prior research [15, 30, 31, 84]. However, these investigations have been limited to activities in which algorithmic or normative stopping rules with tangible and measurable factors are employed—for instance, software testing and various aspects of design. There are many decision points in the development of an IS for which information must be gathered and assessed for adequacy. As one example, user interfaces are often designed using a prototyping technique [32]. Throughout the evolution of the interface, analysts make assessments as to the sufficiency of the design to determine when to stop the prototyping cycle. It is reasonable to believe that cognitive stopping rules are being applied in these situations and that the decisions being made are affected by the consequences of stopping-rule use.

## Limitations

As with any micro-level empirical investigation, there are limitations that must be acknowledged when interpreting the results reported here. Given the lack of prior research on stopping-rule use in requirements determination, a laboratory task with professional systems analysts was considered to be the most effective method for isolating the analyst behavior under study. This method also provides the basis for a number of limitations. First, the grocery shopping task used for this research guaranteed low application of domain knowledge for all participants. While this desirably controls for application domain experience effects across the sample, it also masks the fact that practiced analysts often have a higher level of domain experience to guide their activities. Similarly, quantifying experience as self-reported years as an analyst fails to consider the impact of number of projects and project size on experience.

The requirements determination strategy used in this study also provides some possible limitations. In general, analysts use some combination of the following strategies to determine requirements: (1) ask users of the proposed system, (2) investigate features of an existing IS, (3) experiment with development of a proposed system, and (4) analyze characteristics of the task to be supported by the proposed IS [21]. In the present research, for control reasons, the requirements determination approach was confined to “ask users of the proposed system.” In addition, the analyst was limited to interactions with only one user. The development of multiple perspectives is often critical to requirements determination success [69], and contemporary methodologies focus on user involvement and rapid development strategies. Therefore, it would be impossible to generalize these results to the other strategies without additional research.

Our measurement of cognitive stopping rules may also have constrained the outcomes of this research. Decision-making theory has established that people use different problem-solving strategies for different tasks [46]. In fact, a person may alter stopping rules in mid-task as the characteristics of the environment or task change, resulting in the use of multiple stopping rules. Since stopping rules have never been investigated within the requirements determination process, it was most useful for our analysis to begin by identifying basic stopping-rule use. However, more complex strategies of stopping-rule use may exist in actual systems development efforts.

Finally, the taxonomy of requirements used in this study focuses on information requirements. As a result, infrastructure and other technical requirements are not considered explicitly in the coding scheme presented here.

## Recommendations for Future Research

This research is a first effort at understanding the cognitive stopping behavior of systems analysts during requirements determination. There are many aspects of this study that would benefit from additional research. For example, future research could investigate the existence of stopping rules in addition to those already identified in the behavioral decision-making literature. Knowledge surrounding the practice of requirements determination suggests that motivational stopping rules (i.e., rules driven by intrinsic factors in the analyst or by external factors such as time, budget, and environment) may exert a strong influence on analysts’ stopping behavior. These rules may impact the IRD process alone or in combination with cognitive stopping rules. It is also possible that analysts may use a combination or hybrid version of the cognitive stopping rules presented here. Therefore, it is important to investigate motivational stopping rules, additional cognitive stopping rules, and adaptive use of stopping rules as tasks change.

It is critical that additional research into the impacts of stopping-rule use include deeper examination of differing problem domains and requirements determination strategies. It is likely that the familiarity of the problem domain would have an impact on the effectiveness of the stopping rule invoked. Thus, research on different types of tasks would be useful. In addition, it is important to investigate the impact of stopping rules on other requirements determination strategies. For example, joint application development (JAD) techniques, in which many stakeholders participate in analysis and design activities, are recommended for obtaining user requirements [20, 39, 50]. In the contemporary context of IRD, it is important to examine both analyst and user stopping-rule utilization. Moreover, it is possible that the group dynamic present in JAD sessions would reduce, or even eliminate, the negative consequences of stopping-rule use during the requirements determination process.

Finally, this study has shown that the use of some stopping rules resulted in a more complete set of requirements than the use of other stopping rules, and that analyst experience contributes to stopping-rule utilization. However, since stopping-rule use is a cognitive activity and thus subject to the limitations of cognition, it is possible that the use of all stopping rules causes some level of underspecification regardless of the expertise of the analyst. Therefore, the research model presented here should be extended to include interventions that may mitigate the negative effects of stoppingrule use. For example, various prompting methods discussed in the literature have been shown to be effective in eliciting information from users and other stakeholders in a variety of domains (e.g., [10, 11, 82]). Perhaps these prompting methods are also effective in overcoming underspecification of requirements. This and other research can improve our understanding of analyst stopping behavior.
