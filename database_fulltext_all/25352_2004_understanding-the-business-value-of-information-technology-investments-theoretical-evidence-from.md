# Understanding the Business Value of Information Technology Investments: Theoretical Evidence from Alternative Market and Cost Structures

MATT E. THATCHER AND DAVID E. PINGRY

MATT E. THATCHER is an Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at the Eller College of Management at the University of Arizona, where he has been a member of the faculty since 1997. He holds a B.S. in economics and an M.A. and Ph.D. in information technology from the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. His research examines the economic effects of IT, including the business value of IT investments, the impact of patent policies on technology innovation, product design, and consumer welfare, and the strategic uses of information in the services industry.

DAVID E. PINGRY is a Professor of Management Information Systems and Economics at the Eller College of Management at the University of Arizona. Previously, Professor Pingry was on the faculty at Virginia Tech; he has held visiting faculty positions at Purdue University and Texas A&M; and he held the Treibick Visiting Endowed Chair in the Connecticut Information Technology Institute at the University of Connecticut. His current interests are decision support systems, patent policy, and IT productivity.

ABSTRACT: This paper develops a series of two-stage duopoly models of quality– price competition and a series of monopoly models of quality–price choice in order to examine the impact of information technology (IT) investments on firm profit, firm productivity, and consumer welfare. We solve the duopoly and monopoly models for four cost functions, where each function makes a different assumption about the form of the marginal cost of production. These models are used to conduct a twoby-four comparison [(monopoly, duopoly) × (four cost functions)] of the impact of IT investments on economic performance. The analysis reveals that together market structure and cost structure play a critical role in determining the form of the relationship between IT investment and economic measures. Specifically, moving from monopoly to duopoly and moving from zero marginal cost to marginal cost as a function of quality increase the number of economic measures for which the directional effects of IT investment are ambiguous, or depend on model parameter values.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: business value, consumer welfare, economic analysis, information technology investments, information technology value, productivity.

FOR ALMOST TWO DECADES, EMPIRICAL STUDIES in the information technology (IT) value literature have examined the impact of IT on many measures of economic performance, including firm productivity ([6, 7, 14], see [10] for a recent review), firm profitability ([4, 12, 14], see [10] for a recent review), consumer surplus [5, 8, 14], and intermediate performance measures such as product quality and output levels [1, 11, 17, 19]. Given the diversity of empirical results and the complexity of the relationships, recent work has explored IT value from a theoretical perspective [18, 22, 23, 24]. This paper extends this theoretical work by developing a series of twostage duopoly models of quality–price competition and a series of monopoly models of quality–price choice in order to examine the impact of IT investments on firm profit, firm productivity, and consumer welfare. We solve the duopoly and monopoly models for four cost functions, where each function makes a different assumption about the form of the marginal cost of production. These models are used to conduct a two-by-four comparison [(monopoly, duopoly) × (four cost functions)] of the impact of IT investments on economic performance.

Thatcher and Oliver [23] modeled IT value in the context of a monopolist that produces a single product, where marginal production cost is assumed to be a function of product quality. They showed that a monopolist leverages IT to design a better-quality product that it offers to consumers at a higher price. Although this adjustment to product design unambiguously leads to an increase in output levels and maximizes firm profits, it may do so at the expense of productivity. Specifically, Thatcher and Oliver found that the directional impact of IT on productivity (defined as the ratio of revenues to total costs) depends on the relationship between the firm’s indirect fixed costs and the market size.

Thatcher and Pingry [24] extended this monopoly model to examine the impact of IT on consumer surplus and on alternative measures of productivity (i.e., the ratio of revenues to direct production costs and the ratio of social welfare to direct production costs). They found that while IT investments unambiguously increase product quality and output levels, resulting in increases in firm profits and consumer surplus, the investments also unambiguously decrease the productivity measures. Alternatively, when a firm is unwilling or unable to improve product quality, then IT investments are shown to increase both profits and productivity. These relationships are shown to be robust to the socially optimal setting in which a social planner chooses product quality and price to maximize social welfare. In addition, the results of the monopoly model are shown to be robust for products that are offered free of charge (e.g., free online content), but provide indirect benefits to the firm (e.g., more advertising fees). Together, these analytical models highlight the way in which product quality moderates the relationship between IT investments and economic value in a monopoly setting when marginal production cost is assumed to be a function of product quality.

Quan et al. [18] extended the original monopoly model of Thatcher and Oliver [23] (which did not consider the impact of IT on consumer welfare) to a one-stage duopoly where each firm chooses its product quality and price simultaneously. They used numerical examples to show that, in contrast to the monopoly results, the directional effects of IT on profits and productivity (as well as price, demand, revenues, and costs) may depend on the relative sensitivity of the demand to the qualities and prices of the competing firms’ products (i.e., the parameters of the demand function).

Thatcher [22] extended the monopoly model of Thatcher and Pingry [24] (which considers consumer welfare and alternative measures of productivity) in two ways. First, he extended the model to a two-stage duopoly (first quality, then price). Second, he analyzed the economic impact of IT investments for products characterized by zero marginal production costs $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , information goods). In this context, Thatcher showed that the duopoly results are consistent with the monopoly results presented in Thatcher and Pingry [24] if a firm’s demand is more sensitive to the price (relative to the quality) of the competing firm’s product.

This paper extends this theoretical work by developing a series of two-stage duopoly models of quality–price competition and a series of monopoly models of quality– price choice in order to examine the impact of IT investments on firm profit, firm productivity, and consumer welfare. We solve the duopoly and monopoly models for four cost functions, where each function makes a different assumption about the form of the marginal cost of production. We model these eight combinations of market structure and cost structure to isolate the factors that drive the directional effects (i.e., positive, negative, or ambiguous) of IT investments on economic measures. Three of the eight model combinations have been partially examined in the literature [18, 22, 24]. The introduction of the five new models, four of which include a new cost parameter (see the marginal-cost-of-production parameter, v, which does not include quality-related costs, in Appendix Table D1) not previously considered in the literature, enables a more integrative analysis of IT value, leading to six new propositions and two new observations based on a total of 96 IT effects. One new finding from this analysis is that IT investments that lower v will improve firm profits, firm productivity, and consumer welfare in both the monopoly and duopoly cases. Alternatively, in the context of the integrative model, when considering the IT investments examined in the literature above, the number of directional effects of IT investments on economic measures that are ambiguous increase as the market structure moves from monopoly to duopoly. However, the analysis further shows that the nature and the degree of these ambiguities critically depend on the functional form of the marginal cost of production.

## The Duopoly Model

## Duopoly Model Description

WE CONSIDER A TWO-STAGE MODEL OF DUOPOLY COMPETITION. In the first stage, Firms 1 and 2 simultaneously choose a level of product quality. In the second stage, each having observed the quality of its competitor, the firms simultaneously set prices.<sup>1</sup> We now formally describe the game, beginning with the second stage. If, at the first stage, Firm 1 chooses product quality $s _ { 1 }$ and Firm 2 chooses product quality $s _ { 2 } ,$ then the demand for Firm $i \mathrm { \ ' } _ { \mathrm { s } }$ product at the second stage is given by (see Table D1 in Appendix D for model notation)

$$
Q _ {i} = a - \lambda (p _ {i} - b p _ {j}) + \theta (s _ {i} - c s _ {j}), i, j = 1, 2, i \neq j,\tag{1}
$$

where $a , \lambda , \theta > 0$ are constants; $p _ { i }$ and $p _ { j }$ are the prices set by Firm i and Firm j, respectively; b is a factor that determines the negative effect of a decrease in the competitor’s price on each firm’s demand; and c is a factor that determines the negative effect of an increase in the competitor’s quality on each firm’s demand. We assume that $0 < b , c < 1$ , which implies that the demand for each firm’s product is affected more by changes in its own quality and pricing decisions than those of its competitor. Similar forms of this demand function have been used in the IT literature [2, 3, 18, 22].

The cost to Firm i of designing, developing, and manufacturing a product of quality $s _ { i }$ is given by

$$
C _ {i} = f s _ {i} ^ {2} + (v + h s _ {i}) Q _ {i}, i = 1, 2,\tag{2}
$$

where $f , \nu , h > 0$ are technology parameters that characterize the efficiency of the firm’s cost structure. Given Equation (2), a firm’s choice of product quality affects costs in two ways. The first cost term, $, f s _ { i } ^ { 2 }$ , is the fixed production cost, where f is the rate at which the marginal cost of further improvements in product quality rises as quality increases. This cost term represents the R&D costs associated with the design and development of a product of given quality. The quadratic form of the fixed production cost has intuitive appeal; is widely used in the industrial organization and IT literatures [2, 3, 13, 16, 20]; and has been used to analyze a diverse range of products, including software products and information goods, medical services, and airline services. The second cost term, $( \nu + h s _ { i } ) Q _ { i }$ , is the total variable production cost. The marginal production cost, $\nu + h s _ { i } ,$ is divided into two components. The first component, v, represents the marginal production cost not including the quality-related costs. Given a quality level $s _ { i }$ set by the firm, the marginal production cost increases by $h s _ { i }$

The realization of parameter f in Equation (2) characterizes the efficiency of the firm’s product design and development capability, while the realizations of parameters v and h characterize the efficiency of the firm’s manufacturing and distribution capability. In turn, the realizations of these cost parameters depend on, among other factors, the IT infrastructure (e.g., physical IT assets and methodologies) implemented by the firm to support the production process. For example, Marsden and Pingry [15] point out that when a firm purchases a decision support system, it uses the system to lower the marginal cost of searching for problem structure (or lower the marginal cost of designing a solution), which, in turn, allows the firm the freedom to search for structures that improve the quality of the solution to the problem. Examples of IT investments that may lower the fixed-production-cost parameter, $f ,$ include computeraided design tools, simulation and visualization tools, collaborative technologies, and prototyping tools. Examples of IT investments that may lower the marginal-production-cost parameters, v and h, include customer relationship management applications, technical support applications, and electronic distribution systems. In the fourth section, we consider changes to each cost parameter (realized through specific IT investments) separately to develop some initial insights as to their individual effects on the firm’s quality and price choices and on firm profits, firm productivity, and consumer welfare.

The revenue function and total profit function, respectively, for Firm i are given by

$$
R _ {i} = p _ {i} Q _ {i}, i = 1, 2\tag{3}
$$

and

$$
\pi_ {i} = R _ {i} - C _ {i}, \quad i = 1, 2.\tag{4}
$$

Firm productivity is defined as the ratio of output value to its related input value. In this model, the output value generated by the firm’s production process is the revenue earned by the firm, and the input value is the cost associated with the production process. Therefore, the productivity function of Firm i is given by<sup>2</sup>

$$
\rho_ {i} = \frac {R _ {i}}{C _ {i}}, i, j = 1, 2.\tag{5}
$$

The total consumer surplus function is given by (see Appendix A for a graphical representation of consumer surplus)

$$
\omega = \frac {Q _ {1} ^ {2} + Q _ {2} ^ {2}}{2 \lambda}.\tag{6}
$$

Finally, consistent with the literature [18, 22, 23, 24], in this model IT investments (represented as reductions in cost parameters) undertaken by the firm are assumed to be costless. This assumption provides the best-case scenario for observing improvements in firm profitability and firm productivity from IT investments.

## Duopoly Model Solution

The competition between firms is analyzed by solving for the subgame perfect equilibrium of the two-stage game. After determining equilibrium qualities and prices, we use comparative static analysis in the fourth section to examine the impact of reductions in each cost parameter on the equilibrium decisions and economic performance. Given Equations (1) through (4), the equilibrium choices of quality and price are (see Appendix B for all duopoly derivations)

$$
s _ {i} ^ {*} = \frac {V X}{Y}, i, j = 1, 2\tag{7}
$$

and

$$
p _ {i} ^ {*} = \frac {f (a + v \lambda) (2 - b) (2 + b) + X (h a - v \theta (1 - c))}{Y}, i, j = 1, 2,\tag{8}
$$

where

$$
V = a - v \lambda (1 - b),\tag{9}
$$

$$
X = \theta (2 - b c) - h \lambda (2 - b ^ {2}),\tag{10}
$$

and

$$
Y = f \lambda (2 + b) (2 - b) ^ {2} - X (\theta (1 - c) - h \lambda (1 - b)).\tag{11}
$$

(Note: The definitions of $V , X ,$ and Y above are used for convenience, since these terms are repeated frequently in other equations.)

To ensure that a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists, it must be the case that $Y > 0 .$ . To guarantee the concavity of the profit function in quality, it must be the case that $Z > 0$ , where

$$
Z = f \lambda (2 + b) ^ {2} (2 - b) ^ {2} - X ^ {2} > 0.\tag{12}
$$

Together these two conditions imply the following:

$$
f > f _ {\min} = \max \left[ \frac {X (\theta (1 - c) - h \lambda (1 - b))}{\lambda (2 - b) ^ {2} (2 + b)}, \frac {X ^ {2}}{\lambda (2 - b) ^ {2} (2 + b) ^ {2}} \right].\tag{13}
$$

In addition, to ensure that firms choose positive quality levels in equilibrium, we assume $V , X > 0$ , which implies the following logical constraints:

$$
v <   \frac {a}{\lambda (1 - b)}\tag{14}
$$

and

$$
h <   \frac {\theta (2 - b c)}{\lambda (2 - b ^ {2})}.\tag{15}
$$

Equations (13) through (15) characterize the relationships between the cost parameters and demand parameters that must be satisfied. As in all theoretical work, the question of whether the conditions are consistent with the real world is an empirical question. However, we note that these conditions are similar to those imposed in previous models of quality–price competition in a duopoly setting [2, 18, 22].

Substituting Equations (7) and (8) into Equations (1) and (4)–(6) yields the equilibrium values presented in the second column of Table D2 (Appendix D). In addition, the derivative of each equilibrium value with respect to each cost parameter (f, v, and h) is presented in the third, fourth, and fifth columns, respectively, of Table D2. In the Analysis of IT Value section, we will evaluate these derivatives to determine the directional impact of IT investments (represented as reductions in the cost parameters) on economic measures.

We have solved the two-stage duopoly model of quality–price competition, assuming that the firms’ cost structures are characterized by Equation (2). However, some products (e.g., information goods, software applications) are characterized by zero marginal production cost, implying a cost function where the marginal-cost parameters in Equation (2) are $\nu = 0$ and $h = 0$ . Other products are characterized by positive marginal production costs that do not depend on product quality, implying a cost function where $\nu > 0$ and $h = 0$ . Finally, recent theoretical work in the IT value literature [3, 18, 23, 24] related to this paper has assumed that products are characterized by positive marginal production costs that depend completely on product quality, implying a cost function where $\nu = 0$ and $h > 0$ . We examine the impact of IT on economic value under these alternative assumptions about the form of the marginal production cost. Indeed, as we will see in the fourth section, the specific form of the cost function critically affects the relationship between IT and economic value.

## Analyzing Alternative Cost Structures

Since we have solved the duopoly model for the most complex cost function $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \nu >$ 0 and $h > 0 )$ , deriving the equilibrium values and partial derivatives for the three alternative cost structures is straightforward. Each of the cost structures discussed above is a special case of Equation (2). To determine the equilibrium values and partial derivatives for a given cost structure, we simply take the equations presented in Table D2 (Appendix D) and set the appropriate cost parameter(s) equal to zero. As an example, consider the equilibrium choice of quality, assuming the most complex form of marginal cost, presented in Equation (7). To determine the equilibrium choice of quality assuming the least complex form of marginal cost, we substitute $\nu = 0$ and $h = 0$ into Equation (7), which gives

s _ {i} ^ {v, h = 0} = \frac {a \theta (2 - b c)}{f \lambda (2 + b) (2 - b) ^ {2} - \theta^ {2} (2 - b c) (1 - c)}, i, j = 1, 2.\tag{16}
$$

## The Monopoly Model

IN ORDER TO CONDUCT A COMPREHENSIVE, two-by-four comparison [(monopoly, duopoly) × (four cost functions)] of the impact of IT investments on economic performance, we solve the monopoly model for the same four cost functions as in the duopoly case.

## Monopoly Model Description

We consider a single-product monopolist that chooses a quality–price combination that maximizes profits. The demand for the monopolist’s product is given by

$$
Q _ {m} = a - \lambda p _ {m} + \theta s _ {m},\tag{17}
$$

where m denotes the monopoly case. The cost, revenue, profit, and productivity functions of the monopolist are given by Equations (2)–(5), respectively. The total consumer surplus function in the monopoly case is given by

$$
\omega_ {m} = \frac {Q _ {m} ^ {2}}{2 \lambda}.\tag{18}
$$

## Monopoly Model Solution

Given Equations (2)–(4) and (17), the monopolist’s equilibrium choices of quality and price are (see Appendix C for all monopoly derivations)

$$
s _ {m} ^ {*} = \frac {(\theta - h \lambda) (a - v \lambda)}{4 f \lambda - (\theta - h \lambda) ^ {2}}\tag{19}
$$

and

$$
p _ {m} ^ {*} = \frac {2 f (a + v \lambda) + (\theta - h \lambda) (h a - v \theta)}{4 f \lambda - (\theta - h \lambda) ^ {2}}.\tag{20}
$$

To guarantee the concavity of the profit function in quality and in price and to guarantee a unique maximum, the determinant of the $2 \times 2$ Hessian matrix must be positive. Therefore, the following condition must hold

$$
4 f \lambda - (\theta - h \lambda) ^ {2} > 0 \Leftrightarrow f > \frac {(\theta - h \lambda) ^ {2}}{4 \lambda}.\tag{21}
$$

In addition, to ensure that the monopolist chooses positive quality level in equilibrium, we assume the following logical constraints:

$$
a - v \lambda > 0 \Leftrightarrow v <   \frac {a}{\lambda}\tag{22}
$$

and

$$
\theta - h \lambda > 0 \Leftrightarrow h <   \frac {\theta}{\lambda}.\tag{23}
$$

Equations (21)–(23) characterize the relationships between the cost parameters and demand parameters that must be satisfied in the monopoly case. We note that these conditions are similar to those imposed in the duopoly solution (i.e., Equations (13)– (15)) presented in the “Duopoly Model Solution” section.

Substituting Equations (19) and (20) into Equations (17), (4), (5), and (18) yields the equilibrium values presented in second column in Table D3 (Appendix D). In addition, the derivative of each equilibrium value with respect to each cost parameter (f, v, and h) is presented in the third, fourth, and fifth columns, respectively, in Table D3.

Similar to the duopoly case, since we have solved the monopoly model for the most complex cost function (i.e., v > 0 and $h > 0 )$ , deriving the equilibrium values and partial derivatives for the three alternative cost structures is straightforward. To determine the values for a given cost structure, we simply take the equations presented in Table D3 and set the appropriate cost parameter(s) equal to zero.

## Analysis of IT Value

## Propositions

TABLES D2 AND D3 (APPENDIX D) PRESENT THE DERIVATIVES of the equilibrium economic measures with respect to each cost parameter for the duopoly and monopoly cases, respectively. We use the conditions derived from each model (i.e., Equations (13)–(15) for the duopoly case and Equations (21)–(23) for the monopoly case) to sign these derivatives and, therefore, determine the directional impact of IT investments, which lower each cost parameter, on economic performance. A reduction in a cost parameter (↓ in f, v, or h) may have either a positive impact (↑) on economic performance, a negative impact (↓), or an ambiguous impact (7). We define an impact as ambiguous if the directional impact of IT (or the sign of the derivative) depends on the realizations of the cost and demand parameters in the model. Table D4 (Appendix D) summarizes the results of this algebraic exercise and shows the directional impact (↑, ↓, or 7) of a reduction in each cost parameter (↓ in f, v, and h) on six measures for all eight combinations of the two market structures and four cost functions. A total of 96 directional effects are presented in Table D4. The results for one of the eight combinations (i.e., the monopoly case in which $\nu = 0$ and h > 0) are analyzed in Thatcher and Pingry [24], and the results for another of the combinations (i.e., the duopoly case in which $\nu = 0$ and $h > 0 )$ are partially analyzed in Quan et al. [18]. A review of the results presented in Table D4 leads to the following propositions, which are summarized in Table D5.

Proposition 1: For all combinations of market structures and cost functions, any IT investment that lowers f, v, and h unambiguously increases product quality.

In the context of the models presented here, it is always optimal (or profit-maximizing) for a firm to utilize any IT investment to improve product quality. Therefore, product quality plays a critical role in determining the impact of IT on economic value.

Proposition 2: For both monopoly and duopoly, IT investments that lower v unambiguously increase firm profits, firm productivity, and consumer welfare.

The underlying belief that IT investments should improve economic performance (as embodied in measures such as profits, productivity, and consumer welfare) has driven much of the empirical effort to resolve the IT productivity paradox and the IT profitability paradox [10]. Proposition 2 supports this underlying belief for IT investments that specifically lower v. However, as we will see below, Propositions 3 and 4 do not support this belief for IT investments that specifically lower either f or h.

Proposition 3: For monopoly, IT investments that lower f or h unambiguously increase firm profits and consumer welfare, but unambiguously decrease firm productivity for all cost functions.

Proposition 4: For duopoly, IT investments that lower f or h have an ambiguous impact on firm profits for all cost functions.

Together, Propositions 2 and 3 show that for the monopoly case, the directional effects of IT investments on profits, productivity, and consumer welfare are unambiguous for all cost functions. In addition, these propositions show that for the monopoly case, IT investments that lower v will increase productivity while investments that lower f or h will decrease productivity. Therefore, the impact of an IT investment on productivity depends critically on which cost parameter the investment affects. Together, Propositions 2, 3, and 4 show that the ambiguities of the effects of IT investment on profits are limited to the duopoly case and are associated only with IT investments that improve f or h.

In addition, Proposition 4 shows that there exist theoretical conditions in the duopoly case that provide support for the phenomenon that Clemons [9] refers to as strategic necessity. The idea behind strategic necessity is that “instead of becoming a source of lasting competitive edge, most strategic information systems become new and essential aspects of doing business . . . i.e., profits will be competed away. Since the key resources of management information systems (MIS) applications are commodities available to all competitors, all competitors with similar MIS strategies can develop similar systems and benefits such as reduced costs or improved service” [9, p. 79].

Proposition 5: For duopoly,

i. IT investments that lower h have an ambiguous impact on consumer welfare.

ii. If h = 0, then IT investments that lower f unambiguously increase consumer welfare. Otherwise (i.e., if h > 0), IT investments that lower f have an ambiguous impact on consumer welfare.

Together, Propositions 2 and 3 show that for the monopoly case, IT investments that lower any of the cost parameters unambiguously increase consumer welfare. However, Proposition 5 shows that in duopoly the directional impact of an IT investment that lowers h on consumer welfare is ambiguous, or depends on the realizations of the model parameter values. Furthermore, the directional impact of an IT investment that lowers f on consumer welfare is ambiguous for the two cost structures that include h > 0, but is unambiguously positive for the other two cost structures. In other words, Proposition 5 shows that there exists a theoretical set of conditions for which IT investments that lower f or h actually hurt consumers.

Proposition 6: For duopoly, $i f \nu = 0 ,$ then IT investments that lower f or h unambiguously decrease firm productivity. Otherwise (i.e., $i f \nu > O )$ , IT investments that lower f or h have an ambiguous impact on productivity.

Interestingly, although the impact of v on profits, productivity, and consumer welfare is unambiguously positive in all cases (see Proposition 2), the presence of $\nu > 0$ in the cost function leads to ambiguity in the effects of f or h on productivity. Whereas Thatcher and Pingry [24] found that IT investments that lower f or h (assuming $\nu = 0 )$ in monopoly decrease the productivity measure (consistent with Proposition 3), Quan et al. [18] found that the impact assuming the same cost function becomes ambiguous in duopoly (consistent with Proposition 6). However, Proposition 6 provides additional insight. Namely, the directional impact of f or h does not depend only on market structure but rather on the combination of market structure and cost structure.

## Observations

Propositions 1 through 6 capture the significant effects of IT investments that reduce f, v, or h on product quality, firm profits, firm productivity, and consumer welfare in the duopoly and monopoly cases. Independently, the propositions offer important insights. However, collectively, they uncover broader patterns of the directional effects of IT investments. These patterns are presented in the following observations.

Observation 1: For each cost function, we observe that moving from monopoly to duopoly increases the number of directional effects of IT investments that are ambiguous.

From Propositions 2, 3, and 4, we know that the effects of v on the economic measures are always unambiguous, the effects of f, v, or h are all unambiguous in the monopoly case, and the effects of f or h on profits in the duopoly case are all ambiguous. Thus, as the market structure moves from monopoly to duopoly, the directional effects of v on profits, productivity, and consumer welfare remain unambiguous. However, as the market structure moves from monopoly to duopoly, all four of the effects of f and both of the effects of h on profit become ambiguous. In addition, 7 of the 12 effects (for all cost functions) of f or h on firm productivity and consumer welfare move from unambiguous in monopoly to ambiguous in duopoly. To take a more detailed look, consider the cost function in which $\nu = 0$ and $h = 0$ . As the market structure moves from monopoly to duopoly, 1 of 3 effects (i.e., on profits, productivity, and consumer welfare) becomes ambiguous. For $\nu > 0$ and $h = 0 , 2$ of 6 effects become ambiguous. For $\nu = 0$ and $h > 0 ,$ , 4 of 6 effects become ambiguous. Finally, for the most complex cost function ( $\mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \nu > 0$ and $h > 0 )$ , 6 of 9 effects become ambiguous. In summary, when considering IT investments that lower f or h, 13 of 18 effects move from unambiguous to ambiguous as the market structure moves from monopoly to duopoly. This analysis leads to Observation 1.

Observation 2: For duopoly, we observe that an increase in the complexity of the firm’s cost structure increases the number and percentage of directional effects of IT investments that are ambiguous.

As shown in the previous paragraph, in the duopoly case the number of effects on profit, productivity, and consumer surplus to examine by cost structure is 3 (v = 0 and h = 0), 6 (v > 0 and h = 0), 6 (v = 0 and h > 0), and 9 (v > 0 and h > 0), for a total of 24 effects. Of these effects, the number that are ambiguous (in duopoly) by cost structure is 1, 2, 4, and 6, respectively. In absolute terms, the number of ambiguities is higher for the cases with more complex cost functions. Of course, it is difficult to interpret absolute numbers, since the number of effects differs across cost structures. However, we note that the percentage of effects that are ambiguous (33 percent, 33 percent, 67 percent, and 67 percent, respectively) is at least as high for the cases with more complex cost functions. In addition, when netting out the v effects, which are always unambiguous, the percentage of effects that are ambiguous becomes 33 percent, 67 percent, 67 percent, and 100 percent, respectively. This analysis leads to Observation 2.

Summarizing Observations 1 and 2, in the context of these models the number of directional effects of IT investments on economic measures that are ambiguous increase as the market structure moves from monopoly to duopoly and as the cost structure becomes more complex.

## Contributions and Limitations

THIS PAPER GRAPPLES WITH THE THEORETICAL RELATIONSHIP between IT investment and economic performance. As with any theoretical exercise, many assumptions were made. Included were assumptions about how IT investment affects firm costs, the functional forms of the cost functions, the functional forms of the demand functions, the two-stage approach (first quality, then price), the profit-maximizing behavior of the firms, the consideration of two market structures (monopoly and duopoly), and the symmetry of the duopoly firms. As always, the particular natures of the assumptions combine to drive the results presented. However, our analysis makes clear that, even when making these limiting, albeit common assumptions in the literature, there seem to be only two universal theorems:

1. Investments in IT will improve product quality (Proposition 1) and

2. Investments in IT that lower v will increase firm profits, firm productivity, and consumer welfare (Proposition 2).

Alternatively, when considering IT investments that lower f or h, Observations 1 and 2 emphasize that in the context of the eight models examined in this paper, it cannot be concluded that profit, productivity, or consumer welfare will generally increase (or even move in the same direction). In fact, these observations show that the number of directional effects of IT investments on economic measures that are ambiguous increases as the market structure moves from monopoly to duopoly and as the cost structure becomes more complex. However, Propositions 3 through 6 explain the complex relationships among IT investments, cost structure, market structure, and economic performance measures for each of these models.

We note that the large number of theoretical ambiguities may not be empirical realities. In fact, it is not reasonable to assume that all the theoretical subcases (or combinations of market structure, cost structure, and model parameters) examined in this paper are equally likely, or even exist at all, in the real world. In addition, although the theoretical relationships in the models presented are complex, they are simplified by the assumption that IT investments affect only one cost parameter at a time. Again, this assumption seems unlikely in the real world. Any single IT investment may impact the cost function in multiple ways, further increasing the difficulty of teasing out a unified IT value theorem.

Future research may focus on relaxing many of the limiting assumptions in this model. For example, the model can be extended to examine the strategic interactions, and the resulting economic impact of IT investments, when the cost structures are asymmetric (i.e., one firm has a more efficient cost structure than its competitor) and when the demand functions are asymmetric. In addition, the model can be extended to consider the economic impact of IT investments when firms make sequential (as opposed to simultaneous) choices in product quality in the first stage of the model. Together, the baseline cases presented in this paper and the model extensions proposed above (and others) will provide a more robust understanding of IT value in different contexts and competitive settings.

Future research may also focus on empirically testing the propositions derived from the theoretical model presented in this paper. Specifically, IT application-level studies may help explain whether the relationships among IT investments, product design, and economic performance described in this paper hold in real-world settings. In conclusion, while the search for a comprehensive IT value theorem is far from over, we hope that this paper provides a contribution.

## NOTES

1. Unlike the one-stage model, the two-stage model “reflects the assumption that price decisions are made after the quality decisions, since the choice of a quality level reflects a longterm decision which cannot be changed as easily or frequently as prices” [2, p. 1182]. The twostage approach is intuitively appealing and consistent with much of the economics literature that commonly examines duopoly competition in product quality and price as a noncooperative, two-stage game (first quality, then price) [2, 16, 20, 21]. For comparison, we have also solved the duopoly model using the one-stage approach. While the one-stage approach generates different equilibrium values for this model, the signs of the derivatives of economic performance measures with respect to cost parameters (which determine the directional impact of IT investments on economic performance) are consistent across the approaches.

2. At this point, we note that Thatcher and Oliver [23] and Quan et al. [18] incorporate the indirect fixed costs of doing business in the denominators of their productivity measures. However, including the indirect fixed costs in the input value implies that two firms, making the same IT investments, and making the same optimal adjustments to price and quality (since optimal choices are not affected by the indirect fixed costs), will realize different levels of productivity only because one firm has more equipment depreciation than the other or because one firm has more administrative costs than the other. Therefore, using direct production costs (i.e., Equation (2)) as the input value for the productivity measures has more intuitive appeal. This measure of productivity is consistent with that used by Thatcher [22] and Thatcher and Pingry [24].

## Appendix B. Derivation of Equilibrium for the Duopoly Model

AT THE SECOND STAGE, GIVEN Equations (1)–(4), the first-order conditions characterizing equilibrium are

$$
\frac {\partial \pi_ {i}}{\partial p _ {i}} = a - 2 \lambda p _ {i} + \lambda b p _ {j} + \theta s _ {i} - \theta c s _ {j} + v \lambda + h \lambda s _ {i} = 0, i, j = 1, 2, i \neq j.
$$

Solving the first-order conditions for the price reaction functions gives

$$
p _ {i} \left(p _ {j}\right) = \frac {a + v \lambda + \lambda b p _ {j} + s _ {i} (\theta + h \lambda) - s _ {i} \theta c}{2 \lambda}, i, j = 1, 2, i \neq j.
$$

Since

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} \pi_ {i}}{\partial p _ {i} ^ {2}} = - 2 \lambda <   0, i, j = 1, 2,
$$

the profit function given quality levels is strictly concave in prices. The point at which the price reaction curves intersect determines the equilibrium price levels in Stage 2. Therefore, solving the price-reaction functions for $p _ { 1 }$ and $p _ { 2 }$ gives the equilibrium price for each firm in Stage $^ { 2 , }$ given the product quality decisions made in Stage 1:

$$
\begin{array}{c} p _ {i} ^ {*} \left(s _ {i}, s _ {j}\right) = \frac {(a + v \lambda) (2 + b) + s _ {j} (\theta (b - 2 c) + h \lambda b) + s _ {i} (\theta (2 - b c) + 2 h \lambda)}{\lambda \left(4 - b ^ {2}\right)}, \\ i, j = 1, 2, \quad i \neq j. \end{array}
$$

At the first stage, both firms’ quality-reaction functions (which gives the best action for Firm i given that Firm j chooses $s _ { j } )$ are derived by solving the first-order conditions:

$$
\frac {\partial \pi_ {i}}{\partial s _ {i}} = 2 \frac {(2 + b) V X - s _ {i} Z + s _ {j} X (\theta (b - 2 c) + h \lambda b)}{\lambda (4 - b ^ {2}) ^ {2}} = 0, i, j = 1, 2, i \neq j,
$$

where $V , X ,$ and $Z$ are defined in Equations (9), (10), and (12), respectively. Solving the first-order conditions for the quality-reaction functions gives

$$
s _ {i} \left(s _ {j}\right) = X \frac {V (2 + b) + s _ {j} (\theta (b - 2 c) + h \lambda b)}{Z}, i, j = 1, 2, i \neq j.
$$

The profit function is strictly concave in the quality level $s _ { i }$ if

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} \pi_ {i}}{\partial s _ {i} ^ {2}} = - \frac {2 Z}{\lambda (4 - b ^ {2}) ^ {2}} <   0, i, j = 1, 2.
$$

Therefore, to ensure that the equilibrium solution is a global maximum, the following condition must hold: $Z > 0$ (see Equation (12)).

The point at which the quality-reaction curves intersect determines the quality equilibrium in Stage 1. To ensure that a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists (i.e., the firms’ reaction functions intersect once) it is sufficient that [2, 25]

$$
\left| \frac {\partial^ {2} \pi_ {i}}{\partial s _ {i} ^ {2}} \right| > \left| \frac {\partial^ {2} \pi_ {i}}{\partial s _ {i} \partial s _ {j}} \right|, i, j = 1, 2, i \neq j.
$$

This inequality holds if $Y / ( \lambda ( 4 - b ^ { 2 } ) ) > 0$ , where Y is defined in Equation (11). Therefore, to ensure that a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exists, the following condition must hold: $Y > 0$ . Solving the quality-reaction functions for the equilibrium quality levels in Stage 1 gives Equation (7). Substituting these values into the equilibrium price equations above gives Equation (8). Finally, substituting Equations $( 7 )$ and (8) into Equations (1) and (4)–(6) yields the equilibrium values presented in the first column of Table D2 (Appendix D).
