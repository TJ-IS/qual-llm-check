# An Empirical Investigation of Collaborative Conflict Management Style in Group Support System–Based Global Virtual Teams

SOUREN PAUL, IMAD M. SAMARAH, PRIYA SEETHARAMAN, AND PETER P. MYKYTYN JR.

ABSTRACT: Virtual teams cut across national, organizational, and functional boundaries, often resulting in diversity in team composition. This paper presents the results of a laboratory study involving groupware-supported, culturally homogeneous, and heterogeneous virtual teams where collaborative conflict management style, a team’s cultural orientation as measured by the degree of individualism-collectivism, and group diversity affect several group performance variables. Collaborative conflict management style was positively related to performance, group diversity was found to have a moderating influence between collaborative style and group performance, and collaborative style was influenced by the individualistic–collectivistic orientations. Consistent with prior research, we found that collectivistic orientations help enhance the level of collaborative conflict management style prevailing in teams. Our research also indicates that the process to motivate team members may differ depending on their orientation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: collaborative conflict management, collectivism, group support systems, individualism, perceived decision quality, perceived participation in decision-making, satisfaction with decision process, team diversity, virtual team.

THE IMPORTANCE OF THE USE OF TEAMS in organizational decision-making has been well established. Teams are often formed to tackle a range of issues such as policy formulation, new product development, and strategic direction, to name a few. Traditionally, teams have operated in personal, face-to-face settings, but with the proliferation of advanced communication and information technologies (IT), virtual teams have become commonplace. Today, the term virtual team describes groups of geographically dispersed members who are set up and connected with the help of telecommunication and IT [83], which is consistent with the definition used in this research. Virtual teams continue to gain popularity, as organizations are beginning to discern the capability of using virtual teams and are realizing the potential benefits of using computer and communications technology to support organizational teams. The increasing prevalence of the Internet and the World Wide Web is making it possible for geographically dispersed individuals to get engaged in discussions in online and realtime modes; also, these technologies enable business organizations to take advantage of virtual teams at the global level. The systems that facilitate virtual teams’ operations are in high demand, and researchers and software developers are exploring avenues to improve the performance of these systems.

Virtual teams often consist of members with varying backgrounds, skills, and departmental affiliations, and as a result of globalization, it is not uncommon for virtual teams to be comprised of members with different cultural and technological backgrounds. Moreover, diversity among team members adds complexity to the functioning of virtual teams. Prior studies have examined heterogeneous groups to assess various issues, such as communication and collaboration in virtual teams [47], antecedents of trust in virtual teams [35], the utility of maintaining shared mutual knowledge in virtual teams [10], and the effect of shared mental models on coordination within teams [17]. Nonetheless, only a limited number of studies have examined the effect of some form of diversity on the performance of global virtual teams [71]. Diversity seems to be an understudied issue in virtual team research, thus providing a motivation for this study.

One aspect of diversity in groups is the variation in the cultural values held by group members. Mejias et al. [50] examined group support system (GSS) and non-GSS–supported teams within U.S. and Mexican cultures and found that Mexican participants perceived higher levels of consensus, satisfaction, and participation equity than did the U.S. participants. Similar studies have also been conducted by other researchers examining various outcome variables. Watson et al. [92] examined the findings of an experimental investigation comparing GSS use in two countries and argue that culture can be befittingly called the fourth dimension of GSS research. Whereas Tan et al. [81] examined reduction in status effects in groups using GSS in two cultures, Quaddus and Tung [65] explained the effect of cultural orientation on the relationship between conflict resolution style and decision conferencing use in specific task environments. These differences in team members’ performance and perception can be explained in terms of the differences that exist in various dimensions of national culture discussed in the literature [29, 30]. The dimension of national culture that is considered to have the greatest distinguishing characteristic is individualism–collectivism [32, 84]. In particular, we expect that the individualisticcollectivistic orientation of the team members will have a major influence on their collaborative styles in virtual team interactions.

In addition, in small work groups, conflict among members is likely to emerge for various reasons, including the cultural makeup of the group. Group members’ willingness and ability to collaborate with each other is likely to have a bearing on the overall performance of the group. However, only recently has information systems (IS) research begun to address the role that conflict management styles play in the decision-making of virtual teams (e.g., [53, 70]). Consequently, as prior studies point to the critical role played by the collaborative conflict management style in determining virtual team outcomes, the relationship between individualism–collectivism and conflict handling via collaboration becomes an issue that deserves investigation.

This paper also addresses the question of whether the collaborative conflict management style of the virtual teams has any effect on the performance of the team. In addition, we investigate whether the diversity in global virtual teams affects the extent to which the collaborative conflict management style that prevails in the team would influence the team’s performance. Performance measures investigated include the level of agreement among members and their perceptions of participation, decision quality, and satisfaction with the decision process.

## Literature Review and Theory Development

THE ABSENCE OF A COMPREHENSIVE THEORY combining individualism, collaborative conflict management style, team diversity, and performance of GSS-based virtual teams necessitates integrating related literature. The review below discusses the theoretical background of our research, covering literature on virtual teams, cultural diversity, conflict management styles, and team performance measures. We conclude this section with the presentation of our hypotheses. Our research model showing the relationships among the different variables is presented in Figure 1.

## Virtual Teams

Virtual teams are defined as “groups of geographically and/or organizationally dispersed co-workers that are assembled using a combination of telecommunications and IT to accomplish an organizational task” [83, p. 18]. Jarvenpaa and Leidner [34] characterize a virtual team as a temporary, culturally diverse, geographically dispersed, and electronically communicating work group. Virtual teams are more complex than traditional face-to-face teams, primarily because they cut across time, distance, and organization; and they collaborate using information and communication technology [13]. Research on virtual teams has addressed various important issues, such as trust among the team members [34, 35]; communication and collaboration in virtual teams [34, 47]; conflict and collaborative style in resolving it [53]; maintaining mutual knowledge [10]; strategies to overcome the time–space divide [72]; facilitating computer-mediated communication of interdisciplinary teams [89]; boundary-crossing issues influencing relationship building in teams [63]; effective leadership in virtual teams [39]; challenges faced by virtual team leaders to manage relationship building through electronically mediated interactions across spatial, temporal, organizational, and cultural boundaries [62]; and technology adaptation in interorganizational virtual teams [46].

Kayworth and Leidner [39] described four types of challenges of virtual teams— communications, culture, logistics, and technology. They argued that communication challenges include demands due to loss of social cues and need for trust; cultural challenges are caused due to heterogeneity of teams and biases due to cultural misunderstandings. They also put forth that while logistical challenges occur due to dispersion across multiple geographical zones, technological challenges arise due to technophobia and the need for proficiency across a wide range of technologies. The challenge of culture in virtual teams needs additional examination, as diversity in team composition plays an important role in determining team performance [72]. Authors have also contended that the issue of computer-mediated communication among team members poses major interpersonal challenges to teams [54]. This is due to the lean medium that communication technologies present in comparison to a faceto-face medium [41]. The absence of personal contact, gestures, and so on, has been said to affect the interaction among group members [39, 53].

Conflict in face-to-face teams has been categorized as task conflict and affective conflict [37]. Whereas task conflict is issue oriented, arising from differences in judgment, affective conflict refers to personalized disagreement or individual disaffection. Affective conflict is detrimental to group performance, while moderate amounts of task conflict can be beneficial [37]. Mortensen and Hinds [54] argue that levels of affective and task conflict will be higher in computer-mediated groups, as information exchange is less complete in a technology-mediated communication than in faceto-face interaction. In line with Miranda and Bostrom [52], who found that process structuring and anonymity in GSS-based group decision-making give rise to reduced affective conflict, we believe that affective conflict will be less in a virtual team that uses GSS. A GSS tool enables anonymous interaction among the team members and would therefore aid group members in concentrating on the issues being addressed rather than focusing on who said what [31]. Although the possibility of task-related conflict cannot be avoided, especially when members of GSS-based groups come from diverse cultural backgrounds, it is unlikely that the members will develop major personalized disagreement or individual disaffection in anonymous group interactions. The cultural diversity forces differences in the thought process of the members of a virtual team. In today’s global marketplace, the ability of organizations to take advantage of virtual teams requires the understanding of cultural diversity, especially differences in national cultures of the team members.

## Culture and Virtual Teams

In this paper, we use the term culture to mean “the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes one group or category (nation) from another” [29, p. 89]. Understanding the differences in cultural characteristics across countries can help us understand the different ways of dealing with emerging problems in group settings [30]. The IS literature supports the view that different cultures vary with respect to issues dealing with IS [27, 50, 88, 92, 93, 96]. de Vreede et al. [88] conducted a field study of GSS applications in Africa to investigate the effective use and acceptance of

GSS technology in a cross-cultural context, and their findings support the view that cultural characteristics can lead to significant differences in areas such as technology acceptance, use, and diffusion, in addition to user satisfaction. Mejias et al. [50] investigated the effects of national culture (the United States and Mexico) in a field study that shows significant differences in the relative levels of satisfaction and consensus. In a more recent cross-cultural study of the use of knowledge management systems, Yoo et al. [96] observed that the interaction between culture and technology leads to unique sociotechnological systems that result in different attitudes and behavioral patterns toward knowledge management systems and organizational knowledge in different cultures. These studies highlight the growing importance that culture is playing in today’s technologically diverse global arena.

Hofstede [30] identified five bipolar dimensions of national culture. A country’s position on these dimensions allows us to predict how its society operates. The five cultural dimensions identified are:

• power distance—degree of inequality among people that is considered as normal;

• uncertainty avoidance—degree to which people prefer structured over unstructured situations;

• individualism—degree to which people prefer to act as individuals rather than as members of groups;

• masculinity—degree to which tough values prevail over tender values;

• long-term orientation—degree to which people’s efforts are focused toward the future rather than the present.

Although Hofstede’s [30] five dimensions have served as the basis for research examining cultural differences, we focus on one dimension, individualism–collectivism, for the following reasons. Based on research by Cox et al. [9], Sosik and Jung [78], and Wagner [90], it is evident that the individualism–collectivism dimension plays a major role in cooperative behavior of individuals in a group. Prior research indicates that individualism–collectivism is the most important distinguishing dimension of cultures [32, 84]. Research has also shown that individualistic and collectivistic orientations affect conflict management styles [21]. Quaddus and Tung [65], for instance, used homogenous decision conferencing groups from Australia and Singapore to compare the choice of conflict management styles in two decision tasks. Moreover, in GSS-based virtual teams, members can work anonymously, which mitigates the relevance of masculinity and power inequality that may otherwise be apparent in a face-to-face situation. Therefore, in virtual teams, the individualism–collectivism dimension is an important dimension of culture, as it reflects the extent to which members are inclined toward teamwork and open to accommodating others’ views. Although uncertainty avoidance and long-term orientation of team members are also important, depending on the nature of task that the GSS-based virtual teams perform, it is the individualistic–collectivistic orientation of the members that plays an important role in conflict resolution in groups. Finally, Trubisky et al. [85] argue that differences in communication and conflict resolution styles in groups can be accounted for using the individualism–collectivism dimension of its members.

As the emphasis of this research is on collaboration in GSS-based virtual teams, we focus on the individualism–collectivism dimension. Individualism relates to societies in which ties between individuals are loose and an individual is expected to look after himself or herself and his or her immediate family, whereas collectivist societies are characterized by strong group/family ties, cohesive groups, and unwavering loyalty among group members [78].

## Individualism–Collectivism

Wagner and Moch [91] have analyzed and conceptualized individualism–collectivism based on prior research by Breer and Locke [5] and Hofstede [29]. They define individualism as the condition in which personal interests are accorded greater importance than are the needs of the groups. When conflict between personal interests and group interests occurs, individualists tend to ignore group interests [90]. On the other hand, collectivists tend to give more precedence to the demands and interests of the group. People from individualistic cultures tend to make decisions based on individual needs, whereas those in collectivist cultures tend to base their decisions on what is best for the group as a whole. Individualism–collectivism as a construct consists of different dimensions, such as personal independence and self-reliance, importance accorded to competitive success, value attached to working alone, subordination of personal needs to group interests, and personal pursuits in group productivity [90].

With the onset of multinational organizations and globalization, the immigration of people from one nation to another has greatly increased. Ancona [1] argues that groups operate in cultural contexts to which they adapt and are influenced. Therefore, Hofstede’s strict classification of nations as individualistic or collectivist may not be particularly useful, especially when using cross-national groups consisting of persons from different countries, ethnicities, and social backgrounds. Wagner and Moch [91] view individualism–collectivism as a “continuum composed of intermediate points as well as extremes” [91, p. 286], and argue that very few societies are purely individualistic or collectivistic. It is important to note that this dimension exists in both Western and Eastern philosophy and is a key construct in analyzing cultures [85].

Wagner [90] demonstrated that high collectivism contributes to high cooperation in group work. Cooperation is crucial for achieving success in group work, especially in the context of virtual teams. The diverse backgrounds of the members and the use of lean media, such as groupware to support group communication, may give rise to conflicts in virtual teams. We expect that cooperating team members will collaborate while resolving conflict that may arise in virtual teams.

## Conflict Management Styles

A conflict resolution mechanism is a preferred behavior that people use in order to bring a conflict situation to a settlement. Research has found that individuals vary in their styles of conflict resolution. Prior research has identified typical conflict resolution styles (see [44] for a comparison) based on “concern for self” and “concern for others.” Five patterns of conflict management behavior that typically emerge in group settings have been documented [66, 67, 82]:

• Avoidance style (low concern for others and low concern for self) is associated with intentionally withdrawing from the conflict situation.

• Accommodating style (high concern for others and low concern for self) refers to focusing on areas of agreement and thus smoothing over differences. This style refers to the tendency of being more concerned with others’ needs and views than one’s own.

• Competition style (low concern for others and high concern for self) is described as forcing one’s own views on others. Individuals exhibiting this style of conflict resolution would have no or low concern for others’ interests or needs and would wrestle with the others so that one’s views and concerns would be the dominant ones.

• Collaborative style (high concern for others and high concern for self) pertains to integrating the views of all involved.

• Compromise (moderate concern for all) is associated with finding a middle ground solution. This occurs when members focus on finding a common solution that addresses everyone’s interest.

Our model concentrates only on the collaborative conflict management style. Collaborative conflict management refers to a mechanism by which both parties can satisfy their desires and neither side has to sacrifice considerably. Follett [22] suggests that other styles of conflict management (such as compromise) deal with what already exists, whereas integration (such as collaboration) creates something new. Follett [22] also recommends that using a collaborative style stabilizes the conflict in the group, but in a compromise style the conflict is likely to recur in other forms.

Extant IS research has shown that groups using computer support in decision-making are likely to have less affective conflict and more task conflict, as team members will be more focused in their discussions in comparison to groups without any computer support [54]. Research also shows that groups that make use of computer-supported interactions tend to have greater levels of collaborative conflict resolution than groups that meet face-to-face [52]. These behaviors or attitudes may be compounded by the special circumstances present in a virtual team’s environment, such as the remoteness of the members and the technology-supported interaction, as well as in the type of teams. The importance of conflict management seems to gain additional relevance in cases when groups are heterogeneous.

## Team Type

Diversity in group composition has been an important area of research in social psychology literature for quite some time [51]. It is necessary to recognize the need to extend the research to virtual teams, keeping in mind the increased use of global virtual teams.

Diversity in groups has been classified by researchers as observable and nonobservable, though these are not necessarily mutually exclusive. For instance, observable diversity would include ethnic background, age, or gender, whereas the less visible type would include education or functional background [51]. Research has examined the effect of each type of diversity (see [51] for a review). Heterogeneity due to ethnic diversity was found to have a positive effect on overall cooperative behavior of the group [9, 14]. Watson et al. [92] found that group performance was affected by cultural, ethnic, and nationality variations and changed over time. Anderson and Hiltz [3] compared homogenous and heterogeneous groups using computer-mediated asynchronous communication or interacting face-to-face. Although their results were not very promising, they emphasized the need for further research in this area.

Other variations, such as gender, age, skills, and knowledge, have also been examined by researchers, especially when assessing the effect of diversity on top management performance [61, 75]. The potential effect of group diversity on group performance is undisputed in the literature, but the direction of that effect has been an important question that is still not fully addressed [15]. Researchers, such as Cox et al. [9], Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven [16], and McGrath [48], argue that diversity gives rise to better performance. On the other hand, Ancona and Caldwell [2], O’Reilly et al. [61], and Smith et al. [76] state that group heterogeneity may have negative effects on overall group performance due to the individual’s feeling of dissatisfaction or decreasing sense of identification or social interaction. Their argument assumes that homogeneity promotes trust and ease of communication. It is important to remember that much of the extant research in this area has examined face-to-face teams, and not much has been done to understand homogeneity and heterogeneity in virtual teams, especially those that may operate in an anonymous fashion. Our research adds to the body of knowledge by examining the performance of homogeneous and heterogeneous virtual teams acting anonymously.

## Performance Measures

## Virtual Team Performance

Prior studies involving the performance of group decision-making have commonly included variables such as decision time, member satisfaction, participation, consensus, agreement, and perceived decision quality [7, 28, 45, 53, 79, 80, 86]. In this research we intend to focus on the effectiveness rather than the efficiency of virtual teams and, thus, will exclude decision time from our current research model. Our model includes the variables of member agreement on a final decision, and member perceptions of participation, decision quality, and satisfaction with the decision process.

The level of agreement among group members is important in the context of groups with varying cultural orientations [50], especially when analyzing the effect of heterogeneity of group members. Decision quality is another important variable for any research involving group decision-making. Decision quality–related issues (overall quality and idea quality) have been examined most frequently by researchers in the GSS area between 1982 and mid-1998 [20], an indication of the importance of this category of variables in group decision-making. Decision quality can be assessed by comparing the outcome of group work with a correct or desired solution for certain categories of group tasks, such as intellective tasks. However, unlike intellective tasks, decision-making and cognitive-conflict tasks do not have any correct outcome [48]. Yet decision quality as perceived by the participants is an important dependent variable for these types of group work. Perceived decision quality includes group members’ confidence in the decision outcome and their perceptions of the usefulness of the decision outcome [24]. Satisfaction with the decision-making process is the most widely studied satisfaction measure in GSS research [20]. It is of special interest in this study, as we wanted to know how satisfying the process of group interaction is when members are dispersed over time, space, organization, and national culture. Another issue that seems interesting in this situation is the participation of the virtual team members in the group work. We focus on team members’ perception about participation, which has received considerable attention in GSS research [20].

## Research Hypotheses

Members of global virtual teams can differ with respect to several factors, including place of residence, nationality, and computer skills. Consequently, when these members are grouped together for a decision-making task, the resultant teams can generally be described as being one of two types: homogeneous or heterogeneous. Homogeneous teams would include members who are similar in their backgrounds and characteristics, whereas heterogeneous teams would have members who are different. Taking these variations into consideration, it follows that the output of these groups might be different in terms of some performance measures. In addition, since collaboration and cooperation among group members are very critical for the completion of the task at hand, members’ attitudes toward conflict management become important determinants of the final outcome of virtual teams’ decision-making activities. Hence, there is a need to investigate whether the relationship between the strength of the collaborative conflict management style prevailing in the team and its performance is influenced by the type of a virtual team (homogeneous/heterogeneous). Further, as group members’ tendencies toward individualism–collectivism have been found to play a major role in shaping their cooperative behavior [90], it follows that the strength of these values as displayed by team members may have an effect on the extent to which virtual teams adopt a collaborative conflict management style in their discussions. Therefore, the theory that this paper attempts to advance links all of these issues into a model (Figure 1) that could predict the performance of global virtual teams based on their homogeneity/heterogeneity and their members’ inclinations toward the values of individualism–collectivism.

## Individualism–Collectivism and Collaborative Conflict Management Style

Hofstede’s [29] work on the cultural characteristics of nations provides one of the theoretical foundations upon which this paper is based. Culture affects almost all of the mental programs of individuals (e.g., attitudes, personality, satisfaction, perception, and so on), which in turn are reflected by our behavior [29]. Thus, the collective effort, member interaction, and level of success achieved by a virtual team would be expected to be influenced by the behaviors exhibited by the team members. The values of individualism–collectivism and how strongly or weakly they are adhered to by the members of a virtual team will contribute to how much of the collaborative conflict management style might develop in the team. We expect that a higher proclivity toward individualism would lead to weaker tendencies by members to be collaborative in their attitude toward intrateam conflict. Trubisky et al. [85] found that members from collectivistic cultures used collaborative conflict resolution style more often than members from individualistic cultures. Individualists are more concerned about their own interest and less about the interests of the entire group. Wagner [90] reported that in group discussions, individualistic members who place high emphasis on self-independence are less likely to engage in cooperative behavior, whereas collectivist members who value interdependence among group members are more likely to be cooperative in their behavior. Therefore,

Hypothesis 1: In a GSS-based global virtual team, members’ proclivities toward individualism–collectivism will operate in such a way that the higher the level of individualism, the lower will be the overall level of collaborative conflict management style prevailing in the team.

## Collaborative Conflict Management Style, Team Performance, and Team Type

In general, collaborative styles toward conflict management are shown to have a favorable effect on group performance [18, 52, 59]. Montoya-Weiss et al. [53] reported a positive influence for the collaborative conflict management style on the performance of virtual teams. Similarly, in this research we expect collaborative conflict management style to exert a positive effect on the overall performance of a globally diverse virtual team as measured by members’ perceptions of participation in group work, satisfaction with the decision process, perceived decision quality, and level of agreement on the final decision. We propose these positive effects of collaborative conflict management style in H2a, H3a, H4a, and H5a.

Prior research on diversity and group performance shows mixed results. Several studies point to some advantageous effects for diversity on group performance and group decision-making [8, 51]. Negative effects of diversity include conflict, confusion, and dissolution of minority views [49]. Other studies also point to the possible damaging effects diversity might have if it is not handled well in group decisionmaking [42, 55]. However, the effect of diversity on the predisposition to collaborate with other members of virtual teams is relatively understudied in the literature, especially in the case of virtual teams. Thus, this study proposes and tests several hypotheses (H2b, H3b, H4b, and H5b) that aim at exploring whether diversity alters the way collaborative conflict management style influences the performance of virtual teams.

We expect that members who adopt a collaborative conflict management style would find it natural to cooperate with others and get involved in the activities of the group. Collaborative virtual team members would be more inclined toward active participation and involvement in group work. In addition, members of GSS-based virtual teams can interact anonymously, which reduces evaluation apprehension and conformance of members, thereby fostering higher levels of member participation. Prior research stresses that GSS groups that interact in an anonymous mode generate more critical comments [38, 57]. Moreover, due to the parallel nature of comment posting in a synchronously held virtual meeting, higher member participation will also be promoted. Therefore,

Hypothesis 2a: In a GSS-based global virtual team, the collaborative conflict management style will have a positive effect on member participation in team activities.

We expect that the heterogeneity of a group of decision-makers might play itself out in the resultant pattern of discussions and nature of opinions and viewpoints expressed by members. In the case of cultural diversity, groups are reported to display less effective communication processes [8]. As a result, in a heterogeneous virtual team, the different backgrounds and characteristics of the members might be expected to have adverse effects on group cohesion. The cohesiveness of a group influences the group’s decision-making process in multiple ways [56], and cohesive groups have been found to display a higher level of intragroup communication [43]. However, the adverse effects attributed to heterogeneity can be expected to lead to psychological pressures such as discomfort and weak feelings of belonging to the group, especially in short-lived groups [95]. As a result, some or all members may refrain from seeking or sharing necessary problem information or task details. Therefore,

Hypothesis 2b: Heterogeneity of a GSS-based global virtual team will weaken the positive effect that the collaborative style will have on member participation in team activities.

The collaborative conflict management style involves a high concern for one’s self and others in group [66]. As a result of adopting this style, virtual team members would expend time and effort on synthesizing the differences and strengthening the similarities in their perspectives, which would help ensure more agreeable final decisions [22]. Therefore,

Hypothesis 3a: In a GSS-based global virtual team, the collaborative conflict management style will have a positive effect on the degree of agreement among group members.

Diversity with respect to backgrounds and skills has been reported to cause integration problems in groups [51]. Moreover, when members in GSS-based virtual teams interact anonymously, the opportunity of knowing other members’ background and understanding their views is reduced considerably. We therefore expect that heterogeneity of the virtual team will have a weakening effect on the relationship between the collaborative conflict management style and agreement among members. Therefore,

Hypothesis 3b: Heterogeneity of a GSS-based global virtual team will weaken the positive effect that the collaborative style will have on the degree of agreement among group members.

As group members assume a collaborative conflict management style, the decision situation is analyzed from multiple perspectives. The final decision is arrived at after ample discussion and through the integration of multiple views. As a result, the final decision would appear to be thorough, reflective of every member’s preference, and, hence, fair, leading to higher confidence in the decision [60]. Moreover, as the decision is an integration of the preferences of all or at least a majority of the members, it is likely that members will find the decision relevant and useful. Having confidence in the decision and finding it relevant and useful will enhance group members’ perceptions of decision quality. Therefore,

Hypothesis 4a: In a GSS-based global virtual team, the collaborative conflict management style will exert a positive effect on members’ perceived decision quality.

As noted earlier, heterogeneous groups are likely to have integration problems [51]. When members come from diverse cultural backgrounds, understanding others’ views may be difficult. As a result, group members are likely to arrive at a decision without fully understanding others’ views, resulting eventually in lower confidence in the decision. It is reasonable to expect that the positive effect of collaborative conflict management style on perceived decision quality will be weakened in heterogeneous groups. Therefore,

Hypothesis 4b: Heterogeneity of a GSS-based global virtual team will weaken the positive effect that the collaborative style will have on members’ perceived decision quality.

A GSS-based decision-making process is essentially an electronic meeting process that involves “procedures, deliberations, and methods used by a group” to arrive at a decision [68, p. 68]. Satisfaction with a process has been measured in terms of efficiency, coordination, fairness, and understandability of a process [68]. Feelings of participation and control are also identified as indicators of computer user satisfaction [4]. We expect that the coordination among members will be better in the groups that adopt a higher level of collaborative style. The members of this type of group are expected to participate extensively in the group decision, evaluate multiple perspectives, and have the feeling that the decision process is fair to all participants. Therefore,

Hypothesis 5a: In a GSS-based global virtual team, the collaborative conflict management style will exert a positive effect on members’ satisfaction with the decision process.

In contrast, heterogeneous teams, especially culturally diverse teams, are likely to have a less effective communication process [8]. As a result, members may not be able to understand diverse perspectives and evaluate multiple alternatives. We expect that heterogeneity will weaken the positive effect of the collaborative conflict management style on the satisfaction of team members’ decision-making process. Therefore,

Hypothesis 5b: Heterogeneity of a GSS-based global virtual team will weaken the positive effect that the collaborative style will have on members’ satisfaction with the final decision.

## Research Methodology

## Subjects

THE FOCUS OF OUR STUDY was the collaboration of team members working together in a virtual Web-based, culturally diverse environment. This design led to the identification of subjects from two different organizations: a large Midwestern university in the United States and a prestigious management institute in India. Subjects from U.S. and Indian institutions were volunteers enrolled in each university’s MBA program. A total of 22 groups completed the study: 17 groups with 4 subjects per group and 5 groups with 3 per group. There were 4 U.S.-only groups, 9 India-only groups, and 9 heterogeneous groups.

Our design is consistent with much of the research reported by Fjermestad and Hiltz [20] regarding numbers of groups, groups per cell/treatment, subjects per group, and total number of subjects. Fjermestad and Hiltz found that 18 percent (36 out of 200) of the studies contained between 21–30 groups; our study involved 22 groups. Fjermestad and Hiltz noted that 33.5 percent (67 out of 200) contained fewer than 6 groups per cell; our study involved 13 homogeneous groups and 9 heterogeneous ones. Our study involved 17 groups with 4 subjects per group and 5 groups with 3 per group, which is again consistent with much of the reported research (the modal group size is 3, followed next by 4 persons per group). Finally, our study involved a total of 83 persons; 32.5 percent (65 out of 200) of studies involved fewer than 80 total participants.

Often a concern is raised regarding the use of students as subjects. In their analysis of more than 200 articles relating to experimental research involving GSS, Fjermestad and Hiltz [20] report that 94 percent of those studies involved students as subjects. Furthermore, Briggs et al. [6] found no significant differences between executive business managers and graduate business students in evaluating technology. Also, Remus [69] found no significant difference between line managers and MBA students with little business experience in production scheduling decisions. Given these data, we felt comfortable with the background of our subjects.

A number of studies involving group decision support systems (GDSS) include rewards for participation. All subjects in this research were volunteers, and all received some reward for participating. U.S. participants received extra credit in one of the courses they were enrolled in. Although extra credit is not normally available in the Indian institution, those subjects were eager to participate because of the training and experience they received with the collaboration software. Other than in this study, such software and its use was, for the most part, not available to those students.

Due to the nature of the study, the approximate time difference of ten and a half hours between the United States and India, and the schedules of the students in each location, complete random assignment of subjects to groups was not possible. However, once the availability of the students in each location was known—accounting for the time differences, class schedules, and so on—students were randomly assigned to groups based on their availability.

## Variable Identification

This study examined the influence of two independent variables, one moderating variable and four dependent variables. Data supporting instrument reliability and validity is provided in the Results section below. One independent variable is the collaborative conflict management style of each group. Based on the instrumentation used by Rahim [66] and Montoya-Weiss et al. [53], we adapted five items measuring the collaboration pattern of conflict management within organizational work groups [53]. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert-type scale, with 1 representing “strongly disagree” and 5 “strongly agree.”

The other independent variable is individualism–collectivism. Measures for this variable were adapted from research conducted by Wagner [90]. The dimensions of individualism–collectivism that were measured were personal independence and selfreliance, competitive success, working alone, and effects of personal pursuits on group productivity. All items were measured on a seven-point Likert scale, with 1 representing “strongly disagree” and 7 “strongly agree.”

The moderating variable was group type. This variable was dichotomous: group type was considered either homogeneous or heterogeneous. Homogeneous groups were identified as those containing all Indian participants or all U.S. participants. Heterogeneous groups were a mixture of Indian and U.S. subjects as well as subjects from other countries studying at the U.S. university. Consistent with the time and other differences discussed above, the makeup of the homogeneous and heterogeneous groups was considered sufficiently rigorous. Only Indian subjects were members of the Indian homogenous groups, and with just two exceptions, only those born and living in the United States were members of the U.S. homogeneous groups. The two exceptions involved two students enrolled at the U.S. university who were not born in the United States. However, each has attended more than one U.S. university, each has been in the United States for more than ten years, and both speak English as their primary language. Phinney [64] has indicated that language use is an accurate assessment tool for acculturation, which is the process by which an ethnic group gradually changes its behaviors and attitudes to be more like those of the host society [26]. Smither and Rodriguez-Giegling [77] argued that factors such as social contacts, knowledge of host country language, length of time in a new country, and level of education are among the major facilitators of faster acculturation. Also, Goldlust and Richmond [25] have demonstrated that education and length of residence are the most important determinants of immigrant adaptation. Immigrants who reside in a host country for ten or more years were found to have greater levels of adaptation to the host country’s way of life. Thus, all heterogeneous groups involved a mixture of students born and living in the United States and India or students attending the U.S. university who were from different backgrounds and cultures and had spent different lengths of time in the United States. All students who were members of the heterogeneous groups and who were not born and raised in the United States had been in the United States for less than five years, with many just in their first year.

Four dependent variables were measured: satisfaction with the decision process, perceived decision quality, perceived participation, and degree of agreement. Seven items were adapted from the research by Bailey and Pearson [4] and Ives et al. [33] to measure satisfaction with the decision process; of these seven items, the first five measured satisfaction with the decision process, and the last two assessed overall satisfaction and were used to assess predictive validity, which is addressed in the Results section. Each item was measured on a five-point Likert-type scale, with 1 as “strongly disagree” and 5 as “strongly agree.” Similarly, five items also adapted from research by Bailey and Pearson [4] and Ives et al. [33] were used to measure perceived decision quality. Of these, items one through four measured perceived decision quality, and the fifth item was used to assess predictive validity, which is discussed in the Results section. The same Likert-type scale used for measuring satisfaction with the decision process was used for measuring perceived decision quality. Perceived participation was measured by six items adapted from research by Jarvenpaa et al. [36] and Watson and Michalesen [94]. Similar to what was discussed above, the sixth item was used to measure predictive validity and is addressed in the Results section. The items were measured by a five-point scale, with 1 representing “almost never” and 5 “almost always.” The degree of agreement among group members was also a dependent variable. This was measured as the percentage of members voting in favor of the final decision proposed by a group. Instrument items are outlined in Appendix A.

## Task Description

McGrath [48] integrated a number of approaches to categorizing tasks relevant to group research, and DeSanctis and Gallupe [12] provided additional insight on tasks in the context of GDSS. Based on these categorizations, the task chosen for this research was a decision-making task “for which the preferred or agreed upon answer is the correct one” [48, p. 62]—that is, a preference task. In other words, it is a task that requires the selection of an alternative for which there is no objective criterion regarding correctness [12]. Furthermore, given that the participants were students, it was felt that their involvement would be stronger and that they would relate better if the task was one in which they would easily associate. Thus, the task chosen was the selection of a computer use fee for students enrolled in an online university.

Each participant was provided with a written and an online copy of the task that was integrated into the group support software used for the study. A description of the task appears in Appendix B, and the tool to support collaboration is described below.

The task description provided the participants with information regarding why the online university was considering the implementation of a computer use fee, and it also listed five options that the participants were to consider:

• Assessing a flat fee for each undergraduate and graduate course.

• Assessing two fees: one for all undergraduate courses and a separate, higher fee for all graduate courses.

• Assessing a graduated fee. Students enrolled in courses deemed less IT-intensive, such as many liberal arts courses, would pay less than students enrolled in more IT-intensive courses, such as computer programming and database.

• Assessing a fee for use. A user would provide an ID and password in order to receive online assistance. The student’s university account would be assessed the fee.

• Assessing a fee on the basis of where the student lived. Since the university was an online university, students could come from many different countries. This option was based on the assumption that students from better-off countries should pay more than students from less-developed ones.

Experimental procedures did not provide for identifying and recommending any option other than the five listed above.

## Collaboration Tool and Training

All of the participants were experienced with using Web tools, but none had previously used the collaboration software employed in this study. The tool used was Consensus@nyWare, a Web-based GDSS. It enabled group members to comment on each of the five computer-use fee options, to ask questions of their group members, to comment on other group members’ comments, and so on. The tool also allowed for voting on and ranking of the options.

Subjects were scheduled into a training session that dealt with all phases of the study at times that were convenient to the students. The sessions in the United States and in India were conducted in computer laboratories that were also used for the actual experiment. The training was conducted by three of the researchers, one in India and two in the United States, each of whom had extensive experience in the collaboration tool, its features, and its use. Each session lasted about two hours.

Students were supplied with appropriate training materials during this session. These included a one-page list of commands, features of the software, and a description of each feature and how it was used. The use of the features and commands was facilitated by the fact that the tool itself was Web-based, thereby providing for familiar browser-like navigation. During the training sessions, each trainer demonstrated a fictitious task, making use of all of the tool’s commands and features that were also used in the computer-use fee task employed during the actual study. Students were seated at laptop workstations equipped with a mouse and were able to view the use of the tool’s features and commands and the procedures to carry out the fictitious task. Any questions asked by the students were answered by the trainer. Subjects were not exposed to the actual computer-use fee task at any time during the training session.

## Experimental Procedures

Consistent with time zone differences and related logistics, subjects were randomly assigned to groups and were informed by one of the researchers as to when they would participate. Anonymity among group members was maintained throughout the study. As stated previously, there were four U.S.-only groups, nine India-only groups, and nine heterogeneous groups.

At no time during any of the group sessions were there more than eight persons in either the U.S. or Indian computer labs. The labs were large enough to space the subjects in such a manner that no subject was able to observe what others were doing. Subjects were seated at individual workstations and for reference were provided with copies of the task descriptions and summaries of relevant software commands, which they had thoroughly practiced during the training session. The experiment involved one session per group, with each session consisting of the following activities:

• Activity 1—each group member commented on the advantages, disadvantages, obstacles, and so on, of each of the five options. The software allowed each subject to read each fee option, comment on options, comment on other group members’ statements, questions, and so on. When finished, each group member rated the five options from 0 (least appropriate) to 4 (most appropriate) and viewed the rating results for his or her group.

• Activity 2—each group member commented on the group’s rating that concluded Activity 1. This activity centered on discussion about why or why not the highest rated option was good, and so on, and resolved conflict if more than one option ended up having a similar or almost similar rating at the end of Activity 1. Near the end of Activity 2, each group member voted “yes” or “no” to accept the proposed fee option.

• Activity 3—each group member completed a posttest questionnaire. Data collected during this phase included demographic data, IT experience, all dependent variable measures, conflict management style, and individualism–collectivism.

Given the nature of the task—that is, a preference task—we felt that it was important to allow participants to introduce comments, discuss the options, comment on other’s suggestions/comments, and so on, and therefore not to place an artificial time limit on the session length for any group. Accordingly, all subjects were allowed as much time as they desired. In fact, most groups took between 60 and 90 minutes to complete all activities associated with the study, which is considerably longer than the majority of the research reported by Fjermestad and Hiltz [20].

Each session was observed unobtrusively by at least one of the researchers in the United States and India. However, we were able to capture data relating to comments, questions, and so on, made by the participants. A review of these data showed that the subjects communicated well with each other, that they made meaningful comments, and, where appropriate, expressed their concerns or disagreements with other members’ discussion points. More complete information regarding usage is presented in the Results section.

## Reliability and Validity

Four experts on group decision-making and attitude measurement conducted an initial review of these measures to establish their face validity. Subsequently, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated. With the exception of the instruments for individualism and collaborative conflict management style, the measurement scales used had not been tested and validated before. Thus, in view of the exploratory nature of this research, a cut-off value of 0.70 was considered acceptable [58]. An alpha of 0.833 was found for satisfaction with the decision-making process; 0.903 for perceived decision quality; 0.816 for perceived participation; 0.773 for individualism; and 0.768 for collaborative conflict management style.

Content validity can be established by having the instruments evaluated by the experts [11]. Our instruments were based on prior research on individualism [90], conflict management styles [53], user satisfaction [4, 33], and participation [36, 94]. In addition, the experts who participated in our pilot study sessions reviewed and provided feedback on the instruments.

## Use of the Collaboration Software

Care was taken to ensure that participants from each institution were experienced with several IT aspects, which included word processing, spreadsheet, database management, the Internet and the Web, messaging systems such as electronic mail and chat, conferencing systems such as NetMeeting, and collaboration support software.

Subjects completed a questionnaire that included identifying their level of experience with each of the above types of technology on a five-point scale, with 1 representing “not at all,” 3 “moderately,” and 5 “extensively.” Overall experience was determined by computing an average score for each person’s experience with all of the above technologies. The mean for overall experience was 3.16, with a standard deviation of 0.57. There were no significant differences in overall experience based on a participant’s gender, age, nationality, or country of residence.

Even though participants possessed a high degree of familiarity with IT as described above, we felt that it was important to examine how well they used the collaboration software. In small groups, even one member’s unfamiliarity with the technology may affect group work significantly. Our training session was well planned, implemented, and focused on providing the subjects with appropriate knowledge regarding the collaboration software. In addition, at least one of the researchers monitored all participating groups unobtrusively and noted that each participant was able to use the technology and participate actively in the group process. No system or network problems occurred during any group session, and all subjects were at ease with the technology. Overall, the participants were comfortable with the GSS and quite involved with the task activities.

Overall experience was significantly correlated with experience with collaboration software $( r = 0 . 6 7 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and GDSS $( r = 0 . 4 8 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . In addition, individual members perceived a high level of participation in the process $( \bar { x } = 4 . 3 2 , s = 0 . 2 5 0 )$ We also found that individual members’ perception of participation did not vary significantly across groups. To examine participation further, we correlated group members’ participation with their actual participation reflected in the comments submitted by group members in Activities 1 and 2. Recall that the experiment was conducted anonymously, so it was not possible to attribute any comment to any one individual. However, at the group level the average perceived participation was positively related to the actual comments submitted in Activity $2 \ ( r = 0 . 5 1 7 , p = 0 . 0 1 )$ ). The comments submitted in Activity 1 when groups discussed all options were not significantly related to perceived participation. This implies that the groups that discussed more about the final choice of the decision (Activity 2) had higher perceived participation. Also, analysis of variance (ANOVA) results showed no significant differences in the comments submitted in Activities 1 and 2 between the homogeneous and heterogeneous groups. Finally, although experience with collaboration software and GDSS was low for most participants, with mean scores of 1.59 and 1.53, respectively, this was not unexpected. This is why we paid particular attention to the training program.

Based on these findings and observations, the emphasis we placed on the collaboration software training, and the group members’ comments and perceived participation, we feel that all subjects possessed sufficient experience to actively participate in the study, and group members did reasonably participate.

## Hypothesis Testing

The hypotheses were tested using regression analyses with a level of significance of 0.05. Any weak significance level in the range of 0.05 to 0.10 was treated as suggestive of the nature of relationships between the variables. As the unit of analysis was the group, we used group-level aggregate scores for the variables in our statistical analyses. The use of groups as the unit of analysis is consistent with prior research [23, 87]. We also tested the different assumptions for regression, such as normality and homogeneity of residuals, and found no violations.

Collaborative conflict management style was regressed on the individual measure of individualism. The regression analysis results revealed that the higher the individualism of a group, the lower is the collaborative conflict management style followed by the members of the group $( \beta = - 0 . 4 0 4 , p \leq 0 . 0 3 , R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 2 )$ . The explanation could be that the higher the level of individualism of the members in a group, the lower will be the tendency to be concerned for others, thus resulting in lower levels of the collaborative conflict management style. The result supports H1.

As some of our hypotheses (H2b, H3b, H4b, and H5b) were on moderating effects, we relied on the approach suggested in prior literature on the use of moderator variables [74, 97] and tested our hypotheses in several steps. First (step a), we regressed the dependent variables on the main effect (collaborative conflict management style) to test H2a–H5a. Next (step b), we regressed the dependent variables on the main effect and moderator effect (group types). Finally (step c), we regressed the dependent variables on the main effect, moderator effect, and the interaction between the main and moderator effects. We tested the significance of the interaction effect by comparing the regression models in steps a, b, and c. F-test comparisons of the regression coefficients of the models were carried out, and the regression model representing step c was found to be significantly different (at a 5 percent level of significance) from the other two models (from steps a and b) for perceived decision quality and degree of agreement in groups. In addition, we found that group type had no relationship with any dependent variable. This implied that group type has a moderating effect on the relationship between collaborative conflict management style and some of the dependent variables (perceived decision quality and degree of agreement in groups) in our research.

The first regression analyses demonstrated statistically significant relationships that collaborative conflict management style had with satisfaction with the decision-making process, perceived decision quality, and degree of group agreement. However, the analysis did not demonstrate any significant effect of collaborative conflict management style on perceived participation. The results, summarized in Table 3, support H3a, H4a, and H5a, while H2a remains unsupported in this study.

The results of the third regression analysis, presented in Table 4, show the significant effect that the interaction of collaborative conflict management style and group type have on perceived decision quality and group agreement. However, the moderating effect of group type on satisfaction with the decision-making process was not demonstrated in the statistical analyses. Thus, H3b and H4b received support in this study.

## Discussion

In this research, we examined two important issues of GSS-based virtual teams. We found that an individualistic orientation of the members of a virtual team influences its collaborative conflict management style. Consistent with findings of prior research on individualism [90], we found that the higher the individualistic orientation of a virtual team, the lower is its collaborative conflict management style. When the members of a virtual team are predominantly individualistic, there is very little tendency to pursue the interests that are shared collectively by all in the group unless the collective interest matches the self-interest. These virtual teams are less likely to follow a collaborative style to resolve any conflict that may arise in the course of group work. This is reflected in group members’ self-reported perceptions about the collaborative conflict management style of the group.

Our second finding was related to collaborative conflict management style and performance of GSS-based virtual teams. As hypothesized in this paper, when virtual teams follow a collaborative conflict management style, the performance of the team seems to improve in terms of decision process satisfaction, perceived decision quality, and degree of group agreement. However, collaborative conflict management style seems to have no significant effect on the perceived participation of virtual teams. The perceived participation values were high for all groups $( \bar { x } = 4 . 3 2 1 ; s = 0 . 2 4 9 )$ , apparently as a result of the high level of anonymity and parallel communication support provided by the Web-based GSS. We feel that the technology support might have eclipsed the positive effect of the collaborative conflict management style on perceived participation.

As expected, the group type (homogeneity/heterogeneity) has a moderating effect on the relationships of collaborative conflict management style with perceived decision quality and group agreement. The moderating effect was not observed for decision process satisfaction. Figures 2 and 3 graphically depict the statistically significant moderating effects demonstrated in this study. It appears that the effect of collaborative conflict management style is more prominent for heterogeneous groups. At low levels of collaborative conflict management style, homogeneous virtual teams seem to outperform heterogeneous teams in terms of perceived decision quality and group agreement. However, the heterogeneous teams that are strong on collaborative conflict management style seem to perform better than their homogeneous counterparts. These teams have a significant number of members who prefer to work with others and who value group interests more than their personal needs. They are likely to select a decision outcome that integrates diverse views of the members. They view the final decision of their group as fair, comprehensive, and perhaps the best, given that it has been analyzed from different perspectives. As a result, the decision quality perceived by the group members improves.

We also found that agreement among members in heterogeneous groups that have high collaborative orientation was better than among their homogeneous counterparts. Although the finding may appear to be counterintuitive, it seems to have its roots in the sociological model of consensus [73]. Consensus is described as a collective process, and “the type and extent of consensus is dependent on the type and extent of coordination required between the members of the group” [73, p. 41]. Heterogeneous groups generate diverse interpretations of decision situations. Yet when the group members collaborate to build consensus around the diverse interpretations, collective learning results [19]. When group members understand other members’ perspectives and realize that others understand their perspectives, a higher level of consensus results [73]. In our experiment, when heterogeneous groups collaborated extensively, the members discussed in-depth to understand diverse perspectives of the decision. We analyzed the depth (i.e., the number of levels) of discussions of the groups in Activity 2 when they discussed the final decision and attempted to reach consensus. We focused only on the groups that had a score of four or higher on collaborative conflict management style and found that the heterogeneous groups had more in-depth discussions than the homogeneous groups $( \bar { x } _ { \mathrm { h e t e r o g e n e o u s } } = 6 \mathrm { a n d } \bar { x } _ { \mathrm { h o m o g e n e o u s } } = 3 . 6 2 5 )$ . The members of these heterogeneous groups might have developed a better understanding of other members’ perspectives, which might have helped these groups to reach a higher level of consensus in the decision process. However, it would be improper to draw any conclusion from these indications, mainly because only 12 groups had high scores on collaborative conflict management style (4 or above). Whether heterogeneous groups with high collaborative conflict management style have a better understanding of diverse perspectives on a decision, and hence have a higher level of agreement than their homogeneous counterparts, is an interesting area for future research.

## Implications for Managers

Managers who are involved with global virtual teams today need to consider carefully the two team composition variables that we addressed in this research: cultural orientation, especially individualism versus collectivism and the level or degree of diversity among team members—that is, homogeneous versus heterogeneous. Our findings show that these variables do matter directly or as a moderator for several group performance attributes, and that, depending on the mix of cultural values and diversity, managers may face different types of challenges. For example, managers may need to exert different types of strategies to motivate team members who possess more of an individualistic orientation. Although “cooperation among individualists can be predictably induced only by the contingent receipt of personal incentives (for example, merit pay for effective team work), . . . cooperation among collectivists can be motivated solely by contingent receipt of outcomes that benefit the entire membership (for example, safe work environment)” [91, p. 285]. Likewise, it may be that different mixes of cultural orientation and degree of diversity may be more or less appropriate for different types of tasks faced by virtual teams. Complex tasks (such as fuzzy or judgment types) are cognitively more demanding than simple tasks [98], so it may be worthwhile to examine in future research whether performance in complex tasks is more sensitive to variations along the culture-diversity mix of virtual teams than that involving relatively simple tasks.

Managers may face other challenges as well. Culturally heterogeneous virtual teams may have difficulties in reaching high levels of collaborative conflict management style as compared to homogeneous groups. However, it appears that once the heterogeneous groups reach a reasonable level of collaborativeness, they start outperforming the homogeneous teams on some of the performance measures, such as perceived decision quality.

Global virtual teams usually rely on technologies, such as distributed GSS, that present challenges for coordination of group work. In addition, these technologies are usually lean on some cues, such as gestures, that characterize human communication. Managers may have to introduce proper facilitation techniques to address the deficiencies in coordination and human communication cues that are usually associated with the work of groupware-supported global virtual teams.

IN THIS RESEARCH, WE EXAMINED ONLY ONE dimension of cultural diversity—individualism. Other dimensions, such as uncertainty avoidance and long-term orientation, may have to be explored before any generalized conclusion on the performance of global virtual teams can be reached. We formed virtual teams involving subjects from the United States and India. The residents of these countries primarily differ on the individualism–collectivism dimension. In order to address other dimensions of culture, this study should be replicated by involving subjects residing in other countries.

The participants in this study were graduate business students who were excited at the prospect of using a Web-based collaborative technology and getting involved with an electronic meeting. However, as is true with most laboratory research, it was difficult to ensure that the subjects put forth their best effort to arrive at the decision. We attempted to assess this by analyzing the discussion of the meeting participants. We found that the average numbers of comments submitted in Activities 1 and 2 were 41.8 and 19.3, respectively. The software had the provision to have threaded discussions, and we found that the average numbers of levels of discussion were 6.9 and 4 for Activities 1 and 2, respectively. The number of comments and the depth of discussion in Activities 1 and 2 reflect that the participants spent reasonable effort in performing the experimental task. We also examined comments to identify if any system-related remarks were made by the groups. None were made in 19 out of 22 groups. With regard to the other three groups, there were a total of just three comments that could be construed as system-related—for example, “I missed out on [hitting] the indicator button,” and “software problems are hampering.” It was not clear that even this last comment was directed at the group support software; it could have referred to Internet issues between India and the United States. Two independent coders examined the remarks, and the intercoder correlation for total system-related comments is 0.85 $( p < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ . The analyses of these comments seem to imply that subjects were not uncomfortable with the system.

We used GSS to engage the virtual team members in the group work. GSS cannot convey various cues such as gestures that characterize normal human communication. It would be interesting to employ other modes of group communications, such as electronic chat, voice chat, and video conferencing, in future studies on virtual teams. Likewise, different types of support provided to virtual teams may be an important factor as well. For example, Pauleen [62] studied the issues affecting team leaders involved with virtual teams. It would be informative to complement that research with ours by examining leadership, or even types of facilitation, with virtual teams that involve homogeneous and heterogeneous groups from culturally diverse backgrounds.

The subjects in our experiment were from the United States and India and were about ten and a half hours apart. As the meetings were synchronous, some members worked during the daytime while others had to work at night to participate in the team work. This might have resulted in the perception of sacrifice among some subjects from India who had to work at night to participate in the experiment. This may not be unrealistic, given today’s global business activities. However, team members’ perceptions of sacrifice and its effect on the performance of virtual teams is an issue that can be explored in future research.

We pointed out earlier that the degree of experience with GDSS and collaboration software by our subjects was limited at best—an issue that was not unexpected, given the probable smaller number of academic institutions that might have this type of software. This is one of the reasons that there was considerable effort in developing and implementing the training program that each subject participated in. Future experimental research should consider determining the extent of experience with the specific tool used in the study, which could lead to more precise relationships and results.

Finally, the use of a particular software tool and the user’s comfort with that tool may have affected the way in which the members interacted to achieve the goal. Although it is difficult to overcome this limitation in IS research, especially in experiments, it is important for IS researchers to keep this issue in mind.

## Conclusion

SEVERAL CONCLUSIONS CAN BE DRAWN FROM THIS RESEARCH. We found that collaboration is the key to improving the performance of culturally diverse virtual teams using GSS. The members of these teams do not interact face-to-face and have very few clues regarding other members’ orientation and views on the group work. Achieving harmony in this type of a group is not easy unless individual members are willing to collaborate. We also demonstrated that the individualistic–collectivistic orientation of the team members influences the level of collaborative style pursued by a team. This highlights the importance of training and motivating team members to collaborate in group work.

We also demonstrated that collaboration among the members of virtual team is crucial in improving the degree of agreement, perceived decision quality, and satisfaction with the decision process. However, we did not find any significant effect of collaborative conflict management style of virtual teams on the perceived participation of team members. Participation might have been influenced by process and communication support provided for the GSS-based virtual teams. Although no generalization can be made from the findings of one experiment, the results provide enough motivation to pursue in-depth research on cross-cultural virtual teams that use GSS.

We examined the performance of culturally homogeneous and heterogeneous teams and found indications that heterogeneity may, in fact, enhance the perceived decision quality when team members are willing to collaborate. Contrary to popular belief that group diversity may have a negative effect on group consensus, we found that heterogeneous groups with high collaborative conflict management style can achieve a higher level of agreement. The findings seem to imply that heterogeneity by itself may not be detrimental to the performance of virtual teams. The critical issues are the communication among members and the willingness to collaborate with others on the teams.

Although articulating diverse views on the task and attempting to understand others’ perspectives, members of heterogeneous groups seem to enrich the collective understanding which plays a crucial role in augmenting group performance. While no such conclusion can be made from the findings of this study (which addressed a different research question), the results provide enough motivation for future study on how communication and collaboration in virtual teams impact the development of collective understanding and hence improve the performance of the teams.

## Computer Use Fee Program

AS A STUDENT ENROLLED AT OnlineUniversity.edu, you have come to realize how important information technology is in the completion of your courses and related academic experiences and requirements. Since all of your courses, discussions, and interactions with faculty and classmates are conducted over the Internet, you can understand how important it is for our university to have the best information technology available.

To this end, the university is considering whether to impose a computer use fee. The purpose of the fee will be to provide instructional support to students enrolled in the university. Instructional support can include the following:

• Maintaining an online help desk. Persons staffing the help desk would be available via e-mail and chat lines to answer general computer- and software-related questions.

• Hiring student workers to monitor specific courses. For example, a certain number of students could be hired to be available at different times to answer specific course-related questions.

• Upgrading telecommunications equipment as needed to ensure the fastest and most reliable Internet connections.

• Purchase of appropriate software licenses for particular courses as well as for general online computer use.

The university provost has requested input from a committee of students to determine the best way to implement the Computer Use Fee Program. In this regard, there are a number of options available. They include:

• Assessing a flat fee to every undergraduate and graduate course offered by the university. Each student taking the course would pay the fee.

• Assessing a flat fee to every undergraduate and graduate course offered by the university. Students enrolled in undergraduate courses would pay less than students enrolled in graduate courses.

• Having a graduated type of fee structure. This would mean that courses that make little use of computers and software in the course would carry a lesser fee; courses, such as those in management information systems (MIS), where computer use is much higher, would carry a higher fee.

• Assessing a “fee for use” type of structure. Each time a student requested any kind of support, such as asking a question from a support person working the help desk, the student would be required to input an ID and password. The student’s university account would be assessed a fee. Students would have to pay for all such assessed fees by the end of the semester.

• Assess the fee on the basis of where the student lives. The rationale is that a student living, say, in most parts of the United States may be more capable of paying a higher fee than those residing in countries not on the “high income” list of the World Bank.

Since the university provost has appointed you to this committee, it is your responsibility to make a recommendation to the provost as to the best alternative. You and other members of your committee may choose from among the alternatives listed above. However, you must provide the provost with only one alternative.
