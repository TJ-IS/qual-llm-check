# Controlling Technical Debt Remediation in Outsourced Enterprise Systems Maintenance: An Empirical Analysis

Narayan Ramasubbu and Chris F. Kemerer

Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Technical debt refers to maintenance obligations that stem from violations of established standards during the development and subsequent maintenance of enterprise systems. Technical debt remediation is particularly challenging in the outsourcing context due to information asymmetry between client and vendor teams. Control balancing—the periodic adjustments to the control configurations of outsourced projects—has been proposed as a process to help mitigate these information asymmetry challenges. Using data collected from 1,824 real-world projects, we tested to what extent control balancing improves the remediation of technical debt. After controlling for a number of technical and environmental factors, including system size, system lifespan, and contract parameters, we find that control balancing can benefit technical debt remediation, but primarily when processes for migrating technical debt-laden systems to new technological platforms have been identified. We highlight the role of technical debt in influencing the efects of relational flexibility in inter-firm engagements.

## KEYWORDS

Technical debt; control balancing; project controls; software maintenance; enterprise systems; IT outsourcing; software project management

## Introduction

When programmers take shortcuts and violate established design and programming standards during the lifecycle of enterprise systems, they accumulate future system maintenance obligations, which in the information systems (IS) and software engineering literatures have been referred to as technical debt [5, 71, 84]. Studies have reported that technical debt decreases the reliability of enterprise systems and that identifying technical debt is often challenging due to increased system complexity, which creates multiple pathways for error propagation and hinders program comprehension [71]. Moreover, processes for remediating technical debt are not well established and are not integrated with established project management processes [72]. As a result, remediating technical debt has proven to be challenging, often resulting in multi-fold increases in system maintenance expenditures for firms [16, 87].

Market observers have reported that, in an efort to better manage the remediation of technical debt, firms tend to outsource the maintenance of their debt-laden enterprise systems to specialized vendors [56]. However, not all technical debt embedded in a client’s enterprise system is visible, making it a potent source of transaction hazards when the system is outsourced for maintenance to an outsourcing vendor. In the information systems (IS) discipline there is a rich research literature on information technology (IT) outsourcing that has examined various firm-level antecedents and consequences of software outsourcing, and from this literature a number of prescriptions have been developed for improving the performance of software outsourcing engagements [50, 51, 52]. However, despite the fact that technical-debt laden systems can exacerbate the challenges that arise from information asymmetry between clients and outsourcing vendors, examination of issues related to technical debt has, to date, been primarily focused on more narrow software issues. Thus, the eficacy of outsourcing engagements in remediating technical debt and the conditions that afect it are unclear.

Control balancing, an engagement process in which a client and a vendor make adjustments to project control configurations over the lifecycle of outsourced projects, has been theorized to reduce information asymmetry, improve shared understanding, and aid in the derivation of ideal engagement specifications [8, 32]. For client and vendor teams working on technical debt remediation, engaging in control balancing may help to develop a shared understanding of how a technical debt-laden system functions, which may, in turn, aid the development of problem-solving specifications. However, prior research has suggested that client firms may be reluctant to engage in control balancing activities with external vendor teams in order to, for example, protect intellectual property (e.g., idiosyncratic business logic), and/or to gain an upper hand in any potential contractual renegotiations with the outsourcing service provider [10, 11, 79]. In this study we bring together the technical debt and control balancing literatures in the context of IT outsourcing in order to pose the following overall research question: Does control balancing in outsourced enterprise systems maintenance projects improve remediation of technical debt? The degree and circumstances under which this is true could help develop a contingency perspective of the benefits of control balancing and drive the appropriate adoption of control balancing in practice.

We empirically answer the research question in two steps. First, we model the decision to engage in control balancing process as an endogenous choice of the client and the vendor firms. We posit that both the visibility of technical debt embedded in an enterprise system and the maturity of distributed work processes positively impact the extent to which a client firm engages in control balancing activities with the vendor. In contrast, volatility of the service system, that is, unplanned changes to business processes and the corresponding business logic in the enterprise’s systems due to environmental uncertainty, would be expected to curtail the client firm’s control balancing eforts. After this first stage prediction of the extent to which a client firm engages in control balancing, in the second stage of our analysis we assess the impact of control balancing activities on the realized levels of technical debt remediation. For estimating this two-stage model we utilized the level of industry competition faced by the client firms and the extent of financial risks in the projects as instruments to predict the extent of control balancing observed in the technical debt remediation projects. We posit that a higher level of industry competition and financial risks faced by the client firms would deter them from disclosing proprietary information and engaging in control balancing, whereas those factors are unlikely to directly influence the technical debt remediation activities undertaken by the technical personnel of the external (outsourcing) service provider.

We estimated the empirical models using data from 1,824 real-world projects executed by an outsourcing service provider. Results supported our postulations and revealed a heterogeneous impact of control balancing on technical debt remediation. We found that the efects of control balancing depend on the long-term engagement prospects between the client and the vendor firms. When the client and vendor teams were able to identify a long-term engagement process for migration of the debt-laden systems to a new technological platform, engaging in control balancing had a positive efect on remediation of technical debt. However, when such a long-term engagement plan did not emerge, engaging in control balancing actually had a negative efect on the remediation of technical debt. We build on these results to discuss implications for further research on control balancing, on IT outsourcing, and on technical debt and software project management.

This study makes three primary contributions. First, by analyzing the eficacy of technical debt remediation in real-world projects, we contribute to a better understanding of important contextual factors that influence the management of technical debt in enterprise systems. Prior research has highlighted the critical need to integrate processes needed for technical debt management and software project management [72]. Addressing that need, this study brings together technical debt remediation and an important aspect of software project management—configuration of controls—in the context of outsourced software projects. Second, we introduce technical debt, an important system design aspect, to the IT outsourcing literature, which opens new grounds for examining how processes and policy approaches required to manage technical debt may interact with other known transactional hazards to influence performance outcomes in outsourcing alliances. Finally, prior examinations of both technical debt and IS control configurations have mostly utilized qualitative process approaches and case studies, and scholars reviewing the corresponding literature streams have highlighted the need for conducting large-sample empirical investigations [15, 84, 86]. In this study, we utilized data from 1,824 commercial, outsourcing engagements to empirically model the interrelationships between control balancing and technical debt embedded in the enterprise systems of 424 clients across 11 industry sectors, which serves to complement and extend prior insights drawn from grounded theory-based process models and case studies that have been reported in prior research.

In the following sections of the paper, we describe the theoretical background of the study and present our hypotheses, after which we provide a description of our data collection procedures. Then, we present our analysis and results, which is followed by a discussion of the implications of our findings.

## Theory and Hypotheses

## Project Control Configurations and Control Balancing

Remediating technical debt in enterprise systems is a complex endeavor that involves harnessing the eforts and cooperation of a variety of stakeholders in an organization [72]. When this task is outsourced, it becomes even more complex due to the presence of hazards that impede transactions between client and vendor teams [2, 9, 42, 77, 81]. In order to manage such complexity and to gain control, that is, influence the behavior of various participants towards achieving organizational goals, organizational theorists have shown that firms deploy a variety of control mechanisms including bureaucracy, power, surveillance, socialization processes, and performance management using explicit measurement of outputs [e.g., 24, 25, 39, 65]. The specific types of controls deployed by managers are known to be contingent on a variety of operational conditions, including the ability to provide specifications for tasks, knowledge of the production process, ability to measure outputs, and the presence of environmental uncertainty [17, 65]. In organizational theory control is recognized as a purposive activity in which regulation of stakeholder behavior is achieved through a “cybernetic” process, that is, a process with a feedback loop that adjusts managerial enforcements based on performance measurement and comparisons of the realized performances with organizational goals and standards [31].

Building on these organizational theory perspectives of control, IS scholars have developed frameworks for controlling the information systems development process, and there is a rich literature on information systems controls<sup>1</sup> [15, 18, 43, 44, 86]. Early work in this literature constructed portfolios of controls that firms used to manage their internal systems development processes and outlined the specific ways in which the controls were implemented in the software projects within the firms. For example, Kirsch [43] utilized survey data from 32 real-world enterprise systems development projects to explain that the utilization of the four modes of control, namely, behavior, outcome, clan, and selfcontrol, depicted interdependencies with one another and with the level of systems development knowledge possessed by project sponsors who executed the project controls. Furthermore, task characteristics, role expectations, and skills of both end users and systems development personnel also were reported to play an important role in influencing the construction of controls portfolio [44, 45].

Beyond the consideration of behavior control and outcome control modes, studies have also utilized a “structure controls” approach to consider standardization of systems development activities and delegation of authority for project decision-making (decentralization). For example, Nidumolu and Subramani [63] present a matrix of control framework that combines the formal and structure control approaches, and show empirical evidence using survey data from 58 firms to support their postulation that performance improves when performance management criteria is standardized across projects and when decisionmaking on project methods is decentralized. Researchers have also sought to integrate the IS control perspective with project management, team alignment, and project risk perspectives to develop multi-level theories that consider cross-level dependencies between project management processes, manager-team alignment, project risks, and controls that impact project, user, and developer-level performance outcomes [e.g., 40, 61].

While the early work on IS controls primarily considered software projects executed internally within firms and involved co-located project personnel and plan-based development methodologies, follow-up research has extended those early considerations of IS controls to more recent developments in the field, including outsourced projects, distributed (or virtual) project teams, and projects that utilize flexible or agile system development methods. For example, Moody et al. [59] report the discovery of “field control” in cyber infrastructure projects where diverse teams from academia, government, and industry form collective entities within a field and subscribe to common purposes to advance the field. Experimental studies involving behavior controls and virtual teams have reported that enforcement of behavior controls weakens trust between team members and contributes to increased self-perception bias that weakens the impact of both reneging and fulfilling behaviors of virtual team members on trust [67].

Examinations of IS control portfolios in outsourced software development projects have revealed that outcome controls tend to be the dominant control mode in such projects [13, 53, 73, 82]. Behavior, self, and clan controls tend to be added to the control portfolios of outsourced software projects only later in the lifecycle of the projects, depending on their performance. Tiwana and Keil [82] illustrated the diferences between utilization of IS controls in internal and outsourced software projects, and they showed that transaction hazards present in the outsourcing context may lead to inefective realized controls, and that greater utilization of IS controls may not necessarily enhance project performance. Studies have also found that uncertainty in the environment, often manifested as requirements volatility, impacts the construction of the IS controls portfolio and moderates the relationship between execution of IS controls and performance outcomes [1, 57, 73, 82].

Our synthesis of recommendations in the IS controls literature for improving control efectiveness, particularly in the contexts of outsourcing, distributed teams, and agile methods use, revealed four related proposals. First, boundary-spanning activities between vendor and client teams that improve the trust and knowledge sharing between them have been found to significantly improve the efectiveness of IS controls [e.g., 29]. Second, newer forms of control modes, such as those that orient towards monitoring the evolution of project outputs (emergent outcomes rather than simply evaluating final outcomes), and those that focus on hybridizing formal and informal IS controls (rather than considering them in dichotomous arrangements), have been proposed to tackle the needs of distributed and crowdsourced modes of systems development [26, 34]. Third, mechanisms that aim for control ambidexterity by carefully selecting and combining formal and informal control mechanisms have been advocated. For example, Tiwana [83] explored the interactions between formal and informal control modes in IS control portfolios deployed in 120 outsourced software projects and found that combinations of clan control and processbased behavioral controls improved development flexibility and enhanced the fulfillment of project goals. Gregory and Keil [33] proposed that projects can achieve control ambidexterity through shared authority of project managers who adopt contrasting management styles (e.g., bureaucratic vs. collaborative). Fourth, and finally, mechanisms that leverage structural, relational, and cognitive social capital assets, such as accessibility to important resources, relationships that improve trust, and shared cognition and sensemaking, have been proposed and shown to strengthen team-based clan controls, knowledge integration, and mutual understanding that enhances performance [12, 38, 47].

In addition to the above recommendations, recent reviews of the gaps in the IS controls literature highlight the need to study how controls are enacted, along with a study of recent contextual antecedents and performance consequences of IS control portfolios [15, 86]. Although prior research had acknowledged that specific control modes and the extent to which they are utilized by controllers may vary across the diferent phases of projects [e.g., 13, 46], the antecedents and performance consequences of such dynamic reconfigurations of IS control portfolios have been characterized as understudied [15, 86]. To address this gap we build on the concept of control balancing [8, 32]. Control balancing refers to the targeted, iterative adjustments made to three dimensions of control configurations utilized in IS projects, namely control types (procedural, hybrid, and social), control degree (tight and relaxed), and control style (unilateral and bilateral). Utilizing a grounded theory approach and a longitudinal case study, Gregory et al. [32] illustrated how control balancing happens in an outsourced project setting. We build on this control balancing framework to better understand remediation of technical debt in outsourced projects.

## Control Balancing and Technical Debt

In this section, we first hypothesize the important antecedents of engaging in control balancing in outsourced technical debt remediation projects. Then, we present our hypothesis on the impact of control balancing activities on technical debt remediation.

## Technical Debt Visibility

Practitioner interviews and case studies have revealed that a significant portion of technical debt in enterprise systems is a result of unintentional programmer actions, ignorance, and/ or lack of oversight on important system-related decisions [84, 87]. Since such inadvertent technical debt is undocumented, client firms do not have full visibility of the actual levels of technical debt embedded in their enterprise systems. Even when technical debt accumulation has happened because of conscious design decisions, the lack of proper integration between project management processes and technical debt management processes in firms may result in erosion of knowledge about specific accumulations of technical debt [72]. In prior research, explicit codification of knowledge and targeted coordination among subject matter experts who span the boundaries between client and vendor teams have been shown to improve performance outcomes [e.g., 49, 54]. However, attrition of software developers and business personnel over the lifespan of long-lived enterprise systems leads to an erosion of tacit knowledge about idiosyncratic design decisions and the associated technical debt taken by teams. Hence, clear visibility of technical debt embedded in a firm’s enterprise system can be challenging to achieve.

Conversely, when a client firm has greater visibility of its technical debt, its team members are likely to have a better understanding of the system complexities and of the potential ripple efects that may result when vendor teams attempt remediation of the technical debt [71]. As a result, such client teams should be willing to reduce knowledge asymmetries with vendor teams and to coordinate more closely for diagnosis and problem solving. In contrast, when client teams are ignorant about the level of technical debt in their enterprise systems, joint eforts for diagnosis and problem solving are unlikely, due to the greater likelihood of knowledge asymmetry between the client and vendor teams. Thus, we predict that technical debt visibility would be positively associated with the extent of engagement in control balancing activities, which is our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): All else equal, the greater the visibility of technical debt to a client, the greater the extent of a client’s engagement in control balancing activities.

## Service System Volatility

A service system is the fused environment of enterprise systems, business processes, and actors enabled by and acting through the enterprise systems [76, 85]. Higher levels of volatility in the service system may result from changes to the business processes, the corresponding changes to the business logic embedded in the enterprise systems, other end user-driven adaptations to use cases, and/or technological changes. When a firm’s service system volatility is high it creates an uncertain environment for coordinated decision-making among stakeholders regarding long-term system design [1]. As a result, the design space for resolving technical-debt related problems becomes more expansive and unstable in a volatile environment. Unstable design specifications often lead to excessive rework [23], which could prohibitively increase the coordination costs for clients. In turn, those factors increase the risks of noncompliance with contractually mandated service levels for vendors, which would contribute to an erosion of trust and commitment in the outsourcing engagements [27]. Also, in an outsourcing context, higher levels of environmental volatility have been known to exacerbate other transaction hazards, including inefective knowledge transfer, which further mitigates the chances of close coordination between client and vendor teams [81, 82]. Based on these arguments we posit that service system volatility would be negatively associated with the engagement in control balancing activities in technical debt remediation projects. Thus, our second hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): All else equal, the greater the volatility of the service system, the lower the extent of a client’s engagement in control balancing activities.

## Maturity of Distributed Work Processes

Work dispersion in software teams refers to the distribution of project tasks to team members situated in development sites separated by geographical distance and time zones. Such distributed software teams are known to face a myriad of challenges resulting from such factors as unbalanced task configurations, status diferences, and imbalance in power structures and experience levels of team members [20, 54, 64, 68]. Time zone diferences between software teams have been reported to create coordination problems that are negatively associated with team performances [20]. Furthermore, asynchronous coordination due to time zone diferences has been known to result in increased levels of conflicts and rework [35].

To tackle these challenges related to work dispersion, firms have adopted specialized process mechanisms and tools for distributed work that go beyond software development methodologies typically found in co-located settings [68]. Those processes attempt to reduce information asymmetry and improve common ground, familiarity, and trust between distributed software teams [6, 69]. Adoption of such work processes specially designed for distributed teams would help to satisfy the technical, temporal, and process coordination needs of distributed teams [19]. As a result, distributed software teams achieve perceived proximity relationships and dependency awareness, which help improve the efectiveness of the conveyance of project information between the distributed team members and converge towards a shared meaning of project goals and plans [21].

We expect client firms who encounter higher levels of work dispersion in their environment to be particularly sensitive to the communication and coordination needs of boundary spanning activities, and to be more willing to engage in a coordinated style of control for projects with vendor teams. The process infrastructures put in place to handle internal work dispersion and coordinating unstructured tasks at client firms are likely to benefit from enactment of closer coordination with vendor teams. Hence, we expect client firms who have implemented high maturity processes for addressing distributed work to be relatively better prepared to handle project activities that span client-vendor boundaries and, therefore, be more likely to engage in control balancing activities with vendor teams. We hypothesize this expectation as following:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): All else equal, the higher the maturity of distributed work processes at a client, the greater the extent of the client’s engagement in control balancing activities.

## Technical Debt Remediation

Resolving technical debt involves complex and unstructured tasks that require an indepth knowledge of both the business context and the technological design aspects of enterprise systems. To plan and execute such a complex activity, a careful examination of the costs and benefits of making modular and architectural adjustments to the service systems is necessary [71, 87]. While explicit codification of the knowledge necessary to perform complex and unstructured tasks has been found to be beneficial in problem solving activities [62], availability of such codified knowledge in the context of long-lived enterprise systems maintenance has been limited due to the way incremental changes are enacted on these long-lived systems [71, 84]. Thus, projects that focus on remediation of technical debt rely on tacit knowledge of both technical and business actors in the service systems, which often necessitates coordination of expertise through reciprocal interactions among expert groups [49]. Furthermore, modular and architectural changes to service systems undertaken to remediate technical debt are known to cause ripple efects that may lead to a variety of unplanned outcomes including system disruptions and business outages [71]. Thus, in projects that focus on remediating technical debt, stakeholders need to go beyond pre-planned tasks and service level considerations, and they must operate in a high trust environment for achieving optimal coordination when unplanned incidents occur.

In an outsourcing context, engagements between client and vendor teams are governed through contractual agreements. While there is considerable variance in the control type, degree, and styles outlined in IT outsourcing contracts [2, 10, 11, 77], a majority of the contracts specify stringent procedures for enforcing outcome controls through service level agreements, and client firms often begin outsourcing engagements with a tight degree of control on the various services expected from the external vendor teams [28, 51]. However, in complex projects, such as those involving remediation of technical debt, both client and vendor teams learn as the project evolves, and engagement provisions that permit contingency-dependent adjustments to the ex ante agreed upon procedures would be expected to be desirable [55, 79]. Studies have reported that relational factors (such as relational flexibility and social embeddedness) in the outsourcing relationships between clients and vendors teams facilitate such desirable contingency-based adjustments, which deliver performance benefits and higher levels of trust and commitment [7, 30, 55].

Building on the aforementioned findings, two desirable attributes emerge for controlling technical debt remediation projects: (1) provisions for learning-induced adjustments throughout the lifecycle of the projects, and (2) accommodation of relational flexibility in the client-vendor engagements. Adoption of control configurations that are dynamic and that can be altered according to the contingencies that arise facilitate a “balance” between formal and informal controls [8, 46]. Furthermore, as Gregory et al. [32] have shown, such a balancing act that moves a client-vendor engagement from the purview of authoritative control configurations toward coordinated- and trust-based control configurations would improve shared understanding between the client and teams. Such a shared understanding can be expected to aid the conduct of performance-enhancing boundary spanning activities that help achieve better coordination and efective knowledge transfer between the teams [48, 54, 66]. Based on these arguments, we posit in our final hypothesis that control balancing activities would enhance the performance of technical remediation projects:

Hypothesis 4 (H4): All else equal, the greater a client’s engagement in control balancing activities, the greater the remediation of technical debt embedded in the client’s enterprise system.

## Data Collection

We collaborated with a multinational software services vendor firm and its clients to gather data on software projects whose focus was on remediating technical debt. The vendor is a leading global IT consulting and business process services firm with a global workforce of greater than 150,000 employees and an annual sales revenue of over 5 billion USD in 2018. The vendor firm agreed to share data for our study, and we specifically worked with the firm’s business unit that focused on maintenance of a leading COTS enterprise resource planning system. Under the purview of a non-disclosure agreement, the vendor firm also facilitated our collaboration with the business units of the client firms who had outsourced their systems for maintenance. After securing agreements with the client and vendor project personnel, we were provided access to the archived project data repositories of 1,824 project engagements pertaining to 424 diferent US-based client units that had been completed by

December 2017. The clients were distributed across 11 of the 12 industries in the Fama-French industry classification.<sup>2</sup> Since the vendor firm followed high-maturity software processes that complied with the CMMI<sup>®</sup> Level-5 standards,<sup>3</sup> and since the archival data had been audited several times by the internal controllers at both the vendor and client firms, we can place high confidence in the integrity of the data we had collected for our study. We augmented this archival project data with industry-level data from Compustat and our own survey-based data, as we further describe in the following sections.

Figure 2 presents an overview of the steps involved in the technical debt remediation projects. As shown in the figure, the process begins at $\mathbf { t } _ { 1 }$ with the client’s Request-For-Proposals (RFP) and ends at $\mathrm { t } _ { 6 }$ with project closure. The vendor selection process is completed at $\mathbf { t } _ { 2 } ,$ and at $\mathbf { t } _ { 3 }$ when the contract terms are initially drafted, the scope of the project is often uncertain. To address this, a scoping activity is undertaken at $\mathrm { t } _ { 4 }$ that helps the explicit identification of technical debt embedded in the system and the finalization of contract terms at $\mathbf { t } _ { 5 } .$ We assess the client’s initial visibility of technical debt based on this scoping activity; the scope of the project may change if the vendor unearths new technical debt as the project progresses (between $\mathrm { t } _ { 5 }$ and $\mathbf { t } _ { 6 } )$ . If such a scope change occurs, we accordingly update the client’s visibility of technical debt. Control balancing activities are also observed between $\mathrm { t } _ { 5 }$ and $\mathbf { t } _ { 6 } .$ Finally, at $\mathrm { t } _ { 6 }$ during project closure, we assess the performance of technical debt remediation by measuring the percentage of identified technical debt included in the project scope that was successfully resolved.

We gathered data for our analysis in three diferent steps. In the first step, we collated data from the project metrics databases at the vendor. Similar to the approach outlined in recent research [72], the vendor firm utilized an integrated process infrastructure that combined the steps needed for software project management and technical debt management for its engagements with the client firm. Thus, the archival data that we collected from the project metrics databases for the study was suitable to derive the technical debt and software project-related variables featured in our conceptual model. However, those metrics from the project teams did not include contract-related information, and in our second step we collected data related to the outsourcing engagement, including the contract’s price, flexibility provisions, and duration. In the third and final step, we gathered detailed information related to the control balancing activities conducted in the projects using a survey of project management and service delivery personnel at the vendor and client firms. Since we could not individually observe (i.e., in the sense of in situ or ethnographic observation) the 1,824 projects, the survey played an important role in gathering data on control balancing activities.

Figure 2. Project timeline.

For the survey on control balancing we utilized the framework developed by Gregory et al. [32] to identify the specific control balancing activities that are undertaken by software project teams. In this framework, project control configurations are measured using three dimensions, control type (procedural, hybrid, and social), control style (unilateral, bilateral), and control degree (unilateral, relaxed). Control balancing refers to the adjustments made to the project control configurations throughout the lifecycle of projects. Such adjustments, for example, may transform a project’s overall control regime from an authoritative control configuration (procedural, tight, unilateral) to a coordinated (hybrid, tight, bilateral) or a trust-based control configuration (social, relaxed, and bilateral).

All projects in our sample began with an initial control configuration that has been characterized as authoritative control in the literature $( \mathfrak { t } _ { 5 }$ in Figure 2). This is, at least in part, due to the nature of the fixed price contracting processes prevalent in the industry. We also speculate that the CMMI level-5 environment at the vendor—a high process maturity and plan-based environment—may have contributed to this initial choice of control configuration. As a project progresses, the control configuration adopted by the project may change.<sup>4</sup> We were able to trace such control configuration changes using the trail of changes in project process templates and in task-based cost accounting processes followed by the projects, as we explain next.

For each project in our sample, we sought input from at least four project management and service delivery personnel, split evenly between the vendor and client firms. After confirming whether the project involved any control balancing activities, the respondents were asked to report specific task identification codes reported in the project metrics database that can help track the activities that pertained to actions taken on project control configurations. Then, we utilized the reported task identification codes to trace the efort spent on control balancing activities conducted in the project. Finally, drawing inspiration from the literature on moves [e.g., 74, 87] and utilizing the time stamps on the project task identification codes, we clustered the discrete actions taken on project control configurations in each of the projects for deriving a count measure of the control balancing moves enacted in the projects.

For example, as illustrated in Figure OS2 in the online supplement, project personnel reported “yes” for the binary response in our survey on control balancing and then proceeded to report task identification codes related to the seven activities: four for the first configuration change and three for the second configuration change. We matched these task identification codes with the project timesheet database to derive the total personhours spent on the control balancing activities. Then, based on the logged timeline of the task executions, we grouped them into two distinct Control Balancing Moves $( C B M _ { I }$ and CBM<sub>2</sub>).

When the survey respondents reported conflicting information, or when we could not properly identify the reported task identification codes in the project databases, we individually followed up with the survey respondents to resolve the issues. Thus, we were able to verify the self-reported information by mapping them with the audited information in the project metrics databases. Overall, after triangulating the survey and project task accounting data, we derived four measures of control balancing: (1) a binary measure (CBLB) that indicates the presence or absence of control balancing in a project, (2) a count or “moves” measure (CBLM) based on our grouping of the task codes based on their execution timelines,<sup>6</sup> (3) an efort measure (CBLE) derived using the task codes reported by project personnel, which indicates the extent of project efort spent on control balancing activities, and (4) a composite self-reported measure (CBLS) that captures the extent of changes in a project’s control configurations (measured using the control type, control style, and control degree dimensions).

## Analysis and Results

## Empirical Specification and Instruments

We conceptualized engaging in control balancing as a choice influenced by technical debt visibility, service system volatility, and the maturity of distributed work practices in projects, and, in turn, control balancing influences the level of technical debt remediation realized in the projects. While examining the efects of these hypothesized variables, we controlled for a host of project-, system-, contract-, and industry-level factors as summarized in Figure 1. Accordingly, to empirically assess the relationships in our conceptualization, we specified the following system of equations shown in Equation (1) and Equation (2):

$$
\begin{array}{r l} C B L = & \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} (T D V) + \beta_ {2} (S S V) + \beta_ {3} (M D W) + \beta_ {4} (H H I) + \beta_ {5} (R S K) + \beta_ {6} (P S Z) \\ & + \beta_ {7} (A M T) + \beta_ {8} (E X P) + \beta_ {9} (E S L) + \beta_ {1 0} (C P R) + \beta_ {1 1} (C F L) + \beta_ {1 2} (C D N) + \varepsilon \end{array}\tag{Eq1}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} T D R = & \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} (C B L) + \gamma_ {2} (T D V) + \gamma_ {3} (S S V) + \gamma_ {4} (M D W) + \gamma_ {5} (P S Z) + \gamma_ {6} (A M T) \\ & + \gamma_ {7} (E X P) + \gamma_ {8} (E S L) + \gamma_ {9} (C P R) + \gamma_ {1 0} (C F L) + \gamma_ {1 1} (C D N) + \delta \end{array}\tag{Eq2}
$$

To empirically address the endogenous choice of control balancing (CBL) in the system of equations, we adopted the instrumental variable approach [4, 75]. Similar to prior research [30], we utilized parameters related to the client, industry competition (HHI) and the extent of financial risks in the project (RSK), as instruments.

Prior research has reported that a firm’s competitive environment influences its investments in both enterprise systems and IT outsourcing [58], and that firms operating in more fragmented technology markets pursue intellectual property protection in a more aggressive manner than those operating in more concentrated markets [88]. In outsourcing engagements clients are known to be wary of potential leakage of intellectual property including idiosyncratic business logic and business processes enacted through their customized service systems [10, 11]. While conducting technical debt remediating activities vendor teams are likely to learn more about idiosyncratic customizations of business rules embedded in the client service systems as a result of working closely with client teams. Thus, for client teams, the risk of inadvertently releasing unique changes that they enacted on commercially available enterprise systems to the vendor teams is particularly high. Prior research has observed such knowledge spillovers among end-users at diferent firms through external service providers [36]. Therefore, client firms interested in mitigating the risks of intellectual property leakage are likely to adopt an authoritative control approach, and they would be less willing to reconfigure project controls to facilitate coordinated or trust-based activities with vendor teams. Such reluctance to engage in control balancing is more likely to be observed in client industries where competition is intense (i.e., the market is fragmented across multiple firms) and when financial risks in the projects are high. Higher levels of competition and financial risks would prompt client firms to more actively guard their intellectual property, and activities that could potentially leak valuable intellectual property would be curtailed. Thus, when industry competition and financial risks are high, we expect client firms to be less inclined to engage in control balancing with vendors in order to reduce the chances of spillover of intellectual property embedded in their service systems to the competitive market through the external vendor.

As previously explained, we posit that HHI and RSK influence CBL directly as client firms wary of intellectual property leakage would be less likely to engage in control balancing activities. However, there is no strong theoretical basis to expect a systematic direct relationship between the client-level parameters, HHI and RSK, on the performance of the contractually mandated vendor teams in remediating technical debt. That is, we expect HHI and RSK to impact technical debt remediation primarily through the channel of CBL. In our sample, the correlation between HHI and CBLE is negative (-0.066) with a p-value of 0.004, but the correlation between HHI and TDR is not significant at usual levels (0.028; p-value = 0.220). Similarly, the correlation between RSK and CBLE is significant (-0.076; p-value = 0.001), but the correlation between RSK and TDR is not strong (-0.019, p-value = 0.405). Utilizing HHI and RSK as instruments, we estimated Equations (1) and (2) using the twostage least squares (2SLS) procedure, with the first stage results corresponding to Equation (1) serving to test H1-H3, and the second stage results corresponding to Equation (2) serving to test H4. ## Technical Debt Remediation

Turning to the estimation results of Equation (2) shown in Table 4, we see that the coeficient of CBL is negative (-0.572) with $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ . This result indicates that a percentage increase in project efort expenditures toward control balancing activities decreases the amount of technical debt remediation by about 0.6 percent. This revealed negative relationship between CBL and TDR is counter to our expectations posited in H4. Prior research has not specifically explored the relationship between CBL and TDR¸ Hence, to uncover the potential reasons for the lack of support for our hypothesized positive relationship between CBL and TDR, we held follow-up consultations with practitioners at our research site and conducted post-hoc analysis.

## Post-hoc Analysis

For getting feedback on our results from project personnel at the vendor and client firms, we coordinated with two project managers and one service delivery manager at the vendor firm who volunteered to hold internal focus group sessions with project personnel from client firms. A total of 98 stakeholders from 31 client units participated in three focus group meetings that each lasted for about an hour. In these meetings we explained our analysis and presented results, answered practitioner questions, and then posed the following question for group discussion: why do we see a negative relationship between CBL and TDR?

A majority of the discussion points in the focus group meetings centered on the themes of system replacement and potential subsequent engagements with the vendor team for long-term migration of the system to emerging technological platforms. Although we had controlled for expected system life (ESL) in our models, the experts who participated in the focus group discussions speculated that other unobserved factors related to the identification of a long-term migration plan for the technical debt-laden enterprise systems could be a key contingency impacting the relationship between CBL and TDR.

To explore this possibility, we did a follow-up survey with the project teams in our original sample (not the personnel in the focus group) with the goal of identifying whether their technical debt remediation activities were conducted in the presence of a long-term system migration plan in place. The results of the survey indicated that 596 projects out of the total 1,824 projects in our sample had discussed a long-term migration plan with stakeholders during technical debt remediation activities, even though system migration was not explicitly included in the list of contractually mandated tasks for any of the 1,824 projects. Thus, based on practitioner’s feedback, we speculate that, in some of the projects, personnel involved in control balancing activities may have acted as “knowledge brokers”, enacting both explorative and exploitative knowledge sharing across the client and vendor teams [37, 66]. The identification of a migration plan during control balancing activities is a project-level observable event that could serve as a proxy for whether knowledge sharing ambidexterity was present during control balancing.

To incorporate the variance in the identification of migration plans and the underlying knowledge sharing ambidexterity across projects in our analysis, we split the sample (596 and 1,228) and once again estimated our models for each of the samples. To meet the identification needs, such a split sample analysis is more conducive than a moderation analysis approach because of the presence of an endogenous regressor in our model [CBL in Equation (2)], which is identified with two instruments, namely, HHI and RSK [4].

Detailed regression results of the split sample analysis are presented in Tables S5 and S6 of the Online Supplement for the subsamples with and without system migration plans, respectively. A relevant empirical result from this analysis is that the sign of the coeficient on control balancing (CBL) varies between the two samples. With the identification of system migration plans, control balancing is estimated to positively influence remediation of technical debt $( \gamma _ { 1 } = 0 . 3 1 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which does align with our original prediction in H4. In contrast, when system migration plans were not identified, control balancing is estimated to negatively influence remediation of technical debt $( \gamma _ { 1 } = - 0 . 4 5 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . These post-hoc results reveal an important contingency: control balancing activities resulted in higher levels of technical debt remediation only when personnel involved in those activities acted as knowledge brokers and formulated long-term platform migration plans for their technical debt-laden systems.

## Discussion

In order to examine the factors that influence the remediation of technical debt when firms outsource the maintenance of their enterprise systems to external service vendors we brought together three distinct literatures: (1) Information Systems Development (ISD) controls, (2) IT outsourcing, and (3) Technical Debt. In this section, we discuss the implications of our findings for each of those research literatures, acknowledge the most likely limitations of the study, and identify opportunities for future research.

## Implications for Research on ISD Controls

We utilized a three dimensional and configurational perspective of controls in software development proposed by Gregory et al. [32], which considers both the control degree and the control style dimensions in addition to the control type dimension that was examined in prior empirical studies. As shown in Figure 3, the three dimensions present a rich design space for examining control balancing activities and facilitating the observation of diferent states of controls in a software project at various junctures of the project’s lifecycle. In this study’s empirical context, all the projects started with procedural controls that were unilaterally evoked by client firms who sought a tight degree of control (point “A” in Figure 3). Some client teams enacted control balancing moves that shifted the state of controls within their projects to a hybrid type of control, with more coordination with vendor teams and a more relaxed degree of control (point “B” in Figure 3). Results showed that technical debt visibility and the maturity of distributed work processes were positively associated with the chance of enacting the control balancing moves and with their intensity. However, service system volatility and client industry competition were negatively associated with both of these outcomes. Our analysis also showed that a project team’s control balancing move may not always result in desired performance enhancements, demonstrating the need to develop a contingent view of the performance benefits of control balancing in IT outsourcing engagements.

While this study can further pave the way for quantitative examinations of control balancing activities in software projects, we note that the control balancing moves that we examined (A,B in Figure 3) do not cover the entire range of the design space shown in Figure 3. The antecedents and consequences of other types of control balancing moves that are located in the unexplored regions of the design space may be diferent, and they would benefit from being systematically examined in future research. To address this, we believe that it would be fruitful to map the various patterns of control balancing moves enacted in a diverse set of IT outsourcing engagements with their corresponding antecedents and business performance outcomes. Such a schema depicting the patterns of moves has been utilized in prior research for developing both theoretical propositions and practitioneroriented guidelines [e.g., 74, 87]. Furthermore, building on the empirical approach presented in this study, an examination of the presence of trade-ofs and/or equifinality among the diferent IS control configurations within the design space illustrated in Figure 3 would help address the managerial challenges related to the selection and execution of optimal control configurations for specific business scenarios. For example, an analysis illustrating the trade-ofs between flexibility (the ability to accommodate requirement changes) and eficiency (e.g., reduction in coordination costs) between diferent control configurations would help managers to better understand the costs and benefits associated with their preferred control configurations and help them make informed choices.

## Implications for Research on IT Outsourcing

The study presents two key implications for research on IT outsourcing. First, our analysis revealed that technical debt plays an important role in influencing the extent to which control balancing — a manifestation of relational flexibility — occurs in an outsourcing engagement. Building on this finding, future research can examine how visibility of technical debt impacts other important contract-related choices and their performance consequences in an outsourcing engagement, including the extent of flexibility provisions and the specificity of service levels agreements. Utilizing a transaction cost perspective, prior research has demonstrated the presence of trade-ofs between costs related to ex ante planning and ex post problem resolutions in IT outsourcing engagements [e.g., 60]. Examining how technical debt impacts such trade-ofs would help managers to better understand the performance implications of system design factors, and to consider them carefully while making decisions related to the governance of IT outsourcing engagements.

Second, our results reveal that the performance benefits of engaging in control balancing are contingent on other factors, such as knowledge brokering and exploration of opportunities that stem from technological evolution. Thus, our results add to the growing set of evidence in the IT outsourcing literature that points towards a contingent view of the benefits of relational flexibility in outsourced software projects [30]. While this study examined the performance consequences related to technical debt remediation, we did not have the opportunity to study the efects of control balancing on other issues, such as vendor profitability, and contract renewal. Further research is needed to systematically examine if control balancing leads to symmetric benefits for both clients and vendors, and if the eficacy of control balancing moves are contingent on other dimensions of relational governance in an outsourcing engagement.

## Implications for Research on Technical Debt Management and Project Management

Remediating technical debt is an expensive and risky endeavor [71, 87]. While there is a trend in the industry to outsource the remediation of technical debt in long-lived enterprise systems to specialized vendors [56], this study highlights the challenges in achieving desired performance outcomes in such engagements. Our results indicate that improving the visibility of technical debt contributes to reducing the knowledge asymmetry between clients and vendors, and it aids the remediation of technical debt embedded in enterprise systems. Our prescription for firms is to institute software project management processes that improve the visibility of technical debt and help codify the tacit knowledge related to technical debt. As our analysis showed, selecting an appropriate configuration of project control and executing control balancing moves in alignment with the project environment is also an important factor in improving the remediation of technical debt. However, established software project management practice standards and frameworks, such as the PMBOK<sup>®</sup> family of guides and standards,<sup>7</sup> do not currently directly address either the steps required to enhance technical debt visibility or the management of project control configurations [72]. Thus, we see a need for developing specific practice guides on both technical debt management and control balancing, as well as on integrating them with established project management frameworks.

## Conclusion

By bringing together the distinct literature streams on IS project controls, IT outsourcing, and technical debt, this study adds to the emerging perspectives on control of inter-firm engagements and it paves the way for new research inquiries into the remediation of technical debt in long-lived enterprise systems. By providing empirical evidence for the heterogeneous performance impacts of control balancing we contribute to the further development of a contingent view of the benefits of relational flexibility in IT outsourcing engagements. We hope that our work would spur further research into the transaction hazards that stem from technical debt embedded in enterprise systems, the potential equifinality of project control configurations, and the contingencies that influence the launching of diferent types of control balancing moves and their performance consequences in IT outsourcing engagements.
