# How Information and Communication Technology Shapes the Influence of Culture on Innovation: A Country-level Analysis

Terence J.V. Saldanha<sup>a</sup>, Babu John-Mariadoss<sup>b</sup>, Michelle Xiao Wu<sup>c</sup>, and Sunil Mithas<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX, USA; <sup>c</sup>MIT Institute for Data, Systems, and Society (IDSS), Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA, USA; <sup>d</sup>Muma College of Business, University of South Florida, Tampa, FL, USA

## ABSTRACT

Advances in Information and Communication Technology (ICT) and the distributed nature of innovation in a culturally heterogeneous world raise an important question about how ICT and culture jointly influence innovation. We argue that ICT shapes the influence of culture dimensions on national innovation in nuanced ways, depending on the nature of the dimensions. In particular, cultural dimensions that pertain to norms, formality, and structure (e.g., power distance and uncertainty avoidance) are often considered detrimental to innovation, whereas culture dimensions that pertain to the pursuit of goals and achievement (e.g., individualism, masculinity, and long-term orientation) are often considered conducive for innovation. We hypothesize that ICT amplifies the positive efects of individualism, masculinity, and long-term orientation on national innovation—and, attenuates the negative efects of power distance and uncertainty avoidance on national innovation. Our analyses of data on 66 countries from 2007 to 2015 broadly support our hypotheses about ICT amplifying the positive efects of individualism and long-term orientation, as well as our hypotheses about ICT attenuating the negative efects of power distance and uncertainty avoidance on national innovation. The findings suggest that ICT amplifies the efects of culture dimensions related to the pursuit of goals and helps overcome barriers to innovation that arise from a culture’s focus on norms, formality, and structure. In turn, these findings can help managers and policy makers to make judicious decisions regarding ICT investments to transcend or leverage the country’s cultural characteristics for innovation.

National innovation; patents; ICT; national culture; country-level study; World Values Survey; GLOBE; cultural dimensions; information technology investment; innovation

## Introduction

Since innovation is a key driver of a country’s economic prosperity, the business press and academic literature continually seek to identify reasons for variation in innovation across countries.<sup>1</sup> One such reason that has received significant attention is culture. For example, the business press attributes Sweden’s innovativeness to its open, transparent, individualist, and low power distant culture [6]. Such cultural characteristics are often viewed as reasons for Sweden’s ability to compete with countries such as the United States or South Korea on innovation. As another example, Israel’s open environment and low power distance contribute to its innovation [15]. The high level of innovation in the United States is attributed largely to its willingness to take risks [72]. Such observations from the business press about culture’s role in innovation also mirror observations from academic literature [e.g., 38] that recognize the influence of national culture on innovation via patterns of behavior influenced by society and the cultural environment [60]. In particular, specific dimensions of culture either hinder or encourage innovation. For example, power distance discourages innovation by promoting hierarchy, which impedes communication and collaboration among people of various social strata [37, 57]. Conversely, individualism encourages innovation since people in individualist cultures tend to have more leeway to independently pursue innovative ideas [60].<sup>2</sup>

Some argue that in addition to culture, information and communication technology (ICT) has a positive and direct efect on innovation [e.g., 39]. Information and Communication Technology (ICT), such as the internet and mobile devices, has revolutionized how people connect and collaborate. ICT infrastructure (e.g., wireless technology) boosts collaboration and communication, suggesting the importance of a nation’s ICT infrastructure for its innovation outcomes. Notably, culture and ICT have often been studied separately; rarely has prior research considered the joint influence of ICT and culture on national innovation.

In this study, we examine the influence of ICT on innovation beyond a direct efect and consider how ICT shapes the efect of culture on innovation. Our motivation to study moderating efects of ICT comes from technological advances that influence how people interact, thereby enabling collaboration among individuals and entities in a country [3]. We build on the premise that innovation requires the discovery of new knowledge and the movement of knowledge to the point of use, processes largely driven by the level of ICT in a country [10, 13, 32]. We posit that due to ICT’s impact on collaboration and communication, ICT alters culture’s influence on innovation fundamentally. Thus, we argue that ICT moderates the efect of culture on innovation via mechanisms such as coordination, collaboration, and knowledge sharing.

We pose the following research question: How does a nation’s ICT shape the efect of dimensions of national culture (i.e., power distance, individualism, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity, and long-term orientation) on national innovation? We theorize that ICT shapes the efects of cultural dimensions on innovation in nuanced ways by magnifying the efects of some while attenuating the efects of others. Specifically, we argue that the moderating efect of ICT difers based on whether the cultural dimensions are defined in terms of their focus on norms, formality, and structure or whether they are defined in terms of characteristics regarding the pursuit of goals and achievement.

On the one hand, for culture dimensions that describe how a society responds to norms, formality, or structure (e.g., power distance and uncertainty avoidance), ICT helps overcome hurdles to innovation created by cultural responses to norms, formality, and structure. Thus, we theorize that ICT attenuates the negative efects of power distance and uncertainty avoidance on innovation. For example, ICT provides innovators with efective means to collaborate and transfer codified knowledge, thus diminishing the negative efect of power distance on innovation. Here, power distance represents an adherence to formality and an acknowledgement of power and authority in society. Similarly, since ICT supports the pursuit of innovation despite the uncertainty caused by a limited ability to predict innovation outcomes, ICT can help attenuate the negative efect of uncertainty avoidance on innovation.

On the other hand, in contexts where the cultural dimension is a response to how societies view the pursuit of goals and achievement, ICT enhances the capacity for innovation-related achievements. More specifically, masculinity reflects an emphasis on performance and goal-driven advancement [37]. Long-term-oriented cultures, meanwhile, emphasize future or long-term goals [26], and individualist cultures place greater emphasis on individual goals than group goals [67]. Thus, we theorize that ICT amplifies the positive efects of individualism, masculinity, and long-term orientation on innovation. For instance, ICT gives innovators in individualist cultures more capacity to develop their ideas freely and fills gaps in collaboration to support idea execution, thereby amplifying the efect of individualism on innovation.

We test our hypotheses using a nine-year longitudinal dataset of 66 countries from 2007 to 2015 by leveraging national culture measures from three separate datasets (World Values Survey, Hofstede, and GLOBE). We find that ICT attenuates the efects of power distance and uncertainty on innovation and amplifies the efects of individualism and long-term orientation on innovation. We find no impact of ICT on the efect of masculinity on innovation. Our results are robust to endogeneity and other tests. The study contributes to innovation literature by theorizing and empirically showing how ICT shapes culture’s efects on innovation. Our results suggest that ICT helps overcome barriers to innovation that arise from a culture’s focus on norms, formality, and structure, while amplifying the efects of cultural dimensions that pertain to the pursuit of goals. We highlight the nuanced interplay of ICT and culture on innovation to the extent that ICT does not influence efects of all cultural dimensions on innovation in a uniform way. For practice and policy, our study provides new insights into the heterogeneous efects of ICT on the influence of diferent cultural dimensions on innovation. Our results suggest that policy makers need to support ICT investments to leverage or mitigate the efects of cultural characteristics driving innovation.

## Theoretical Development and Hypotheses

## Background and Prior Literature

We draw on two streams of literature that focus on the efect of culture and ICT on innovation, and discuss them in turn.

The first stream relates to culture’s influence on innovation. Culture is the homogeneity of characteristics that diferentiates one human group from another [62] and describes the characteristic profile of a society with respect to its norms, values, and institutions [26]. More formally, culture is “the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one human group from another [and is an] aggregate of common characteristics that influence a human group’s response to its environment” [26, p. 25]. Over time, culture becomes institutionalized through norms, authoritative structures, and standard operating procedures. Some aspects of culture such as authority and hierarchical structures inhibit resource use without prior approval [23]. Often viewed at the national level, culture captures an aggregate of common individually valued priorities in a society [62].

Prior studies recognize the efects of specific dimensions of culture on innovation [37, 46, 57, 60] such as individualism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity, and long-term orientation. Individualism refers to “a loosely knit social framework in which people are supposed to take care of themselves and of their immediate families only” [26, p. 45]; while collectivism represents a preference for a tightly knit framework in society, in which individuals see themselves as members of a particular group. Individualism facilitates innovation since people in individualist cultures are more likely to independently originate and pursue innovative ideas [60, 74]. Individualism is associated with autonomy, independence, personal vision, and freedom to take action—all crucial for innovation [46, 57]. Power distance is “the extent to which a society accepts the fact that power in institutions and organizations is distributed unequally” [26, p. 45]. Power distance discourages innovation because it hinders vertical communications and promotes hierarchy, the centralization of power, control over subordinates, and resistance to change, all of which are crucial determinants of innovation [57]. People in cultures low in power distance often challenge each other, displaying characteristics similar to the organic structures conducive to innovation [37]. Uncertainty avoidance is the extent to which members of a culture feel threatened by uncertain or unknown situations and the lack of structure [26]. Uncertainty avoidance impedes innovation since innovation by nature is associated with risk and change and its outcomes are uncertain [57]. Masculinity is characterized by a proclivity for competition, assertiveness, and craving for rewards [26]. Cultures high in masculinity are more inclined to innovation since such cultures place more emphasis on rewards, recognition, and achievement than feminine cultures do [57]. Long-term-oriented cultures foster virtues focused on future rewards [26]. Cultures high in long-term orientation are more innovative because they are oriented toward future results and are more receptive to change compared to short-term oriented cultures, which focus more on the past and respect for traditions [64].

The second stream of literature pertains to the influence of ICT on innovation [33, 35, 36, 39, 54]. ICT leads to innovation via improved knowledge capabilities, collaboration, crossfunctional integration, information processing, and coordination [12, 51]. ICT also enhances capabilities to execute innovation processes, and helps leverage external sources of knowledge for innovation [20, 53, 63].

Despite the aforementioned bodies of work, we know of no study that examines how ICT influences the efect of culture on national innovation. Accordingly, this study helps to fill this research gap.

## Attenuating Efects of ICT

Figure 1 shows our research model, and Table 1 summarizes the key arguments underlying our hypotheses. First, we contend that ICT attenuates the negative efect of culture when barriers to innovation arise from a culture’s response to norms, formality, and structure. We begin by explicating why power distance and uncertainty avoidance pertain to norms, formality, and structure. Power distance represents an adherence to formality and norms because it reflects an acknowledgement of the degree of power and authority in society [26, 37]. Individuals in a country with high power distance are more likely to respect hierarchy in society compared to individuals in a country with low power distance. For example, in China, which is a high power distance country, “inequalities amongst people are acceptable. The subordinate-superior relationship tends to be polarized and there is no defense against power abuse by superiors. Individuals are influenced by formal authority and sanctions . . . People should not have aspirations beyond their rank” [29]. In contrast, western societies generally have low power distance. Power distance reflects how people view power relationships (i.e., superior/subordinate relationships), including the degree to which people not in power accept that power is distributed unequally. Power-distant societies have low levels of horizontal communications and high rigidity, both of which reduce creative thinking and idea generation [57]. Thus, power distance impedes innovation by promoting hierarchy, thereby deterring communication and collaboration between people with diferent roles in society [37, 57].

Uncertainty avoidance reflects a culture’s response to the lack of structure such that cultures with a high level of uncertainty avoidance feel threatened by uncertain situations and the lack of structure; in turn, people in such cultures require structure and order with clear norms and guidelines [26]. Israel, for example, has high uncertainty avoidance, and there is an emotional need there for norms and precision. China, meanwhile, has low uncertainty avoidance, consistent with the argument that Chinese people are generally comfortable with ambiguity [29]. Cultures with high uncertainty avoidance are characterized by a slow rate of change or an outright resistance to change. People in such cultures show a greater propensity to maintain the status quo when confronted with uncertain or ambiguous situations that lack structure [67]. Since innovation is based on new ideas with uncertain execution outcomes, uncertainty avoidance presents a roadblock to innovation[57].

## How ICT Shapes the Efect of Power Distance on National Innovation

We posit that ICT attenuates the negative efect of power distance on innovation for two reasons. First, ICT helps innovators connect, enhances information access [45], and provides communication channels for efective collaboration [49]. For example, internetenabled platforms provide communication venues that make it possible for people to share and build on ideas to generate innovation [43, 73]. ICT facilitates the sharing of information and ideas and encourages innovators to share ideas openly and freely with superiors (or leaders) who possess greater power. This helps superiors (or leaders) in cultures with high power distance to overcome gaps in communication and collaboration created by hierarchies and, in turn, to make informed decisions when implementing innovative ideas. Thus, ICT mitigates the impeding efect of power distance on innovation by shrinking communication barriers among people at various power levels.

Second, power distance hinders innovation by making it harder for people to access innovation resources conveniently and quickly. ICT facilitates access to resources (e.g., funds, people, skills)

that promote idea execution [42], providing innovators with more freedom and the ability to execute ideas independently. For example, internet-enabled micro-funding reduces the need for approvals or resources from powerful individuals or formal institutions to execute ideas, thereby expediting idea execution. As another example, ICT-enabled platforms in China, a high-powerdistance country, generate innovation opportunities by providing microloans to users [31]. Thus, ICT helps innovators overcome procedural hurdles and gain access to resources.

In sum, because ICT helps connect innovators and facilitates access to resources, ICT lowers the barriers to innovation caused by power distance. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis H1A : Information and Communication Technology (ICT) attenuates the negative efect of power distance on national innovation, such that countries with higher ICT experience a weaker negative efect of power distance on innovation.

## How ICT Shapes the Efect of Uncertainty Avoidance on National Innovation

We contend that ICT attenuates the negative efect of uncertainty avoidance on innovation for three reasons. First, in the idea-generation stage of innovation, a significant hindrance for innovators is uncertainty regarding how the market may react to new ideas [41]. Thus, innovators in cultures with high uncertainty avoidance tend to avoid uncertain situations because they perceive uncertainty as a threat [27], leading to lower levels of innovative activity. By enabling the availability of quick feedback using ICT-enabled avenues (e.g., on internet-enabled forums), ICT can help innovators cope with uncertainty and work more eficiently in the face of uncertain information. Instead of guessing what might work, innovators can make adjustments in near real-time using ICT to get input from potential users [43]. Thus, innovators can get a better and earlier sense of potential market response to their ideas. By supporting innovators’ engagement in uncertain work environments, ICT can lessen the resistance to innovation that is a hallmark of cultures with high uncertainty avoidance. Thus, by reducing risk through quicker feedback from the potential market, ICT helps people cope with uncertainty in innovation, thereby mitigating the influence of uncertainty avoidance on innovation.

Second, by enhancing collaboration and coordination between innovators, ICT expedites the innovation process, thus making innovation development less risky. This encourages innovators in cultures with high uncertainty avoidance to increase their innovation activities. Specifically, ICT reduces risk in innovation and increases the likelihood of innovation success by enhancing communication and the co-development of ideas [42]. By reducing perceived risk through enhanced collaboration and coordination among innovators, ICT mitigates the negative influence of uncertainty avoidance on innovation.

Third, in the idea-execution stage of innovation, ICT facilitates dynamic improvements and continuous adjustments [40]. ICT provides opportunities for innovators to adapt idea execution based on feedback from users to better suit their ideas for the market. This allows dynamic reconfiguration and makes the pursuit of innovation more structured and less uncertain. Thus, ICT’s ability to facilitate dynamic improvements in idea execution reduces innovators’ perception of the risk involved, thereby encouraging innovation in cultures with high uncertainty avoidance.

Since ICT facilitates feedback on innovative ideas during idea generation, expedites the innovation process, and enables dynamic improvements in innovation execution, ICT lowers the perceived risk caused by a limited ability to predict innovation outcomes. In turn, this reduced perceived risk makes people in cultures with high uncertainty avoidance more likely to pursue innovation. Hence, we posit:

Hypothesis H1B : Information and Communication Technology (ICT) attenuates the negative efect of uncertainty avoidance on national innovation, such that countries with higher ICT experience a weaker negative efect of uncertainty avoidance on innovation.

## Amplifying Efects of ICT

We argue that ICT amplifies culture’s positive efects on innovation, which arise from dimensions related to the pursuit of goals. We begin by explaining how individualism, masculinity, and long-term orientation relate to the pursuit of goals. Individualist cultures place greater emphasis on individual goals than group goals and prefer to take a singular rather than group approach to goal achievement [67]. For example, the United States has a high score on individualism, which reflects the loosely-knit framework of American society, where people look after themselves and their immediate families only, relying minimally on authorities for support [29]. People in individualist cultures show a greater propensity to be self-suficient than to be an integral part of a social group [67]. Individualism facilitates innovation since people in individualist cultures have greater leeway and drive to independently pursue goals and innovative ideas [60, 74].

Masculinity highlights a society’s emphasis on performance and advancement toward goals [37]. In the United States, which has a high masculinity score, “behavior in school, work, and play are based on the shared values that people should strive to be the best they can be and that the winner takes all” [29]. Meanwhile, in Norway, which has a low masculinity score, “trying to be better than others is neither socially nor materially rewarded,” reflecting a less focus on goals [29]. Masculine cultures emphasize performance, advancement, and challenge while feminine cultures emphasize a friendly atmosphere, nurturing others, and equality [37]. Since innovation conveys rewards and prestige, masculine cultures are more innovative than feminine cultures [57].

Long-term-oriented cultures emphasize future or long-term goals and adaptation to new circumstances [26, 64]. In Belgium, for instance, a culture with high long-term orientation, people are willing to adapt traditions to changed conditions and have a strong propensity to plan for the future [29]. Long-term-oriented cultures emphasize dealing with change, persevering, and preparing for the future. Since innovation returns are generally achieved in the long term, such cultures are more likely to pursue innovation [64]. In contrast, shortterm-oriented cultures foster virtues that are focused on the past, present, and respect for tradition, creating an environment that impedes innovation [26].

## How ICT Shapes the Efect of Individualism on National Innovation

We argue that ICT amplifies the positive efect of individualism on innovation. First, in the idea-generation stage of innovation, ICT enables innovators to collect information and feedback on the feasibility of their ideas; this in turn enables them to improve their ideas independently for innovation [7, 8]. Thus, ICT helps innovators in individualist cultures independently generate and develop ideas [60]. Second, in the execution stage of innovation, innovators need to collaborate to pursue their ideas cohesively [46]. ICT enables innovators in individualist societies to find resourceful and efective collaborators beyond their close circle of contacts, thereby assisting with idea execution [43]. The ICT-enabled social networks, for example, facilitate the development of weak ties [34] such that innovators can communicate and collaborate with individuals beyond their close contacts. Ultimately, in individualist societies where collaboration is typically low [21], ICT fills the void by extending the network of potential collaborators for innovators. In other words, ICT shrinks the gap in collaboration in individualist cultures, strengthening individualism’s efect on innovation.

In sum, ICT gives innovators in individualist cultures more capacity to develop their ideas freely and fills gaps in collaboration to support idea execution. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis H2A : Information and Communication Technology (ICT) amplifies the positive efect of individualism on national innovation, such that countries with higher ICT experience a stronger positive efect of individualism on innovation.

## How ICT Shapes the Efect of Masculinity on National Innovation

We argue that ICT amplifies the positive efect of masculinity on innovation for three reasons. First, ICT increases critiques on ideas by enabling the aggregation of judgments from people with diverse opinions [8, 17]. Internet forums, for example, increase the scale, speed, and spread of idea critiques [48]. At the same time, this flood of feedback can overwhelm innovators [75]. Since assertiveness is valued in masculine societies, people in such societies are more likely to overcome critiques on their ideas, resulting in a more conducive environment for innovation. In contrast, a flood of critiques surfacing from ICT can overwhelm people in feminine cultures who display lower assertiveness, thereby producing an environment less conducive to innovation.

Second, ICT increases competition by making it easier for individuals to engage in innovation. Specifically, internet and digital platforms enable more people to develop innovative ideas [68]. Since masculine cultures emphasize ambition, challenge, and achievement [57], competition fostered by ICT makes innovators in masculine societies more motivated, creating a positive momentum for innovation and enhancing masculinity’s efect on innovation.

Third, ICT increases the size and accessibility of potential markets for innovations by connecting people faster and more easily [16]. Accordingly, ICT scales up rewards and accelerates the timeframe leading to rewards from innovations. For instance, internet platforms increase market accessibility for innovative ideas in a short time, enhancing the potential for rewards from innovation. Given masculine societies’ emphasis on material rewards and goals, ICT’s ability to increase rewards motivates individuals in masculine societies to innovate [57].

Altogether, ICT increases critiques and competition for ideas and scales up rewards from innovation. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis H2B : Information and Communication Technology (ICT) amplifies the positive efect of masculinity on national innovation, such that countries with higher ICT experience a stronger positive efect of masculinity on innovation.

## How ICT Shapes the Efect of Long-term Orientation on National Innovation

We argue that ICT amplifies the positive efect of long-term orientation on innovation for two reasons. First, in long-term oriented cultures, people are more receptive to change and able to adapt to new circumstances [64]. Since innovation requires an adaptive approach [58], long-term-oriented cultures promote an environment where innovation can flourish. ICT brings about quick changes in new technologies, thus amplifying the importance of a long-term-oriented culture’s adaptability and receptiveness to change. Conversely, in countries with low ICT, the relatively slow rate of change reduces the importance of adaptation.

Second, in long-term-oriented cultures, people do not expect immediate results [26] and are willing to wait for an extended time for their innovation to come to fruition. Hence, long-term-oriented societies are more likely to pursue long-term innovation activities. Such innovations, however, are often complex and may take a long time to accomplish. As such, many innovation eforts fail due to a lack of resources or feedback from users. ICT enables access to innovation resources (e.g., internet-enabled fundraising) and ofers opportunities to accumulate such resources. In addition, ICT, such as internet-enabled platforms, helps innovators test new and complex ideas by facilitating the processes of sharing knowledge and gathering feedback [8, 25]. Thus, ICT makes complex innovations more feasible, amplifying the efect of long-term orientation on innovation.

In sum, ICT fosters changes that require adaptation, typical in long-term-oriented cultures, and facilitates complex innovations, which are more likely to be pursued in longterm-oriented societies. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis H2C : Information and Communication Technology (ICT) amplifies the positive efect of long-term orientation on national innovation, such that countries with higher ICT experience a stronger positive efect of long-term orientation on innovation.

## Method

To test our hypotheses, we collected longitudinal data spanning nine years from 2007 through 2015 from independent secondary data sources including ITU World Telecommunications Indicators database, Global Competitiveness Reports, and World Bank databases. These data sources provide data on ICT, innovation, and other country-level metrics, which make them suitable for our analysis. We used three sources for culture data—World Values Survey (WVS), Hofstede, and Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Efectiveness (GLOBE) data— discussed in further detail in the following sections.

## Variables

## Innovation

We use patents as our innovation measure, specifically the number of (eventually granted) patent applications made in the focal year by residents of a country, and we calculate the ratio per millions of inhabitants in the country. We use the logarithmic transformation to reduce skewness. Patents are an important measure of a country’s innovation output [1, 20, 66] and are externally validated through the patent examination process. We collected patent data from the Global Competitiveness Dataset [56]. We use patent application data one year subsequent to the year of the ICT data to incorporate a time lag from inputs to outputs [53]. Using patent application date rather than issue date is a common practice in the literature since a) application date is the earliest point at which we can identify new capabilities, representing the best measure of time when patentable work was actually completed, and b) application date avoids methodological issues caused by lags between patent application and issue.

## Information and Communication Technology (ICT)

We use four major variables to capture the pervasiveness of ICT for facilitating collaboration in a country: fixed telephone lines per 100 people (Telephones), internet users per 100 people (InternetUsers), mobile phones per 100 people (MobilePhones), and secure internet servers per million people (InternetServers). ICT infrastructure (mobile phones, telephone lines, servers, and the internet) ultimately facilitates ICT applications that run over that infrastructure, thereby helping residents communicate efectively and share ideas. We measure ICT for each year using a standardized summation of the four variables.

## Culture Variables

We test our hypotheses using three sets of culture data. For the dimensions of individualism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity, and long-term orientation, we use culture variables provided by Hofstede’s country-level culture measures [27].<sup>3</sup> Hofstede standardized each measure to range from 0 to 100.<sup>4</sup>

We also use data on culture computed from the WVS dataset.<sup>5</sup> We obtained data on Individualism, Power Distance, and Uncertainty Avoidance from prior research [4], which used WVS data to compute these variables (see Online Supplemental Appendix).<sup>6</sup> These computed variables have high reliabilities and correlations with Hofstede’s original dimensions [4].<sup>7</sup> Specifically, we use data from Beugelsdijk et al.’s [4] cohort 2, consisting of people born after 1958, since people from that cohort (age group) are likely to be in the workingage population during our study’s time period. Our results are unchanged regardless of which cohort’s data we use. Although the time period for Beugelsdijk et al.’s [4] aforementioned data does not exactly match the years of our study (2007-2015), using this data is reasonable since WVS scores are fairly stable over time. As Hofstede and Minkov [28] note, “We know from empirical evidence that values as measured by the World Values Survey and similar questionnaires tend to be quite stable over time. Except in the case of measurement errors, scores between the 10-year rounds of the WVS change only slowly” (p. 502). For robustness, we conduct an analysis computing Power Distance, Uncertainty Avoidance, and Individualism based on WVS data for the specific years of our study, using the method given in Beugelsdijk et al. [4], which involves the computation of cultural dimensions from WVS data after conducting a factor analysis [4]. The Online Supplemental Appendix shows details of this computation. Results from both analyses using WVS data are qualitatively similar.

Finally, we use the following GLOBE cultural dimensions [30], which are conceptually similar to Hofstede’s dimensions: power distance, collectivism (inverse of individualism), uncertainty avoidance, assertiveness, and future orientation.<sup>8</sup> The triangulation of results using three culture datasets enhances the robustness of our findings and suggests that our results are not sensitive to the specific datasets used.

## Control Variables

Research and Development (R&D) per capita and Gross domestic product (GDP) per capita account for innovation investment and the availability of economic resources to transform inputs into innovation, respectively [60]. Intellectual Property (IP) Protection accounts for encouragement for innovation via protective measures. Competition controls for the reduced payof to innovators in competitive environments. Population accounts for the size of the economy that may influence innovation processes [1]. Education, Capacity to attract talent, Availability of research and training, and Availability of scientists and engineers account for the literacy and availability of innovation talent and human capital [60, 66]. Workforce participation, measured as Age dependency ratio [1], accounts for human capital. We control for Trade openness and Foreign direct investment (FDI), as well, as they can spawn idea exchange and innovation [1]. Economic freedom index of the country’s institutions controls for encouragement to innovators based on economic freedom [1]. Finally, year dummies account for time heterogeneity.

## Accounting for Potential Endogeneity: Instrumental Variable Models

First, we use the Hausman-Taylor instrumental variables model, which allows us to specify and estimate endogenous variables without the strong assumption (of random-efects models) that all variables are uncorrelated with country efects. Another benefit of Hausman-Taylor models is that external instruments are not needed but are instead constructed from within the model; model consistency and instrument validity are tested by the Hausman test [22]. Hausman-Taylor models are also more eficient than fixed-efects models. In all our models, Hausman tests gave a non-significant test statistic, indicating model consistency and instrument validity. In addition, Sargan tests gave non-significant statistics, suggesting instrument validity and model appropriateness.

## Accounting for Potential Endogeneity: Generalized Method of Moments Models

We account for additional considerations related to endogeneity. A country’s historical circumstances may afect its innovation, which means a dynamic efect may explain innovation. In addition, unobserved (to researchers) country-level characteristics can confound relationships. For example, countries may have historical traditions that afect their innovation and their likelihood of developing ICT. Further, endogeneity may arise due to unobserved factors that explain why countries invest in ICT; failure to account for this may lead to biased estimates. Relatedly, countries that innovate may invest more in ICT.

To account for these considerations, we use the Arellano-Bond dynamic panel model which is a Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) estimator that addresses endogeneity. This estimator is similar to a two-stage least squares estimator, but instruments are constructed from moment conditions, utilizing the panel’s time dimension. This estimator is commonly used to address endogeneity and is recognized as an efective way to address problems of dynamic endogeneity, unobserved heterogeneity, and simultaneity [e.g., 54]. The Arellano-Bond model considers dynamics by including a lagged dependent variable as a regressor. The model addresses time-invariant heterogeneity across countries by using a first-diferences approach.<sup>11</sup> The model allows for possible ‘feedback efects,’ a type of endogeneity across time periods. For example, a shock in innovation in one period may lead to changes in ICT in the next period, and such efects, if not accounted for, violate the exogeneity assumption. Arellano and Bond [2] show that lagged values of the dependent variable can serve as instruments for the first-diferenced dependent variable, and lags of endogenous variables can serve as their own instruments. We perform Sargan’s J-test to assess instrument validity by examining moment conditions. In all models, the null was not rejected, indicating instrument appropriateness. Arellano and Bond [2] tests indicated no second-order autocorrelation in residuals. All models use robust standard errors.

## Additional Robustness Checks

We conduct further robustness tests. First, given our use of unbalanced panel and fixedefects models, sample bias is a potential concern. To address this concern, we use Wooldridge’s [70] method, which suggests that sample bias is not a problem if selection is uncorrelated with idiosyncratic errors. We estimate fixed-efects models by adding a selection indicator with a one-period lag. This variable models the presence of countries each year by indicating the years missing for each country.<sup>13</sup> The indicator was nonsignificant, implying no sample imbalance bias.

In addition, we perform several diagnostic checks to ascertain the stability of results. We tested for normality of residuals, multi-collinearity, and outliers, and found no problems or violations of assumptions. Common method bias is not concerning because the data were obtained from diferent secondary sources. Finally, analyses using system GMM estimators gave similar results and are omitted for brevity. Overall, these tests indicate that the results are robust.

## Exploratory Analysis

Our arguments for why ICT attenuates efects of power distance and uncertainty avoidance are undergirded by a common theme characterizing these two cultural dimensions: they both pertain to an adherence to procedural norms, formality, and structure. Meanwhile, our arguments for why ICT amplifies efects of individualism, masculinity, and long-term orientation are undergirded by a common theme characterizing these three cultural dimensions: they all pertain to the pursuit of goals and achievement. As an exploratory analysis, we combine the dimensions of culture based on these underlying themes, and test ICT’s moderating efects on the influence of these combined dimensions on innovation. We created a variable UAPD as the mean of UncertaintyAvoidance and PowerDistance, as well as a variable IML as the mean of Individualism, Masculinity, and LongtermOrientation.<sup>14</sup>

Table 6 shows the results of this exploratory analysis. Though we interpret results using WVS data (columns 1 and 2), results using Hofstede data (columns 3 and 4) and GLOBE data (columns 5 and 6) are similar. In column 1, the coeficient of UAPD is negative $( \beta = - 0 . 0 4 6 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and the coeficient of IML is positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 7 6 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , as expected. This is consistent with the argument that uncertainty avoidance and power distance hinder innovation, while individualism, masculinity, and long-term orientation facilitate innovation. In column 2, the interaction of UAPD and ICT is positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 1 6 6 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), suggesting that ICT attenuates the combined negative efect of uncertainty avoidance and power distance on innovation. In column 2, the interaction of IML and ICT is positive and significant $( \beta = 0 . 2 1 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ suggesting that ICT amplifies the combined positive efect of individualism, masculinity, and long-term orientation on innovation. These findings are consistent with our main findings and support our contention that ICT attenuates cultural dimensions pertaining to norms, formality, and structure while amplifying cultural dimensions pertaining to the aggressive pursuit of goals.

## Discussion

## Findings

Our study has two key sets of findings, consistent across three culture datasets and a variety of estimation approaches. First, we find that ICT attenuates the negative impact of cultural dimensions related to procedural norms, formality, and structure on innovation. For power distance specifically, this finding suggests that ICT helps innovators transcend the barriers posed by power distance by facilitating access to collaborators and resources. For uncertainty avoidance specifically, this finding suggests that ICT helps innovators cope with uncertainty, expedites the innovation process, and enables dynamic improvements.

Second, we find that ICT amplifies the positive efects on innovation of cultural dimensions related to the aggressive pursuit of goals. In particular, ICT amplifies the efect of individualism on innovation; thus for innovators in individualist cultures ICT gives more capacity to develop ideas freely and fills the gap in collaboration to execute ideas. The finding that ICT amplifies the efect of long-term orientation on innovation suggests that because long-term-oriented societies are more receptive to ICT-related changes, such societies are better able to adapt and develop complex innovations facilitated by ICT.

## Theoretical Contributions and Implications for Future Research

Our study provides two main theoretical contributions. First, we highlight that ICT shapes the influence of cultural dimensions on innovation. Despite calls for research to study the moderating influence of contextual variables on the efect of culture on key outcomes [38], the contingent efect of ICT on the influence of culture on innovation has not yet been studied.<sup>15</sup> Most prior research has focused on studying direct efects of culture on innovation, neglecting contingent efects of the ICT environment. Our study highlights that ICT attenuates the efects of uncertainty avoidance and power distance―dimensions of culture that pertain to an adherence to procedural norms, structure, and formality. Conversely, ICT amplifies the efects of individualism and long-term orientation―dimensions of culture that pertain to an aggressive pursuit of goals. Our results suggest that the interplay of ICT and cultural dimensions in the context of innovation is nuanced to the extent that ICT does not influence the efects of cultural dimensions in a uniform way. The study reveals the important role ICT plays in shaping how local environmental or national factors influence innovation. Our study goes beyond direct efects of ICT on innovation identified in prior research [e.g., 39] by articulating that ICT moderates the efect of culture on innovation via intervening mechanisms such as coordination, collaboration, and knowledge sharing. Future work can study how ICT’s moderating influence may difer across industries.

Second, we contribute to the literature on ICT impact on national outcomes such as lifeexpectancy [44], corruption [59], productivity [13, 14], well-being [18], and electricity consumption [52]. A research implication is to understand policies and government interventions that may fuel ICT-enabled innovation in diferent cultural environments. Our study identifies the crucial role of ICT in influencing the impact of a location-related factor (i.e., culture) on innovation. Considering ICT in light of culture may thus change the way innovation ecosystems function to make countries more competitive in innovation.

Our study suggests many avenues for future research. Further studies can investigate how ICT shapes the influence of other location-related factors (e.g., educational institutions) on innovation. A promising approach could involve studying how various configurations of location-related and contextual factors influence innovation [47]. Future work can also study how ICT complements or substitutes for other forms of innovation resources. For example, as technology moves to the cloud and with the evolution of gigeconomy-based labor markets, how these forces jointly complement or substitute for each other in influencing national innovation presents an interesting research agenda. Another fertile avenue for research is to identify and empirically study additional mechanisms underlying the moderating efects of ICT in influencing the impact of culture on innovation at various stages (e.g., ideation stage and execution stage). Finally, future research can examine how government policies and regulations related to digital communications and platforms, investments in digital literacy and skills, censorship, and net neutrality influence the impact of ICT on innovation [61]. The efects of these phenomena on innovation are particularly salient given the democratization of information and potential moves by several countries to regulate technology platforms.

## Managerial and Policy Implications

Our findings are crucial to managers and national policy makers. While a nation’s ICT investments often represent a substantial public investment, a minimal amount is known about ICT’s impact on the influence of culture on innovation. Our results suggest that ICT shapes the efects of beliefs and values shared by a group of people (i.e., their culture) in ways that can alter their impact on innovation. Thus, ICT investments have significant implications for national policy. Accordingly, our results can help policy makers make judicious decisions on ICT investments to transcend or leverage the country’s cultural characteristics for innovation, ultimately calling for ICT investments to support the cultural characteristics that drive innovation.

Furthermore, since culture plays an important role in innovation, countries with certain cultures are often considered more appropriate locations for innovation investments. Global firms distribute their innovation activities across countries to tap into local pools of talent. Key considerations for maximizing innovation often involve deciding where to locate innovation facilities and how to customize the level of ICT investment in diferent locations. When scouting for global locations for innovation activities (e.g., overseas subsidiaries or talent), firms can overcome deficiencies or leverage advantages of certain cultures for innovation by investing in ICT since ICT can significantly shape culture’s efects on innovation [50].

Managers and policy makers often strive to develop a culture that nurtures innovation. Many governments implement policies that encourage their citizens to pursue innovation. Our findings indicate that governments need to enrich the country’s ICT infrastructure to support such policies. Our results also suggest that with advances in ICT that support collaboration, the efects of culture on innovation can be amplified or diminished. For countries with high power distance or uncertainty avoidance, our study suggests that innovation policies should focus on ICT infrastructure investments to mitigate the negative efects of these dimensions on innovation. Countries with individualist or long-termoriented cultural values benefit greatly from ICT investments since ICT builds on the positive efects of these dimensions on innovation. Overall, in a global and culturally heterogeneous world, our findings ofer policy makers a better understanding of how ICT shapes the influences of cultural dimensions on innovation.

## Limitations

Our results should be viewed in light of our study’s limitations. First, we used patents to measure innovation since it is a widely used measure of innovation in the literature [e.g., 1, 33, 53, 66]. Yet, patents are not a perfect measure of innovation since not all inventions are patentable. Still, patents constitute one of the only observable manifestations of inventive activity with a well-grounded claim. In addition, our study does not account for how ICT and culture jointly afect other innovation dimensions (e.g., type), nor does it consider how ICT impacts culture’s influence across innovation stages. Further, although we measured ICT following prior research [18, 32], the “metric of the use of ICT (telephones, internet users, mobile phones) may not be capturing all of the diferent dimensions of which ICT may be comprised” [18]. Still, this measure for ICT is appropriate since communication and collaboration are enabled by a country’s ICT infrastructure. Future work can use other ICT metrics to further explore joint efects of ICT and culture on innovation.

## Conclusion

In an era when innovation is increasingly a barometer for success and when innovation activities are distributed globally across cultures, our study ofers novel insights, imploring practitioners and scholars to account for how ICT amplifies the efects of some dimensions of culture on innovation while attenuating the efects of others. For information systems scholars specifically, this study highlights how the intertwining of culture and ICT has nuanced implications for innovation. While our study used one such perspective to understand the influence of this relationship on innovation, there may be other ways to examine this interplay and tease out underlying mechanisms. Our study also ofers guidance to practitioners and policy makers in light of increasing globalization, which will make it ever more important to transcend barriers and leverage opportunities posed by culture for innovation.

Babu John-Mariadoss (bjohnmar@ttu.edu) is an Associate Professor in the Department of Marketing and Supply Chain Management at Texas Tech University. His research interests have focused on the theory and practice of marketing strategy, including B2B relationships, sales and branding strategy, and international marketing. Dr. Mariadoss’s papers have been published in the Journal of Marketing Research, Journal of Marketing, MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, Journal of Service Research, among others.

Michelle Xiao Wu (wuxiao@mit.edu) is a Senior Scientist in the Data Science Lab at MIT Institute for Data, Systems, and Society (IDSS). Before joining IDSS, she was an assistant professor at Carson College of Business, Washington State University. She received her Ph.D. in Operations Management from Booth School of Business at University of Chicago. Dr. Wu’s research interests focus on innovation in operations management and business analytics. Her research has been published in such journals as Manufacturing and Service Operations Management, International Journal of Production Research, and others. Dr. Wu partners with a number of global companies to conduct data-driven research, improving their pricing and supply chain management.

Sunil Mithas (smithas@usf.edu; corresponding author) is a World Class Scholar and Professor at the Muma College of Business at the University of South Florida. He earned his Ph.D. from the Ross School of Business at the University of Michigan. Dr. Mithas has taught at the Robert H. Smith School of Business at the University of Maryland, and has held visiting positions in Australia, Germany and Hong Kong. He has worked on research or consulting assignments with such organizations as A. T. Kearney, Ernst & Young, Johnson & Johnson, the Social Security Administration, and the Tata Group. He is the author of two books and numerous research publications in the leading journals. Dr. Mithas’s research has won best-paper awards and has been featured in practice-oriented media such as MIT Sloan Management Review, Bloomberg, and CIO. com.
