# Handling the Eficiency–Personalization Trade-Of in Service Robotics: A Machine-Learning Approach

Schahin Tofangchi<sup>a</sup>, André Hanelt<sup>a</sup>, David Marz<sup>b</sup>, and Lutz M. Kolbe<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Digital Transformation Management, University of Kassel, Kassel, Germany; <sup>b</sup>Information Management, University of Göttingen, Digital Transformation Research Center, Göttingen, Germany

## ABSTRACT

While multiple mechanisms for value creation from big data analytics (BDA) exist, their application in everyday life can create trade-ofs, particularly in the context of service robotics where the dispersion of autonomous digital technologies creates potentials for data-driven eficiency gains. Attending to personal preferences in such contexts, increasingly vital for customer acceptance, may run counter to eficiency, thus constraining value creation and rendering eficiencypersonalization trade-ofs a key managerial challenge. For the case of autonomous vehicles (AVs), we formalize this trade-of and design a machine-learning approach to handle it by drawing on a unique dataset comprising 35,000 drives by 1,850 users. We consider the realtime dynamics and user interactions that afect the decisions of AVs and develop a model extension that allows for leveraging the properties of sharing business models to make better-informed decisions. Our study contributes to information systems (IS) AV research by providing an artifact that targets both eficiency and personalization of AV operations as well as the dynamic balance between the two. With the focus on everyday life contexts, our study points to the value of incorporating trade-ofs between competing goals as well as human-centered perspectives in information systems designs for research on BDA value creation. For practitioners, our work provides a practical and generalizable approach to realize the potentials of service robots without risking customer acceptance.

## Keywords

Autonomous vehicles; machine learning; service robots; real-time computation; sharing economy; trade-of learning

## Introduction

The use of big data analytics (BDA) is no longer confined to the optimization of business processes within the firm as it is now an integral part of customer-facing firm oferings. For instance, players such as General Motors, Google, Uber, and Tesla are hotly competing in the bid to ofer services involving autonomous vehicles (AVs) [6]. Here, on the one hand, the use of BDA promises to unlock fleet-level eficiency gains—a valuable target given such fierce competition. On the other hand, attending to personal preferences instead of robotlike optimizations is leading customers toward accepting and valuing AVs [63]. As the personalization of the vehicles may be detrimental to overall eficiency, a delicate tension emerges that may threaten the profitability of such innovative cases of BDA in everyday life.

Recently, information systems (IS) research has revealed important insights into BDA value creation (e.g., [31]). In particular, prior work has illustrated the potential of BDA value creation via increased eficiency [55] and more personalized services [42]. For example, BDA has been used to improve and accelerate supply chain activities within organizations by optimizing the use of resources (e.g., cash, equipment) [14] and to personalize online services through machine-learning (ML)-based recommendation systems [3]. However, this prior IS work either focused on particular value creation mechanisms in isolation (e.g., [42]) or treated them as additive in nature (e.g., [31]). Such assumptions, however, might no longer be adequate as BDA increases in complexity [17]. In particular, the further implementation of BDA in business practice, especially in processes involving customers in everyday life situations, might create substantial trade-ofs between the various value creation mechanisms [58].

One specific context where trade-ofs in value creation with BDA become obvious is in the application of service robots, defined as “information technology in a physical embodiment, providing customized services by performing physical as well as nonphysical tasks with a high degree of automation” [39, p. 405]. Employed in everyday life contexts and driven by BDA, service robots promise eficiency gains from the perspective of providers (e.g., [54]). However, in the pursuit of these potentials, a central tension between systemlevel goals (such as eficiency) and individual-level goals (such as personalization) emerges since automatized processes optimized for overall performance, when they are deeply integrated into people’s lives, may run contrary to personal preferences [85].

AVs are a particularly relevant service robotics case [39] as visions for a future sustainable mobility system have been articulated to build upon “an integrated network of driverless, electrical vehicles that are connected, coordinated and shared” [13, p. 181]. The amount of data generated in connected vehicles and the ability to transfer that data through ubiquitous digital infrastructures, along with the technological possibility of being able to analyze them, have paved the way for the continual derivation and application of eficient driving patterns [5]. This potential is amplified in vehicle fleet sharing economies [78]. However, from a business operator’s perspective, attractiveness to customers is as important as the potential eficiency gains. Research has shown that attending to personal preferences and enabling enjoyable rides are of utmost importance for the acceptance of AVs (e.g., [23]). While existing IS research emphasizes the relevance of both eficiency and addressing the driver’s personal preferences for AV acceptance (e.g., [24]), to date, BDAbased solutions—with ML as its main tool—to adapt the vehicle’s driving policies (i.e., the rules according to which an AV behaves) in accordance with both of these objectives are lacking. However, developing solutions that support such driving policies is a relevant endeavor to actualize the full potential of AVs in terms of eficiency gains as well as to drive their adoption (e.g., [8, 45]). Furthermore, while trade-ofs in AV operations have been considered recently (e.g., [20]), we currently lack design studies that could provide artifacts to contribute to both the eficiency and personalization of AV operations and balance the trade-of between the two. As the driver’s personal preferences may run contrary to eficient driving policies, addressing this trade-of is of particular relevance in the context of AVs.

Given its relevance for value creation through BDA and the importance to consider not only technical aspects but also human factors as well as dynamic trade-ofs between them in data-driven automation, the problem setting previously described is of particular interest for IS research. Therefore, in this study, using the example of AVs, we develop an ML method for automatically handling the trade-of between eficiency and personalization. In particular, we consider the following research problem: How can eficient and user-preferred driving patterns be prescriptively and dynamically modeled in AVs and how can trade-of weights be inferred automatically to continuously trade of eficiency and personalization against each other?

We develop a level-1 design science artifact [29] that is capable of deriving driving policies that determine a vehicle’s configuration to drive both eficiently and according to the consumer’s preferences. A “vehicle configuration” denotes an instantiation of the variables that are deemed important in a driving context (e.g., velocity) at a given point in time. We further refer to an observation of a routinely applied sequence of vehicle configurations in response to a particular environmental state as “driving patterns.” For our artifact development, we draw on a unique longitudinal dataset, comprising sensor data for approximately 35,000 drives, with 54 million data points gathered from electric vehicles while carsharing. The artifact comprises (1) a recurrent neural-network (NN)-based [36] prescriptive model for driving policies built with the goal of minimizing energy consumption, (2) a recurrent NN-based model of the consumer’s preferred driving patterns, and (3) a novel ML approach for determining trade-of weights between energy-eficient and personalized driving patterns. Since AVs are particularly well suited for sharing-based business models [43], we extend our models to leverage the potential of sharing economies to accelerate the vehicles’ learning process and enable better-informed decisions. We take advantage of the large datasets arising from sharing environments by (1) modeling the dynamics of energy consumption based on the data flow generated by all consumers and (2) creating a profile for each user that is initialized based on the average user’s preferences and is adapted over time to reflect individual user preferences.

Our contribution is twofold. First, we contribute to existing IS research on BDA value creation by introducing the notion of trade-ofs between BDA value creation mechanisms and the context of service robotics through the conceptualization and formalization of the eficiency-personalization trade-of in AV services. Although the eficiency-personalization trade-of is well-established in the field of customer service (e.g., [83]), it has thus far been neglected in the context of BDA value creation. Thereby, we also expand the concept of BDA value creation that has predominantly been dealt with within purely organizational settings to social, everyday life contexts, where such trade-ofs are particularly relevant. Second, we contribute an artifact that addresses these issues using ML methods in the context of AVs. Our study counteracts the dearth of design artifacts in IS AV research that cater to actual driving policies. The artifact does not only support eficient driving policies of AVs as well as the automatic adjustment of an AV’s settings according to the driver’s preferences—two of the much discussed potentials of AVs and factors influencing their acceptance (e.g., [8, 45]). More importantly, our approach is able to handle and balance possible trade-ofs between these factors in real time, thereby supporting to fully exploit the potentials of AVs as well as promoting their adoption. Our evaluation shows that the recurrent NNs converge in a reasonable amount of time and have a significant advantage over existing baseline approaches, being 3.5 times as accurate as the baseline approaches for eficiency predictions and six times as accurate for preference estimations. Our additional empirical analyses (see Online Supplemental Appendix A) indicate the practical value of our artifact for future AV operations, as we found that potential AV carsharing customers would opt for and substantially value personalized driving alongside exhibiting a higher willingness to pay for an ofering that would make use of our approach. Additionally, we study the generalizability of the applied design approach to a broader range of analyticsbased decision-making problems and derive propositions for the artifact’s behavior based on its mathematical formulation. Based on our work, we derive important implications for IS research and managerial practice.

## Background

## Eficiency-Personalization Trade-Ofs in Value Creation Through Big Data Analytics

BDA can be described as the “application of statistical, processing, and analytics techniques to big data” [31, p. 390]. The increasing abundance of data (e.g., [47]) and the ability to generate actionable insights from large volumes of structured as well as unstructured information (e.g., [14]) have elevated interest in BDA in both academic and business realms in recent years (e.g., [15]). However, IS scholars have also emphasized that in order of any BDA project to become truly successful, they must realize value for the organization (e.g., [31]). As Chiang et al. [15, p. 384] state: “Analysis of data without generating value ofers no contribution to an organization, regardless of whether data are big or small.” Consequently, IS researchers have called for a stronger focus on value creation through BDA in recent years (e.g., [5]).

Studies in the IS realm dealing with BDA value creation (see Table 10 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B for an overview) were conducted in diverse contexts such as advertisement/marketing (e.g., [26]) or service innovation (e.g., [47]), and provide diferent BDA-based artifacts, drawing for example on predictive analytics (e.g., [52]) or text mining techniques (e.g., [21]). While several mechanisms for value creation are discussed in this literature (see [31]), we can deduce that two value creation mechanisms and their respective value targets seem to be of particular importance: on the one side, improving eficiency is seen as one of the primary foci in many BDA applications [31], mainly through the optimization (e.g., [11]) and automation (e.g., [21]) of business practices. Popovic et al. [62], for example, examined the application of BDA in the manufacturing sector and its potential to decrease production times and operating expenses. The combination of sensors attached to production assets (e.g. assembly machines) and predictive analytics, for instance, allows for a real-time analysis of the assets’ operational conditions and the detection of any problems, thus keeping the unavailability of production processes to a minimum.

On the other side, the delivery of enhanced customer experiences through the personalization of products and services is much discussed (e.g., [42, 86]). For instance, Lehrer et al. [47] describe how BDA technologies enable firms in the e-commerce sector to automatically adjust user interfaces to align with the customer’s individual characteristics by combining historical behavioral patterns and current navigation behavior with the help of predictive analytics in order to predict the probability that a customer will buy certain products, thus allowing for the display of related products and providing an individualized customer experience.

What stands out is that current IS literature on BDA value creation applied either a unidirectional perspective on BDA value creation involving eficiency and personalization by focusing each of these factors in isolation or it has treated them as additive in nature by neglecting potential conflicts between them. However, these value creation mechanisms might also cause substantial trade-ofs as BDA contexts become more complex [17] and encompass, for example, both system-level (e.g., from a service provider perspective) and individual-level goals (e.g., from a customer perspective) [58]. In particular, BDA value creation in these contexts might give new impetus to challenges highlighted by service marketers revolving around the question: “How to provide [an] eficient, standardized service at some acceptable quality level, while treating each customer as a unique person?” [70, p. 107]. Several studies in this area have shown that a high level of personalization may come at the expense of less eficiency, while services with the least personalization provide the greatest eficiency (e.g., [83]).

From a theoretical perspective, trade-ofs can be handled by balancing practices; that is, “recurrently devising compromises among contrasting demands” [30, p. 75]. Following a more technical perspective, while IS research is often concerned with analytics tools that provide decision-makers with the right information [2], the field of operations research applies algorithmic solutions that are predominantly from the area of mathematical optimization to diferent trade-of scenarios [40]. For example, the literature has been concerned with diferent variations of the prominent traveling salesman problem, reflected in constantly recurring issues, such as vehicle routing problems (e.g., [16]), diferent (multiobjective) variations of the knapsack problem—used for portfolio management [73], logistics, and transportation (e.g., [4])—and general discrete and continuous optimization techniques, applied to the eficiency-quality trade-of [61] and the eficiency–risk trade-of [41]. While such approaches are suited to finding solutions to conflicting problems through heuristics-based mathematical approximations (e.g., [19]), they are reliant on extensive manual labor by decision-makers who are required to specify the trade-of weights [80]. Thus, in operations research, the process of balancing trade-ofs is ultimately sourced to decision makers, and trade-of problems are often viewed from an organizational perspective where solutions are formulated through long-term planning (e.g., [33]). However, such approaches can be impracticable where trade-ofs are dependent on a large number of variables, their conditions change dynamically, or decision makers are unable to objectively resolve a trade-of.

Another insight from the analysis of current IS research on BDA value creation is that scholars have limited their studies to intra- or inter-organizational contexts such as supply chain management [14] or brand advertising [86]. Contexts that directly involve customers or that require interaction with individual customers in everyday life settings have been neglected so far. One such context and in which the eficiency-personalization trade-of in BDA value creation becomes particularly apparent is in the field of service robotics. Due to advances in electronics, telecommunications, and sensor technology, robots are no longer bound to an industrial context but are able to operate in everyday life environments (e.g., [54]). In these contexts, service robots, such as self-driving cars, are characterized by a high degree of autonomy, closeness to the end users, and real-time reactions to their environment [71]. Due to these characteristics, service robots represent a promising field of application for BDA for the following two reasons: First, service robots can greatly benefit from BDA and ML capabilities, “which help the service robot to better understand and react to the environment and context in which it is acting” [71, p. 272]. Second, service robots hold the promise of providing personalized and eficient services [81], representing two main BDA value creation mechanisms. However, while eficiency advantages and benefits primarily occur on a system level (e.g., on the level of a fleet of robots operated by a central provider), value creation in the context of service robots involves serving human beings, rendering an individual-level perspective particularly relevant. Handling this trade-of in the context of service robots thus becomes crucial, as their success is highly dependent on the customer’s satisfaction with the outcomes achieved through the use of service robots [39]. However, manually inferring and specifying trade-of weights in advance become impracticable because situational trade-ofs emerge dynamically from human–machine interactions. Here, trade-ofs cannot be treated as objective problems and thus they cannot be handled by a single decision-maker, but instead require the consideration of individual perspectives. New methods are necessary to continuously determine user preferences and react to new circumstances in a real-time manner [71]. In this regard, BDA [1] paired with the autonomy of service robots such as AVs [39] can open up new opportunities.

## Big Data Analytics and the Case of Autonomous Vehicles

AVs can be defined as vehicles whose operation “occurs without direct driver input to control the steering, acceleration, and braking and are designed so that the driver is not expected to constantly monitor the roadway while operating in self-driving mode” [57]. The architecture of AVs includes several sub-modules that are interconnected with a coordination and control module [7]. This interconnected set of sub-modules can be considered an analytical chain required to solve problems on diferent abstraction levels such as human–machine interaction (e.g., through voice control) that can be allocated to a higher level of abstraction as they build upon modules at lower levels, for example for trajectory planning (e.g., [82]). Given their characteristics and capabilities, AVs exhibit great potentials to support societies in achieving sustainability, with regards to its economic, environmental, and social dimensions (e.g., [51]), for example by enabling a more eficient trafic management and reducing emissions as well as trafic accidents [13].

In recent years, AVs have received much attention from IS scholars (see Table 11 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B for an overview). While the fields of interest also include technical solutions to improve the identification of objects, movements, and road conditions (e.g., [12]), or legal and ethical aspects (e.g., [34]), three key insights emerge from the analysis of the current research. First, IS scholars emphasize potential eficiency gains by integrating AVs into transportation systems. It should be noted that in the context of AVs, eficiency primarily manifests in a low energy consumption through the reduction of fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions [24], thus rendering a focus on energy eficiency particularly relevant. For example, Nasri et al. [56] describe how AVs can reduce emissions, fuel consumption, and travel times by being connected, and thus by being able to drive close to each other and at the same speed, acceleration, and with the same steering profile, and they developed a stochastic programming methodology for optimizing the routes and speeds of AVs making deliveries under uncertain trafic conditions. Overall, IS scholars have illustrated not only the potential advantages of AVs over traditional cars regarding eficient driving patterns (e.g., [74]) but also that AVs hold the promise to improve the energy eficiency of the mobility and transportation sector in its entirety (e.g., [20]).

Second, AVs have increasingly become subject to IS technology-acceptance research. In this regard, studies emphasize the importance of addressing the driver’s personal preferences to promote the adoption of AVs. According to Bornholt and Heidt [8], for example, an increased convenience via smooth driving patterns is a relevant acceptance driver for AVs. Similarly, the findings of Ernst and Reinelt’s [23] empirical study indicate that perceived enjoyment, expressed through enjoyable, fun, and exciting driving experiences, can significantly influence the acceptance of AVs. Overall, there is a consensus that understanding and addressing users’ preferences is crucial for the successful difusion of AVs (e.g., [24]).

Thus, as exemplified by these first two key insights, energy eficiency and personalization are much discussed concepts in the context of AVs in extant IS research. However, while some IS scholars (e.g., [8, 24, 45]) consider both of them, stating for example that environmentally friendly driving policies on the one side as well as convenience for the driver on the other side are perceived by the users as benefits of AVs, these factors are mainly treated as additive in nature—potential interdependencies or even trade-ofs were neglected so far. However, the driver’s personal preferences regarding the AV’s configurations may run contrary to eficient driving policies, for example if the driver prefers a high temperature in the vehicle. Furthermore, examining and addressing this eficiencypersonalization trade-of is of particular relevance in the context of AVs to fully unfold their potentials in terms of enabling a high energy eficiency while simultaneously catering to the driver’s preferences and thereby, fostering their acceptance. In the past, IS scholars have already examined diverse trade-ofs in the context of AVs (see Table 11 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B). Dlugosch et al. [20], for example, applied a combination of data analytics and an agent-based simulation model based on real-world data on carsharing trips and charging locations to address the trade-of between economic and sustainability-related considerations (fleet size, charge point density, service levels) in the context of shared electric AVs. Overall, while these studies provide valuable insights into potentially conflicting goals in the context of AVs and also approaches to handle them, the trade-of between eficient driving policies and the personalization of AVs according to the driver’s personal preferences has as mentioned not gained much attention.

As a third and final key insight, existing IS AV work (see Table 11 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B) illustrates the potential of BDA—and with ML as its main tool—for AVs. Due to their capability of being able to collect and process large amounts of diverse data (e.g., [27]), AVs may leverage ML to exploit the potential of these data for diferent purposes such as the perception of roads and users and the dynamic adjustment of trajectory planning in cases of roadblocks (e.g., [12]). Yang et al. [84], for example, propose two frameworks based on convolutional neural networks to improve the scene understanding of an AV and thereby, solving problems raised in autonomous driving such as the detection and classification of pedestrians or other vehicles.

While these studies indicate the potential of ML in association with AVs, previous solutions provided by IS scholars have primarily focused on issues such as trajectory planning and the identification of objects and road conditions (e.g., [12]), whereas design studies dealing with eficient driving policies and the personalization of an AV according to the driver’s preferences are scarce. Given the amount of data generated in AVs and the ability to analyze them, applying BDA technologies to learn from previous rides and about the driver’s personal preferences might significantly improve an AV’s energy eficiency as well as the driving experience, thereby supporting to fully unfold their potential and to foster their acceptance. So far, however, corresponding studies in the IS realm have mainly examined efects of AVs on energy eficiency in urban transportation based on real-world data (e.g., [20]) or collected user-generated data on social media platforms and derived user perceptions of AVs (e.g., [22]). Furthermore, IS scholars often merely discuss the potential of ML approaches to support an AV’s driving policies, but do not develop and provide appropriate design artifacts (e.g., [27]).

In conclusion, while the concepts of energy eficiency, personalization, and BDA seem to be of particular relevance in the context of AVs, they have predominantly been considered in isolation, thereby neglecting the potential as well as consequences of combining them. On the one hand, BDA-based solutions might support energy eficient driving policies of AVs, for example by learning from previous rides and optimizing the vehicle’s acceleration, braking, or steering under similar conditions in future drives. On the other hand, BDA may enable AVs to learn from the direct interactions between the driver and the vehicle, for example regarding the desired temperature within the vehicle, music volume, and driving styles, and to adjust their settings automatically according to the driver’s personal preferences. As both energy eficiency and attending to personal preferences are critical factors to fully exploit the potentials of AVs as well as promoting their acceptance, developing solutions that support such driving policies are a relevant endeavor; yet, corresponding design studies are to date missing in IS research. Furthermore, important interdependencies between these concepts such as the eficiency-personalization trade-of [83] and BDA-based solutions to handle such trade-ofs were also not considered so far. However, this trade-of enters a new dimension in the highly dynamic context of AVs, as ubiquitous and highvelocity data [1, 85] enable hitherto unseen possibilities to increase the firms’ ofering variabilities while also mitigating against declines in energy eficiency by automating the process of determining trade-of weights through multi-objective analyses. Figure 1 summarizes important directions, exemplary insights and relevant research gaps in prior IS research on AVs related to the concepts of energy eficiency, personalization, and BDA.

Aside from IS research, the topics of energy eficiency, personalization, and BDA in the context of AVs have been independently dealt with in adjacent fields, such as computer science and operations research. ML has been used for example to estimate and improve energy eficiency in the area of personal mobility, where recurrent NNs exhibit high predictive power and can be used to prescribe eficient driving patterns [9]. Furthermore, the dificulty in predicting granular behavioral patterns [52] makes personalization through ML-based user-preference modeling a complex problem that may be addressed through diferent approaches. Considering the case of fully automated vehicles, Kuderer et al. [44] propose an approach for personalizing the driving experience based on initially provided examples. However, the drawbacks of this approach lie in its static nature (i.e., it neglects the fact that user preferences may vary depending on the situation and time) and in its isolated training of the ML models involved in the AV’s analytical chain. In summary, ML-based personalization for AVs is either limited to particular modules (e.g., lane-keeping), falls short of individualization, or does not consider variations in user preferences over time. Existing approaches neglect user preferences in AVs being influenced by the events of the immediate past [32] and also fail to address how particular ML approaches, such as recurrent NNs [36], can be used to model time-varying user preferences based on timeseries data. Furthermore, most existing multi-objective approaches tend to have the same limitation as trade-of approaches in mathematical optimization in that they rely on the manual specification of trade-of weights [38]. While multi-objective reinforcement learning [77] constitutes an approach that is not afected by this limitation and is able to automatically infer trade-of weights, it neglects how the preferences regarding the tradeof weights may change over time, and is therefore not suited to dynamic contexts relying on real-time, continuous adaptations.

## Research Setting

We develop a situated artifact—that is, a level-1 design science artifact [29]—that solves a particular problem in a situated context (i.e., deriving driving policies based on energy consumption and individual preferences). Owing to the special importance and deep involvement of individuals in this context, we follow a human-centered design approach [25]. Despite being developed for this particular problem, we deliberately describe some artifact components in an abstract manner to allow for a certain degree of generalizability. We instantiate these abstract components in the Evaluation Section. We focus on a set of simple variables that define our vehicle configurations—determining driving patterns and attending to a user’s preferences—and derive a data-driven approach to determining a configuration that minimizes energy consumption while maximizing the user’s driving experience at the same time. To do so, we require a model relating vehicle configuration to energy eficiency and a model of each individual user’s preferences under diferent conditions. Moreover, we need a method that draws on these models in order to determine a vehicle’s configuration in every situation to cater to both the user’s preferences and the vehicle’s energy eficiency. Overall, the proposed design artifact is capable of supporting both energy eficient driving policies of AVs and the automatic, real-time adjustment of an AV’s configurations according to the driver’s personal preferences, while simultaneously handling the eficiency-personalization trade-of based on real-time data. With this orientation, the design builds upon existing knowledge and addresses key gaps in IS AV research (see Figure 1). Figure 2 illustrates the specific anticipated contributions of our design artifact for this stream of work.

Since we are dealing with a distributed, real-time learning problem (i.e. the data are processed by multiple vehicles and produced at a high pace while the vehicles are in use) involving large amounts of data, we adhere to the division-of-labor (DoL) framework [76] to develop our ML application. This framework ensures the application’s practicability by appropriately handling real-time data and their efectiveness by using distributed analysis modules to improve its analytical capabilities. The DoL framework enables a modular composition of an ML application. Leveraging the concept of task division, it divides the analytical tasks into subtasks of lower complexity. An application based on the DoL framework—henceforth referred to as a DoL application—solves sub-tasks to obtain a solution to the overall task. Based on this approach, it may yield results of a higher quality than those obtained without the use of task division. The framework comprises two computational component types: the expert and the central executive (CE).

Apart from handling task division, experts and CEs satisfy a set of additional properties that contributes to the overall usability and comprehensibility of DoL applications (see Table 1). In our case, the CE does not specifically satisfy the feedback property, but we move this property to the experts because experts interact with users in real time and can process the feedback immediately. Both experts and CEs possess an additional optional property— sustainability—that requires them to adapt their learning models in real time (i.e., updating their models rather than entirely recomputing them whenever new data are seen), thus ensuring the system’s scalability.

After developing our artifact, we evaluate it using a dataset that comprises sensor and booking data provided by a carsharing company. As the data were not collected in AVs but in cars entirely controlled by users, we cannot rely on the raw dataset to perform the evaluation. Instead, we apply some transformations and restrictions to the data in order to properly evaluate the artifact with respect to its objective in the context of autonomous driving. We describe the details of how we handle these data in the Evaluation Section.

## Division-of-Labor Application for Resolving the Eficiency-Personalization Trade-Of in Autonomous Vehicles

We design the ML application so that it learns to make decisions on a predefined set of vehicle driving parameters (configuration) with the goal of minimizing energy consumption and maximizing personalization according to individual preferences. Thus, the application contributes to the analytical capabilities of AVs and is concerned with high-level vehicle configurations, such as velocity, acceleration, air conditioning, and gears, rather than with lower-level capabilities, such as road and obstacle detection. It makes “soft” decisions that are based on the vehicle’s sensory input but is ultimately restricted by decisions made at the lower levels of the vehicle’s analytical chain. Since the application decides on high-level configurations that need to be adapted frequently throughout a drive, it receives inputs and makes decisions at intervals of seconds or milliseconds. We divide the given inputs into two sets of variables: one set comprising data on the vehicle’s environment that are immutable by the application and one set comprising the vehicle’s configuration parameters that may be modified by the application.

The application learns and predicts in a real-time manner by processing the data one element at a time (rather than through batch learning). It comprises three ML models. First, we employ a recurrent NN—in particular, a long short-term memory (LSTM) network [36]—to model the relationship between the vehicle configuration and the energy eficiency. Recurrent NNs are supervised learning models that can be applied to time-series data. LSTMs constitute the state of the art in this context and have shown great performance for various learning problems involving time series (e.g., [50]). We train this model to learn the energy eficiency of a drive based on the sequence of inputs received. Second, we use a further LSTM to build a contextsensitive model of user preferences regarding modifiable configurations. We are faced with a paradox because, in order to be able to build such a model to enable autonomous decisions by the application, we require data on configurations set by the user. Acquiring these data requires a violation of the application’s autonomy. We address this issue by largely letting the application choose the configuration and allowing the user to manually set individual configurations. Users will use this function to adjust the driving policies when they are dissatisfied with their existing driving experience. We treat the manual adjustments as real-time user feedback that we use to adapt the preference model. In contrast to Kuderer et al.’s [44] approach, our proposed system does not learn the users’ driving patterns but acts autonomously in a laissez-faire manner until the user intervenes. In this way, the system learns to drive according to the user’s preferences as a passenger rather than as a driver. We compare both our models for energy eficiency and our preference model with a number of baseline models in the Evaluation Section. Third, we develop a method that resolves the trade-of between energy eficiency and personalization using the aforementioned models to perform a weighted optimization of energy eficiency and personalization to enable a decision to be made on the vehicle’s configuration at each time step.

## Central Executive

The CE is in charge of maintaining a model of the energy eficiency and a model of the average user’s preferences. Both these models are independent of particular situations and users, satisfying the DoL property of general knowledge. The model of the energy eficiency and the model of the average user’s preferences are LSTMs with sets of parameters $\theta ^ { ( E ) }$ and $\theta ^ { ( P ) }$ (i.e., neural weights) that the CE initializes randomly. In this study, we use LSTMs [28] without peephole connections. LSTMs possess a set of hyperparameters (hidden layer size, the activation function of the hidden layer, forget gate bias, and learning rate) that we specify in accordance with the given data in the Artifact Instantiation Section. Users need to sign up for carsharing prior to using the AVs and are each assigned to a unique expert by the CE. When a previously unseen user starts using a vehicle, the CE creates an expert for this user and initializes it with the current versions of the energy eficiency model and the average user’s preference model. While a vehicle is in use by some user $u _ { i } ,$ sensor data retrieved by the vehicle—comprising a set of environmental variables denoted as $e _ { u _ { i } , t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { p }$ and a set of configurable variables (the current state of the vehicle’s configuration) denoted as $c _ { u _ { i } , t } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { q } - \mathrm { a t }$ each time step t are first processed by the relevant expert who forwards these data to the CE. We stack these two vectors to obtain the whole set of sensor data $s _ { u _ { i } , t } = \left( \begin{array} { c } { e _ { u _ { i } , t } } \\ { c _ { u _ { i } , t } } \end{array} \right)$ . The length of the time steps is determined by the frequency at which sensor

data are retrieved by the vehicle and should generally be in the range of milliseconds to seconds. The expert further transmits information on whether the user has manually set the configuration parameters and, therefore, has locked them for modification by the ML application; this information assumes the shape of a scalar that we denote as $l _ { u _ { i } , t } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ and it is set to 1 if any configurable variable is locked. Upon receiving these three inputs, the CE stores $s _ { u _ { i } , t }$ in a list $S _ { u _ { i } , d }$ and, if $l _ { u _ { i } , t } = 1$ , it updates the preference model using backpropagation [49] with a small learning rate by feeding it $e _ { u _ { i } , t }$ as the input and $c _ { u _ { i } , t }$ as the label for the backpropagation process.

Once a drive has concluded (when the user signs out of the car), the expert informs the CE and transmits the overall energy eficiency of drive $d ,$ computed as $\begin{array} { r } { E _ { u _ { i } , d } = \frac { d i s t a n c e \_ t r a \nu e l l e d _ { u _ { i } , d } } { e n e r g y \_ c o n s u m p t i o n _ { u _ { i } , d } } } \end{array}$ . The CE uses this information to update the energy eficiency model following the aforementioned backpropagation process by sequentially feeding the model each entry from the list of sensor data $S _ { u _ { i } , d }$ and using $E _ { u _ { i } , d }$ as the label. $S _ { u _ { i } , d }$ is subsequently deleted.

## Expert

The experts are responsible for modeling individual user preferences. Each expert is associated with one user $u _ { i }$ and holds a model (an LSTM) of their preferences, similar to how the CE holds a model of the average user’s preferences, which is used to initialize the expert’s user preference model. Thus, the expert initializes the LSTM weights with the weights stored in the CE’s preference model. At each time step, an expert chooses the vehicle’s configuration based on the LSTM that models its energy eficiency, whose outputs for an input vector $s _ { u _ { i } , t }$ we denote as $E { \left( { { s } _ { u _ { i } , t } } \right) }$ , and on the LSTM that models the user’s preferences, whose outputs for an input vector $e _ { u _ { i } , t }$ we denote as $\textstyle P { \bigl ( } e _ { u _ { i } , t } { \bigr ) }$

To determine the vehicle configuration at any given time, we query the preference model for the user’s expected preferred configuration. We then employ a gradient-based approach that uses said configuration as a starting point, taking a step toward a more eficient configuration. To do so, the expert requires the derivative of the energy eficiency model with respect to its input, denoted as $\frac { \partial \dot { E ( x ) } } { \partial x }$ (the formulae, along with the derivations of the LSTM output and its derivative, are described by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber [36]). At each time step t, an expert possesses information on the lock state of the configurable parameters, taking on the shape of a vector $L _ { u _ { i } , t } \in \mathbb { N } ^ { q }$ , where $L _ { u _ { i } , t , j }$ denotes the number of time steps for which the j-th configurable parameter will remain locked for changes by the expert. Whenever the user manually adjusts the j-th configuration parameter, the expert sets $L _ { u _ { i } , t , j } = l o c k \_ t i m e .$ , which is the number of time steps for which a lock remains active. Taking into account the lock state, the expert computes the new sensor data vector $s _ { u _ { i } , t } ^ { \prime }$ as:

$$
\begin{array}{c} p \\ j = 1 \end{array} : s _ {u _ {i}, t, j} ^ {\prime} = s _ {u _ {i}, t _ {j}}, \quad \begin{array}{c} p + q \\ j = p + 1 \end{array} : s _ {u _ {i}, t, j} ^ {\prime} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c} s _ {u _ {i}, t, j}, \text {   if   } L _ {u _ {i}, t, j - p} > 0 \\ P (e _ {u _ {i}, t}) _ {j - p} + \gamma_ {u _ {i}} \cdot \frac {\partial E (P (e _ {u _ {i} , t}) _ {j - p})}{\partial P (e _ {u _ {i} , t}) _ {j - p}}, e l s e \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

That is, the set of environmental variables populating the first up to the p-th dimension of $s _ { u _ { i } , t }$ remains unchanged, and the set of configurable parameters populating the $( p + 1 )$ -th up to the $q \cdot$ th dimension of $s _ { u _ { i } , t }$ is updated through our gradient-based approach, unless they are currently locked. We introduce a trade-of parameter $\gamma _ { u _ { i } } { > } 0$ that determines the size of the step taken toward an eficient configuration. Since we would like the trade-of to dynamically adapt to user preferences, we introduce two further parameters: the trade-of momentum $m _ { u _ { i } } \in ( 0 , 1 )$ and the trade-of momentum extinction $\alpha _ { m } \in ( 0 , 1 )$ . Note that $\alpha _ { m }$ is a static parameter that assumes the same value for all users. With $n ^ { \left( L _ { u _ { i } , t } \right) }$ denoting the number of locked configurable parameters associated with user $u _ { i }$ at time t, an expert adapts the trade-of parameter according to (2) and the trade-of momentum according to (3).

$$
\gamma_ {u _ {i}} \leftarrow \gamma_ {u _ {i}} + \left(0. 5 1 - \frac {n ^ {\left(L _ {u _ {i} , t}\right)}}{q}\right) \cdot m _ {u _ {i}}\tag{2}
$$

$$
m _ {u _ {i}} \leftarrow m _ {u _ {i}} \cdot \alpha_ {m}\tag{3}
$$

The coeficient $\left( 0 . 5 1 - \frac { n ^ { \binom { L _ { u _ { i } , t } } { q } } } { q } \right)$ assumes values in the range $\left[ - 0 . 4 9 , 0 . 5 1 \right]$ , only being negative when the number of locked variables exceeds the number of unlocked variables, and it is multiplied by the trade-of momentum to determine the magnitude of the change applied to the trade-of parameter. Subtracting the ratio of locked variables from a value of 0:51 rather than 0:5 results in a small bias in the trade-of toward energy eficiency. For example, if the numbers of locked and unlocked variables are equal, the trade-of parameter will increase in value and slightly favor energy eficiency. The trade-of momentum extinction gradually slows down the process of adapting the trade-of parameters for a particular user. The intuition behind slowing down the adaptation is that a high rate of adaptation is required when the user first enters the application and a gradual decrease in this rate as the application becomes more acquainted with the user helps avoid any oscillatory behavior of the vehicle. Apart from the adaptation of the trade-of-specific parameters, the learning processes of experts involve the adaptation of the preference model, which they carry out in the same way as the CE does. Finally, at the end of a drive, the expert transmits the overall drive energy eficiency, $E _ { u _ { i } , d } ,$ to the CE. This information is not transmitted at each time step because the energy consumption is not accurately measurable with such small time intervals.

## Evaluation

After describing the instantiation of the artifact, the evaluation is divided into two parts: the evaluation of the standalone energy eficiency prediction model and the evaluation of the preference estimation model. More detailed evaluations of the two individual models based on their adaptability (i.e., a decline in undesired behavior over time) on diferent levels of abstraction (per drive, individual-level adaptability, and overall adaptability) can be found in Online Supplemental Appendix D. We use a dataset that we collected in cooperation with a German carsharing provider comprising booking and driving data. The carsharing provider operated 97 vehicles, collecting data on 1,856 distinct users and 34,987 drives. For an additional validation of the proposed artifact, we use the Mafalda dataset [65] comprising data from 37 drives collected from seven personal vehicles. Online Supplemental Appendix C provides details of these datasets and the preprocessing that was undertaken. While we evaluate the sub-modules in isolation using the given datasets, we deal with the overall application behavior in the Discussion Section. Note that we do not use regularization methods in training the predictive models because we observe that the prediction error on unseen data barely deviates (less than three percent for all models) from the prediction error on already-seen data. Regularization methods may be necessary to prevent overfitting with other datasets and more complex models.

## Artifact Instantiation

As a design science artifact addressing a problem in a particular context [29], the DoL system developed in this paper is mostly composed of components that can be used as is. Nevertheless, we introduced a set of parameters to our DoL application that we left uninitialized because they are dependent on the particular user base and the environment in which the application is deployed, although, for many of these parameters, the impact of their initial values is small and diminishes over time. The artifact instantiation, firstly, involves a number of experiments using the datasets to optimize the LSTMs’ hyperparameters with respect to the error rate. In each experiment, we optimize for one of the hyperparameters while fixing the rest. Thereby, we approximate the optimal configuration without jointly optimizing for all hyperparameters at once, which would lead to a large search space and an impracticably long runtime. Each experiment is carried out by training and evaluating the LSTM on data of a randomly selected share of 30% of the users (= 71 users). We repeat this experiment five times for each hyperparameter and select the values that produced the lowest average error rate – computed as the mean absolute error. We repeat this process for networks with one hidden layer and two hidden layers. The experiments reveal the optimized hyperparameters (see Table 2) and architectures (see Figures 4 and 5): an LSTM with a single hidden layer for the energy eficiency prediction and two hidden layers for the preference estimator.

Secondly, the artifact instantiation deals with instantiating the following trade-ofrelated parameters before we can deploy the application: the trade-of parameter, the trade-of momentum, the trade-of momentum extinction, and the lock time. We initialize the trade-of-related parameters as $\gamma _ { u _ { i } } = 0 . 0 1$ (trade-of parameter), $m _ { u _ { i } } = 0 . 0 1$ (trade-of momentum), $\alpha _ { m } = 0 . 9 9$ (trade-of momentum extinction), and $l o c k \_ t i m e =$ 60 (i.e., locks remain active for a duration of one minute at a time). The only constants among these parameters are $\alpha _ { m }$ and lock time, whose instantiations may be crucial for the application’s performance. In general, $\alpha _ { m }$ should be chosen to be rather large to let the application learn for a large enough period of time. The lock time should not be too long, since this will lead to extended time periods in which users have to manually interfere, potentially decreasing their satisfaction. However, we designate the lock time to be long enough to let the application learn the user’s preferred driving patterns from the manual configurations.

## Evaluation of the Preference Estimation

To assess the quality of the preference estimation module, we assume that the configurations selected by the users and recorded by the carsharing vehicles reflect the users’ true preferences, given the corresponding environmental conditions. Under this assumption, we measure the preference estimation error at one point in time as the average absolute deviation in the prediction of each configurable parameter from its true value. We compare the performance of our main approach with a number of baseline approaches (see Tables 5 and 6), as was already done with the energy eficiency predictor. In addition to using the baseline models used for the energy eficiency prediction, we further consider some densitybased approaches, since they may potentially be well suited to modeling diferent preference levels. We specifically use a Gaussian probability density function and a kernel density estimator (KDE) [60] with two kernels with desirable analytical properties that have been proven to perform well; namely, the Gaussian kernel and the Cauchy kernel [69]. With these density-based approaches, we predict a user’s preferred configuration by taking the vehicle’s current configuration and moving to a local maximum according to the respective probability density function. For the Gaussian probability density function, the local maximum is equal to the global maximum.

## Discussion and Implications

## Discussion of Results

We have employed a multi-model ML application that learns eficient driving patterns and user preferences independently from one another and combines them through a trade-of module. As opposed to single-model ML applications, which treat a problem as a single, coherent task, our application treats the given problem as an array of three sub-problems, isolating the actual decision-making process, which is, on an abstract level, solely determined by the trade-of parameter. We evaluate the proposed artifact using two datasets: Our main dataset—the carsharing dataset—serves as a basis for a thorough and robust evaluation of the artifact. The additional Mafalda dataset is used for validating the results and ensuring the artifact’s generalizability.

Regarding the energy eficiency prediction with the carsharing dataset, we note that, due to min–max scaling and a wide value range, the normalized energy eficiency values have a low variance (see Online Supplemental Appendix C). Thus, its mean value can serve as an approximation of the true values and the baseline approaches achieve seemingly high prediction accuracies. However, taking into account the low variance of energy eficiency and the fact that the mean estimator is just as efective as the other baseline approaches are, their performance appears to be less impressive. They rely heavily on a high correlation between the dependent variable (energy eficiency) and the independent variables. Thus, their poor performance can be explained by the low correlation between energy eficiency and the independent variables. On the other hand, with the proposed approach, we follow other researchers in using recurrent NNs (in particular, LSTMs) for energy eficiency predictions and achieve a prediction accuracy that is approximately 3.5 times as high as those of the baseline approaches. By taking into account time-varying relationships, LSTMs can achieve higher accuracies by going beyond a static analysis of correlations. For the Mafalda dataset, although the mean normalized energy eficiency is much higher, resulting in generally larger error rates for all models, we once again observe that the LSTM’s predictive accuracy is 40 times higher than those of the baseline approaches. With these results, we confirm the efectiveness of LSTMs for energy eficiency predictions, which has already been shown in prior studies (e.g., [9]).

The estimation of preferences poses a more complex task as opposed to predicting energy eficiency, which is an objective quantity determined by the laws of physics. This high complexity is also reflected in the hyperparameter optimization process: While the energy eficiency prediction reaches peak accuracy with one single hidden layer and a small number (three) of neurons, the preference estimation strongly benefits from increasing the number of hidden layers and using a high number (in total, 6,300) of neurons. We use a two-layer LSTM for our evaluation but note that the predictive performance may be further enhanced by employing even more hidden layers. However, we refrain from considering more hidden layers in our hyperparameter optimization due to the computation time that would have been required. In accordance with the high complexity of this problem, the prediction errors are higher than for the energy eficiency predictions, amplified by the high mean values of the normalized preference that cause the error rates to generally be higher. Nevertheless, with the proposed two-layer LSTM, we observe a performance that is nearly six times as high as those of the baseline approaches using the carsharing dataset and is approximately twice as high as when using the Mafalda dataset. The baseline approaches perform similarly as well as the mean estimator does, with some outperforming the mean estimator. However, the estimations produced by the KDEs are remarkably inaccurate. Their poor performance may be due to the high number of local maxima in their probability density functions that the KDEs approximate and which can be far from the global maximum.

Finally, regarding the general applicability of the proposed artifact, our evaluation confirms that the models can be generalized to other datasets, even with very diferent attributes. For both datasets in our evaluation, we incorporated almost all available attributes, performed general-purpose data-preprocessing techniques (min–max scaling, dummy variables), and made only a small number of specifications: environmental variables, configurable variables, a measure of energy eficiency, and the initial trade-of parameters. Thus, the proposed approach is not specific to our main dataset but can be applied to other contexts with minor adaptations. Furthermore, we only tuned the LSTM hyperparameters on the main dataset, showing that they are also efective for the Mafalda dataset. However, in practice, it is advised to tune the hyperparameters with respect to the particular context in order to achieve high performance.

## Analysis of the Application’s Overall Behavior

Given the limitation that we cannot evaluate the overall system in an actual autonomous setting, we derive statements on its behavior based on a mathematical analysis of the trade-of process. To do so, we assume that a vehicle configuration chosen by the user reflects that user’s true preference and that a deviation of a certain magnitude of the vehicle’s decision from this preference results in an eventual intervention by the user (i.e., a manual adjustment that we may interpret as user feedback). Whenever such a situation occurs, the application imposes a lock on the afected configuration parameter(s) and sets its/their value(s) to those that were directly selected by the user.

In the following, we analyze the overall application—a composition of the energy eficiency predictor and the user-preference estimator. The evaluation of these individual models (see Online Supplemental Appendix D) reveals that both improve their accuracy over time and, more importantly, they outperform all baseline approaches considered in this study. The overall application autonomously makes decisions based on a trade-of module. Since there is no commonly defined objective way in which to assess the quality of a performed trade-of, we restrict ourselves to analytically deriving propositions that define the frame of our application’s behavior. To do so, we abstract from the performance of the energy eficiency and preference models and make the simplifying assumption that they produce perfect predictions. This assumption allows us to focus on the trade-of module and neglect the complexity arising from inaccurate predictions.

The application’s most fundamental behavior is its attempt to balance the vehicle’s configuration between a preferred and an eficient configuration. More specifically, the chosen configuration will always lie between the preferred and the closest locally optimal configuration regarding energy eficiency. The balancing is achieved by taking the preferred configuration as a starting point and making a step toward an eficient solution. The size of this step is determined by the trade-of parameter, whose value is, in the long run, determined by the trade-of momentum. While, in the beginning, the chosen configuration is biased toward the user’s preferences, the term $\begin{array} { r } { \bigg ( 0 . 5 1 - \frac { n ^ { \left( L _ { u _ { i } , t } \right) } } { q } \bigg ) \cdot m _ { u _ { i } } } \end{array}$ in (2) makes this bias shift over time toward energy eficiency if the user rarely interferes with the vehicle’s decisions. Thus, we arrive at the following two propositions.

Proposition 1a: If the user does not interfere with the vehicle’s decisions at all, the vehicle will always choose a configuration that is locally optimal according to its energy eficiency model.

Proposition 1b: If the user always interferes with the vehicle’s decisions, the decisions will become completely biased toward the user’s preferences.

Proposition 1a implies that in the “worst case,” in which the user does not provide the system with any data, the system neglects user preferences and learns to choose configurations that approximate the optimal energy eficiency. Thus, the system’s behavior in such a case will correspond to that of other already existing solutions that aim to solely optimize energy eficiency or other system-level goals. Proposition 1b considers the opposite case, in which the user keeps driving manually and thus keeps providing the vehicle with data. In this case, the vehicle will completely neglect energy eficiency and be heavily tuned to its observations of the user’s preferred driving patterns. Proposition 2: The trade-of module converges when the trade-of momentum turns to zero.

## Implications for Information Systems Research

In this study, we designed an ML approach to handle the eficiency–personalization tradeof in the context of service robotics. From our work, we deduce two important implications for IS research. First, we introduce the notion of trade-ofs in the context of BDA value creation. Although the eficiency–personalization trade-of, for example, is well-established in the field of customer service and has also received attention from IS scholars in prior studies (e.g., [83]), to date, the value creation mechanisms and related BDA targets have been treated in isolation or as additive in nature [31]. The increasing complexity of the relevant BDA contexts, however, “requires an open and interrogative attitude toward the possible novelties introduced by big data and the range of trade-ofs that may emerge as the phenomenon difuses throughout [the] economy and society” [17, p. 54]. A key implication for IS research is to incorporate theoretical accounts attending to contradictions [64] when investigating and designing BDA applications. While this holds true for a variety of current and future BDA contexts, it gains particular importance in instances of everyday life automation, where tensions may arise between “interests and needs of individuals on the one hand, and the somewhat opportunistic strategic moves of businesses (and governments) on the other” [58, p. 22]. Whereas to date such tensions have, in the best case, been described conceptually, in this study, we propose a novel and practical approach for handling the trade-of between energy eficiency and personalization that arises when vehicles are required to autonomously make decisions on their driving patterns. Similarly, our approach is applicable to other contexts of service robotics [54]. For instance, in recent years, employing robots in nursing or elderly care has been increasingly discussed in relation to physical and psychological demands, the societal costs that this type of work involves, and the demographic developments in most developed societies. Here, the tradeof between eficiency and personalization also arises. On the one hand, data-driven robotic processes can increase the number of patients cared for and extend the service. On the other hand, humans who substantially vary in their preferences and states and are deeply involved in this process may reject being treated like an object or resource in a business process.

Second, we expand the concept of BDA value creation in IS research from predominantly organizational, to social, everyday life contexts. In these contexts, with the assurance that “humans are not organized like a digital construct” [53, p. 401] nor are they machine-like in general, BDA approaches that are woven into the fabric of everyday life call for a humancentered perspective [25]. If applications of high levels of data-driven automation fail to incorporate such a perspective, they are likely to risk the acceptance and trust of the surrounding social system [72]. This holds particularly true for the case of service robots, where the dehumanization of services might threaten customer satisfaction [71]. With our approach, we add to the research that has called for a human-centered perspective in IS design instead of only targeting the closure of technology-centered problems (e.g., [25]). In the case of BDA in the context of service robotics, such a perspective goes beyond an initial system design according to human preferences, and it requires the ongoing involvement of the individual as an integral part of a human-centered system, also in terms of operating the respective service. In such operations, catering to the peculiarities of human individuals as well as granting agency to them are key design implications. Therefore, we designed an artifact not only by building on human driving patterns but also by providing humaninteraction possibilities to alter the automated driving process. Our approach makes use of an intermediate level of automation, allowing the computer to act autonomously but granting the opportunity for interventions to the human [59]. Thereby, we mitigate the perceived low interpretability of complex ML applications [68] and allow users to understand and trust the decisions of the proposed system and to undertake interventions if necessary.

## Implications for Developing Autonomous Vehicles and Sharing Business Models

Our work has important practical implications, especially for developers of AVs and operators of shared fleets. Gathering individual user preferences and learning from them to increase satisfaction with the driving experience is of utmost importance for AVs, as our additional empirical analysis (Online Supplemental Appendix A) reveals. Furthermore, the additional ability of maximizing the $\mathrm { A V s } ^ { \prime }$ energy eficiency decreases not only operating costs (as a functional value of BDA [31]) but also environmental pollution, thereby addressing consumers’ sustainability concerns—an important task for firms acting in industries that are characterized by consumers’ sensitivity to environmental friendliness [37], as a symbolic value of BDA [31]. Additionally, our proposed approach is not restricted to trade-ofs involving the variables used in this study. Being a data-driven approach, it can also be adapted to other tasks involving trade-ofs, such as in navigation or other assistance applications. Since we will experience an evolution toward fully automated vehicles, the potential of our approach can also be realized in interim phases along this path.

Regarding the applicability of the proposed approach, AV developers need to ensure the connectivity of the vehicles, at least at the beginning and at the end of the drive, such that the computational CE and experts can be synchronized. Furthermore, the vehicles require a small amount of computational capacity and storage space to handle the models that are generated. A feedback module or an option to manually drive the vehicle should also be incorporated to enable monitoring of the user’s satisfaction. Once these technical requirements are satisfied, AVs can make use of the full potential of the artifact. The real-time data processing employed by the artifact not only yields energy eficiency gains [31] and allows for leveraging the potential of big data [1], but in this case it also enables privacy-preserving data analytics: Most of the computations are performed by computational experts in the vehicles in a decentralized manner and only summary statistics for each user are transmitted to the CE, which then further aggregates these statistics over all users. Thereby, the artifact enables ethical ML without compromising the quality of the models.

Apart from that, due to the increasing amount of data available to operators of sharing business models, ML models can be trained faster than in single-user environments, allowing users to benefit from other users’ participation in the sharing economy. One may observe this phenomenon in the Evaluation Section, in which we show that the predictions of energy eficiency in the first few drives are less reliable or stable in comparison to drives that occurred at later points in time. Similarly, one may leverage sharing contexts to build models of average user preferences that serve as reasonable initial guesses for previously unseen users. The users’ comfort can thereby be kept at a high level, since they will not be forced to drive manually over extended periods of time to provide data on their preferences. Furthermore, managers are not required to make costly investments in researching the relationship between user demographics and their preferences because our proposed approach ofers a way to integrate demographic and contextual data to automatically infer preferences. We conclude that sharing business models ofer powerful ways in which to address analytical problems by enabling ML not only on a macroscopic level, as in prior studies (e.g., [20]), but simultaneously on a microscopic level. Implemented in practice, the models of average user preferences would not only prevent irrational driving policies but would also allow for faster adaptations to individual consumers, thereby increasing trust in the application and helping to draw larger audiences by addressing their individual expectations. Similarly, today, drivers for car-hailing services such as Uber and Lyft occasionally provide personalized rides by either explicitly querying their customers’ preferences or implicitly assuming them and then adapting their driving style accordingly. Since drivers are not inherently motivated to conform to customer preferences, car-hailing services often implement a rating system, which motivates drivers to provide a satisfying customer experience by attending to individual preferences. With the proposed approach, such rating systems and the manual querying of customer preferences will become obsolete as providers become increasingly capable of having preferences automatically inferred through the implemented DoL system.

## Limitations and Future Research

Although the practical evaluation of automatic driving modules, in general, is constrained due to the fact that autonomous controllers can hardly be tested in real trafic due to the potential dangers associated with machine failures, researchers have developed a series of methods for AV control. In this area, virtually all researchers have resorted to simulationbased evaluations, for example, for the basic ML-based control of AVs [67], safety-warning and driver-assistance applications [48], and autonomous reinforcement-learning-based controllers for ground vehicles [18]. Considering the dataset we have used, we realize that a larger set of attributes may enable a more meaningful evaluation of our application. While one of our objectives is to model preferred driving patterns, our evaluation only considers two or three configurable attributes. In practice, one may identify further attributes whose configurations are subject to user preferences, as shown in Online Supplemental Appendix E. Although we do not incorporate all of these attributes in our study, the proposed system —being based on LSTMs, which can automatically unveil temporal relationships in the data —allows for these attributes to be added without having to make changes to the system’s structure or perform intense feature engineering. We neglect the interaction of controlling modules with other modules in a fully functional AV and restrict our research to developing controlling modules situated at high levels of the AVs’ analytical chain and intertwining them as part of a coherent application.

Our proposed approach serves as a first step toward handling the eficiency–personalization trade-of dynamically and in a data-driven way. Regarding the predictive performance, we note that, on the given dataset, the LSTMs achieve high levels of accuracy for both the energy eficiency prediction and the preference estimation. Through these high accuracies, we have laid the foundation for efective decision making by the trade-of module. We derived testable propositions for this trade-of module based on both reasonable and extreme assumptions. An important future goal would be to test the proposed design in real-world settings and assess actual customer feedback in terms of their trust in autonomous driving. Furthermore, the application’s modularity allows for easily replaceable individual modules. Future research could, therefore, explore the suitability of other ML models for modeling user preferences and methods for boosting the process of finding a trade-of between energy eficiency and personalization (e.g., by tuning the trade-of parameters across users rather than individually for each user). Furthermore, the field of application of our approach is characterized by a meta problem that we address—the trade-of between eficiency and personalization as a key concern that goes beyond the field of mobility and transport. Future work could investigate this topic in other contexts in which high levels of data-driven automation can be deployed to raise eficiency but are delicate to implement, because there is a need for the deep immersion of humans in the respective contexts. For instance, the healthcare sector comprises various areas that are relevant and worth studying in this regard to yield further societal benefits.

## Conclusion

Service robots and AVs in particular are an instance where two developments—high levels of data-driven automation for system-level optimization as well as deep involvement in human lives—come together. Such contexts, which have not yet received much attention from IS research on BDA, can imply much more complexity than contexts where BDA might operate inside the boundaries and at the will of a particular firm, due to the incorporation of the actions and perceptions of outside actors and self-determined individuals. In particular, BDA value creation via eficiency may conflict with attending to individual preferences. Our work contributes by, first and foremost, suggesting a situated artifact as a possible handling approach for this important challenge that can serve as a guideline for designs in further contexts. As a secondary contribution, by conceptualizing and formalizing the eficiency–personalization trade-of for the case of AVs, we introduce the notion of the contradictory consequences of BDA value creation mechanisms and the context of service robotics in everyday life, where such trade-ofs are particularly relevant, to the stream of BDA value creation in IS research, thereby opening new avenues up for future work. From a practical point of view, our work lights the way toward an application of service robotics that is sustainable from environmental, economic, and social perspectives. Environmental and economic sustainability may be fostered by the better utilization of resources, for instance, in a sharing economy environment, and by data-driven energy eficiency gains that foster the performance of environmentally friendly forms of transportation. The simultaneous consideration of personal preferences and privacy as well as agency provided to the individual may foster social acceptance. While the development of service robotics in many contexts is still in its infancy, it is important to already account for factors of sustainability in this rather early stage to arrive at truly transformational digital innovations that have a meaningful positive impact on human society.

## About the Authors

Schahin Tofangchi (corresponding author; schahin.tofangchi@dak.de) has conducted research at the Chair of Digital Transformation Management at the University of Kassel, Germany, designing machine learning systems and conducting their evaluation with respect to various business cases. Dr. Tofangchi is currently Head of the AI Factory at DAK-Gesundheit, a large German health insurance company, where his work revolves around ethical AI in the broader context of digital transformation. He specializes in real-time machine learning for personalized healthcare and uncovering medication interactions, and in the big data analytics for consumer-centric products and services. His research has appeared in the leading IS conferences such as the International Conference on Information Systems.

André Hanelt (hanelt@uni-kassel.de) is an associate professor for Digital Transformation Management at the Faculty of Economics and Management at the University of Kassel. His research is concerned with the design of digital business models that build upon big data analytics and digital ecosystems, particularly in established industries and contexts such as the automotive sector. A particular focus of his work is value creation and capture in systems comprising a large number and variety of distributed actors. Dr. Hanelt’s research has appeared in the IS and management journals, such as European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, and Journal of Management Studies, as well as in the leading conferences such as the International Conference on Information Systems.

David Marz (david.marz@uni-goettingen.de) is a research associate at the Digital Transformation Research Center, situated at the Chair of Information Management at the University of Göttingen, Germany. His research is concerned with changing customer preferences and expectations in the digital age, with the focus on the increasing intertwining of digital technologies and social interactions, and on the adoption of smart, connected innovations. His work has been published in such journals and proceedings as Journal of Management Studies, Annual Meeting of the Academy of Management, and Academy of Management Specialized Conference on Big Data and Managing in a Digital Economy.

Lutz M. Kolbe (lkolbe@uni-goettingen.de) is full professor of Information Management at the University of Göttingen, comprising the interdisciplinary Digital Transformation Research Center, the Smart Mobility Research Group, and the Digital Health Research Group. His research focuses on the self-driving and shared mobility, as well as healthcare information systems, applied for example in the context of developing countries. Dr. Kolbe’s publications appear in such journals as Journal of the AIS, MIS Quarterly Executive, and Information Systems Journal, as well as in several conference proceedings, such as those of International Conference on Information Systems.
