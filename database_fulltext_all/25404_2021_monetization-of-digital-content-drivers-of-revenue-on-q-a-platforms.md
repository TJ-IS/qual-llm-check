# Monetization Of Digital Content: Drivers Of Revenue On Q&A Platforms

Hua (Jonathan) Ye<sup>a,b</sup>, Xueping Yang<sup>c,d</sup>, Xinwei Wang<sup>d</sup>, and Theophanis C. Stratopoulos<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>MIS Division of Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK, USA; <sup>b</sup>School of Accounting and Finance, University of Waterloo, Waterloo, Canada; <sup>c</sup>School of Business Administration, Zhejiang Gongshang University, Zhejiang, China; <sup>d</sup>ISOM Department, University of Auckland, Auckland, New Zealand

## ABSTRACT

Valuation of digital content has important individual, organizational, and microeconomic implications. Yet, the existing understanding of digital content consumption is focused primarily on consumption of free content. This means, that digital content is not a source of revenue for contributors and not directly tied to the revenue or expenses of these platforms and that it is practically impossible to capture its value in national income accounts. Academic and anecdotal evidence seems to indicate that sharing of revenue from paid viewership in paid question-and-answer (Q&A) platforms is a factor that diferentiates successful platforms, such as Weibo Q&A, from unsuccessful ones, such as Google Answers. Yet, the growing paid Q&A literature does not shed much light on our understanding of the drivers of revenues from paid viewership. Our study focuses on the unique revenue sharing of paid viewership among stakeholders because this feature makes paid Q&A a success and helps ensure the sustainability of the hosting social media platform. This study draws upon signaling theory to identify the drivers of the sales of paid viewership. We further draw on the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation and the literature on social media trend and the literature on information perishability to hypothesize the diferential moderating impacts of question price and content perishability. Consistent with the literature, we found the positive impacts of social media status and social endorsement on the sales of paid viewership. Extending past literature, we found an inverted-U impact of social feedback and the diferential moderating efects of question price and content perishability on the sales of paid viewership.

KEYWORDS Content monetization; paid Q&A; sales of paid viewership; content perishability

## Introduction

The digitalization of content has spawned numerous new business models and monetization opportunities [13, 72] such as the subscription [3, 64, 75] and the pay-per-item model [33, 50, 51]. Paid question & answer (Q&A)—in its manifestations through Weibo Q&A [57]—is a new social media content monetization model [38, 94]. It difers from prior Q&A models such as Yahoo! Answers, Quora and Google Answers in three fundamental ways. First, askers can obtain personalized answers from members of the network (answerers) for a price [78, 94]. Second, it charges viewers a nominal flat fee to view the answer. Third, all stakeholders (askers, answerers, and the platform) share the revenues from paid viewership [38, 91]. Understanding what drives sales from paid viewership, the overarching focus of this study, has important practical and research implications.

The revenue (i.e., sales from paid viewership) sharing mechanism incentivizes both askers and answerers to contribute content [36, 77]. This in turn heightens the engagement of viewers [52] and their content purchase on the platform [14]. Arguably, this mechanism helps avoid the dwindling user trafic and unsatisfactory content sales, which are factors attributed to the failure of past paid Q&A platforms such as Google Answers [8, 32]. Understanding the drivers of sales from paid viewership may help ensure the sustainability of paid Q&A platforms, and, hence, it is of vital importance to platform managers as well as askers and answerers.

Related paid Q&A literature is extremely prolific and can be classified based on its focus from the following four angles/perspectives: 1) the behavior of askers [e.g., 58, 84, 92, 93]; 2) the behavior of answerers [e.g., 38, 57, 94]; 3) user engagement [e.g., 52]; and 4) the quality of answers [e.g., 36, 60]. Yet, the study of the sales of paid viewership in paid Q&A remains uncharted. Without a nuanced understanding of the sales of paid viewership, the literature misses a crucial component needed for developing viable content monetization models.

Several distinct characteristics of paid Q&A platforms provide the foundation for our exploration of the sales of paid viewership. First, contrary to the consumption of content in free content platforms, users who want to view an answer in a paid Q&A platform must pay a fee. The fee, albeit low, changes viewers’ behavior away from mindlessly browsing free content and motivates them to evaluate whether the answer is worth the payment [94]. In addition, paid Q&A is a typical experience product whose quality cannot be directly observed. It can be evaluated only by consumption [9]. Therefore, we argue that prior to their purchase, viewers are likely to search for useful signals to predict quality [90, 94].

Second, in a typical subscription model, users pay the subscription fee to gain access to all content on the platform. This means that the platform is the focal point of transaction and platform features afect transactions between users and the platform [3, 72]. By contrast, in a paid Q&A model, the platform is simply a medium—a third party [91]—on which an exchange of content among peer users takes place, and user features [94] matter to these transactions. Thus, we argue that answerers’ characteristics and users’ social interactions with answerers [7, 31, 94] may serve as a set of useful signals that potential viewers can use to inform their purchase decisions in paid Q&A platforms. More specifically, we propose that social signals, such as the social media status of an answerer [31], as well as social endorsement (e.g., likes) and social feedback (e.g., comments) that an answer receives [14, 90] are the drivers of the sales of paid viewership.

Third, unlike free Q&A [e.g., 49], paid Q&A requires askers to prepay a question price to answerers for answering their questions [38]. This question price paid by the asker is visible to all members on the paid Q&A platform. Therefore, we argue that potential viewers may use the question price as a useful economic signal for making the decision on whether the answer is worth the payment. Drawing on the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation [e.g., 22], we posit that question price triggers viewers to engage in a systematic processing of social signals and thus can alter the efects of these signals on viewers decision to pay the viewership fee.

Finally, our existing understanding of content consumption is primarily related to free [e.g., 21, 69] or subscription-based content [e.g., 3], and is developed under the assumption that content value does not change with time [5, 61]. However, the content of paid Q&A platforms, and social medial in general, tends to be perishable [55]. For example, a YouTube video is estimated to be out of date within 20 days and a tweet on Twitter only lasts 18 minutes [55]. Content perishability, the degree to which user interests and attention of the content decay with time, may change how viewers attend to social signals [22] and therefore afect their decisions on whether to pay to view the answer.

In summary, our study aims to examine two research questions: (1) What are the antecedents of the sales of paid viewership? and (2) How do question price and content perishability moderate the impacts of the antecedents on the sales of paid viewership? To answer these questions, we track a random sample of questions from Weibo Q&A over time and construct panel data. The results indicate that social media status and social endorsement positively afect the sales of paid viewership while social feedback has an inverted-U impact on the sales of paid viewership. Furthermore, question price enhances the impact of social endorsement and the inverted-U impact of social feedback but undermines the impact of social media status on the sales of paid Q&A. Moreover, content perishability reduces the impacts of social media status and social endorsement but strengthens the inverted-U impact of social feedback.

This study makes three principal research contributions (see details in the theoretical contribution section). First, it contributes to the paid Q&A literature by documenting that certain social signals (i.e., social media status, social endorsement, and social feedback) are the drivers of sales of paid viewership. In particular, this study identifies a heretofore undocumented relationship (i.e., the inverted-U impact of social feedback). Second, the findings add to our understanding of the impacts of unique features associated with paid Q&A (i.e., question price and content perishability) on paid viewership sales. Third, our study extends signaling theory by integrating it with the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation and the related literature to account for the interactions between question price and various social signals and between content perishability and various social signals. The findings also inform practice on how, and under what conditions the sales of paid viewership in paid Q&A can be enhanced.

Perhaps, the most important contribution of our study is on the multifaceted discourse related to the valuation of digital assets, such as data and digital content. The literature on digital content consumption [21, 63] is focused primarily on consumption of free content [48, 68, 69] on platforms that rely on advertising fees. These platforms contribute to the problem of valuation of digital assets. First, while these platforms rely on user generated content, contributors do not receive any direct monetary payment from the platform and have no control over their intellectual property [12, 45]. Second, seeing that the content is not directly tied to the revenue or expenses of these platforms, it is not captured in the financial statements of these firms. The content appears as intangible asset [11, 53, 70], thus contributing to the growing discrepancy between market valuation and book value of firms that rely on such digital content/intangible assets [76]. Third, the digital content is not directly reflected in the financial statement of firms or income of contributors. It is practically impossible to capture its value in national income accounts. The paid Q&A model, to the extent that it is a viable alternative, can help in dealing with some of these valuation issues. In a paid Q&A platform, revenue from paid viewership is shared among all stakeholders. Our study contributes to this data valuation conversation by focusing on one of the factors (revenue from paid viewership) that is critical for the financial success of all stakeholders and by extension the viability of the paid Q&A model (i.e., an alternative to free content models).

## Theoretical Background

In this section, we first review the literature to identify the research gaps that this study strives to address. Next, we introduce the theoretical foundation of this study, that is, signaling theory, to identify the antecedents of the sales of paid viewership. Finally, we theorize the moderating efects of the unique contextual features of paid Q&A (question price and content perishability) by using the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation and the related literature.

## Prior Research on Paid Q&A

Past paid Q&A literature can be classified in the following four streams. The first stream of research focuses on the antecedents of askers’ payment intention [93], the drivers of askers switching from free to paid Q&A [58, 92], the impact of askers’ question framing on their Q&A profit [84], and their gaming behaviors in paid Q&A [38]. For example, Zhao et al. [93] examined the antecedents of askers’ payment intention from the perspective of cost and benefit. They found that financial costs, financial benefits, social support, selfenhancement, and entertainment are important antecedents of the perceived value of Q&A, which in turn afects askers’ payment intention. Interestingly, Jan et al. [38] found that certain askers game the paid Q&A system for profits. They found that users can make a profit not only by answering questions but also by asking good questions. Askers may profit by colluding with the answerer or asking many questions (with an extremely low question price) to bait viewers to pay for viewership.

The second stream of research focuses on answerers’ behaviors, for example, the number of questions answered [94] and their response speed of answering a paid question [38]. For example, Zhao et al. [94] found that answerers’ reputation and the asked price afect the number of questions they answered. Jan et al. [38] found that payments can solicit a quick response from experts. The third stream of research focuses on the impacts of adopting the paid Q&A mechanism on user engagement [52]. For example, Kuang et al. [52] found that the adoption of paid Q&A increases viewers’ platform engagement, including posting, liking, sharing, and commenting. The fourth stream of research studies answer quality [e.g., 36, 60]. According to Hsieh et al. [36] and Chen et al. [16], the adoption of paid Q&A does not afect the quality of answers. Mousavi et al. [60] created an algorithmic solution for measuring answer quality and adjusting answer orders to encourage high-quality subsequent answers.

However, limited research discusses the sales of paid viewership of answers in paid Q&A. Studies [92,93,94,49,39] imply that question price, answerers’ characteristics, and answers’ characteristics could afect the sales of paid viewership. Yet, a minimal amount of research has theorized and empirically validated such impacts. Our study builds on signaling theory to identify the antecedents of the sales of paid viewership, which is introduced next.

## Signaling Theory and Antecedents of the Sales of Paid Viewership

Signaling theory explains how individuals use signals to reduce the uncertainty associated with selecting among a choice set in the face of incomplete and asymmetrical information [73, 74]. In situations where the quality or value of a choice cannot be directly observed, decision-makers use observable actions or characteristics as signals to conjecture unobservable attributes and likely outcomes and make decisions accordingly [6].

In paid Q&A, viewers cannot evaluate the quality of the answers without paying [94]. The cost of answer access motivates viewers to evaluate the worthiness of the payment. Viewers thus need to attend to observable signals to make the decision. According to this theory, individuals need to rely on signals to conjecture unobservable attributes between a signaler and a receiver and make decisions accordingly [74]. In the context of the sales of content products [14, 90], signalers are entities that provide information about the content. It can be the creator of the content, the content itself, and users who consume the content [31]. Although these entities may not deliberately generate and send out the signals, their behaviors can be used by those who observe the signals [90]. Following this literature, we argue that observable signals can originate from the answerer and the answer itself [31, 90]. At the answerer level, reputation such as the social media status of an answerer may afect the reach of the posted information [68] and users’ purchase decisions [94, 95]. At the answer level, question price, social endorsement (likes), and social feedback (comments) that an answer receives may afect its sales.

## Unique Contextual Features of Paid Q&A

Question price and content perishability diferentiate paid Q&A from free Q&A and other similarly themed content monetization mechanisms (e.g., Google Answers). First, unlike free Q&A [e.g., 49], paid Q&A requires askers to prepay a price (known as question price) to answerers for answering their questions [38]. The price paid to answerers in paid Q&A is market-driven rather than fixed as in the subscription model. Distinct answerers command their specific prices. Second, online content like paid Q&A tends to have a short shelf life [55]. This means that it behaves like a perishable product, and its value decays as its temporal relevance lessens [17]. To account for the impacts of these unique contextual features on the sales of paid viewership, we draw on the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation for question price, and the related literature for content perishability.

## Question Price and the Cognitive View of Signal Attention and Interpretation

Although signaling theory can explain how signalers provide information to receivers, it cannot explain how receivers attend to, interpret, and act upon the information [6]. Signaling theory assumes that each signal is equally noticed by receivers [67]. Yet, decisionmakers are bounded rational and cannot attend to all available signals [71, 80]. Ambiguity arises when multiple viable interpretations surround a given piece of information [6]. Such signals may be ignored or cannot inform decision making [67]. In light of this challenge, the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation is developed to complement signaling theory [22].

The cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation posits that multiple and often conflicting signals are attended to and interpreted with two types of attentional control mechanisms, i.e., heuristic versus systematic processing [22]. Heuristic processing is automatic and triggered by recent stimuli [66]. For heuristic processing, individuals rely on cognitive simplifications to cope with uncertainty and information asymmetry [23]. Using heuristic processing, individuals make decisions based merely on a subset of available information [46]. Individuals are likely to rely on more available context information, for example, the identity of signalers [23], to make decisions. By contrast, systematic processing is non-automatic [66], and is driven by high-level goals [46]. The presence of a high-level goal stimulates an active search for information relevant to that goal [22]. Using systematic processing, individuals engage in an efortful search for relevant information to achieve that goal [71]. This means that a decision-maker will attend to as many signals as possible to inform their decisions [66].

Individuals only act upon signals that have been noticed [6]. Systematic attentional control can help decision-makers attend to more observable signals than heuristic attentional control [22]. In the context of paid Q&A, we argue that the payment of an answer viewership fee stimulates the systematic processing of viewers [20]. This triggers viewers to search for more signals for decision making and afect how they interpret such signals for actions. Furthermore, this view posits that multiple signals can work together to afect decision making [22, 67]. We expect the economic signal of question price may interact with social signals of the answer and answerer characteristics to inform viewer actions. While prior literature explores the direct impact of question price [e.g., 14, 16,36], limited research has studied its moderating efect. Thus, we explore the moderating efect of question price in this study.

## Content Perishability and Literature on Social Media Trend

Content perishability refers to the degree to which user interests and attention of the content decay with time. Content of high perishability is intended for only a specific point in time and has a short period of temporal relevance [17, 83]. Once the specific timepoint passed, the usefulness or value of content drops dramatically [17]. For example, the answer to a question about daily or weekly stock picks loses its value drastically afterward. Another example of perishability would be the trailers of upcoming movies, whose value drops by the time the movies are shown. The sooner user interests in the content drop, the more perishable it is.

Prior literature on social media trend has a similar standpoint on the temporal nature of online content [61]. It posits that user interests in a particular piece of content grow and fade rapidly over time [89]. Social media platforms tend to encourage an increased speed of content production and consumption to attract high trafic and maintain their sustainability [31, 89]. The resultant increased number of items on social media platforms heightens the competition for the limited amount of user attention [87] and dramatically shortens the attention intervals of a single piece of content [59]. The shortened attention interval further increases the perishability of social media content.

Past literature on information perishability studies mainly the direct impact of perishability on various outcomes, including information use and sharing [17], information relevance [81], and growth of follower networks [88]. Although studies [e.g., 17, 81] imply that perishability may indirectly afect the sales of online content, little research has studied the moderating impact of perishability on content sales. Thus, our study aims to explore the moderating of content perishability on the sales of paid viewership.

## Antecedents of Paid Viewership

Status refers to the reputation, respect, prestige, and admiration aforded by others and is a fundamental human motive [1]. The link between status and individual behaviors and performance has been empirically validated in various contexts, such as knowledge contribution in online communities [7], mobile app creation on mobile phone platforms [47], impulse purchase in social commerce [95], and the sales of knowledge products [14]. More relevant studies find that the desire for status strongly motivates individuals to answer more questions and exert efort [94], which contributes to performance [26, 28]. Past literature suggests that status in terms of the number of followers determines content consumption [21, 31] and askers’ willingness to pay [94, 95, 96]. Therefore, answerer’s social media status can be a useful signal that viewers can process to evaluate answer quality prior to purchase decision (i.e., payment of viewership fee).

Hypothesis 1 (H1): Social media status positively afects the sales of paid viewership.

![](/api/attachments/7QQTHBM7/fulltext/images/3b4413056cca829b6db606d87b88b223e303db1a4e726e572a777bcec9afdc56.jpg)  
Figure 1. Research model of paid Q&A.

Social media postings can receive social endorsement by being favored (liked) by one’s social network [21, 68]. Liking or favoring content is an important signal that afects viewers’ content consumption [14, 21]. Past literature on social media suggests that socially favoring a post represents an endorsement and referral [34, 56, 62]. Such an endorsement brings credibility to content quality and increases content consumption. For example, individuals tend to consume music favored by their friends or by the community [21]. Similarly, online courses with more upvotes (likes) receive more sales [90]. Additionally, the number of likes represents users’ satisfaction and positive attitude towards a post [41]. Past literature also finds that when integrating the “likes” into marketing messages, product sales increases [42, 90]. As per signaling theory [18], this endorsement of liking increases the likelihood that a viewer pays to view the answer, that is, the sales of paid viewership.

## Hypothesis 2 (H2): Social endorsement positively afects the sales of paid viewership

Past literature suggests that social feedback via commenting increases the richness of information related to the product and decreases the uncertainty associated with decision making [27]. Social feedback reveals consumption experience and is particularly important for experience products [14, 86], such as paid Q&A. Social feedback can also raise buyers awareness [10] and, hence, is positively related to content consumption and purchase [14, 85, 90]. As social feedback grows, viewers can gradually receive more quality-related information and consumption experience about paid Q&A. Viewers are gradually persuaded by the information in social feedback and attracted to pay for the answer.

However, as social feedback accumulates, critical information about the answer or its important elements will be leaked out via the comments (i.e., spoilers) [44]. The literature on spoiler efects finds that spoilers kill viewers’ curiosity, and the gratification/enjoyment of content consumption [24, 29], thereby reducing viewers’ intention to consume the spoiled content [19, 43, 44]. In a similar vein, social feedback may contain revelatory information and serve as spoilers to the answer of paid Q&A. As social feedback accumulates beyond a tipping point (vertex), viewers can guess what the answer is from the comments [38]. This efect reduces viewers’ willingness to buy the answer. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Social feedback has an inverted U (concave) relationship with the sales of paid viewership.

## Moderating Efects of Question Price

According to the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation, signals of high observability are likely to be processed by heuristic attentional control [22, 66]. A high question price is a strong economic signal of answer value, that is likely to trigger viewers curiosity [71] and drive them to search for additional information [46]. As viewers are processing more signals, this is likely to reduce the diagnostic value of the answerer’s social media status, leading to a decline in its efect. Since the signal from an answerer’s social media status, an available context signal [23], is already attended to by automatic heuristic processing, viewers will take it for granted and allocate less attention to it. Therefore, we expect:

Hypothesis 4 (H4): Question price negatively moderates the impact of an answerer’s social media status on the sales of paid viewership.

Compared with answerers’ characteristics, signals from the answer’s social endorsement and social feedback are at a lower attention level and hence less observable [66]. Such signals do not receive enough attention from decision-makers if they are heuristically processed [22]. A strong economic signal (i.e., a high question price) triggers viewers’ curiosity [71] and search for additional information [46]. Thus, a high question price motivates viewers to attend to less observable signals. Consequently, viewers pay more attention to the social endorsement signals of a Q&A when they use systematic processing to inform their decisions. Therefore, we expect that

Hypothesis 5 (H5): Question price positively moderates the impact of an answer’s social endorsement on the sales of paid viewership.

The cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation argues that individuals normally use heuristics to process observable information for cognitive simplification [22]. Given that social feedback is less observable, information from social feedback is less likely to be attended to and hence less likely to afect viewers’ decision-making. A question with a low price may not trigger viewers to use systematic processing. However, a highly priced question may trigger viewers to use systematic processing. Viewers are more likely to search for detailed peer feedback information from comments. The systematic processing motivates viewers to efortfully search and attend to the rich information in social feedback to inform their purchase decision as per the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation [22]. By efortful search, viewers are likely to attend to the leaked critical information from comments. This decreases their curiosity and gratification and deters them from further paying to view the answer due to the spoiler efects [24, 29]. Thus, the systematic processing elicited by a high question price makes the curvilinear relationship between social feedback and the sales of paid viewership steeper and the tipping point comes sooner than later (i.e., shift to the left) (see Figure 2 for illustration). As a result, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 6 (H6): A rising question price makes the inverted U-shaped relationship between an answer’s social feedback and the sales of paid viewership steeper and shifts the tipping point (vertex) to the left.

## Moderating Efects of Content Perishability

The literature on social media trend suggests that the sales of digital content on social media are influenced by potential viewers’ attention to and interest in it [14, 90]. Due to the ease of creation and widespread dissemination of social media content, users’ attention can be easily captured by more emergent content, leading to a quick decay of user attention [15] and high perishability of digital content. This then threatens the sales of digital content even if such content has superb other features [4, 92] because perishability determines the duration of the answer’s temporal relevance [17].

![](/api/attachments/7QQTHBM7/fulltext/images/ee3a802da7e5773314910511733437f073acdd9a2f48e87e6442b32d6dd7e516.jpg)  
Figure 2. The illustration figure on the moderating efect of question price.

If we start with an extreme case scenario, that paid Q&A is out of date (i.e., of no interest to a user), then social media status, social endorsement, and social feedback should not matter to viewers’ payment decisions. Viewers will discard the paid Q&A without evaluating other aspects. Following past literature [17, 81, 83], we argue that only if the information meets user interest, will viewers shift focus to its other attributes such as economic value, time, virality, quality, and creators’ characteristics, among others. Consequently, as perishability increases, social media status and social endorsement have a lower efect on viewers purchase decisions. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 7 (H7): Content perishability negatively moderates the impact of an answerer’s social media status on the sales of paid viewership

Hypothesis 8 (H8): Content perishability negatively moderates the impact of an answer’s social endorsement on the sales of paid viewership.

Past literature suggests that low perishable content is likely to have lasting user interest and attention and its value drops little with time [17, 88]. Therefore, we expect that a Q&A of low perishability is likely to attract viewer interest and attention for a longer time. Such lasting interest and attention can continuously attract more viewers to pay to view the answer and are likely to mitigate the influence of the spoiler information from social feedback. Hence, the inverted-U curve of the social feedback impact will be flattened. In addition, as the value of low perishable Q&A drops little with time [81], viewers do not have the urgency to make decisions and are likely to search social feedback for the information in a less hasty manner. Thus, the spoiler efect of social feedback is mitigated (i.e., a flatter inverted-U curve) and the tipping point of the inverted-U curve arrives later (i.e., shift to the right).

However, when the Q&A is highly perishable, its value drops significantly with time. Viewers have the urgency to make decision quickly and start a systematic information search for decision making. As social feedback increases to a certain amount, viewers delve into peer comments for the leaked critical information sooner. The Q&A loses its value at a much faster pace and is less likely to attract many viewers to pay to view the answer. Hence, when content perishability is high, the spoiler efect of the leaked information is amplified significantly and quickly, reducing the positive impact of social feedback on the sales of paid viewership. As a result, the inverted-U curve is sharpened, and the tipping point of the U-curve arrives sooner (i.e., shift to the left). Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 9 (H9): A rising content perishability makes the inverted U-shaped relationship between an answer’s social feedback and the sales of paid viewership steeper and shifts the tipping point (vertex) to the left.

## Methodology

## Research Setting

Our research setting is based on Weibo Q&A, a Chinese social media platform launched in December 2016. The platform belongs to Sina Weibo, one of the most popular social media sites in China. By September 2018, Sina Weibo had 446 million monthly active users. Among them, over 190 thousand are celebrities or specialists in various industries. As shown in Figure 3, Weibo Q&A creators include question askers and answerers. A typical Q&A is initiated by an asker who posts a question to an answerer. The asker needs to prepay the question price set by the nominated answerer and obtains a full refund if the answerer does not reply within three days. Any user of the platform may be an asker, but only authorized users (e.g., online celebrities, specialists, or opinion leaders) can become answerers, and they are allowed to charge for answering questions.

Once the answerer answered the proposed question, a link is generated that discloses the question, but not the answer. The link is automatically broadcasted to the answerer’s social network. Users who want to view the answer (viewers) must pay a flat fee of one renminbi (1

## Data Collection

We collected data from Weibo Q&A (see the screenshot of answerers and popular questions in Figure A1 and the screenshot of a Q&A in Figure A2 in Appendix A). We crawled the web data by employing a keyword search method to obtain all answered questions on September 15, 2017. We obtained a list of newly answered questions in the following five days, resulting in 536 unique Q&As. We then collected periodic data of this list of Q&As every three days until November 21, 2017, before all sampled Q&As became free of charge.<sup>2</sup> Each observation recorded the data of Q&A, the answerer, and the asker. In total, we obtained 23 cycles of periodic data. After removing some of the Q&As that were deleted by askers during our data collection period, 417 Q&As remained with a panel dataset of 9,591 observations.

## Variable Measurement

The key dependent variable in our empirical analysis is the sales of paid viewership, which is the number of times an answer is viewed.<sup>3</sup> Each time is worth 1 RMB. Our major independent variables include various signals (social medial status, social endorsement, and social feedback). Following prior research, we measured answerers’ social media status with follower volume, i.e., the number of an answerer’s followers [94]. Answer’s social endorsement signal was measured by the volume of likes that the Q&A receives [68], and social feedback was measured by the number of comments the Q&A receives [86].

We measured question price using the specific price set by answerers on their Q&A pages. We measured content perishability using the expiration duration minus the half-life of answer sales, that is, content perishability = 90 – (half-life). In our study, the shorter the halflife of a paid Q&A, the more perishable it is. The half-life of answer sales represents the number of days required for obtaining half of the paid answer sales. Half-life is a formula used to measure the time required for a quantum to reduce to half of its original value. It is frequently used to describe the decay of information goods. We employed the formula of journal citation half-life suggested by Tsay [79] to compute the viewership half-life of a paid Q&A. Calculation follows the formula: $t _ { h } = T _ { M } + P _ { M N ^ { * } } ( C _ { M } - 0 . 5 ) / ( C _ { M } - C _ { N } )$ , where $t _ { h }$ is the estimated number of days that one Q&A achieves 50 percent of the total sales. We calculated the accumulated percentage of sales each time. $C _ { M }$ and $C _ { N }$ are percentage values that are left and right nearest to 0.5, respectively. $T _ { M }$ is the number of days until time M, and $P _ { M N }$ is the number of days between time M and N. The default value for $t _ { h }$ is zero when the total sale volume is zero. We also included control variables that may impact the dependent variable, including the volume of forwards that an answer receives, the number of users that the answerer follows, the number of asker’s followers, the number of asker’s postings, and the number of users that the asker follows. ## Model Estimation

We tested the proposed hypotheses with our panel dataset. A Hausman (1978) test is significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 5 1 . 0 8 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , suggesting that the fixed efects estimation is preferred. Apart from the factors we focused on, some unobservable factors may confound our results. When these factors are stable over time (e.g., viewers’ cultural characteristics), the fixed efects panel model (FEPM) can be properly employed to account for endogeneity issues [82]. Therefore, we estimated a FEPM for hypothesis testing. Considering potential endogeneity issues, we applied two-stage least squares (2SLS) and Poisson panel model for robustness checks.

Considering data skewness, we performed log-transformation on the dependent variable, independent variables, and control variables. The normality test on the log-transformed data shows a normal distribution of residuals. Our dependent variable is the sales of paid viewership. The subscript i in the equation represents the paid Q&A, and subscript t represents the time point. We estimated the following panel data model for the main efects:

$$

$$

for $i = 1 , 2 , \ldots 4 1 7 .$ , and $t { = } 1 , 2 , \ldots 2 3 ; \beta$ is the coeficients’ estimates. $\mu _ { i }$ and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ are the random error terms, to control for the idiosyncratic efects. Since question price and content perishability are time-invariant variables, we did not include them in our estimation model.

As moderator variables (question price and content perishability) are time-invariant, the traditional interaction terms method to test moderating efects may introduce biases [37]. Thus, we transformed them into low and high levels by mean-splitting the sample. The value for the low levels of question price and content perishability is 0, whereas the value for their high levels is 1.

## Robustness Checks

We conducted the instrumental variable analysis to address the potential endogeneity issues. We identified three instrumental variables (IVs) for the independent variables. First, an answerer’s following volume can influence the answerer’s follower volume. Answerers may receive reciprocal followers from their followings [35]. Yet, the following volume may not afect the sales because other users need to pay to view it. Second, we coded the ofline status of answerers (answerer’s ofline status) as the IV according to the information they provided in their Weibo profile. Two authors coded ofline status independently with an agreement rate of 97 percent. We coded ofline status as 1 if the answerer was a field expert or worked in an esteemed institute. Ofline status afects an answerer’s follower size on social media due to status migration [54] but cannot afect the sales of one particular paid answer. Ofline status can also bring in endorsement and support from ofline [54], afecting social endorsement and social feedback. Third, we collected data for the answerer’s platform tenure as the IV. A longer platform tenure afords the user more time to develop followers [28] and accumulate more social capital [25] (e.g., endorsement and feedback) but may not afect the sales of paid viewership.

We assessed the validity of the IVs. First, we tested the minimal canonical correlation between SMS and the IVs, which was statistically diferent zero. This result demonstrates the relevance of our instrumental variables. Second, we used Cragg-Donald Wal F-statistics to test weak instruments. The F-statistic values of these IVs were greater than 10—the typical threshold required to address the weak instrument problem [2]. Thus, our IVs are valid.

We then applied 2SLS with the FEPM to correct for potential endogeneity. The main efect results of 2SLS in Model 1 of Table 5 remain the same as those in Table 3. The moderating efect results of 2SLS in Table 6 are the same as those in Table 4, except for H7. All major findings still hold, supporting the robustness of our research findings.

We also conducted a Poisson panel analysis to check if our findings are robust across estimation models. The main efect results of Poisson analysis shown in Model 2 of Table 5 remain the same as those in Table 3. The moderating efect results of the Poisson analysis in Table 7 are the same as those in Table 4. All findings hold. Overall, the robustness checks increase the confidence in our findings.

## Discussion

Academic [92] and anecdotal evidence [8, 32] seems to indicate that sharing of revenue from paid viewership in paid Q&A platforms is a factor that diferentiates successful platforms, such as Weibo Q&A, from unsuccessful ones, such as Google Answers. Yet, the growing paid Q&A literature does not shed much light on our understanding of the drivers of revenues from paid viewership. In this study, we drew on signaling theory to hypothesize the role of social signals (i.e., social status, social endorsement, and social feedback) as antecedents of the sales of paid viewership, and we theorized how two unique features of paid Q&A platforms (i.e., question price and content perishability) moderate the efects of the social signals on the sales. We tested our proposed hypotheses using panel data from Weibo Q&A. The results of our empirical analysis, as well as several robustness checks, support our hypotheses. ## Theoretical Contributions

Our findings make several important contributions to the existing literature. First, our current understanding of content consumption [48, 68, 69] is focused primarily on the free model. In this study, we identified several design features in the paid Q&A model that make it incompatible with the free model. By focusing on these features, we contribute to the digital content consumption literature [21, 63]. Unlike the free model, in a paid Q&A platform, users who want to consume content (Q&A) in a timely fashion must pay a viewership fee, and revenues from sales of such fees are shared among the key stakeholders. In addition, prior Q&A literature has not explored the antecedents of revenues from paid viewership. Thus, it makes our study one of the first to empirically validate the direct positive impact of social media status and social endorsement on the sales of paid viewership. Moreover, and contrary to prior literature that proposes a linear relationship between social feedback and the sales of paid viewership [e.g., 86, 90], we proposed and empirically validated that the relationship is curvilinear (inverted-U).

Second, in addition to sales from paid viewership, question price and content perishability are two design features that diferentiate the paid Q&A model from the previously studied free models. Prior literature studies mainly the direct impact of question price [e.g., 14] and content perishability [e.g., 81]. Our study extends the literature by identifying diferential moderating efects of question price and content perishability. Our research ofers a more nuanced understanding of how question price and content perishability alter viewers’ attention to, and evaluation of, various signals for payment decisions. This study thus represents a much-needed enrichment of the paid Q&A literature.

Third, to the best of our knowledge, prior literature attempts to employ signaling theory in various settings [6], but not in paid Q&A. Past studies of signaling (notable exception Plummer et al. [67]) also generally do not account for the situations when decision makers have to attend to conflicting signals. This study extends signaling theory by integrating the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation to account for how various signals are attended to and work together to afect viewers’ decisions on content purchase. Our study further extends signaling theory by integrating social media trend literature and information perishability literature to account for the moderating impacts of content perishability. Thus, it extends the applicability of signaling theory to an emerging but important context (paid Q&A). The set of signals identified, and the model of their interactions can guide researchers to explore consumer decision making in other contexts where product value decays.

Although the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation posits the interaction among signals, few studies postulate the directions of these interaction efects and empirically validate them. This study contributes to this cognitive view [22, 66] by hypothesizing and empirically quantifying the interaction efects between question price and other social signals. The findings enrich our understanding and extend the cognitive view by suggesting that signals can enhance and/or attenuate one another in afecting signal receivers’ decision making. Furthermore, extending the exploration of Plummer et al. [67] of the interactions among social signals, this study provides empirical evidence for the interactions among the economic signal, content perishability, and social signals.

## Managerial Implications

Our study has several important managerial implications. It sheds light on the possibility that social media platforms such as Facebook and Twitter may be able to switch from an ad-revenue dominant business model [63, 75] to a paid Q&A business model. Our findings provide guidelines for such social media platforms on how to monetize users’ content. In particular, social media platforms should encourage content producers to strengthen and improve both economic and social signals that are conducive to content sales. First, platforms may direct their marketing strategy toward these identified and examined signals. For example, the social media status of an answerer on Weibo has a significant efect on the sales of paid viewership, so it is critical for a digital content platform to build an efective status-ranking mechanism that can facilitate the verification of members’ social identities [65]. Second, the interaction and endorsement from other users on the answer afect the sales of paid viewership. Platform managers can thus encourage viewers to engage in the interaction and raise answer awareness.

Additionally, our research suggests that digital content platforms should consider providing guidelines to answerers regarding the pricing strategy for enhancing their social image. As suggested in the findings, the efect of question price on answer sales is not monotonic. Depending on the context, it may increase or decrease the impacts of various signals on the sales of paid viewership. Platforms may want to inform their answerers of such efects so that they can consider this when setting the question price. For example, a high question price combined with a high social endorsement can be a good strategy for answerers. Second, practitioners should establish a mechanism to guide viewers’ information disclosure behavior. Our findings suggest that while social feedback should be encouraged to attract potential viewers’ interest and curiosity, a control mechanism to discourage the information leakage from the feedback is necessary to alleviate potential negative impacts.

Although the paid Q&A model intends to profit from producing perishable content, the less-perishable content can sustain non-paying viewers’ attention. This will generate more user engagement and improve platform sustainability [52]. Our research suggests that platform managers can guide the production of less-perishable content. They can encourage askers and answerers to co-create the questions and their answers that are less perishable [86].

## Limitations and Future Research

Our findings should be interpreted in terms of their limitations. First, our research model is derived from and tested in one platform (Weibo Q&A). Nevertheless, our findings may be generalizable to similar paid Q&A platforms, such as Fenda.com and Zhihu.com [38, 94]. Future research can replicate our model in other types of paid content platforms and test the generalizability of our findings. Also, we collected our data in China, a country with numerous unique characteristics, such as the influential role of government regulations on culture, which may afect individuals’ online behaviors. Future research can benefit from accounting for the idiosyncratic features of China and testing the generalizability of our model in Western countries.

Second, we did not study the process of question answering. We cannot capture the amount of efort that answerers have put into answering, that is, the content production cost. Future research can explore what makes answerers produce high-quality answers, and how answerers determine the question price. What the optimal pricing strategy is for such content remains unknown. In addition, the research was conducted at a question-answer level. Future research can explore this phenomenon from a platform perspective, that is, whether such a function will increase user-to-user and content-to-content interactions [87].

Third, the data we used to test the research model are suficient but limited. Future research can collect more data from this site and validate whether our findings still hold. Fourth, we only focused on the volume of the comments instead of their sentiment. Future research can conduct a textual analysis of the comment sentiment [e.g., 39] and examine its impact on paid viewership. Fifth, this study used objective data collected from the website. Future research can explore the underlying behavioral mechanisms of viewers in signal attention and interpretation using methods such as surveys or interviews. Lastly, future research can explore viewer behaviors from the perspective of herding [40].

## Conclusion

Valuation of digital content has important individual, organizational, and microeconomic implications. Yet, our existing understanding of digital content consumption is focused primarily on consumption of free content. This means, that digital content is not a source of revenue for contributors and not directly tied to the revenue or expenses of these platforms, and that it is practically impossible to capture its value in national income accounts. The past attempts to monetize such content for stakeholders have not been successful (i.e., Google Answers). Recent models, such as paid Q&A are a viable alternative, but the existing paid Q&A literature does not focus on the single issue that matters to such models (sales from paid viewership). Our study focused on the unique revenue sharing of paid viewership among stakeholders because this feature makes paid Q&A a success and helps ensure the sustainability of the hosting social media platform. This study drew upon signaling theory to identify the antecedents of the sales of paid viewership. We further drew on the cognitive view of signal attention and interpretation and the literature on social media trend and the literature on information perishability to examine the diferential moderating impacts of question price and content perishability. Consistent with the literature, we found the positive impacts of social media status and social endorsement on the sales of paid viewership. Extending past literature, we found an inverted-U impact of social feedback and the diferential moderating efects of question price and content perishability on the sales of paid viewership.
