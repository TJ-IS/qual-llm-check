# Designing Online Virtual Advisors to Encourage Customer Self-disclosure: A Theoretical Model and an Empirical Test

Sameh Al-Natour <sup>a</sup>, Izak Benbasat <sup>b</sup>, and Ron Cenfetelli<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Ted Rogers School of Management, Ryerson University, Toronto, Ontario, Canada; <sup>b</sup>Sauder School of Business, The University of British Columbia, Vancouver, Canada

## ABSTRACT

Virtual advisors (VA) are tools that assist users in making decisions. Using VAs necessitates the disclosure of personal information, especially when they are employed in personalized contexts such as healthcare, where disclosure is vital to providing valid and accurate advice. Yet, extant research has largely overlooked the factors that encourage or inhibit users’ from disclosing to VAs. In contrast, this study investigates the determinants of users’ intentions to self-disclose, and examines how VAs can be designed to enhance these intentions. The results of a study in the context of skin care advice reveal that the intention to disclose to a VA is not only the product of a rational process, but that perceptions of the VA and the relationship with it are important. The results further show that a parsimonious set of design elements can be used to endow a VA with desired characteristics that enhance the willingness to disclose. The study contributes to our understanding of the factors influencing users’ intentions to provide personal information to a VA, which extend beyond the expected benefits and costs. The study further demonstrates that social exchange theory can be applied in contexts in which humans are interacting with automated VAs.

## KEYWORDS

virtual advisor; recommendation agent; decision aid; online disclosure; online decision making

## INTRODUCTION

Virtual advisors (VA), also known as product recommendation agents or intelligent decision aids, are software tools that engage in conversations with users, and educate and assist them in making decisions [58; 89]. These tools are a subset of advice giving systems (AGS) that are focused on engaging employees and customers, and making product and service recommendations that increase personalization and sales (termed “cognitive engagement” tools; 33]). In recent years, VAs have gained commercial acceptance and are now deployed on websites and embedded in mobile applications and consumer products, and correspondingly have enjoyed a substantial research attention [3; 4; 90]. Given their potential for improving the quality of decisions made by users, investigating the antecedents of their increased utilization has been a prime focus. As a result, studies have examined the determinants of users’ evaluations of VAs, as well as factors afecting the decision processes and outcomes when using them [e.g., 3; 43; 85; 89].

Regardless of the context in which they are employed, or the specific type of technology or algorithm that powers them (e.g., AI), when employed in a human-assistive capacity, VAs are tasked with collecting inputs from their users, and processing these inputs to generate specific recommendations. Hence, VA (and other similar AGS) research has been characterized along the Input, Process, and Output stages [89; 90]. In the Input stage, the VA acquires the necessary inputs for its processing; which can be inferred based on the user’s past behaviors or other sources of information, or can be explicitly elicited from the user. In the process stage, the focus is often on the algorithms used by the agent to generate its recommendations. In the output stage, the focus is on the type of recommendations provided, and how they are presented.

In this paper, we focus on the input stage, and specifically on contexts in which the inputs are explicitly provided by users. These could include a variety of domains where VAs have replaced human advisors tasked with providing personalized advice based on consumers specific circumstances, such as when seeking specialized advice about health and personal wellness (a major area of online retail that has experienced significant growth in recent years), or any of the other fast-growing areas of Internet-mediated personalized services and care [39]. In such contexts, users disclose information about their needs as well as pertinent personal information during what is called the requirements elicitation stage [22]. Yet, despite the importance of accurate and complete elicitation for the successful utilization of VAs, extant research has focused more on the process underlying the reasoning of a VA as well as the outcome of that process [17; 85; 85; 90; 89], presupposing that users have no concerns regarding such disclosures.

This is largely because most research on VAs has focused on their assistive role with preferential choice problems [89], where VAs help users in selecting products based on a pre-defined set of attributes. In these studies, users are given a set of recommendations at the Output stage, and the focus is often on increasing users’ trust and acceptance of the recommendations [90]. The Input stage is largely under-emphasized, because it is assumed that users are willing disclose their product preferences. However, VAs are now utilized in a variety of contexts and employed in a judgment-type capacity, where a significant amount of personal information is solicited (in contrast to product information), such as in healthcare or financial advising [19; 52]. They are also acting as conversational agents popularized by the large technology companies, or are being embedded within websites and mobile applications, to serve in a variety of capacities as AGS that utilize personalized inputs obtained during often prolonged and less-structured interactions. In other words, in today’s technology-driven business processes, VAs are fast replacing humans in domains that require the solicitation of highly sensitive information. This has been especially evident during the COVID-19 pandemic, where human-to-human interaction has been limited. Hence, unwillingness to disclose information at the Input stage could impede the increased utilization of VAs, especially in contexts where sensitive information is solicited [2; 21].

User interactions with VAs have several distinguishing features. First, they are characterized by a form of interdependence between the user and the VA, where the VA relies on the user to provide the information for its proper functioning, and the user depends on the advisor to faithfully use the disclosed information for her benefit. Second, user-VA interactions involve multiple disclosures made in sequence. Therefore, the user has the opportunity to observe and evaluate how the VA responds to earlier disclosures before deciding on subsequent ones. In essence, these interactions resemble a dyadic interaction where those involved observe and evaluate the actions of others before deciding how to proceed [7; 68]. Third, given the personalized nature of the advice sought, disclosure episodes tend to be longer in duration, and involve more specific types of information compared to those made to a website or a traditional product recommender system [83].

In summary, the value of a VA increases for more specialized and complex decisionmaking settings, since in such contexts users rarely have suficient expertise or cognitive capacity to make optimal choices [89]. With this increased complexity comes an increase in the specificity of the solicited information as the advice becomes more personalized [90,91,92,93,95]. Hence, users’ evaluations are not only influenced by how their preferences are gathered, but also by the type of information that is solicited [89]. This trade-of between the benefits gained from disclosure and the increased specificity and sensitivity of the information disclosed suggests that when deciding to disclose, the perceived risk of information misuse is not the only concern and could be overshadowed by other considerations, such as the expectation that the VA would deliver on its promises of providing personalized advice. Furthermore, given the interdependent context in which these disclosures occur, relational and contextual factors will likely an important role in determining one’s willingness to disclose [11; 44]. Hence, in VA interactions the focus is not only on the quality of the outcomes, but also on the interaction experience. Therefore, we address the following research questions:

(1) What are the determinants of users’ intentions to self-disclose information to VAs?

(2) How can the design of VAs be manipulated to enhance users’ willingness to selfdisclose?

To answer the first question, and consistent with research confirming that users attribute human-like characteristics and motives to intelligent agents [16; 90], we conceptualize user-VA interactions as a form of social interaction that resembles interpersonal ones. Consequently, we utilize social exchange theory [79], and its extensions, to identify the antecedents to user’s self-disclosure intentions to VAs. In doing so, we focus not only on the utilitarian determinants of self-disclosure, but also on relational factors that afect selfdisclosure intentions. In essence, similar to interpersonal contexts, we view users’ selfdisclosure to VAs as both a rational process where self-disclosure benefits and costs are assessed and compared, as well as a social interactional process that is influenced by contextual and relational factors as well as what transpires during the interaction itself [11; 44].

To answer the second research question, we investigate the role of design elements in shaping perceptions of a VA, and examine how these perceptions afect the identified determinants of self-disclosure intentions. This has been recognized as an important area of future research in online disclosure, where a need to investigate how the characteristics of the technology can afect perceptions that influence users’ self-disclosure has been emphasized [14,15].

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 ofers a review of literature on self-disclosure. Section 3 presents the research model and develops the hypotheses. The research method and the results of an experimental study are reported in sections 4 and 5. The paper then concludes with a discussion of the results, limitations, and contributions.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

## Virtual Advisors and the Requirements Elicitation Stage

Virtual advisors have been deployed in domains as varied as e-commerce, accounting and finance, and healthcare [3; 19; 52; 58; 90]. For instance, the Ottawa Hospital Research Institute in Canada maintains a list of more than one-hundred decision aids for use in healthcare (https://decisionaid.ohri.ca/), which address a host of diseases, conditions, and healthcare-related choices. More recently, these VAs are embedded in mobile applications, of which more than a hundred-thousand are estimated to be available. Understandably, depending on the context in which VAs are employed, the type of information solicited could range from product requirements to highly sensitive information about one’s health and finances.

Research on VAs has investigated various aspects of the requirements elicitation stage. The main focus has been on the diferent types of elicitation techniques (feature-based vs. needs-based and explicit vs. implicit preference elicitation methods) [55], the type, amount, and nature of input solicited [32; 43; 92; 93], as well as contextual factors that help determine the optimal design of the requirements elicitation method, such as timing [53] and trade-of dificulty [57]. However, despite the recognized importance of requirements elicitation to VA utilization, research has largely ignored what may inhibit or encourage users to self-disclose to VAs. This gap in research is more evident in light of the extensive research that has focused on trust in user-VA settings [83].

A notable finding in VA research has been the view of them as social actors [16,17,1819] whose form, actions, and behaviors alter users’ perceptions and behaviors [7]. For instance, the extent to which an advisor makes trade-ofs among alternatives explicit [57], or manifest a desired personality when assuming an anthropomorphic representation [48], afect users’ perceptions of the advisor and how they behave when interacting with it. Thus, in this paper we accept that users perceive their interactions with VAs as interpersonal, and apply the same rules and expectations as when they interact with other individuals, especially during the requirements elicitation stage.

## Self-disclosure and Its Drivers

Self-disclosure refers to the communication of private information to another [44]. This includes any information that refers to the self, such as personal dispositions, events in the past, or current or future plans of action. Anchored in social exchange theory [79], research has treated self-disclosure as a cognitive process that involves rewards and costs [7]. The theory posits that relationships are formed through a series of exchanges that are characterized by self-interest and interdependence [17], and views self-disclosure as a product of a cost-benefit analysis, in which the subjective utility of disclosing is compared to the subjective risk.

As predicted by rational choice theories, individuals try to maximize the return of an exchange by either lowering the perceived costs relative to the perceived benefits, or maximizing the benefits relative to the costs [31]. When people engage in interactions that are characterized by interdependence and the need to disclose, information is the resource being exchanged [79], and individuals apply a subjective cost-benefit analysis to decide whether to disclose or not [7]. Adopting the social exchange view of disclosure, consumer research has confirmed that the willingness to disclose personal information depends in part on the perceived costs of doing so weighed against the rewards that are attained as a result of these disclosures [63]. In user-VA interactions, this resembles the user’s exchange of information for personalized advice.

A major criticism directed at the cost-benefit view of self-disclosure is that it overlooks that self-disclosure is in essence an interpersonal behavior situated within an interaction [31,32,33]. Antaki et al. [11, 12] notes that “self-disclosure is a social performance which must be brought of in interaction, and has its interactional context and its interactional consequences” (p. 181). Therefore, researchers have also looked at the relational aspects of the exchange, and its dialectical nature [62], where it is suggested that the characteristics of the interaction in which disclosures occur, and what goes on during it, influence why people self-disclose in certain situations but not others. As a result, proponents of the view of selfdisclosure as an interactional process have advocated for the need to consider the characteristics of those involved, the nature of their relationship, and contextual factors [11; 28]. As Choi et al. [24], who employ social exchange theory to study disclosures in social networks remark: “Although diferent views of social exchange have emerged, theorists agree that the behavior of the other party in a social exchange and the relationship structure in which the social exchange occurs are important considerations in an individual’s assessment of the social exchange” (p. 677). Hence, when examining users’ disclosures, we need to be cognizant of the VA’s behaviors and the structure of the user-VA relationship.

We adopt this broader view of self-disclosure as a transactional, yet an interpersonal social process, where decisions regarding disclosure are influenced both by the perceived benefits and costs, and by the context in which self-disclosure occurs. For instance, since self-disclosure involves significant risk-taking, trust is important for the discloser to feel comfortable in a relationship [84]. Hence, the influence of trust on self-disclosure has been researched in psychology and elsewhere [87]. Similarly, to understand the specific context of a relationship, one needs to understand its structure and the nature of the interdependence governing the social exchanges within. As Rusbult and Van Lange [66] note, interdependence “presents a logical analysis of the structure of interpersonal situations, ofering a conceptual framework in which [such] situations can be analyzed” (p. 351).

The process of self-disclosing is also influenced by the context in which self-disclosures occur, such as the cues and signals exchanged between the discloser and the recipient [21; 51; 77]. These could include the type and nature of the communication exchanged, as well as the behaviors of those involved. For example, providing additional information to justify one’s request for information, can act as a signal (or an informational cue [77]) that the requester is transparent and has nothing to hide [51; 77]. Similarly, the disclosure recipient can provide additional cues such as expressions of concern or reciprocating disclosures [7]. In fact, in social exchange, self-disclosure has been tied to responsiveness [7], where appropriately responding to self-disclosures is seen as especially important in dyadic disclosure relationships that are characterized by high dependence and uncertainty about the other’s intentions [56], and even a more significant catalyst for continuing disclosure than that manifested through reciprocity [25].

Research in online contexts has often adopted a rational view of self-disclosure, often in the form of a privacy calculus [8; 35; 89]. Studies within this stream of research have shown that online consumers are willing to trade personal information for some benefits [8; 50]. Essentially, the logic forming the basis of these studies is similar to that of social exchange theory, but they adopt a more constrained view by only focusing on privacy as the main motivator of disclosure. A related stream of research has specifically looked at the trade-of between the perceived benefits of personalization and the resultant heightened privacy concerns, in what has been termed the personalization privacy paradox [13; 75]. In this context, and similar to user-VA interactions, information is traded for personalized service, whereas the provision of that information heightens privacy concerns [2; 21; 8; 89]. Hence, consistent with social exchange, the perceived benefits of personalization (personalized advice from VAs) are compared to the perceived risks associated with privacy, when deciding whether to participate in the personalized service or not.

Information Systems researchers have also recognized the importance of contextual factors [21]. Studies have shown that self-disclosure determinants extend beyond benefits and costs to other considerations, such as trust [35]. Other research has focused on understanding the drivers of the perceived benefits and costs. These studies have shown that factors such as information sensitivity [36] can shape perceptions of the benefits and costs as well as other self-disclosure determinants. Given the potential for uncertainty and information asymmetry in online transactions [8,9,10], research has specifically looked at the efects of transparency on online self-disclosure [36]. For example, Awad and Krishnan [13] investigated the relationship between a website’s information transparency features and consumer willingness to share information for online personalization. They found that some specific features of the context could enhance information transparency, which in turn afects the willingness to be profiled. Similarly, Zimmer et al. [96] showed that providing reasons for collecting particular information, encourages customers to selfdisclose.

## RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESES

The research model (see Figure 1) contains a number of determinants of users’ self-disclosures to VAs that are derived from our earlier discussion. To inform the structure of our model, we employed the framework proposed by Al-Natour and Benbasat [5] for the study of users interactions with an information technology (IT) artifact. They propose that users’ evaluations of IT artifacts extend beyond aspects that are utilitarian in nature, to those that are relational and social in nature. When applied to the context of user-VA interactions, this framework in essence integrates the two views of self-disclosure - viewing it as both a rational process and as an interpersonal one. They further posit that the design characteristics of an artifact can be used to manifest certain characteristics that are observed by users within their interaction. Beliefs about these characteristics (termed object-based beliefs), subsequently afect users’ beliefs about the outcomes of using the artifact (behavioral beliefs) and their relationship with it (relationship beliefs). These latter beliefs in turn afect users’ intentions regarding whether, and how, to use that artifact. In the context of this study, these intentions concern whether or not to self-disclose..

## Research Model: Construct Selection

As discussed earlier, anchored in social exchange theory, self-disclosure in interpersonal contexts is viewed as a product of a cost-benefit analysis. Hence, the research model includes two behavioral beliefs about the benefits and costs of disclosing information elicited in this exchange. Specifically, we examine benefits and costs that are inherent to, and obtained through the advice-giving task itself. This is consistent with research that emphasized the importance of focusing on the benefits and costs that are derived specifically from the goals of the interaction [24; 59]. Similarly, research that has looked at the personalization privacy paradox [13; 23; 89], has shown that the benefits of personalization, such as personalized advice, are salient and compared to the perceived privacy costs in determining the intentions to self-disclose the information needed to use the personalized service [23; 89]. Therefore, in this study we focus on the perceived benefits of obtaining personalized advice (labelled perceived outcome expectancy), and the perceived costs associated with the loss of control over the disclosed information (labelled perceived loss of privacy).

As discussed earlier, criticisms have been echoed in relation to the study of disclosures as solely a function of benefits and costs [13]. Given that social exchange theory emphasizes the self-interested and interdependent nature of the disclosure relationship, in this study we focus on two relationship beliefs about the user’s perceptions of the relationship with the VA, namely trust and interdependence. As highlighted in the literature review, in interpersonal contexts, trust is shown to mitigate the uncertainty of disclosing to others’ given the self-interested nature of social exchanges. In online contexts, trust is a “confidence belief” that positively influences the willingness to disclose personal information, by reducing the uncertainty regarding the intentions of those who are the target of disclosures [35]. Similarly, interdependence has received attention in self-disclosure research (e.g., [66]) as an important criterion of social exchange relationships that afects decisions to participate in these exchanges [17; 31], and has further been proposed to be a salient and a relevant belief in contexts of users’ interaction with IT artifacts [5]. In summary, while interdependence describes the dynamics of the exchange relationship, trust, which acts as a risk-coping mechanism [35; 87], represents an evaluation of the VA’s perceived level of self-interest.

As discussed earlier, self-disclosure is also afected by the characteristics of the target of disclosures, and how they behave during the interaction in which the disclosures take place [28]. Therefore, the proposed model incorporates two object-based beliefs that address the characteristics and behaviors of the advisor during the interaction. While a number of salient characteristics and behaviors could be influential, in this study we focus on the role of signals and cues communicated by the VA on influencing disclosure willingness [23; 55], since these signals and cues can be manipulated via the VA’s design – a major objective of this study. Consistently, we focus on the role of informational signals [77], in the form of explanations, on perceptions of the VA’s transparency, and the efects of additional cues such as expressions of concern (in the form of expressive speech acts) on perceptions of the VA’s responsiveness [7]. As overviewed earlier, both transparency (a factor that reduces privacy risks, enhances trust and perceived benefits) [51; 77], and responsiveness [25] have been shown to be especially important in dyadic disclosure relationships that are characterized by high dependence and uncertainty about the other’s intentions [56]. Similarly, has emphasized the influential role of transparency in afecting perceptions of disclosure benefits and costs [5; 75; 96].

## Self-Disclosure as a Function of Benefits and Costs

We define outcome expectancy as the extent to which users believe that disclosing the solicited information will help them receive better advice. From a user’s perspective, using a VA consists of providing inputs concerning one’s needs and deciding what to do with the provided recommendations [89]. When these inputs are elicited in the form of requests for self-disclosure, users perform an evaluation of the extent to which providing that information improves the quality of the advice. These beliefs concerning the potential efects of selfdisclosure on the quality of outcomes, will then afect the extent to which the user is willing to disclose [59]. This is consistent with research on the personalization privacy paradox, where perceived benefits are shown to increase the perceived value of disclosing, and subsequently lead to increased willingness to disclose the information needed to personalize the service [89]. Similarly, the study of self-disclosure in online and interpersonal contexts has established that self-disclosure intentions are driven by the perceived benefits to be attained as a result of self-disclosure [35; 79].

## H1: Perceived outcome expectancy positively influences the intentions to self-disclose.

One major potential cost of self-disclosures that has received significant research attention is perceived loss of privacy [24; 36; 50]. It addresses the extent to which the user believes that disclosing the elicited information will lead to a loss of control over who has her personal information and how it is handled [88]. Consumer privacy literature suggests that disclosure requests increase the perceived risk of loss of privacy [63]. The motivation to avoid such risk is derived from the customer’s desire for privacy and to minimize unwanted financial, social and other kinds of harm that could result from one’s loss of privacy [13]. Similarly, studies adopting the privacy calculus have shown that expectations of loss of privacy act to reduce the willingness to provide information and discourage actual disclosure [36]. Research looking at the personalization privacy paradox suggests that perceived risks associated with privacy act as a counter-balance that reduces the value of personalization [75], and hence reduces the willingness to disclose [23].

When viewed within the context of expectancy theories, loss of privacy symbolizes an outcome to be avoided. The motivation for engaging in a behavior that allows for this outcome is influenced by the probability assessment that an action (self-disclosing) will result in a certain outcome (loss of privacy), the subjective assessment that a given level of performance (incompetent information handling) will lead to a particular outcome (loss of privacy), and the value that an individual places on that outcome (loss of privacy) [80]. Hence, it is the perceptions of privacy risks, rather than objective privacy risks, which shape users’ perceptions of loss of privacy, and subsequently drive users’ intentions to self-disclose [20].

H2: Perceived loss of privacy negatively influences the intentions to self-disclose.

## Relational Antecedents to User Self-Disclosure

As noted earlier, since self-disclosure involves significant risk-taking, perceived trust is important for the discloser to feel comfortable [84]. Wheeless and Grotz [87] conceptualize trust as “a process of holding certain relevant, favorable perceptions of another person which engender certain types of dependent behaviors in a risky situation where the expected outcomes that are dependent upon that other person(s) are not known with certainty” (p. 251). Hence, adequate levels of trust are necessary for self-disclosure. This is confirmed by Steel [73], who shows that varying degrees of disclosure are related to varying degrees in perceptions of trust.

In consumer contexts, trust acts as a risk-coping mechanism that reduces uncertainty and increases one’s willingness to provide personal information [63]. In online selfdisclosure, trust acts as a “confidence and enticement” belief [35] that embodies the expectation that the party will not engage in opportunistic behavior [96]. In the case of a VA, we propose that users’ intentions to self-disclose are influenced by their overall trust in the VA. This belief embodies the assurance that the advisor will not engage in behaviors that can harm the user as a result of these disclosures [35], and that it has the competence to prevent such harm [60].

H3: Trust in the advisor positively influences the intentions to self-disclose.

The second relationship variable examined in this study is perceived interdependence. Interdependence concerns the extent to which the interacting entities influence one another’s experiences [66], and hence concerns the degree to which an individual relies on an interaction partner in so much as her outcomes are influenced by the partner’s actions [66]. Interdependence can be decomposed into three components: 1) the level of dependence: the degree to which an individual relies on or is restricted by an interaction partner, 2) the basis of dependence: whether outcomes are controlled by unilateral or joint action, and 3) the covariation of interests: the degree to which partners’ interests correspond [66]. Variation in any of these components can afect the extent to which the relationship between interacting partners is perceived to be interdependent [5]. Given the goal-oriented nature of the user-VA social exchange, we believe that the most salient and relevant dimension is that of co-variation of interests.

Since perceptions of the nature of interdependence afect decisions to participate in social exchange relationships [17; 31], it has received significant attention in self-disclosure research [66]. In user-VA interactions, the user has a high level of dependence on the advisor. Essentially, the user is “relying” or “needing” the advisor, and her self-disclosures make her vulnerable, since there is no guarantee that the advisor will act in a prosocial manner. Alternatively, the advisor depends on the user to provide information so it can accomplish its objectives. Hence, to enhance perceptions of interdependence, there must be a feeling that both interests are congruent.

When interdependence is perceived to be low, the willingness to self-disclose is reduced. This is due to a number of reasons. First, with high interdependence, the benefits to be gained from exploiting another’s disclosures are minimized, and self-disclosure can act to solidify the “us” stance [66]. Second, in situations of mutual dependence, such as when the interests covary, the threat of retaliation and non-cooperation becomes a deterrent for the exploitation of self-disclosure, and as a result, self-disclosure increases [40]. Finally, when the level of dependence is reduced so that the advisor’s actions minimally afect the outcomes of the interaction, self-disclosures become less risky and exploitation less probable [74]. Hence, increased levels of interdependence, through increased perceptions of covariation of interests encourage disclosures.

H4: Perceived interdependence positively influences the intentions to self-disclose.

## The Efects of the Advisor’s Characteristics and Behaviors

Self-disclosure is afected by the characteristics of the target of disclosures, and how that target behaves during the interaction in which the disclosures take place [28]. This view has also been adopted in IS research that has advocates the importance of situational and contextual factors on influencing the determinants of the intentions to disclose (e.g., [89]). This study examines two beliefs addressing the perceived characteristics and behaviors of the VA.

Perceived Responsiveness: Partners are perceived to be responsive when they address the communications, needs, wishes, or actions of the person with whom they are interacting [61]. This can be accomplished by reciprocating one’s disclosures, or even more efectively by acknowledging the afect contained in a disclosure (termed evaluative responses) and/or tailoring the communication to the topic of disclosure (termed topical responses). Hence, disclosers are more likely to believe a target is responsive when their communication is perceived to be understanding (accurately capturing the discloser’s needs and feelings), validating (confirming that the discloser is an accepted individual), and caring (showing concern for the discloser) [25]. Hence, in this study, we define responsiveness as the extent to which the VA is perceived to be accurately inferring the user’s feelings and concerns, and appropriately responding to them.

An important dimension of perceived responsiveness is projecting awareness and understanding of the discloser’s needs and concerns. In addition, responsive communication signals willingness on the part of the target to tailor the exchange to the issue at hand [34]. In the context of users’ disclosures to VAs, this serves to demonstrate that the advisor understands what the user considers important, and thus, has a suficient understanding of the user’s needs, and the motivation to find a suitable solution. Therefore, perceived advisor responsiveness strengthens the user’s belief that quality of the personalized advice will be enhanced as a result of revealing the elicited information. In fact, such perceptions that the advisor has internalized the user’s needs have been shown to enhance evaluations of the advisor and the user’s desire to use it [55].

This is consistent with findings in interpersonal contexts, where interactions that are characterized by responsiveness result in a harmony of thought and common understanding [30]. These perceptions of highly coordinated interactions strengthen users’ beliefs that the information provided will be properly incorporated into the decision making, thus, enhancing perceptions of performance expectancy. Similarly, when the advisor is perceived to be attentive and involved in its interaction with the user, the belief that the needs and concerns communicated through self-disclosure will be internalized and suficiently considered is strengthened.

## H5: Perceived responsiveness positively influences perceived outcome expectancy.

Responsiveness, in its most general sense, communicates that the advisor cares about the user, understands her needs and concerns, and is willing and motivated to help [25]. It thus manifests an understanding of the seriousness and sensitivity of the information disclosed, and consequently, a recognition of the significant harm that mishandling this information can cause [56]. Therefore, perceptions of a responsive VA can lead to a reduction in perceptions of loss of privacy, as the VA is viewed to be supportive and compassionate [26]. Similarly, when an advisor is perceived to be attentive, it reduces perceptions that the advisor will intentionally cause harm, thus reducing the likelihood that privacy loss will result from self-disclosure.

## H6: Perceived responsiveness negatively influences perceived loss of privacy.

Perceptions of responsiveness represent how well the VA understands and feels about the user’s needs and concerns. This familiarity serves as an appropriate context to interpret the VA’s other behaviors [83]. For instance, when the VA communicates an accurate understanding of the user’s needs, it strengthens her belief that the advisor has the competence to understand these needs, and subsequently, use them as inputs for decisionmaking. Furthermore, portraying responsiveness through manifesting care and concern, similarly, strengthens the user’s belief in the advisor’s benevolence, which is behaviorally marked by caring about the trustor and acting in her interest [84]. Such empathic responses have also been shown to strengthen trust [38].

This proposition has found support in VA research. Komiak and Benbasat [55] posit that users’ perceived personalization of a VA, or “the extent to which the [VA] understands and represents his or her personal needs” (p. 944), enhances trust. In the context of our study, such personalization is manifested through VA responsiveness. Similarly, Wang and Benbasat [83] proposes that users form trust based on their interactive experiences with VAs, and that users’ experiential trust is shaped by the extent to which these experiences are harmonious [84,85,86].

## H7: Perceived responsiveness positively influences trust.

Responsiveness is further proposed to influence perceptions of interdependence through increasing perceptions of covariation of interest. Interdependence concerns the extent to which the interacting entities influence one another’s experiences. By communicating understanding of, and care for the user’s needs, the advisor highlights its comprehension of these needs, and further indicates its intention for these needs to determine the final outcome [25]. In so doing, the advisor is surrendering control and signaling that outcomes are determined through joint action.

## H8: Perceived responsiveness positively influences perceived interdependence.

Perceived Transparency: In addition to responsiveness, the study examines the efects of perceived VA transparency as another antecedent to self-disclosure determinants. We define transparency as the perceived knowledge of the VA’s inner workings. This conceptualization focuses on the processes followed to solicit inputs, process them, and produce recommendations. As such, this view of transparency is more general in that the transparency of a virtual advisor is not only assumed to aford the user an insight into why a recommendation is made, but also could make the user aware of why a certain question is being asked, and how the user’s responses will be used and processed, to mention a few.<sup>2</sup> IS research has found that transparency of the technology reduces privacy risks, and enhances trust and perceived benefits [13; 96].

A transparent advisor allows the user to understand the way it works [29]. This ensures that the user knows what the suitable inputs are, and interacts with the advisor accordingly. Alternatively, when a user has an inaccurate mental model of how the advisor works, she may provide inappropriate inputs [81], which will diminish the benefits attained from its use. Hence, transparency afects the actual and the perceived benefits, because the user is now more eficient in her interaction with the VA, and also because it provides the user with a foundation on which to form her beliefs that a certain outcome will be obtained as a result of disclosing information.

Research on the personalization privacy paradox has also ofered similar conclusions. For instance, Xu et al. [89] show that the perceived benefits of a personalized service increase when the process is overt (i.e., known to the user). Similarly, in personalized advertising, customers perceive greater benefits when firms engage in information collection that is overt as opposed to covert [5, 6]. Therefore, when the VA is transparent, and hence the process is overt, the perceived benefits of personalization, represented by perceived outcome expectancy, will also increase.

## H9: Perceived transparency positively influences perceived outcome expectancy.

When the inner workings of the VA are known, the user can better assess the costs of her self-disclosures [36]. For instance, describing how the information solicited is used, and highlighting the need for that information, provides the user with a justification for disclosure. Holding everything else constant, this increased knowledge reduces the uncertainty about how the VA uses information [29; 82], and consequently reducing the perceived privacy risks.

These ideas have found support in online disclosure research, were perceived transparency was shown to reduce uncertainty about how information is handled [36], and subsequently reduce the risk of self-disclosure [13]. Similarly, when the process underlying a personalized service is overt, the perceived privacy concerns are reduced, which reduces the risk of loss of privacy and increases the willingness to participate in the service [5; 89]. Finally, perceptions of information relevance resulting from enhanced transparency have also been shown to reduce perceptions of disclosure risks when providing information to a website [96].

## H10: Perceived transparency negatively influences perceived loss of privacy.

Research has discussed the role of the transparency of a VA as an antecedent to users’ trust [94]. Specifically, transparency has been proposed to reduce the information asymmetry relating to what the advisor does, and why and how it does it [7], and hence, increase users’ trust in the advisor [82; 83; 84]. Similarly, a VA providing justifications as to why it solicits certain information, or how this information is used, helps to bridge the “intentions gap” resulting from the uncertainty about its motives [82]. Likewise, transparency increases the user’s familiarity with the VA, which increases trust in it [55]. Finally, research that has advocated that online self-disclosure is a behavioral process has indicated that transparency reduces the uncertainty information use, and increases users’ feelings of control [1], which subsequently enhances perceptions of trust in the technology [20].

## H11: Perceived transparency positively influences trust.

Finally, increased transparency can also afect perceptions of interdependence with the VA. Firstly, bridging the intentions gap between the user and the advisor, helps to afirm goal congruency and covariation of interest [82]. Secondly, transparency in regards to what’s being done with the information provided, and how it is being integrated within the VA’s decision-making can influence perceptions that the outcomes are the result of joint action, thus, enhancing perceptions of interdependence. Finally, by ofering an insight into the VA’s inner workings, the user is able to understand the VA’s processes [89] and judge the extent to which the work of the VA is instrumental to achieving the outcomes, and the degree to which the she relies on the VA.

H12: Perceived transparency positively influences perceived interdependence.

## The Role of Design Characteristics

A main objective of this study is to investigate how VAs can be designed to enhance users’ willingness to disclose. As highlighted earlier, virtual advisors are software tools that engage in conversations with users, and educate and assist them in making decisions. Therefore, a VA has a dual role both as a conversational partner and a decision aid. In this study, we focus on these two main roles of a VA, and identify three design elements that address them, and which also are proposed to cue characteristics of responsiveness and transparency. In essence, and as discussed earlier, in this study we focus on the role of signals and cues communicated by the VA on influencing disclosure willingness The selection of these elements was partially based on research showing that explanation facilities are efective means to ofering insights into the inner workings of VAs [82]. They therefore can act as informational signals communicated throughout the dyadic interaction. Furthermore, consistent with the view of self-disclosure as a dialectical process [11], and considering that responsiveness is often manifested verbally [25], we utilize speech acts [69] to identify how the VAs utterances can be used to manifest certain behaviors and characteristics. These design elements can act as cues that can be interpreted within the context of the interaction.

Explanations: Self-disclosure is more likely when the process is made overt [96]. In the context of users’ disclosures to VAs, this can be accomplished by providing information justifying the need for the solicited information and describing how it is used [13]. Similar to the explanations provided by human decision makers to explain their choices, explanation facilities provide information regarding the actions and the motives of a VA, which has been shown to increase acceptance of VAs and perceptions of the transparency of their inner workings [82; 94].

Wang and Benbasat [82] have diferentiated between two types of explanations ofered by VAs. Why-explanations are used to provide justification for the questions asked and the recommendations provided. How-explanations describe the line of reasoning used by the advisor and outline the logical processes involved in arriving at its recommendations. When applied to the context of self-disclosures, why-explanations communicate the reasons for asking a certain question, and hence convey the relevance of the solicited information to decision-making. Alternatively, how-explanations express how the information solicited is used and incorporated. Explanations provided by VAs are akin to decisional guidance [8; 48], which concerns “the degree to which and the manner in which a system guides its users in constructing and executing decision-making processes” [80, p. 57]. Such guidance can be predefined or dynamic. Hence, both types of explanations can be pre-programmed into the functionality of the VA, or ofered based on responses from its user.

By describing the reasons for soliciting information, why-explanations reduce the uncertainty regarding the advisor’s intentions. Hence, they increase the transparency of the VA’s inner workings. Additionally, why-explanations communicate care and openness, as they reafirm the VA’s desire to assist the user, and directly address any concerns about disclosing the information. Therefore, we also propose that why-explanations positively influence perceptions of responsiveness.

## H13: Why-explanations increase perceived transparency (13a) and responsiveness (13b).

In general, how-explanations reduce the uncertainty regarding how the solicited information is used, and the process followed for making a decision. Therefore, howexplanations, whether dynamic or pre-defined, can positively influence perceptions of the advisor’s transparency. Furthermore, given that dynamic how-explanations are generated based on users’ responses, they manifest reciprocity and attentiveness. In essence, howexplanations allow the VA to articulate how it intends to assist the user based on the information disclosed. By describing how the specific responses from the user are used, dynamic how-explanations ensure that the advisor’s communication is tailored to the user’s concerns, acting as a catalyst of perceived responsiveness [61].

H14: How-explanations increase perceived transparency (14a) and responsiveness (14b).

Speech Acts: Research has highlighted the dialectical nature of self-disclosure [61], recognizing it essentially as a communicative act [11]. Therefore, to study self-disclosure, especially in dyads, one needs to study the communicative acts between participants [25]. As Cosmides and Tooby [27] suggest, one approach to doing is to employ speech act theory, which postulates that to communicate is to perform an act, such as stating facts, making requests, making promises, or issuing orders [70]. For example, by making the statement, “I will call you tomorrow,” the speaker commits to an action, which in turn afects the “hearer” [69]. Thus, by uttering the sentence the speaker says something, does something by speaking, and afects the hearer by what is said. Speech acts are performed to make factual statements (assertives), to request someone to do something (directives), to make promises and commitments (commissives), to efect change (declaratives), and to express a personal feeling (expressives) [70].

Research has shown that certain types of speech acts can be used to cue desired perceptions of VAs (e.g., [7]). In this study, we propose that speech acts can be used to efect perceptions of the VA’s responsiveness. More specifically, expressive speech acts, which are used to express a certain psychological state by the speaker of the message [70], such as expressing concern, can be used to manifest understanding and care, and increase perceptions of the VA’s responsiveness.

H15: Expressive speech acts positively influence perceived responsiveness.

## RESEARCH METHOD

## Experimental Task

Study participants were invited to interact with an online VA designed to provide personalized skin care advice. To create a sense of experimental realism, we informed participants that the VA was designed by a new skincare company (fictitious) that needs help in evaluating the new advisor. During the task, the VA asked participants a series of questions (shown in Appendix A) used to determine a person’s skin care needs. To identify relevant questions, we analyzed a number of websites and forums concerned with skin care, and identified a list of factors that are considered when dispensing skin care advice.

The introductory script (available in Appendix A) emphasized the importance of thoroughly experimenting with the VA, and informed participants that they could win cash prizes redeemable towards the recommended solutions. Participants were instructed that they could decide to answer any or all of the questions asked by the VA (the Study Instructions and the Introductory Script are shown in Appendix A). It was made clear that there is jeopardy in choosing to not answer any of the question asked by the VA, and for each question they were provided with the option of not disclosing. Only provide information that you would normally feel comfortable providing to an online vendor. Hence, while they were encouraged to experiment with the VA, we did not confound their intention to self-disclose by asking them to participate in an actual shopping task.

After completing the experimental task, but before being presented with the advisor’s recommendations, participants were directed to an online questionnaire. This ensured that their evaluations of the advisor are not influenced by the quality of its recommendations. To verify that participants took the task seriously, they were tested on what the VA asked, in addition to a number of attention check questions. Those that failed to respond correctly were screened out.

## Treatment Conditions

A between-subjects experimental design with three factors (why explanations, how explanations, expressive speech acts) was used to test the model, with a random assignment to one of the eight experimental groups. The advisor was represented by a two-dimensional avatar placed on the left side of the screen (see Appendix C for sample screenshots and sample explanations and expressive speech acts). As described earlier, guidance ofered by the VA can be predefined or dynamic. In this study, both expressive speech acts and howexplanations were generated dynamically based on the user’s answers. For instance, when asked about skin areas that are of concern, the VA provides an explanation of how the specific response will be factored into its decision. Pilot studies (described in Appendix B) were conducted to inform the design, measurement, and the script used in the study. In Pilot Study #4, in addition to examining the efects of the three design elements used in the main experiment, we examined the efects of static how-explanations and two types of commissive speech acts. The results indicated that these additional design elements have negligible efects in the presence of the three design elements chosen for this study. Hence, for the sake of parsimony, and consistent with our objective of manipulating perceptions of the VA using only a small subset of design elements, the additional design elements were not examined in the main study.

## Sample

The study was conducted on-line using participants recruited from a panel maintained by a specialized market research company called the SampleNetwork (a division of Rex Direct Net, Inc.). The company ofers research solutions to clients desiring quality data, as the high incentives provided to panelist guarantee more considerate responses. To recruit respondents, the company sent email invitations to members of their panel that meet the study’s qualifications (19+ Canadian females). Nonetheless, a number of subjects did not qualify for the study when promoted at the start of the study to provide their gender, age and country of residence. An additional 37 respondents failed to pass the screening test, where they are asked to recall what the VA asked about during their interaction, and hence were dropped out as this could be indicative of the fact that they did not complete the experimental task. Finally, 25 respondents had incomplete responses, due to either voluntary dropping out of the study, or providing an incorrect answer to one of the attention questions. An analysis of the partial responses from the group of dropouts did not reveal any significant diferences when these responses are compared to the complete ones. The final sample consisted of 195 females who represent a cross section of users, and whose characteristics (summarized in Appendix E), and did not difer across the treatment groups.

## Measures

All constructs were measured using multi-item scales (listed in Appendix D). Consistent with similar past research [35], the intention to self-disclose was captured separately for the diferent types of information solicited. The items addressed users’ demographics, skin care needs, skin conditions, skin related habits, socially sensitive habits, changes and experiences, medical history, and opinions on relevant topics. Perceived loss of privacy was measured using the scale developed by White [88] after adapting its items to the context of this study and adding a new item (so the scale has three-items). Outcome expectancy was measured by adapting a scale used to measure usefulness of VAs [7]. Trust was measured using the scale developed by Gefen et al. [42], after it was adapted to the context of $\mathrm { \ V A s . ^ { 4 } }$ New scales were developed to measure perceived responsiveness, transparency, and interdependence.<sup>5</sup> The measurement instrument was validated through the pilot studies, and only minimal changes were made. Specifically, wording changes were made to the items for outcome expectancy, loss of privacy, and interdependence.

## Additional Analyses

Appendices F and G describe a number of additional analyses that are intended to check for the robustness of the results and explore additional considerations. To ensure that the results are not afected by the choice of AMOS as the modeling tool, we ran an additional model using PLS, as well as performed a number of independent linear regressions (see Appendix F). First, the results of the PLS model closely resemble those observed in the AMOS model (see Figure F1 in Appendix F). Furthermore, to ensure that the results are not an artifact of our choice of modeling the dependent variable as a reflective construct, we also analyzed another PLS model in which the intentions to disclose was modeled as a formative construct. The results (Figure F2in Appendix F) show that the efects on intentions are largely the same as those observed in the AMOS model and the reflective PLS model. This suggests that our results are not afected by our modeling choices.

Second, we also confirmed the results of the AMOS model by performing a number of multiple regressions. The results of a regression with the four mediators acting as predictors of the intentions to disclose (see Appendix F) show that the efects of these mediators on the intentions remain almost exactly the same (with the largest diference in magnitude size being 0.002). Likewise, similar results to those observed in the AMNOS model were obtained from four separate regression models that examine the efects of Responsiveness and Transparency on the four mediators.

To examine whether the types of information solicited bears an impact on the efects of the mediators on self-disclosure intentions [21], we analyzed two additional AMOS models focusing on sensitive and non-sensitive information (see Appendix F). The results indicate that perceived loss of privacy does not exert a significant efect on the intentions when the solicited information is not socially sensitive. In contrast, the efects of perceived loss of privacy and outcome expectancy increase significantly when the information solicited is socially sensitive, while the efects of trust and interdependence remain largely the same in the two models. The latter indicates that relational determinants of the intentions to disclose are equally predictive even in the least sensitive of contexts. Overall, the results indicate that collectively the four determinants of the intentions to disclose have significantly larger predictive power when the information solicited is sensitive in nature, with the R2 changes from 18.7% to 44.7%. Overall, the moderation analysis results indicate that for the most part, the hypothesized determinants, especially the relational ones, would be efective even in the least sensitive of contexts.

As described earlier, for the sake of parsimony, we used overall measures to represent multi-dimensional constructs in our model. Appendix G presents the equivalent results if multi-dimensional constructs are used. Specifically, rather than using overall measures for the two exogenous variables, three new scales were developed to measure the dimensions of responsiveness (understanding, caring, and validating), and two new scales where developed to measure the dimensions of transparency (purpose and process). Similarly, both trust and interdependence are treated as multi-dimensional constructs, with trust represented by competence, benevolence and integrity, and interdependence represented by the level of dependence, the basis of dependence, and covariation of interests. The measurement valuation, the efects of the treatment factors on the multi-dimensional exogenous variables, and the results of the structural model when using these multi-dimensional measures are discussed in Appendix G. They indicate a pattern of results that is very similar to those when the overall measures of the relevant variables are used. All four predictors of the intentions to self-disclose exert significant efects, and both exogenous variables (responsiveness and transparency) exert significant efects on the four predictors of intentions.

## DISCUSSION

This experimental study provides support for the proposed model of the determinants of users’ intentions to disclose to VAs. Specifically, the results show that self-disclosure to VAs is a form of social exchange as well as a relational and interactional situated practice. The results corroborate this dual view of self-disclosure. An important finding in this study is the relatively small influence of perceived loss of privacy on self-disclosure intentions, when compared to that of perceived outcome expectancy. This is consistent with research on the personalization-privacy paradox showing that the efects of perceived benefits of a personalized service often outweigh those of the perceived loss of privacy, on the willingness to disclose or actual disclosure behavior [23]. This conclusion has also found support in behavioral studies of privacy decision-making, where making privacy-related decisions is viewed as a process in which cognitive heuristics and biases occur, such as the need for immediate gratification [1, 2, 3, 4]. In our context, the goal-oriented nature of the task, where the benefits are made more salient, could have contributed to enhancing the perceived value of the personalized advice while reducing the perceived costs associated with it.

As described earlier, the efects of trust on self-disclosure intentions have received support in social psychology and consumer literature (e.g., [35; 63; 87]). Hence, the observed large efect of trust on the intentions, and the accompanied relatively high correlation between them $( r = 0 . 5 1 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 5 )$ are consistent with past findings. On the other hand, the efects of perceived interdependence constitute a most novel contribution. The relatively smaller efect of perceived interdependence could be attributed to the relatively high dominance of other constructs in the model. When either trust or perceived outcome expectancy is removed from the model, the efect of perceived interdependence increases significantly in magnitude. Also, as described in Appendix G, when perceived interdependence is treated as a second-order formative constructs indicated by all three dimensions, its efect on the intentions increases.

As the results show, perceived responsiveness acts as a strong predictor of subsequent beliefs. This highlights the importance of responding appropriately to users’ disclosures as means of enhancing perceptions of the benefits, trust in the advisor, and perceptions of shared control and goal congruency that are the hallmarks of interdependence, whilst reducing perceptions of loss of privacy. The efects of transparency on outcome expectancy and interdependence matched in magnitude its efect on trust. The latter efect indicates that knowledge of the advisor’s inner workings can positively influence users’ trust in the advisor, regardless of whether the advisor’s behaviors are perceived to be justified. The efect on outcome expectancy indicates that knowledge of the advisor’s inner workings influences expectations that disclosure will help in attaining a better outcome, likely because this increased knowledge helps users understand the connection between the solicited information and the subsequent recommendation. The results further suggest that the knowledge gained via the advisor’s transparency acts as a conduit for clarifying the advisor’s goals and objectives, and evaluating their congruency with those of the user. It also could be that the mere act of openly providing information concerning the reasons for soliciting information is in itself a form of interdependent behavior that solidifies the “us-stance”.

In general, the efects of responsiveness are stronger than those of transparency, especially when predicting trust and interdependence. As discussed earlier, responsiveness has been shown to be a crucial element in relationship formation and enhancing interaction quality, as it manifests concern and understanding. Hence, its observed large efects are justified. Furthermore, this observed diference in the efects of transparency and responsiveness is also consistent with Al-Natour and Benbasat [5], who propose that the efects of individualistic beliefs are often incorporated within their relevant dyadic counterparts. This could be indicative of a causal relationship between these two variables.

In relation to the second research question (concerning the role of design), the results confirm the prospect of using the design of a VA to encourage self-discourse. The suficiency and capability of the three design elements to manifest the desired characteristics on the part of the advisor, highlight the importance of manipulating an appropriate set of design elements, and further afirm that designing such advisors should be performed with specific goals in mind. As the results indicate, the efects of one design element could extend to a number of perceptions. Given that they manifest both interactivity and openness, not surprisingly, dynamic how-explanations have strong efects on both responsiveness and transparency. Hence, this could further explain the moderately large correlation (r = 0.56) between these latter beliefs. The efects of the other two design elements are more focused on one or the other.

## Contribution to Research and Practice

This study contributes to both research and practice. The main contribution to research is an understanding of the factors that influence users’ intentions to provide personal information to an IT artifact in general, and the intentions to self-disclose to a VA in specific. Specifically, this study ofers an insight into the factors that influence the decision to disclose information of varying types and levels of sensitivity needed for establishing requirements when using a VA.

The study further confirms that social exchange theory, which is often applied in interpersonal contexts, can also be applied in contexts in which humans are interacting with automated VAs. In so showing, this study corroborates the view of VAs as social actors to which users attribute human-like characteristics, as well as apply the same social rules and expectations when interacting with them as they do when interacting with other humans. Therefore, an added contribution is that of extending the predictive scope of social exchange and further corroborating the view of VAs as social actors, the study of which can benefit from interpersonal theories.

The current study further contributes to the growing body of research in online selfdisclosure that proposes that the determinants of self-disclosure go beyond benefits and costs. Viewing self-disclosure not only as a rational process influenced by a comparison of benefits and costs, but also as a relational and an interpersonal interactional situated practice, allows for the identification of other salient beliefs that are not utilitarian in nature and which can afect the decision to self-disclose. To that end, this study identifies and highlights the important role played by salient relational variables, contextual cues, as well as the characteristics of the target of disclosures. Overall, the study is successful in delineating the distinct efects of the diferent determinants of self-disclosure, underscoring the need for a broader and more encompassing approach to the study of self-disclosure antecedents and intentions.

Virtual Advisors are gaining widespread use, such as conversational agents popularized by Google, Apple and Amazon, or those embedded within websites and mobile applications. Furthermore, in today’s technology-driven business, VAs are fast replacing humans in domains that require the solicitation of highly sensitive information, such as health care and insurance. Our findings underscore that the context may largely determine the factors of importance.

Findings in regards to the role of contextual factors draw attention to the important role of the users’ experience when using VAs. The observed efects of the advisor’s social characteristics (responsiveness) and the relationship variables, provide further support to the view of VAs as social actors. In essence, it confirms that interactions with IT artifacts are not only goaloriented, but are also social in nature. Hence, decisions users make regarding these interactions, such as whether to self-disclose, are influenced by the same social processes that influence interpersonal interactions. At a macro level, the results also suggest that users, albeit being aware that the VA is an inanimate object, attribute responsibility and competence to it. This is consistent with the interaction-centric view of Al-Natour and Benbasat [5] that proposes that users use cues within the interaction to form their beliefs when interacting with IT artifacts.

One of the study’s prime contributions is examining how the design of the VA can afect users’ self-disclosure intentions. From a research perspective, the study identifies theoretical bases that can be used to alter perceptions of the VA. From a practical perspective, the study highlights how the use of these design elements induce users to self-disclose.

The study also has a number of other practical contributions. The significant efect of perceived outcome expectancy indicates that users are willing to provide solicited information if they believe that this improves the quality of the advice received. Thus, companies should ensure that VAs highlight these expected benefits, and that it fulfills its part of this exchange by ofering high-quality personalized advice. The efect exerted by loss of privacy reafirms the importance of formulating, communicating, and implementing sound privacy policies and practices. The efects of trust and interdependence highlight the importance of designing interactions that are marked with cooperation and harmony. The large efects of the exogenous variables signify that users’ perceptions of the benefits and costs and the relational aspects of the exchange can be influenced by controlling the characteristics the VA manifests. This stresses the importance of ensuring that they are carefully designed to manifest desired characteristics.

Findings in regards to the multiplicity of determinants that afect user self-disclosure suggest that current practices to induce self-disclosures are insuficient. The relatively small efects exerted by loss of privacy compared to other determinants indicate that designers need to shift their focus to improving the overall interaction experience and considering relational factors. Findings concerning the role of contextual factors draw attention to the importance of designing enjoyable interactions.

## Limitations and Future Research

The study has a number of limitations. First, the proposed model was only tested in the context of skin care advice. Our choice of this context was motivated by a number of factors. First, “health and personal care” constitutes one of the fastest growing product categories in online retail, with estimated sales of \$53 billion in 2019, up 18.6% from 2018, and forecasted sales of \$85 billion in 2022 [37]. Second, given the specialized nature of their attributes, decisions regarding these products are complex, which makes the use of VAs appropriate.

The study examines only a subset of potentially salient perceived benefits and costs. This is justified by the fact that the current study focuses on examining the overall suficiency of the rewards and costs explanation to self-disclosure. Future research should identify and examine the efects of additional benefits and costs. One such cost is perceived loss of face, which refers to the costs associated with revealing information that could lead to a loss of esteem or to a threat of embarrassment [88]. Similarly, social adjustment, which refers to the potential for gaining social approval and adhering to social norms through self-disclosing, is one potential benefit [50].

The results in regards to the percentages of variance explained highlight the need to identify more determinants of loss of privacy, and to a lesser degree outcome expectancy. Some of these determinants could be the other variables examined in the proposed model. Yet, given that the objective of this study was to investigate the determinants of selfdisclosure intentions rather than the interrelationships between these determinants, these relationships were not examined. Future research should also attempt to identify and examine additional antecedents of benefits and costs, which will increase the percentage of variance explained in these constructs.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the only study that attempts to understand how the design of a VA can cue desired perceptions that influence self-disclosure determinants. Future research should focus on identifying other design elements that can cue such perceptions, or those that could manifest newly identified desired characteristics. For example, in his typology of decisional guidance, Silver [71] identifies other types of decisional guidance that can be used to manifest specific characteristics that encourage self-disclosure, such as what is termed participative guidance, which can enhance perceptions of goal congruency and interdependence.

The study was cross-sectional in nature. A longitudinal study can shed some light on how the progression of the relationship afects the willingness to disclose personal information, especially when that information is socially sensitive in nature. The study conducted by White [88] shows that while relationship depth encourages disclosures by reducing the perceived loss of privacy, it also hinders disclosures when the elicited information could result in embarrassment.

## CONCLUSION

Motivated to understand the types and nature of the factors that afect users’ willingness to self-disclose to online VAs, and with a specific goal to identify how the design of these advisors can encourage self-disclosure, this study develops and tests a model of self-disclosure determinants. The model is anchored in the view of self-disclosure as a social exchange determined by its perceived benefits and costs, as well as a relational process, influenced by trust and perceptions of interdependence. The results of an empirical test of the proposed model reveal that both views of self-disclosure have equivalent predictive powers. Interestingly, both types of determinants are predicted by perceptions users form about the VA during their interactions. The latter can be cued through changing the design of the advisor.

The current research begins to fill the wide gap concerning the largely ignored area of self-disclosure in the context of VAs. Nonetheless, future research is needed to identify and test more determinants, and explore the efects of additional design elements.
