# The Value of the Structural Power of the Chief Information Oficer in Enhancing Forward-Looking Firm Performance\*

Cong Feng <sup>a</sup>, Pankaj C. Patel <sup>b</sup>, and Scott Fay <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Marketing, School of Business Administration, University of Mississippi, University, MS, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Management & Operations, Villanova School of Business, Villanova University, Villanova, PA, USA; <sup>c</sup>Department of Marketing, Whitman School of Management, Syracuse University, Syracuse, NY, USA

## ABSTRACT

Drawing on the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm and the top management team (TMT) structural power framework, we investigate the relationship between Chief Information Oficer (CIO) structural power and forward-looking firm performance. In addition, we examine the contingency roles of market turbulence, industry IT intensity, and operating eficiency in shaping the above relationship. Using a sample of 7,185 firm-year observations and a panel fixed-efects model, we find that CIO structural power is positively associated with forwardlooking firm performance (proxied by Tobin’s q). Further, our results suggest that the benefits of CIO structural power are higher under greater market turbulence, higher industry IT intensity, and greater operating eficiency. Our empirical results imply that the board of directors should consider endowing CIOs with a higher structural power, especially when an organization faces greater volatility in industry sales, competes in a more IT-intensive industry, and operates at a more eficient level. This research contributes to the RBV literature and integrates RBV and structural power frameworks to ofer newer insights into the contemporary role of an important TMT member, the CIO.

## KEYWORDS

chief information oficer; structural power; firm performance; market turbulence; industry IT intensity; operating eficiency; resource-based view

## INTRODUCTION

With an increasing need for data-driven decision making, the role of a Chief Information Oficer (CIO) becomes increasingly essential in a modern organization [15, 37, 103]. The growing importance of information technology (IT) to a variety of business functions [115] indicates that CIOs must intensively interact with other members of the top management team (TMT) to achieve a better strategic alignment between IT and business strategy [40, 83]. Although the presence of a CIO signals the importance of IT in the upper echelons, the CIO must have suficient structural power to assume a leadership role [37]. Structural power in the context of a TMT refers to an executive’s power that is based on formal organizational authority and structure [56]. Prior research indicates that structural power is crucial in that it allows executives to be less dependent on other TMT members when making decisions in their functional areas, gain more control over organizational resources, and play an eminent role in shaping an organization’s strategic direction [37, 56, 115]. While early research indicates that CIO appointment stimulates short-term positive reactions from the stock market [33], practitioners have mixed opinions about the status of CIOs in the TMT. Some believe that the role of CIOs has declined in recent years [65] and CIOs are oftentimes regarded as the “last among equals” in the upper echelons [41], while others believe that “CIOs are making headway in the C-suite” due to the increasingly critical role of IT in today’s business [48]. Indeed, without structural power, CIOs may play such a weak role in strategic leadership [37, 66] that they are unable to exert a substantial influence on firm performance [37, 43].

Previous research [e.g., 37, 84, 115, 130] argues that CIO structural power legitimizes a CIO’s actions, provides the CIO with an opportunity to bridge the IT-business gap, and allows the CIO to be an influential executive in the TMT. However, although scholars have explored the linkage between CIO structural power and several intermediate variables such as CIO leadership [37], CIO capability [130], IT assimilation [6], IT governance [22], the ultimate performance implications of CIO structural power remain unclear in the information systems (IS) literature . In addition, according to the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm [19, 52, 127], the eficacy of CIO structural power could be contingent upon other internal and external factors. Based on our literature review<sup>2</sup>, there is scant research on understanding the boundary conditions of CIO structural power. Given the mixed perspectives among academics and practitioners on the role and value of CIOs [33, 48, 65], it is important to address the boundary conditions of CIO structural power to further improve our understanding of the CIO-performance relationship. In practice, the board of directors should be keenly interested in knowing whether to endow CIOs with more structural power and, if so, to what extent, and under what contingencies, can CIO structural power directly contribute to firm performance. Given that CIO structural power may alter the CIO-TMT interactions over time and that the ensuing strategic decisions and implementation are realized across diferent time periods, we consider forward-looking firm performance in our research setting.

To fill the aforementioned gaps in the IS literature, we test the following research questions: (1) How is CIO structural power related to forward-looking firm performance? and (2) How do internal and external factors moderate the relationship between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance? To test the proposed research questions, we draw on a sample of 1,327 public firms from the Execucomp database and merge it with other secondary data sources (e.g., Standard & Poor’s Compustat and the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis). We construct a novel composite measure of CIO structural power by using a principal component factor analysis to combine four indicants of structural power. Using 7,185 firm-year observations over a 10-year period and a panel fixed-efects estimator, we find that CIO structural power is positively associated with forward-looking firm performance. Moreover, our empirical results reveal that market turbulence, industry IT intensity, and operating eficiency strengthen the positive association between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance. Our findings are robust to firm-level time-invariant unobservables, omitted variable bias, alternate measures of key variables, an alternate distributional assumption, alternate sample construction, and alternate model specifications. We also conduct additional analyses to assess the contribution of the Chief Marketing Oficer (CMO) structural power to firm performance relative to the contribution of CIO structural power to firm performance and to understand the relationship between CIO structural power and other firm outcomes. Results from these additional analyses are largely in line with our main findings.

The expected theoretical contributions of the current study are as follows. First, we complement TMT literature in general [e.g., 56] and the CIO literature in particular [e.g., 33] by formally documenting the significant and positive association between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance under a contingent resource-based framework. Our theoretical foundation integrates the RBV [12, 143] and the structural power framework [56, 114, 131]. Second, we complement existing literature on CIO structural power [e.g., 83, 116] and present the first empirical evidence to suggest that CIO structural power is particularly valuable when a company faces a turbulent environment, operates in an IT-intensive industry, and has high operating eficiency. Also, our framework extends existing knowledge on the role of CIO structural power and underscores the need for adopting a contingency perspective around this topic. To our knowledge, no empirical research has previously assessed the contingencies in the relationship between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance based on the external environment (i.e., market turbulence and industry IT intensity) and internal operating capability (i.e., operating eficiency). Third, we propose a new metric that captures the longitudinal variations of CIO structural power, thus allowing for a more nuanced and dynamic examination of CIO structural power. Most previous studies rely on cross-sectional surveys to measure CIO structural power [e.g., 5, 6]. While such a survey-based approach is appropriate for capturing CIO structural power across firms at a single point of time, this approach limits researchers from analyzing longitudinal changes in CIO structural power and the potential internal and external contingencies that influence the efectiveness of CIO structural power over time. Moreover, the survey-based approach may be subject to low response rates, thus preventing researchers from constructing a representative sample. We attempt to address these issues by developing a time-varying, composite measure of CIO structural power using secondary archival data. We corroborate this measure using other well-established secondary data sources (e.g., InformationWeek 500) and therefore interested researchers (and managers) may consider adopting this new measure for their studies, especially ones that need to leverage the longitudinal variations of CIO structural power.

Our expected practical contributions can be summarized as follows. First, we provide evidence to counter industry claims that CIOs’ status was diminishing in recent years. Our measure shows that CIO structural power has been trending upward from 2006 to 2015. Second, our findings rationalize the need for corporate boards to endow CIOs with more structural power, which we find to be instrumental in improving a firm’s forward-looking performance. In addition, our findings highlight that firms should closely monitor and evaluate contingencies (e.g., the degree of market turbulence, the intensity of industry IT capital, and the level of internal operating eficiency) as they may gain additional benefits from elevating the structural power of their CIOs under some conditions more than under other ones.

In the next section, we review the literature on CIOs and structural power in the TMT, followed by the presentation of our conceptual model. Next, we propose formal hypotheses for our conceptual model. Thereafter, we describe the sample, provide details on variable operationalization, present our results, and report robustness tests and additional findings. Finally, we discuss the implications of our study, identify limitations, and provide directions for future research.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## Research on CIOs

Over the past two decades, there has been growing recognition that greater specialization in the upper echelons is important due to greater complexity and uncertainty in the environment [73], and IS research has emphasized the importance of the role of CIOs [e.g., 33, 67, 130]. Online Appendix Table A1 presents a list of representative studies focusing on the CIO-TMT interface. Theoretical justifications for having a CIO present in the TMT include the importance of their informational and managerial roles [67, 130], their influence on firm performance and shareholder value [33, 111], and their facilitation of interfacing among members of the TMT [117], among others. CIOs are the catalysts for improving the application of IT to business problems and providing the necessary IS leadership at multiple levels of the firm to improve functional connectivity and to strengthen vertical relationships around the IT function [16, 17, 37].

It is critical to recognize that the successful implementation of IT requires more than just mastery of the technology itself. As stated by Feld and Stoddard [53, page 74], “[m]aking IT work demands the same things that other parts of the business do – inspired leadership, superb execution, motivated people, and the thoughtful attention and high expectations of senior management”. In other words, CIO leadership plays a critical role in influencing organizational outcomes [37]. Based on the power-influence perspective from the IS literature [37, 50], the impact of CIO leadership is dependent upon the power endowed to the CIO position because executive power is critical for influencing superiors, peers, subordinates, and members outside an organization [37, 56]. Next, we discuss the theoretical background for one of the most widely studied types of executive power, namely, the structural power in the TMT [37, 55, 115].

## Research on the structural power of functional TMT members

In the broader TMT literature, firm performance is contingent on the strategic dynamics in the upper echelons [50, 72, 117]. The interactions among the TMT members that drive information processing and the decision-making necessary to devise and implement strategy requires, among other elements, a more even distribution of power. The success of the TMT requires coordination, communication, and cooperation among its members [55]. Distribution of power among TMT members is critical to ensuring equitable inputs to such strategic discourse and action. Uneven distribution of power can undermine communication and information exchanges because some members may not have suficient influence [109, 131].

Though influence and power come in a variety of forms, much of the TMT literature on power has focused on the elements of structural power [e.g., 4, 37, 55, 153]. Online Appendix Table A2 presents a list of empirical studies in the IS and non-IS literature on the structural power of functional TMT members<sup>3</sup>. As defined previously, structural power refers to the power an executive derives from formal organizational authority and structure. The distribution of power in social relationships has been central to understand how teams develop, manage, and sustain interactions, how team member contributions are reflected in the team processes, and whether the knowledge and abilities of team members are fully utilized [49, 83]. An imbalance in power could lower communication quality, discourage information sharing, and reduce the overall competitiveness of a firm [114]. Structural power may also be the basis for the emanation of other modes of power and influence [113, 125]. Indeed, Chen et al. [37, page 245] suggest that “structural power, as compared to alternative constructs of power, is most strongly associated with an executive’s overall power level”. Without suficient structural power, a TMT member may lack the authority and rank in the corporate hierarchy to propose, develop, or contribute to the strategic agenda. Higher structural power increases contributions and engagement in the strategic decision-making process that can reshape an organization’s prospects.

As illustrated in Online Appendix Table A2, studies in the non-IS literature predominantly focus on Chief Financial Oficer (CFO), Chief Operating Oficer (COO), and CMO structural power. Researchers have found that the structural power of these functional TMT members is associated with audit fee negotiation [13], CEO dismissal when firm performance is low [153], and marketing capabilities [55], among others. However, a fuller understanding of structural power among other functional TMT members remains limited in the broader TMT literature. Feng et al. [55], who study CMO structural power, encourage future research to calibrate the structural power of other functional TMT members to gain a deeper understanding of power distribution within the TMT. We now turn to the IS literature on CIO structural power.

## CIO structural power

The conceptualization of structural power is rooted in Finkelstein’s framework of power in the upper echelons [56]. Finkelstein identifies several bases of power in a TMT, such as structural, expert, and prestige power. Though expertise and prestige form the softer basis of power, structural power is essential to exert necessary influence in the TMT and manage potential conflict [37, 56], allowing a CIO to strategically align IT with other business functions of the firm. Further, structural power establishes a structured system that allows a CIO to exchange knowledge with other TMT members [6], essentially helping the CIO develop business knowledge while equipping other TMT decision-makers with IT knowledge [116]. This formal and structured knowledge exchange process (facilitated by structural power) can lead to IT assimilation [6] and an enhanced shared understanding of the strategic role of IS among TMT members [116].

According to Chen et al. [37], structural power is vital for a CIO to be an efective and influential leader in the IT function. Essentially, “[t]he amount of status and power accorded a person is proportionate to the group’s evaluation of the person’s potential contribution relative to that of other members” [151, page 165]. Indeed, structural power allows a CIO to not only exercise IT leadership to facilitate an organization’s daily operations but also encourage and educate other organizational actors (e.g., peer TMT members) to leverage IT [37]. Rooted in the TMT power framework [56], prior studies have identified how CIO structural power is associated with IT-related outcomes (such as IT governance [22], electronic record sophistication [132], and IS planning [118]) and with CIO-related characteristics (such as CIO leadership [37], CIO authority [115] and CIO capability [130]).

## Conceptual model of the current study

While prior studies [e.g., 6, 37, 118] find that CIO structural power is associated with some intermediate organizational or CIO-related outcomes (see Online Appendix Table A2 for more details), the direct relationship between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance has not been formally documented in the CIO literature.<sup>4</sup> Conceptually, CIOs with higher structural power could play a more pivotal role in driving a firm’s ITrelated investments and commitments, determining the allocations of scarce IT resources, and closing the gap between IT and business [6, 37, 116, 130], all of which may significantly impact the expected performance of the firm (i.e., forward-looking firm performance).

Further, according to the contingent RBV [24, 127], the performance implications of CIO structural power could be dependent on external and internal contextual factors. Therefore, it is important to assess the boundary conditions of the association between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance. Prior RBV researchers argue that a firm’s underlying competency and industry context should be considered in studies of firm performance determinants [19, 52, 143]. Consistent with their argument, we investigate two external moderating factors that reflect industry context, namely, market turbulence and industry IT intensity [74, 79] and an internal moderating factor that corresponds to a core organizational competency, namely, operating eficiency [97, 110].

As to the first contingency, market turbulence induces uncertainty about appropriate resource allocations and other strategic decisions [94, 148]. A CIO with suficient structural power may play a critical role in improving future performance by devising, leveraging, and exploiting IT resources [37, 115, 130] in formulating and implementing strategic responses to market turbulence. Absent the strategic push (from the CIO) for leveraging IT in the TMT, the value of IT in responding to market turbulence may remain tactical. For the second contingency, as IT-related resources and capabilities in an industry increase [74], novel strategic combinations are required to create competitive advantage from future IT investments [120, 126]. Such eforts may require special attention from the TMT and larger structural power allows the CIO to draw such attention from his or her peers [37]. For the last contingency, since operating eficiency represents “a fundamental feature of competitive advantage” [97, page 156], it could serve as a complementary capability to CIO structural power, allowing for faster operational implementation of IT solutions initiated by a powerful CIO and reducing unnecessary waste of valuable IT resources overseen by the CIO. Therefore, the complementary value of operating eficiency and CIO structural power could be instrumental to long-term performance of a firm.

Overall, we expect a significant relationship between CIO structural power and forwardlooking firm performance. We also expect market turbulence, industry IT intensity, and operating eficiency, as contingencies, to shape the relationship between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance. ## DEVELOPMENT OF HYPOTHESES

## CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance

Our first hypothesis posits that CIO structural power is positively associated with forwardlooking firm performance. As one form of power obtained through formal organizational structure and authority, CIO structural power is an essential consideration because many important firm decisions do not have a singular power source but are instead driven by the collective influence of multiple functional areas within the TMT. In this research, we use Tobin’s q as a proxy for forward-looking firm performance.

Introduced by James Tobin [141], Tobin’s q is widely used to understand the influence of functional TMT members [21, 54, 60, 64, 72, 96, 99, 106, 107] on firm performance (see Columns CxO-TMT interface and Tobin’s q of Online Appendix Table A1 for details). Although TMT members may influence backward-looking accounting-based performance, they play an even more important role in shaping forward-looking growth opportunities and investments, managing business risk (which is not reflected in accounting measures), and driving the intangible value of the firm because they are in charge of strategic decisions and implementation of these decisions [98, 107, 122]. Compared to accounting measures of performance that are susceptible to managerial manipulation and reporting bias [18, 52, 134], Tobin’s q captures market expectations about a company’s prospects and thus, integrates various dimensions of firm performance (such as sales, cash flows, and profitability) and is risk-adjusted [52]. In the IS literature, the performance implications of IT investments are frequently evaluated using Tobin’s q [2, 11, 18, 79, 80, 138, 139]. The value of Tobin’s q as an important outcome of IT is highlighted in Bharadwaj et al. [18, page 1011], who call for using “q as an outcome measure, not only because of its inherent advantages over the accounting measures but also because it provides a better reflection of IT’s true contribution to firm value”. In our research context, CIO structural power represents a power distribution across a company’s TMT members that can influence strategic outcomes (such as IT investment and IT resource allocations) and growth prospects over diferent forward-looking time periods. Thus, we consider Tobin’s q to be a conceptually appropriate measure of forward-looking performance in our research context. We expect a positive association between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance (as measured by Tobin’s q) for the following reasons.

First, CIO structural power is essential to increase forward-looking performance when integrating the IT function with core business processes [37, 135]. In essence, structural power creates authority and organized arrangements [56] that allow a CIO to more eficiently manage and allocate IT resources (e.g., IT infrastructure, human IT skills, and IT knowledge) within a business. Drawing on the RBV, IS scholars [17, 143] have argued that firm-specific IT resources enable a firm to diferentiate itself from rivals and such resources are generally dificult to imitate and acquire, thereby allowing the firm to achieve competitive advantage through IT. Without suficient CIO structural power, it may be dificult to generate IT-enabled intangibles, realize IT-led strategies, and ensure critical IT knowledge flows across functions and value chain stakeholders. In other words, CIO structural power can be the catalyst for building valuable, rare, and hard to imitate IT resources, which in turn become the basis for a company’s future performance. Further, according to Finkelstein [56], structural power authorizes a CIO to “pull rank” to resolve conflicts and disputes during the process of leveraging and managing scarce IT resources in the broader strategic context.

Although the IS literature has long advocated for the need to integrate core business processes with the IT function [9, 17, 33], limited CIO structural power could significantly hamper the embedding of the IT function in a TMT. Consistent with the logic from Nath and Mahajan [108], CIO structural power can help improve the criticality, non-substitutability, and centrality of a CIO’s IT-related knowledge provision and the flow of strategic and non-strategic IT information exchange among TMT members [6, 116]. As such, structural power can facilitate CIOs’ involvement in the strategic decision-making process, which could be critical to improving forward-looking firm performance.

Second, in leveraging IT to improve long-term performance, a focus on both tangible and intangible IT resource bundles is necessary. According to Sirmon and Hitt [129], efective resource bundling can be achieved by having a high level of social capital, which creates power and influence [68]. Scholars have long recognized that social capital is an intangible resource that is embedded in the connections among team members [69]. By integrating the role of social capital in the RBV [12], Chisholm and Nielsen [38] suggest that “the service of social capital within an organization helps to create a resource base that is valuable, dificult to replicate, and grows through learning” [38, page 11]. Since CIO structural power reflects formal organizational arrangements that allow CIOs to form a structured connection with other TMT members in the upper echelons [116] and to contribute writ-large to the build-up of TMT social capital, CIO structural power could become an important enabler of superior firm performance.

Moreover, as a CIO interacts with non-IT functions and executives, structural power helps improve internal coordination and maintain mutual prioritization of IT at multiple organizational levels [6, 116]. In particular, CIO structural power may be essential for resolving competing preferences for IT and non-IT resources during the strategic decisionmaking process in the TMT because “strategic decisions follow the desires and choices of the most powerful organizational members” [115, page 613]. As TMT members deliberate merits of infusing and imbuing IT resources in a variety of strategic initiatives, structural power creates legitimacy for the CIO, thus facilitating his or her influence inside the TMT [37] that is necessary to create, develop, and implement organizational initiatives. Also, structural power enables CIOs to have more formal opportunities to interact with other stakeholders (e.g., board members) and to instill the role of IT among these stakeholders early on to achieve a shared understanding [62, 116].

Overall, the multifaceted aspects of structural power and the resulting access and influence can strengthen the strategic dimensionality of IT resources in driving forwardlooking firm performance. For these reasons, we expect that CIO structural power is necessary for the IT function to influence TMT decision making and to align IT with corporate strategies and across departments. This potential for CIO structural power to be a catalyst for improving forward-looking firm performance leads us to hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): CIO structural power in the TMT is positively associated with forwardlooking firm performance.

## Market turbulence and CIO structural power

Our second hypothesis proposes that market turbulence strengthens the positive association between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance. Market turbulence is defined as the rate and unpredictability of change in customer segments and customer preferences [77, 144, 145], which require firms to sense the environment and to reconfigure resources and capabilities to meet changing and less predictable customer needs. We expect market turbulence to strengthen the association between CIO structural power and performance for the following reasons.

First, under higher levels of market turbulence, forward-looking performance is under greater threat as investors and analysts may increasingly question whether sustained value creation is feasible in the face of less predictable markets and a changing environment. Market turbulence increases threats, opportunities, and constraints that, in turn, require the TMT to improve the firm’s prospects by developing a strategic response, better leveraging existing resources to develop such a response and managing uncertainty and risk in a turbulent environment. Facing market turbulence, the TMT must continuously improve the environment-firm fit [44]. Even in catastrophic scenarios, IT can help enhance the recovery of operations [8, 150] and can further improve value creation through IT services [146].

In the upper echelons, the involvement of the CIO through greater structural power could reduce uncertainty about the appropriate response to greater market turbulence. As TMTs attempt to sense and adapt in turbulent markets, CIOs who are equipped with IT expertise and experience [130] can leverage their structural power (complemented by expert power) to address environmental contingencies and sway the strategic agenda in the TMT.

Although expert power can facilitate a CIO’s participation in the strategic planning process, it is structural power that ensures the CIO can voice their concerns and be listened to in the face of market turbulence. Indeed, structural power allows CIOs to actively embed and etch their IT knowledge in the strategic discourse [37] and avoid being just a peripheral player in the TMT. Furthermore, resolving uncertainty is not automatic. Instead, it often requires experimentation and dabbling through the uncertain environment [1, 28]. CIO structural power may provide an impetus that helps the TMT collectively devise and implement the experimentation that is necessary for responding to and mitigating uncertainty.

Second, in their review of RBV in the IS literature, Wade and Hulland [143] propose that IS-business partnerships (i.e., integrating and aligning IS with other business functions), IS planning and change management, and IS-enabled market responsiveness are among the vital resources that can strongly influence a company’s competitive position. Further, they suggest that the relationship between these IS resources and firm performance is jointly determined by the degree of top management commitment to IS and market turbulence; specifically, such relationship is stronger when a firm has a higher degree of top management commitment to IS and faces a more turbulent environment [143]. Based on the above logic and given that CIO structural power could be an indicator of TMT’s commitment to IS and a catalyst to build up IS resources, CIO structural power could have significant performance implications in turbulent business environments.

Tactically, IT systems are critical for collecting and improving understanding of marketing, stakeholder, and logistical data [121, 137]. The circuitry of IT systems across functional areas is important for transmitting and acting on information about the extent of market turbulence and the causes of this turbulence. As the degree of market turbulence increases, firms must innovate oferings, manage costs, streamline operations and logistics, and retrain employees [63, 90]. CIO structural power could help facilitate firm adaptations and relearning in the face of market turbulence. A high-powered CIO can influence TMT decision-making by enhancing the development and implementation of IT systems that interface with multiple functions [6, 118] to improve market responsiveness under a higher degree of market unpredictability. CIO structural power leads to the development of ITrelated intangible assets such as IT systems and IT know-how, allowing firms to proactively transmit and act on information that could impact Tobin’s q directly and at the same time moderate the relationship.

Strategically, greater market turbulence increases information processing requirements [82]. Thus, the CIO’s role in managing information processing becomes even more critical when market turbulence is high. A more powerful CIO could provide the basis for information collection and dissemination, guide the discourse within the TMT on developing a more informed strategic response to a turbulent environment, and provide the necessary interpretation frames and mental models to process information. While a less powerful CIO may be able to fulfill the information provision role, it is unlikely that such a CIO could substantially enhance the information processing capability of the TMT, thus resulting in inferior decision-making. Market turbulence also calls for more innovation. The IT function is increasingly central to product and process innovation [77, 110]. With the increasing use of IT as a basis for building dynamic capabilities, a powerful CIO can further influence the combination of multiple innovations and advancements [62, 132] by guiding the TMT through improved assessment of customer needs, competitor actions, and technological advancements.

Third, the above arguments indicate that, on the whole, adaptive responses would improve forward-looking firm performance as the TMT can significantly improve strategic fit. Although CIO presence would have supplementary benefits even when there is weak CIO structural power, such power is necessary for the benefits to be complementary to TMT decision making. High CIO structural power is needed for CIOs to better address the gaps between current actual capabilities and the ideal ones in a changing environment. A thorough assessment of the firm’s information and resources is necessary for the TMT to identify capability gaps and to develop a new, value-maximizing capability configuration that addresses these gaps [63, 90, 105]. A powerful CIO can not only aid gap identification and assessment, he or she could also be critical to guiding the TMT to a feasible and suitable new capability configuration by using IT to identify unique resource combinations, which are a necessity for improving long-term performance under increasing market turbulence. For these reasons, we conjecture:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): The positive relationship between CIO structural power in the TMT and forward-looking firm performance is strengthened in firms facing higher market turbulence.

## Industry IT intensity and CIO structural power

Our third hypothesis proposes that industry IT intensity strengthens the positive association between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance. The conceptualization of industry IT intensity is rooted in the literature of IS [e.g., 74, 75, 100] and economics [e.g., 136] and further explicated by Benaroch and Chernobai [14, page 742] as “the ratio of IT capital (\$\$) and non-IT capital (\$\$) for an industry segment”. Higher industry IT intensity indicates IT plays a larger role in determining the value of intangible resources, in improving product innovation and quality, and in leveraging supplier and customer relationships [58, 74, 133]. Industry IT intensity can serve as an indicator of the importance of IT capital within an industry [74] and reveal the significance of the transformational impact of IT on an organization [89]. An IT-intensive environment requires more timely and relevant information on a variety of facets impacting marketing, operations, and innovation needs. As a result, a powerful CIO could play an increasingly important role in aligning external information with the internal IT knowledge gap [84, 130], a gap that is ever more important to fill in an industry with a higher IT intensity. We expect higher industry IT intensity to strengthen the CIO structural power and performance association for the following reasons.

High industry IT intensity implies that strategic actions embedded in IT assets can be imitated or substituted given that rivals within the same industry are likely to possess high IT-related capabilities [59, 74]. With strong rivalry in IT capabilities, CIOs may play an important role in identifying and developing additional opportunities to enhance strategic response, improve architectural innovation, and develop novel IT and non-IT resource combinations to improve strategic alignment. Without suficient power endowed to the IT functional head, inter-functional coordination and conflict resolution resulting from such eforts may be dificult [37, 55]. Thus, within an IT-intensive industry, CIOs with high structural power can provide and broker information across functional areas (i.e., serving the roles of educator and information steward<sup>6</sup>), play an elemental role in resolving ITbusiness conflict and misalignment (i.e., serving the roles of integrator and relationship architect), and help improve an organization’s forward-looking performance.

Further, in IT-intensive environments, a powerful CIO not only can maintain close monitoring of the environment but also can leverage his or her endowed influence to develop unique value propositions (i.e., serving the role of strategist) that utilize IT to identify and integrate the firm’s resources and capabilities [45, 130]. When there is higher industry IT intensity, a powerful CIO can play a central role in improving, reconfiguring, and realigning resources to enhance a firm’s competitiveness, especially when the firm’s rivals are increasingly attempting to create competitive advantages based on IT. In implementing strategic actions under higher industry IT intensity, following the past literature on IT intensity and governance [14, 140, 149], CIO structural power could be critical for the implementation of a strong IT cross-functionality that fully integrates the firm’s resources and capabilities. Thus, we conjecture:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): The positive relationship between CIO structural power in the TMT and firm performance is strengthened under higher industry IT intensity.

## Operating eficiency and CIO structural power

Our fourth hypothesis proposes that operating eficiency strengthens the positive association between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance. Operating eficiency refers to the ability to transform operational inputs into outputs by leveraging internal and external resources [92, 123]. Operating eficiency leverages both tangible (e.g., physical assets) and intangible (e.g., human capital) operational resources to enhance competitive advantage [119, 123]. We expect higher operating eficiency to strengthen the association between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance for the following reasons.

First, in an increasingly information-based economy, IT resources are in constant flux. CIOs endowed with higher structural power can manage an organization’s IT portfolio with greater authority [37] and enhance the organization’s IT capability reputation [93]. Based on the complementary capability perspective [81, 101], operating eficiency can serve as a complementary capability to IT capability (enhanced by CIO structural power), allowing an organization to achieve firm-level competitive advantage. Higher operating eficiency implies well-established operational routines that facilitate the conversion of inputs and outputs with lower cost, higher flexibility, better quality, greater timeliness, or a combination of these four operational outcomes. As CIOs leverage their structural power to improve firm IT capability and work with other TMT members to improve strategic IT-business alignment, high operating eficiency could allow for faster operational implementation of strategic IT initiatives and preservation of valuable IT resources without waste. Thus, the complementary value of CIO structural power and operating eficiency could help firms achieve better forward-looking performance.

Second, although CIOs with higher structural power can make decisions with more authority and control, these decisions are not without risk. From an IT risk management perspective [142], as a CIO leverages his or her structural power to enhance forwardlooking firm performance, the lower firm risk facilitated by higher operating eficiency [110, 123] can help hedge the potential risk associated with IT decisions made by the CIO and thereby strengthen the eficacy of CIO structural power. In contrast, lower operating eficiency is associated with organizational rigidity [110], which can significantly hinder future deployment and/or changes of IT resources overseen by CIOs. Even when a CIO is endowed with more structural power, lower operating eficiency may deter IT information exchange, impose challenges to IT implementation, and limit the eficient usage of IT resources within an organization, resulting in the limited ability of the CIO to leverage structural power to improve forward-looking firm performance. As a result, we expect:

Hypothesis 4 (H4): The positive relationship between CIO structural power in the TMT and firm performance is strengthened in firms with higher operating eficiency.

## DATA, ANALYSIS, AND RESULTS

## Data and sample construction

We draw our initial sample from the Execucomp universe, which covers S&P 1500 firms.<sup>7</sup> We then merge this initial sample with financial data from Compustat, industry IT and non-IT capital data from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, segment sales data from Compustat Segment files, and 10-K data from the Notre Dame Software Repository for Accounting and Finance. Although merging data from diferent sources reduces the sample size, doing so allows us to mitigate common method bias. Similar to existing research on TMT [35], financial and utilities firms are excluded. The final sample includes 7,185 firmyear observations representing 1,327 firms from 2006 to 2015. We opt for the post– 2006 period to avoid confounding efects because a significant revision to the accounting disclosure policy for executive compensation became efective on June 15, 2005 [39]. Our final sample covers more than 40 SIC 2-digit industries. Online Appendix Figure A1 provides industry descriptions and also shows that firm distribution in our sample (solid line) is consistent with that in the Execucomp universe (dotted line) for the same period. Online Appendix Table A3 presents the variable definitions and data sources. Next, we detail our key variables of interest.

## Variable measurement

## CIO structural power

As shown in Online Appendix Table A2, most prior studies in IS measure CIO structural power using primary data (especially cross-sectional surveys). Empirically speaking, the survey approach is appropriate to detect cross-sectional variations in CIO structural power. However, when it comes to longitudinal variations, it is typically not feasible to survey the same company repeatedly over a long period of time. The typical low response rate of surveys also makes it dificult to construct a representative sample. Due to these concerns, we turn to secondary archival data to measure CIO structural power.

CIO structural power in the TMT is not directly observable in the archival data. We propose a new measure of CIO structural power by using objective power indicants to infer CIO structural power. Specifically, CIO structural power can be captured by the following dimensions: CIO presence in the TMT, CIO compensation relative to his or her peers, CIO pay rank in the TMT hierarchy, and CIO tenure in a top-paid position. Similar power indicants have been used by Feng et al. [55], who study CMO structural power, and by Kolev et al. [86], who study CEO structural power. Note that in our robustness checks we also consider an additional indicant (i.e., CIO responsibilities) and diferent combinations of the above power indicants as guided by the conceptualizations of executive structural power in existing studies [e.g., 55, 116]. As shown in Online Appendix Table A2, most empirical studies in the CIO literature use a single (e.g., CIO rank) or two power indicants (mostly CIO rank plus one other dimension) to measure CIO structural power, even though research from the non-IS literature indicates that executive structural power is multidimensional and the underlying dimensions could reach as many as five (see Column Measurement of CxO structural power of Online Appendix Table A2). We incorporate insights from both streams of literature in our operationalization of CIO structural power.

First, since a firm’s TMT is responsible for daily decision-making and for allocating organizational assets [57], CIO presence could be indicative of a firm’s desire to allocate power to IT-related executive(s) who can bring IT management skills to the TMT. Further, CIO presence can be “interpreted as signaling both changes in the collective mindset of the business’s senior executive team regarding the importance and role of IT and, implicitly, subsequent efects on the business’s strategies and performance” [33, page 45]. Therefore, we use the proportion of a firm’s named executive oficers with IT-related job titles as the first indicator of CIO structural power. Before determining the proportion of CIOs in the TMT, we need to identify CIO presence correctly. Based on prior studies [33, 98] and industry practice (i.e., executive titles listed on prior InformationWeek 500 rankings), we first text-mine the titles of all executives in the Execucomp database using a combination of the following IT-related keywords: “cio,” “cto,” “technology,” “tech,” “information,” “info,” “system,” “software,” “it,” and “is”. Next, two coders manually examine the titles identified in the first step to further rule out non-CIOs and resolve any discrepancies through consensus (inter-coder agreement is more than 90 percent). Finally, we use the number of CIOs scaled by the total number of reported TMT size as the first indicator of CIO structural power.

Second, we capture CIO structural power based on the total compensation ofered to CIO(s) relative to other TMT members. In order to accomplish organizational objectives, firms frequently use greater compensation to attract talent [55, 104]. Previous research suggests that executive compensation is indicative of an executive’s centrality in an organization and relative pay reflects the relative significance of the executive’s power [32]. Further, compensation committees actively revise pay scales based on market and internal information and create pay diferentials across the corporate hierarchy to better motivate employees [29, 95]. In fact, “compensation can be considered an important indicator of formal power” [56, page 512]. Therefore, we use the total compensation ofered to CIO(s) as a fraction of total compensation of the whole TMT as the second indicator of CIO structural power.

Third, we capture CIO structural power based on the hierarchical position of a CIO. As the “most easily recognizable, legitimate structural position” [23, page 462], the hierarchical position provides a direct signal of an executive’s power within a TMT. To capture the hierarchical position, we use the following metric: $\frac { \mathrm { \ ^ { \prime } T M T \ s i z e } ) - ( \mathrm { C I O \ r a n k } ) } { ( \mathrm { T M T \ s i z e } ) - 1 }$ . Notice that this metric is reverse coded so that larger values are associated with a higher CIO rank. Furthermore, this metric accounts for diferent sizes of a TMT so that, regardless of the size of the TMT, the measure will reside on the interval [0, 1]. In the U.S., public firms are required to disclose compensation paid to their CEOs and other top-ranking executives who are responsible for corporate decision-making (usually the top five executives). Of note, Execucomp contains such compensation information for up to 12 executives in our sample. Generally, the CEO is the highest-paid executive followed by other top executives with varying pay levels. We utilize Execucomp’s EXECRANKANN variable that provides the pay rank of top executives based on total salary and bonus. Using such pay-based ranking among top executives reported by Execucomp, we construct an indicator of CIO structural power. For instance, if a CIO is ranked as the fourth executive in Execucomp and there are 5 executives in total, then this CIO’s positional power is (5-4)/(5-1)=0.25. In contrast, if a CIO is ranked as the second executive on Execucomp and there are 6 executives in total, then this CIO’s positional power is (6-2)/ (6-1)=0.8.

Lastly, we capture CIO structural power based on a CIO’s tenure in a top-paid position. As indicated by prior studies [86, 87], longer tenure in a position reflects greater power held by a top executive. Since Execucomp does not collect tenure information of non-CEO executives [51], we trace an executive’s presence at a firm in Execucomp since the inception of the database in 1992. Similar to prior studies [30, 51], the first year of a CIO’s tenure is equal to the first time when the CIO appears at a firm in Execucomp. Although this approach left-censors CIO tenure, the error is systematic across all CIOs. Formally, CIO tenure is defined as the diference between the current year and the first year of tenure in a top-paid position of the CIO. Although our measure of CIO tenure appears to be conservative, the average of CIO tenure among firms that appoint a CIO in our sample (4.042 years, see Table A5(a)) is in line with a recent industry report that indicates the average tenure of CIOs is less than 5 years [85].

Because the above indicants are highly related to each other (inter-variable correlation ranges from 0.591 to 0.902), we employ a principal component factor analysis to construct a composite measure. Using a composite measure over individual measures of executive power is consistent with many prior studies [e.g., 26, 55, 87]. Factor analysis results showed that all indicants loaded onto a single factor, accounting for 78.91% of the total variance with the following factor loadings: 0.9367, 0.9398, 0.8735, and 0.7956. After rotating the factor loadings, we obtain the Bartlett factor score and then normalize this score to [0, 10] to arrive at our measure of CIO structural power. We conduct six validity tests to demonstrate the validity of the CIO structural power measure. These test results are presented in a later section. As shown in Online Appendix Figure A2, the average CIO structural power in our sample exhibits an upward trend from 2006 to 2015, which is consistent with industry reports that CIOs have recently been gaining more prominent roles within the TMT [e.g., 48].

## Market turbulence

Consistent with Haleblian and Finkelstein [70] and Hambrick and Abrahamson [71], we measure market turbulence using the coeficient of variation of SIC 2-digit industry sales over a five-year moving window including the focal year. This is a widely used measure in the literature and allows us to capture the relative variability of market conditions [52].

## Industry IT intensity

Following Benaroch and Chernobai [14], we use the ratio between IT and non-IT capital stock at the industry-year level to measure industry IT intensity. Specifically, we collect data from the yearly Fixed Assets Accounts Tables (Section 3 – Private fixed assets by industry) provided by the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis [14, 75]. IT capital stock is obtained from Table 3.1E. Current-Cost Net Stock of Private Equipment by Industry (accessed on July 16, 2019). As noted by Benaroch and Chernobai [14, page 746], the assets covered in Table 3.1E are “computer and peripheral equipment, communications, instruments, photocopy and related equipment, ofice and accounting equipment, and software.” We obtain our measure of non-IT capital stock from Table 3.1ESI. Current-Cost Net Stock of Private Fixed Assets by Industry (accessed on November 10, 2019), which provides an estimate of the total net stock of private fixed assets.

## Operating eficiency

Operating eficiency is not directly observable but can be inferred from observational data. We adopt an input-output approach that is frequently used in the marketing and management literatures, namely, the stochastic frontier approach [36, 47]. Essentially, this approach allows researchers to evaluate an entity’s eficiency of converting observed inputs into observed output. Studies have adopted this approach to measure operating eficiency [123], marketing eficiency [47], and innovation eficiency [46]. In our case, we are interested in measuring how far firms are from full operating cost minimization (i.e., operating eficiency).

Following recent developments in stochastic frontier models [88], we implement the state-of-the-art four-factor stochastic cost frontier model to estimate operating eficiency. This model is useful for teasing out irrelevant factors that are not associated with firm (in) eficiency (i.e., irrelevant firm-specific efects and idiosyncratic noise) and for obtaining more accurate measures of firm-specific time-invariant ineficiency and firm-specific timevarying ineficiency, whose product can be regarded as a proxy for operating eficiency. In our empirical implementation, we follow common practice in the literature [88] by first specifying a Cobb-Douglas cost function with operating expense as the output and the total number of employees, fixed assets, and current assets as the inputs [123] and then using the natural log of the Cobb-Douglas model for estimation. The results of the stochastic frontier model are presented in Online Appendix Table A4.

## Forward-looking firm performance

We use Tobin’s q, a market-based measure, to evaluate firm performance. Compared to traditional financial metrics such as firm profitability or revenues that are subject to reporting bias and managerial manipulation [18, 52, 134], a market-based metric provides a more objective, comparable, and forward-looking measure [52]. Please refer to the previous section, CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance, for extensive discussion of the advantages of using Tobin’s q to measure firm performance. Using Tobin’s q as a performance measure is also consistent with prior studies on TMTs [21, 64, 72, 96, 98]. Following these precedents allows us to enhance the rigor of the current study and better integrate our findings into the literature. We define Tobin’s q as the ratio between market value and total assets. We measure the market value of a firm as the sum of common stock, preferred stock, and total liabilities minus current assets [7, 20]. We also measure Tobin’s q using alternative approaches in the robustness checks.

## Control variables

Drawing from prior literature [e.g., 10, 27, 61, 91], we include several variables to control for various TMT characteristics, firm characteristics, and industry diferences. In particular, we follow prior work [64, 86, 91, 99, 122] by including several variables to capture TMT characteristics. First, we control for CEO structural power. Similar to CIO structural power, we use principal component factor analysis to extract a factor that represents CEO tenure, CEO duality (i.e., serving as chairman of the board in addition to serving as CEO), and CEO pay slice (i.e., CEO total compensation as a share of TMT’s total compensation). Second, we control for CEO gender (i.e., Female CEO indicator), and Reported TMT size (i.e., number of executives reported by Execucomp). In addition, we follow previous studies [e.g., 64, 122] by including several firm characteristics that may explain variation in firm performance, including Profitability, Employees, Business diversification, Innovativeness, Capital investment, and Leverage. We also control for industry competition by including a measure of Industry concentration, namely the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index. Lastly, we include firm fixed efects to control for time-invariant unobservables at the firm level, industry-by-year fixed efects to control for industry-level trends, booms, and busts, and year fixed efects to control for time heterogeneities.

## Endogeneity of CIO structural power

Our empirical investigation may sufer an omitted variable bias due to the possibility that some firm-level unobservable factors (such as management practice or firm culture) may be correlated with CIO structural power (i.e., influencing a public company’s decision to allocate more or less structural power to a CIO) but are omitted from the empirical analysis. We adopt the control function approach [14, 124, 147] that allows us to “solv[e] the problem of endogenous explanatory variables (EEVs) in linear and nonlinear models” [147, page 420]. From a methodology standpoint, the control function approach is similar to two-stage least squares, which can be implemented in two steps. First, we regress the endogenous variable (CIO structural power) on all control variables plus an instrument that satisfies the assumption of relevance and exogeneity. Similar to Benaroch and Chernobai [14], we exploit industry variation, that is, the yearly industry median value of CIO structural power, as our instrumental variable. As shown in Online Appendix Table A6, the first stage estimation suggests that the instrument is highly related to CIO structural power, thus providing support for the relevance assumption and potential evidence of validity of our instrument.

Although it is not feasible to test the exogeneity assumption empirically, we argue that our instrument is plausibly exogenous.<sup>9</sup> In particular, it is improbable for there to be an unobserved variable, such as management practice, that leads peer firms in the same industry to move collectively in a way that causes changes in the industry median of CIO structural power to correspond closely to changes in the focal firm’s performance. In other words, the industry median value of CIO structural power can arguably satisfy the requirement of exclusion restriction (exogeneity) because, while it is likely to be associated with a firm’s CIO structural power, it is less likely to be correlated with the error term in the equation that examines performance determinants. To mitigate concern about industrylevel shocks at a given period (e.g., industry booms and busts), we incorporate industry-byyear fixed efects in our empirical analysis [64]. In the second step, we plug the estimated residuals (termed Control function residuals) from the first stage estimation into our main empirical model, allowing us to mitigate omitted variable bias by controlling for missing information that is not accounted for by all the independent variables but is related to CIO structural power.

## Validity tests of CIO structural power

To establish the validity of our proposed measure of CIO structural power, we examine the convergent and discriminant validity of this measure by testing the relationship between CIO structural power and variables from two well-known secondary databases. First, we examine the convergent validity of CIO structural power by testing the relationship between CIO structural power and a firm’s likelihood of being one of the nation’s top IT innovators. InformationWeek publishes an annual ranking of the top 500 innovators of IT (i.e., IW500), which has been used in various studies in IS to proxy for a firm’s IT capability [e.g., 31, 76]. Theoretically speaking, it is expected that firms with powerful CIOs are more likely to receive recognition for their IT capabilities. Indeed, as shown in Column 1 of Table 2, CIO structural power is significantly associated with the likelihood of being included on the list of IW500. We also link CIO structural power to a firm’s strength in privacy and data security as identified in the Kinder, Lindenberg, and Domini (KLD) database.<sup>10</sup> Firms with a powerful CIO are more likely to build a strong IT infrastructure, thus receiving a higher score in the area of IT security. In the KLD database, two variables reflect a company’s strength in privacy and data security within the product dimension, that is, product safety and quality (KLD item: PRO-STR-A) and product safety – privacy and data security (KLD item: PRO-STR-I). ## Analysis and results

Our formal econometric model can be expressed as follows for firm i in year t:

<div class="mineru-algorithm" style="white-space: pre-wrap; font-family:monospace;">
Performance $_{i,t+1}$  =  $\beta_{0} + \beta_{1}(CIO\ structural\ power)_{i,t} + \beta_{2}(Market\ turbulence)_{i,t}$ $+ \beta_{3}(Industry\ IT\ intensity)_{i,t} + \beta_{4}(Operating\ efficiency)_{i,t}$ $+ \beta_{5}(CIO\ structural\ power)_{i,t} \times (Market\ turbulence)_{i,t}$ $+ \beta_{6}(CIO\ structural\ power)_{i,t} \times (Industry\ IT\ intensity)_{i,t}$ $+ \beta_{7}(CIO\ structural\ power)_{i,t} \times (Operating\ efficiency)_{i,t}$ $+ \beta_{8}(Control\ function\ residuals)_{i,t} + Xb_{i,t} + Fixed\ effects + \varepsilon_{i,t+1},$
</div>

where $X b _ { i , t }$ represents a set of control variables discussed above, including CEO structural power, Female CEO, Reported TMT size, Profitability, Employees, Business diversification, Innovativeness, Capital investment, Leverage, and Industry concentration; Fixed efects represent firm, year, and industry trends (i.e., industry-by-year) fixed efects<sup>11</sup>; $\varepsilon _ { i , t + 1 }$ represents random shocks. In our robustness checks, we modify the above specification by including additional fixed efects to remove other unobservables.

Because the Hausman test [78] favors a fixed-efects estimator, we opt for the panel fixedefects regression in estimating all coeficients of interest. We present our estimation results in Table 3, starting with several base models in Columns 1-4 and the full model in Column 5. In discussing our empirical results, we use the results shown in Column 5 of Table 3.

First, we find that the coeficient of CIO structural power is 0.594 (p<0.05), suggesting a positive relationship between CIO structural power and Tobin’s q (i.e., forward-looking firm performance). Regarding the interaction terms, we find that the coeficients of CIO structural power × Market turbulence, CIO structural power × Industry IT intensity, and CIO structural power × Operating eficiency are 0.796 (p<0.01), 0.395 (p<0.01), and 0.322 $\scriptstyle ( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , respectively. These results suggest that the positive relationship between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance is strengthened by market turbulence, industry IT intensity, and operating eficiency, respectively. Of note, we find evidence of endogeneity of CIO structural power given that the coeficient of Control function residuals is significant at the 1% level.

## Robustness checks

We conduct a wide range of tests to examine the robustness of our results. To conserve space, we provide details of each robustness check in Online Appendix B and present empirical results in Online Appendix Tables A7, A8, and A9, respectively. We find strong support for our main findings through 17 robustness checks that employ alternative measures of key variables, alternative distributional assumption, alternative model specifications, alternative sample construction, and alternative mechanism.

## CIO structural power and CMO structural power

Although previous research has documented that CMO structural power is instrumental to firm performance [55], an intriguing extension of the current study is to explore the relative contribution to firm performance between CIO and CMO structural power. We present preliminary evidence in this section by incorporating into equation (1) a similar measure of CMO structural power. Specifically, we conduct factor analysis on the same four power indicants about the CMO and extract a composite measure of CMO structural power. Online Appendix Table A10 presents the empirical results after incorporating CMO structural power. In line with prior research [55], CMO structural power is found to be positively associated with forward-looking firm performance. Further, our main findings related to CIO structural power are still valid under this new specification. More interestingly, according to the results presented in Column (5) of Table A10, the coeficient of CIO structural power (0.589, p<0.05) is larger than that of CMO structural power (0.031, p<0.05), suggesting that CIO structural power has a larger association with forwardlooking firm performance than does CMO structural power.

## CIO structural power and other firm outcomes

Although not the focus of the current paper, we explore two other firm outcomes in this section. First, we study the relationship between CIO structural power and Altman’s Z score, which is a reverse indicator of corporate bankruptcy likelihood [3]. We follow Denis and Mihov [42] to construct a dependent variable (DV) of Altman’s Z score and report the results in Columns 1-2 of Online Appendix Table A11 using Z score at t+1 and t+2, respectively. Second, we study the association between CIO structural power and a firm’s organization capital, such as human capital and distribution systems [112]. We report the empirical results in Columns 3-4 of Table A11 using the natural log of organization capital (provided by Peters and Taylor [112]) as a DV at t+1 and t+2, respectively. We find that market turbulence positively moderates the relationship between CIO structural power and Altman’s Z score. We also find that industry IT intensity positively moderates the relationship between CIO structural power and organization capital. Lastly, we find that operating eficiency positively moderates the relationship between CIO structural power and the above outcome variables.

These results further enrich our understanding of CIO structural power and suggest that, although CIO structural power by itself (the main efect) is not significantly associated with these additional firm outcomes, the eficacy of CIO structural power varies significantly across diferent external and internal organizational contexts. Specifically, a powerful CIO is particularly beneficial for increasing an average firm’s distance from bankruptcy (as measured by Altman’s Z score), especially under a more turbulent market environment. We also find that CIO structural power is more positively associated with a firm’s organization capital if the industry where the firm operates is more IT-intensive. For firms with higher operating eficiency, a powerful CIO is also desirable in that corporate bankruptcy likelihood can be further reduced while organization capital can be further improved.

## DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

## Theoretical implications

The core objective of this study is to determine whether CIO structural power is associated with forward-looking firm performance and to assess whether market turbulence, industry IT intensity, and operating eficiency strengthen this association. Our empirical results support the proposed hypotheses and make the following theoretical contributions. The current research contributes to a significant body of work in IS on the value of CIOs (refer to Online Appendix Table A1 for a list of prior studies on CIOs) and complements existing research on CIO structural power (refer to Online Appendix Table A2). The findings provide support for the organizational power literature, specifically, in indicating the important efects of the power dynamics within the TMT [56]. The positive association we find between CIO structural power and Tobin’s q highlights the value of CIO structural power in realizing the potential benefits of CIO presence that have previously been identified in the literature [33, 152].

It is rarely, if ever, feasible to qualitatively measure a CIO’s level of control, efectiveness, and centrality to information flows. However, we believe that our approach of quantifying CIO structural power using a composite measure based on multiple power indicants from secondary data sources is an important alternative to the survey-based approach (which is the predominant approach employed in the CIO literature). We corroborate this composite measure with diferent well-established databases and find that it demonstrates reasonable validity. This measure has the potential to be adopted by future research around the topic of CIO structural power.

Our study also highlights the important role contingencies play in the relationship between CIO structural power and forward-looking firm performance. Our study is among the first to hypothesize and test the contingent roles of market turbulence, industry IT intensity, and operating eficiency in relation to the performance implications of CIO structural power. Our fundamental argument is that CIO structural power could help improve performance by providing CIOs the influence they need to improve alignment between IS and their firms’ resources and capabilities. Although not directly observable, the positive moderation efects across the above moderators suggest that CIO structural power is eficacious in improving alignment between IS and firms’ resources and capabilities. However, additional research is necessary to examine more closely the mechanisms through which CIOs exert their influence.

We also respond to the call from Feng et al. [55] for research that compares the relative contribution to firm performance by CMO structural power and CxO structural power. As shown in Online Appendix Table A10, we do find that both CIO and CMO structural power are positively associated with Tobin’s q. However, comparing the coeficients of these two variables reveals that CIO structural power seems to have a larger association with a firm’s forward-looking performance than does CMO structural power. Although this is preliminary evidence, we conjecture the reason is that the IT function has a more fundamental influence on a company’s future operations than does the marketing function.<sup>12</sup>

## Managerial implications

CIOs are considered a voice of IT in the upper echelons, championing the value of IS to generate competitive advantages for firms. Although appointing a CIO to the TMT is necessary for IT to have the requisite influence in the upper echelons, the endowment and exercise of structural power for CIOs are also important considerations for firms. In light of recent divergent opinions from practitioners toward the status of CIOs in the TMT [48, 65], we provide clear evidence that CIO structural power exhibited an upward trend between 2006 and 2015. As shown in Online Appendix Figure A2, the upward trajectory in CIO structural power over the 10-year period highlights the potential importance of CIOs within companies and, therefore, our sample (which covers more than 40 industries) provides evidence that counters any perception that CIOs were losing their prominence in recent years. The findings also call on the board of directors to pay attention to the performance benefits of CIO structural power. Our findings suggest that being simply present in the TMT is not suficient for CIOs to improve firm performance. Only when CIOs are endowed with suficient power can companies realize performance enhancement from the functional heads of IT. Our empirical support for the significant association between CIO structural power and performance under three contingencies—market turbulence, industry IT intensity, and operating eficiency—also helps identify which firms would benefit the most from increasing CIO structural power. Overall, we suggest that practitioners should closely monitor their external environments and internal firm competencies to achieve additional benefits from CIO structural power.

## Limitations and directions for further research

Our findings are not without limitations. First, though consistent with CxO research in the TMT literature, the structural power view does not fully explain the behavioral aspects of power dynamics in the TMT. Future research is needed to assess the tools, channels, and modes of CIO influence in the TMT. Qualitative studies may be particularly valuable in this context. CIO’s personality and temperament would also be key factors in explaining the eficacy of CIO structural power. Second, CIOs must also explore means to not only improve their structural power in the TMT but also explore how they can provide and control critical IS resources in a central and non-substitutable manner. Third, the current understanding of the micro-dynamics of the strategy-making process within TMT remains limited. Future studies could leverage more advanced techniques and proprietary data to detect how decisions are made in the upper echelons and how organizational actors with more power influence the decision-making process. Fourth, although we have attempted to reduce endogeneity concerns about CIO structural power by using the control function approach, future studies could leverage exogenous policy shocks to understand the plausibly causal efects of CIO structural power. Fifth, future research could examine the underlying mechanism between CIO structural power and firm performance. It will be useful to identify potential variables that mediate this relationship. Note that in one of our robustness checks, we study the mediating efect of operating eficiency (instead of treating it as a moderator). However, our empirical evidence does not support the idea that operating eficiency functions as a mediator. Sixth, because security law does not require disclosure of firm-level IT investment, our analysis focuses entirely on industry-level IT intensity. It would be useful for future research to examine how the association between CIO structural power and firm performance depends on a firm’s IT intensity relative to its competitors, which would require development of a reliable metric for firm-level IT intensity. Finally, the CIO’s internal and external network of relationships is worthy of investigation. Our findings have demonstrated the variegated role of CIOs in the market and operational facets. CIO structural power is one dimension of power. However, the network position among the external and internal stakeholders provides the basis of much needed softer power necessary for integration and infusion of IT in organizational resources and capabilities.

## Conclusions

In this paper, we explore the relationship between CIO structural power and forwardlooking firm performance, as measured by Tobin’s q. We not only find a positive relationship but also uncover three contingencies that moderate the positive relationship. Specifically, we find that market turbulence, industry IT intensity, and operating eficiency exhibit significant and positive moderating efects on the relationship between CIO structural power and firm performance. The current paper theoretically contributes to our understanding of the value of the structural power framework as a determinant of the eficacy of CIOs. Though existing studies and industry reports have mixed results on the importance of CIOs, we ofer empirical evidence that the status of CIOs was trending upwards over the period of 2006-2015. Overall, the empirical findings identified in the current study highlight the necessity of endowing this key TMT member with suficient structural power to improve firm performance.
