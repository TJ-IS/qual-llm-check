# Detecting Noncompliant Behavior in Organizations: How Online Survey Responses and Behaviors Reveal Risk

Jefrey L. Jenkins, Joseph S. Valacich, Aaron F. Zimbelman, and Mark F. Zimbelman

Brigham Young University, Marriott School of Business, 790 Tanner Building, Provo, UT 84602 USA; The University of Arizona, Eller College of Management, McClelland Hall Rm 430L, 1130 E Helen Street; PO Box 210108, Tucson, AZ 85721 USA; University of South Carolina, Darla Moore School of Business, 1014 Greene Street, Columbia, SC 29208; Brigham Young University, Marriott School of Business, 514 Tanner Building, Provo, UT 84602 USA

## ABSTRACT

Ensuring organizational regulatory and legal compliance is a challenging, high-stakes management task. Overlooking or underestimating noncompliance in organizations can result in substantial fines, damage to a company’s reputation, and, ultimately, loss of business. To help alleviate this risk, we explore whether organizations can assess noncompliance by monitoring users’ answers and related mouse cursor movements in an intelligent online questionnaire. Namely, we propose that noncompliant (compared with compliant) individuals will experience more cognitive dissonance on questions about (1) what constitutes noncompliance behavior and (2) what consequences for noncompliance are appropriate. We predict that increased cognitive dissonance in noncompliant individuals will influence their questionnaire responses—as well as their mouse cursor movements—when answering these questions. We collect data to test our hypotheses in a study from individuals who voluntarily chose to cheat on an online task for monetary gain. The results suggest that the responses to these two groups of questions, along with the associated mouse cursor movement, can work together as a low-cost, scalable tool to help assess noncompliance risk. This paper contributes to theory by explaining how people who are noncompliant tend to provide more lenient answers to questions about what constitutes compliance and the consequences of noncompliance. In addition, we show how mouse-cursor deviation provides theoretical insight into the level of cognitive dissonance that users experience, and that users who are noncompliant show greater deviation on compliance and consequence questions.

## INTRODUCTION

Organizations can face severe consequences for the actions of a handful of employees who are noncompliant with laws and regulations. For example, governments can impose costly fines or sanctions when an organization’s members engage in bribing foreign oficials (per the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, hereafter, FCPA), violate provisions intended to prevent money laundering [12], or engage in fraudulent financial reporting (e.g., various laws under the Securities and Exchange Commission [SEC] or equivalent agencies around the world).

In 2020, Airbus was fined nearly US\$4 billion for violating anti-money laundering regulations [89], and in 2019, the SEC announced that Siemens, GE, and Phillips were being investigated for FCPA violations in China [6]. Yet increasing compliance with such laws can be dificult for organizations, especially in large firms that may have numerous employees in locations spanning the globe (e.g., in 2020, Airbus had 135,000 employees throughout the world). One step is to assess compliance in order to strategically allocate resources (e.g., training, auditing, etc.), which can eventually lead to an increase in compliance. Questionnaires are commonly used for people to self-report their compliance, but selfreports are often inaccurate because of social desirability bias and fear of the consequences if the truth is disclosed [31]. Given the enormous costs of noncompliance, technological innovations that improve firms’ assessments of compliance have the potential to lead to significant benefits.

Drawing on cognitive dissonance theory [13] and framed within the context of workplace deviance [19], we predict that noncompliant individuals (compared with compliant individuals) will adopt more lenient beliefs about what constitutes compliance (hereafter, compliance questions) and the appropriateness of the potential consequences of noncompliance (hereafter, consequence questions). Cognitive dissonance theory proposes that to resolve the psychological discomfort that is the result of a conflict between an individual’s beliefs about themselves and their noncompliant behavior, noncompliant individuals will try to reduce this conflict between beliefs and behavior in predictable ways. We expect that this will result in more lenient answers when responding to questions about compliance and consequences. In addition, consistent with the response activation model (RAM) [58], we predict that the resolution of that cognitive dissonance will influence related mouse cursor movements as noncompliant individuals unconsciously wrestle with an objective response (i.e., the response that most accurately corresponds with their behavior) versus one that allows them to view themselves more favorably.

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a study in which the participants had both the incentive and opportunity to cheat. We objectively observed whether the participants cheated and examined how this influenced the leniency of the participants’ answers and their mouse cursor movements when responding to questions about compliance and consequences. Our results show that answers regarding leniency and, to a degree, mouse cursor movements, significantly difer between compliant and noncompliant individuals. In addition, we show that combining the leniency results with mouse cursor deviation on questions about compliance and consequences has meaningful utility in identifying noncompliant individuals in a logistic regression classification model.

The current study demonstrates the efectiveness of a low-cost, scalable tool in identifying responses that are suggestive of potential compliance risks and workplace deviance. We (1) provide the first empirical test of a conjecture from the practitioner literature and (2) introduce and empirically test a novel interviewing tactic. Practitioner interviewing techniques [25] conjecture that asking about potential consequences can be an efective tactic in forensic interviews because guilty individuals are more likely to suggest that lenient consequences are appropriate (compared to innocent individuals). To the best of our knowledge, we provide the first known empirical test of this tactic. In addition, we contribute by providing initial empirical evidence demonstrating the value of asking about behaviors that could constitute noncompliance.

Our study design also allows us to contribute to prior research on behavior tracking and workplace deviance and deception. In particular, the present study demonstrates that cognitive dissonance can be inferred through more lenient answers and changes in mouse cursor deviations when answering compliance and consequence questions. Although our separate findings for the (compliance and consequences) question responses and mouse cursor movements are interesting in their own right, together, they provide powerful insights into the underlying cognitive mechanisms that may be otherwise ambiguous when examining either question responses or mouse cursor movements in isolation. For example, showing that noncompliant individuals have abnormal mouse cursor movements (relative to compliant individuals) without demonstrating greater leniency on questions about compliance and consequences would suggest that cognitive dissonance was absent. That is, even though the abnormal mouse cursor movements suggest that noncompliant individuals experienced a cognitive or emotional reaction to these questions, we would not be able to appeal to cognitive dissonance theory to explain these findings if they do not change their beliefs about what constitutes noncompliance or the appropriateness of the consequences of noncompliance. Alternatively, if our results showed that noncompliant individuals provided more lenient responses to the questions about compliance and consequences but did not demonstrate diferences in mouse cursor movements (relative to compliant individuals), we would also be unable to infer the presence of cognitive dissonance because this pattern of results could be driven by personality traits that allow individuals to be noncompliant without any cognitive or emotional reactions (e.g., psychopathy). Because we find both diferences in the questions about compliance and consequences and in mouse cursor movements, we can conclude that noncompliant individuals experience cognitive dissonance, which they resolve by rationalizing a more lenient definition of noncompliance and the related consequences.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

Modern firms are required to comply with numerous laws and regulations.<sup>1</sup> For example, the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 prohibits U.S. corporations from bribing foreign government oficials [12]. Other costly regulatory requirements are aimed at preventing acts such as money laundering [e.g., 48], insider trading, dissemination of nonpublic information, or violations of consent decrees or deferred prosecution agreements (e.g., in pharmaceutical companies). Many compliance requirements apply to the entire firm (i.e., every employee), and even relatively small violations by entry-level employees can lead to large penalties (e.g., FCPA violations can result in penalties of up to US\$2 million per violation [12]). In the Airbus example provided in the introduction, significant fines were levied because of the behavior of around 100 individuals in a firm employing approximately 135,000 individuals [8]. Because the entire firm must comply and small violations are deemed significant, ensuring compliance can be a dificult challenge for management— especially for managers of large multinational organizations where laws and customary business practices can significantly diverge.

For many years, organizational researchers have studied workplace deviance and examined the various issues regarding when and why individuals fail to comply with industry regulations or organizational policies and norms [e.g., 57]. Similarly, researchers studying workplace deviance have created a vast body of literature examining why some individuals fail to operate in the best interests of the organization [19]. In this context, workplace deviance is a deliberate, malicious attempt to damage an organization and can occur in both online or nononline contexts [55]. There are two general categories of workplace deviance: interpersonal and organizational deviance [44]. Interpersonal deviance refers to behaviors that are believed to damage relationships with coworkers (e.g., gossiping, lying, coworker backstabbing, and attributing blame); this is not our focus. Alternatively, organizational deviance, which is our focus, relates to behaviors such as loafing, tardiness, absenteeism, property damage, theft, and regulatory compliance. Much of the prior research on workplace deviance has focused on identifying the antecedents of deviant behaviors and how to diminish them [e.g., 5, 9, 36]. Hence, a main objective of the current study is to use technology to detect possible workplace deviance; this goal has not been extensively investigated or resolved [1].

Prior research has suggested a substantial need for low-cost, highly scalable and easily deployable mechanisms that allow firm management to assess and respond to the risk of noncompliance and deviance (hereafter, noncompliance). For example, researchers have started to explore technological innovations such as virtual interviews, which have the potential to reduce costs and increase the efectiveness of compliance mechanisms [59]. Another potentially helpful compliance mechanism is the use of some type of online questioning [30]. For instance, Jenkins et al. [30] showed that when people respond to highly structured online questioning with very unnatural interactions (i.e., the participants had to move the mouse from the lower middle of the screen to the upper left- or right-hand corners of the screen to answer either “yes” or “no” to each interrogative question), these mouse movements could identify people who were concealing information after engaging in a mock crime (see Figure 1).

This allows us to examine whether these tools can be used as risk indicators of noncompliant behavior after individuals freely and deliberately decide to engage in malicious behavior (i.e., nonsanctioned behavior) [cf. 53]. Below, we develop theory on how online questionnaires can be used to identify noncompliant individuals.

## Mouse Tracking as a Scientific Methodology

The current study explores the eficacy of mouse cursor tracking in assessing the risk of noncompliance in a large organization. Over the past decade, scholars in cognitive and neurological sciences have unequivocally demonstrated that fine motor control is influenced by cognitive and emotional changes [14, 16]. As such, the tracking of mouse movements (hereafter, mouse tracking) has become a scientific methodology used to provide objective data about a person’s decision making and other psychological processes [45]. In a review of related studies, Freeman et al. [17] stated that the “movements of the hand . . . ofer continuous streams of output that can reveal ongoing dynamics of [cognitive] processing, potentially capturing the mind in motion with fine-grained temporal sensitivity” (p. 1). More specifically, these studies have utilized mouse tracking to better illuminate various cognitive and emotional processes. For example, mouse tracking has been shown to predict decision conflict [3], attitude formation [58], concealment of racial prejudices [58], response dificulty [24], response certainty [27], dynamic cognitive competition changes [11, 14, 15, 16], perception formation [10], emotional reactions [20, 22], and deception [30] to name a few.

Given its potential to reveal changes in cognitive and emotional states in many contexts, mouse tracking is quickly emerging as an important behavioral research methodology [28]. Mouse tracking complements other mainstream behavioral methods, including surveys, experimentation, and various types of observational studies. In general, mouse tracking reveals “how” a person answers questions or solves a problem and can be embedded into a survey to provide rich behavioral data (as in observational studies) about how a large number of respondents behave when answering questions. For example, Jenkins et al. [28] investigated how mouse tracking could be used to measure social desirability bias in survey respondents (as illustrated in Figure 3).

## Theoretical Foundations of Mouse Tracking

The eficacy of mouse tracking for inferring cognitive states is based on the interaction of several models of how cognitions and fine motor control interact. The stochastic optimized submovement model [39] suggests that people do not always move in a perfectly straight line—which is known as the idealized response trajectory (IRT) [16]—between the starting movement location and ultimate target. Not moving in a straight line is because of a number of physical impairments, interferences, and limitations, such as the wrist being stationary when moving the mouse, which results in curved movements, the inability to precisely estimate force and direction, and physical impairments. Rather than moving in a perfectly straight line, when people move their hand to a target, their movement consists of several submovements. A submovement refers to a correction when moving in the direction of the movement (see Figure 4). The model suggests that the reason why submovements are often present is to minimize total movement time; in using corrective submovements, one can optimize the tradeof between potentially faster, longer, yet less precise movements and slower, shorter precise corrective movements [39]. Typically, people make an initial movement toward an object, and based on real-time visual feedback, they make corrections to that movement trajectory to ultimately reach the target.

Figure 4. Mouse cursor movements from point A to point B, as described by the stochastic optimized submovement model.

Second, we draw on the RAM [56] to explain factors that may cause increased deviation from people optimally moving toward a desired target. When a person wants to navigate to a target, the brain primes a movement response toward the stimulus by transmitting nerve impulses to the hand and arm muscles. The resulting movement, however, is not solely influenced by this intended movement; rather, the movement is influenced by all the stimuli with action-based potential that catch a person’s attention [18, 49, 50, 5253]. A stimulus with action-based potential refers to any, potentially multiple, stimuli that could capture a person’s attention to the point that a person might, even subconsciously, consider moving toward it [5657]. For example, in a multiple-choice survey question, stimuli with actionable potential may include all answers that capture a person’s attention as potential responses.

One reason why the brain programs movements that are not ideal is to optimize the response time in a manner similar to the fight-or-flight response [26]. Namely, it takes time (often several hundreds of milliseconds) to perceive something, to process a response in the brain, and to transmit the response through the nervous system to the muscles that move the hand [41]. To optimize this time, the brain will automatically and subconsciously and preemptively start transmitting responses to the hand to lower the overall response-time cycle. Thus, when two or more stimuli with actionable potential even briefly capture a person’s attention, the responses to both stimuli are programmed in parallel [56]. This priming causes the hand to deviate from its intended movement because the observed hand movement is a product of all primed responses (as shown in Figure 5), both intended and unintended [56].

## How Noncompliance Influences Questionnaire Responses and Mouse Tracking

When individuals are noncompliant, there are multiple possibilities for how they feel about that given behavior. One possibility is that the individuals engage in noncompliance with a complete awareness that their behavior is noncompliant. This is consistent with certain personality traits, such as narcissism, Machiavellianism, and/or psychopathy [43].

Figure 5. Resulting movement from primed movements to multiple stimuli with action-based potentia

Alternatively, individuals may rationalize noncompliance to the point where they no longer recognize that their behavior is noncompliant. A response of this nature would be consistent with self-concept maintenance theory [38], wherein individuals engage in deviant behavior inasmuch as they are able to rationalize that their behavior is not deviant.

Although these possibilities represent extremes in rationalization (no rationalization vs. complete rationalization, respectively), research has suggested that they will not be the predominant drivers of behavior. For example, extreme levels of narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy are believed to be rare [33]. Another possibility is that individuals engage in some rationalization when deciding to be noncompliant but do not fully rationalize their behavior (perhaps by avoiding full engagement with the moral implications of their behavior). When confronted with their behavior, these individuals are likely to perceive it as disconnected from their beliefs, ideals, or values (hereafter, beliefs). Starting with Festinger [13], a robust stream of literature in psychology has demonstrated that when individuals confront a disconnect between their beliefs and their behavior, they experience psychological discomfort or cognitive dissonance. Over four decades of research on cognitive dissonance theory, Harmon-Jones and Mills [21] summarized this concept, which, in essence, supports Festinger’s [13] prediction that when an individual has a belief that is in conflict with his or her behavior, the individual will attempt to modify either the belief or the behavior to reduce the conflict. The goal of reducing the conflict between belief and behavior is to minimize the psychological discomfort or cognitive dissonance caused by the conflict.

We draw on cognitive dissonance theory to predict how the behavior of noncompliant individuals will difer from that of compliant individuals when asked to define what constitutes compliance. In this situation, people who have previously demonstrated noncompliant behavior will be more likely to subconsciously modify or consider modifying their beliefs of what constitutes compliance to make their beliefs more consistent with their behavior relative to people who have not demonstrated noncompliance. For example, when an individual who has cheated on a task is asked to define cheating, that individual will provide a more lenient definition of cheating (i.e., one in which the behavior of the individual is less likely to be considered cheating).

Based on this theory, we predict that people who are noncompliant with a policy will resist selecting a response that defines their behavior as noncompliant, thus showing more mouse movement deviation when answering questions about what constitutes compliance compared with people who are compliant with the policy.<sup>2</sup> In particular, if our prediction holds true as noncompliant individuals subconsciously modify or consider modifying their beliefs of what constitutes compliance to make it more consistent with their behavior, several diferent cognitive answers will capture their attention as they consider prior beliefs and the ways to modify them to reduce cognitive dissonance; the brain will program responses to each of the potential answers according to the RAM [56]. That is, they will be drawn to answer both the more objective response (e.g., corresponding with prior beliefs) and more lenient responses to a compliance question (i.e., responses that better accommodate the behavior of the participant). In doing so, the brain will be considering whether it is possible to rationalize responses that are more lenient; hence leading to less cognitive dissonance for a noncompliant individual. This will cause mouse cursor movements to deviate when answering the question until the brain inhibits some of the competing responses and the person ultimately selects an answer. Although other cognitive, environmental, or emotional factors could also influence mouse cursor movement (e.g., uncertainty about the meaning of a question), we expect those influences to be invariant for compliant and noncompliant individuals (i.e., creating noise, not bias). As discussed further in the analysis section, our design allows us to control for individual idiosyncrasies to reduce the noise potentially caused by these factors. Figure 5 illustrates the resulting movement of two stimuli that capture a person’s attention with action-based potential before inhibition and corrections occur. In summary, we predict the following:

H1a: Noncompliance will result in more lenient answers to questions about what constitutes compliance.

H1b: Noncompliance will result in greater deviation in people’s mouse cursor movements when answering questions about what constitutes compliance.

In addition, we predict that people will give more lenient answers and deviate further when answering questions about consequences when they are noncompliant. Asking questions about potential consequences has been advocated in interviewing practice but never tested empirically. For example, Inbau et al. [25] suggested that asking about potential consequences can be an efective tactic in forensic interviews because they conjectured that guilty individuals are likely to suggest more lenient consequences than innocent individuals.<sup>3</sup> Also, those guilty of workplace deviance are much more likely to take active measures to cover up such maleficence [1]. Thus, intuitively, there is related evidence in the workplace deviance literature that noncompliant individuals respond diferently to questions regarding punishment severity when compared with compliant individuals. Even so, we provide the first empirical test of the eficacy of exploring how individuals respond to questions about consequences to reveal noncompliant behavior [23]. After being noncompliant, people experience cognitive dissonance because of, among other things, the fear of being caught and the consequences that will follow [7, 42]. As such, when answering questions about consequences, people who have been noncompliant are more likely to be lenient in helping reduce this fear. The RAM explains that the consideration of leniency will introduce competing cognitions that will cause programmed movements to both the more appropriate consequences of noncompliance and the preferred consequences of noncompliance [56]. In summary, we predict the following:

H2a: Noncompliance will result in more lenient answers to questions about the consequences of noncompliance.

H2b: Noncompliance will result in greater deviation in people’s mouse cursor movements when answering questions about the consequences of noncompliance.

As mentioned previously, examining both questions about compliance and consequences and mouse cursor movements together allows us to gain insights into the underlying cognitive mechanisms that may be ambiguous by examining one or the other in isolation. Because we predict both diferences in questionnaire responses (regarding compliance and the consequences for noncompliance) and mouse cursor movements, we can gain better insights into whether noncompliant individuals experience cognitive dissonance, which they will tend to resolve by rationalizing a more lenient specification of noncompliance and the related consequences.

## METHOD

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a study that asked the participants to complete an online aptitude test administered using a Qualtrics web-based survey. Performance on the aptitude test determined the amount of a monetary bonus in addition to their base pay. While taking the aptitude test, the participants were given a rule to only use their own knowledge and not use the internet. However, while answering the aptitude questions, the participants could self-select to be noncompliant (i.e., use the internet), and we unobtrusively measured whether the participants were noncompliant. Our incentive structure was deliberately designed to not overtly discourage cheating so that we could obtain a reasonable sample of cheaters to measure the diferences in our dependent variables between those who were compliant and those who were noncompliant. We view the context of the current study to be similar to many realworld settings in which individuals have significant incentives to violate regulations (such as if someone is ofered a bribe to launder money). We then asked the participants to respond to a series of questions about (1) what constitutes cheating (i.e., noncompliance) in our setting and (2) the appropriateness of the potential consequences for cheating. We manipulated the order of these questions with random assignment to the conditions.

The “aptitude test” section consisted of five questions adapted from the Graduate Management Admissions Test (GMAT) (see Appendix A). We modified each question to ensure that the answers were not easily searchable online. On each page, the participants were given the same instructions, including a link where they could find the correct answers online (providing the participants with the opportunity to self-select to cheat).<sup>4</sup> However, they were instructed to access the link only after they submitted all of their responses, making it clear that cheating was not sanctioned. We also informed the participants that they would not be able to advance until they spent at least 30 seconds on each question. We did this to discourage the participants from adopting a strategy of quickly guessing answers without reading the questions.

The link to the solutions took the participants to a website where we were able to track whether the participants accessed the answers prior to completing the study. On this website, the participants had to click to view the answers, allowing us to ensure that those who viewed the answers prematurely did so intentionally. The link was customized for each participant, allowing us to preserve anonymity while identifying the questions they were viewing when they clicked on the link and the moment they clicked to view the answers. Also, if the participants accessed the website without clicking the customized link in the survey, the website did not permit them to view the answers. The participants were classified as cheaters if they clicked to view the answers prior to completing all five questions of the aptitude test.

After the aptitude test, the participants were shown their scores and the corresponding bonus compensation they earned. They were also reminded that they were not to view the answers on the link until after they had submitted their responses. This reminder was intended to make noncompliant behavior (for the purposes of the study) more salient to the participants. Following this reminder, we asked the participants to evaluate what constitutes cheating in our setting and the appropriateness of the potential consequences for cheating.

For our compliance questions (see Appendix B), the participants answered the following: “In your opinion, which of the following behaviors would be considered cheating on the aptitude test you took in this study?” The participants then used a scale ranging from “0 = Definitely not cheating” to “10 = Definitely cheating” to respond to statements such as the following:

● Glancing briefly at the answers to a question that has not been submitted while reviewing the answer to an already submitted question

● Obtaining answers from an outside source before submission

● Using the internet (e.g., Google) to find answers to similar aptitude questions

● Viewing the answers before submitting your responses

For our question about consequences (see Appendix C), the participants answered the following question: “In your opinion, how appropriate would each of the following outcomes/consequences be for someone who cheated on the aptitude test you took in this study?” The participants then used a scale ranging from “0 = Not at all appropriate” to “10 = Very appropriate” to respond to eight diferent statements, such as the following:

● Being reported to Amazon Mechanical Turk

● HIT<sup>5</sup> rejection

● HIT acceptance but no bonus pay

● Being blocked by the requester

To control for possible order efects, we randomized the order in which the participants evaluated cheating behaviors and consequences. In other words, the participants either first answered our questions about cheating behaviors and then answered our questions about cheating consequences (Order = 0) or vice versa (Order = 1). In our analyses, we included this as a control variable for order.

Following our manipulations, the participants answered a series of demographic questions. On the subsequent page, the participants were asked to confirm (yes/no) their responses to the demographic questions. This allowed us to benchmark mouse cursor deviations for each participant. Note here that it was important that all participants answered the same set of benchmark questions. This allowed us to compare how compliant and noncompliant people answered other questions relative to this benchmark, even if these other questions were in a diferent format than the benchmark question. This benchmark was still valid on questions of a diferent format because we were looking at the relative diference between compliant and noncompliant people who answered the same benchmark questions and were also answering the same questions of interest. In other words, even if the question format would naturally cause more deviation compared with the benchmark questions, our hypotheses examined whether this relative diference was even greater for noncompliant individuals. At the bottom of the second page, the participants were asked if they had viewed the answers to the aptitude questions before submitting their responses.

## Mouse Movement Measurement

While the participants filled out the survey questions, we monitored how the mouse cursor moved on the screen at a millisecond precision rate using a custom JavaScript embedded in Qualtrics. The participants who did not use a mouse cursor (e.g., mobile device users) were excluded from the experiment. For each movement on the screen, we recorded the x- and y-positions and timestamps of the movement. For each question, we determined the starting coordinates for answering each question by using the ending location of the mouse cursor on answering the previous question. The endpoint coordinates were determined by where the mouse cursor was located when answering the current question. The Euclidean distance was then calculated between the two sets of coordinates to calculate the idealized response trajectory distance—that is, the minimum distance required to answer the question. We then calculated the actual distance traveled by calculating the Euclidean distance between every point traveled while entering the question. We then subtracted the idealized response trajectory distance from the actual distance traveled to compute the amount of deviation, or the amount of distance beyond the required distance to answer a question.

Because every person naturally difers in the amount of normal deviation in answering a question (because of diferences in computer mice hardware, computer settings, and neuromotor control), we then normalized the distance for each participant. To do this, we calculated the mean and standard deviation for each person on a set of control questions— questions not about cheating that everyone was required to answer. The control questions were a set of yes/no questions confirming the demographic information discussed above. These questions were consistent regardless of whether someone chose to be compliant or noncompliant, hence allowing us to control for various individual diferences that are not controlled for otherwise (e.g., diferences in devices, physiology, demographics, etc.). We then used this mean and standard deviation for the control questions to calculate a z-score for each person’s deviation to the target questions. In other words, there was no theoretical or practical reason for compliant individuals to have significant, systematic movement diferences between the control and target questions. However, if our theory holds that noncompliant individuals experience an increase in cognitive dissonance, they would likely have significant systematic diferences in mouse movements between their control and target questions. Thus, the relative diferences between each person’s movements between the control and target questions were used to identify noncompliance.

We pilot tested our methodology (over a series of five rounds with 95 participants in total) to ensure accurate tracking of cheating behavior and to increase cheating behavior (including variations in our pay scheme and in the wording of our instructions).

## Participants

We collected data from 950 participants recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk.<sup>6</sup> Given the importance of obtaining motivated participants [3536], we selected participants with at least 500 approved tasks and at least a 95% approval rating. We also used the CloudResearch platform to exclude individuals who had previously failed attention and engagement checks and to block duplicate IP addresses and suspicious geocode locations [40]. The participants earned a base pay of \$0.25 and performance-based pay of \$0.40 for each of the five aptitude questions they answered correctly (i.e., maximum compensation is \$2.25). ## ANALYSIS

Prior to analyzing our hypotheses, we performed an exploratory analysis to examine whether our data were consistent with the stochastic optimized submovement model that was utilized as theoretical support in the present paper. Namely, the submovement model claims that when moving the hand (and, in our case, the computer mouse) toward a target, people make an initial submovement in the general direction of the target, which is often followed by one or more corrective submovements to ultimately guide the target. We examined whether our data supports this by calculating the angle of movement, here relative to the x axis, for each x–y-coordinate pair during the movement. We then calculated the delta (i.e., change) between the subsequent angles calculated in prior step. Finally, we calculated the mean and standard deviation for this array of deltas, and based on this, we calculated a z-score for each delta. If the z-score was greater than 1.645, this indicated that the delta (i.e., the change in movement angle) was significantly diferent at $\mathtt { a } = 0 . 0 5$ . We found that, on average, movement angle changed significantly (i.e., z-score > 1.645 and $\mathtt { p < }$ 0.05) 1.46 times (standard deviation 1.65) while the participants continuously moved the mouse to answer a question. This supports our overarching theory that after people make an initial submovement, they often make corrective submovements to ultimately reach the target.

## Classification Accuracy

To further test whether online questionnaires and mouse cursor tracking can work together to help assess noncompliance risk, we created a logistic regression classification model to predict noncompliance. We chose a logistic regression classification model because it is a linear model similar to the linear mixed efects model presented in the previous section. In addition, we chose to use a basic logistic regression in lieu of a more advanced modeling technique, and we did not use any special parameter tuning. We did this so that our results would represent a conservative estimate of performance and could be improved when using more sophisticated techniques in the future.

The logistic regression model predicted a binary variable indicating whether each person was compliant or noncompliant. We used both (a) the participants’ answers to the questions about compliance and consequences and (b) mouse movement deviation on the consequences and compliance questions to predict this binary outcome. We trained the model using a random subsample of 80% of the participants. We then tested the performance of the model on the remaining 20% of the participants. The overall accuracy rate was 72.79%. The area-under-the-curve (AUC)—an aggregate measure of performance across all possible classification thresholds or cutofs<sup>12</sup>—was 0.718. An AUC of 0.7 or higher was considered an acceptable model with a strong efect size [37, 46]. The sensitivity—the model’s ability to correctly identify noncompliance—was 0.644 and the specificity—the model’s ability to correctly identify compliance—was 0.792. 13

The results of the model validation suggest that online questionnaires and mouse cursor tracking can serve as robust risk indicators, allowing managers to more eficiently and efectively allocate resources to ensure compliance within their organizations. For example, imagine a company with 100,000 employees with 1,000 people (1%) involved in noncompliant behavior. Moreover, suppose that the company could randomly audit a maximum of 25,000 people (because of audits being resource intensive) to verify and remedy the noncompliant behavior (e.g., provide additional training to remedy the problem). If a maximum of 25,000 were randomly sampled, the audit would find and remedy 250 noncompliant cases (25,000 × 0.01). Alternatively, the company could administer a survey asking about noncompliance and use a model similar to the one presented in the current paper to select who should be audited. The total number of employees who would be identified as potentially noncompliant by the model would be a function of the model’s ability to correctly identify noncompliance (sensitivity) and compliance (specificity). Given our model’s sensitivity, 644 (1,000 × 0.644) of the 1,000 noncompliant employees would be (correctly) identified as potentially noncompliant. Given our model’s specificity, 20,592 (99,000 × [1 – 0.792]) of the 99,000 compliant employees would be (incorrectly) identified as potentially noncompliant. In total, 21,236 (644 + 20,592) employees would be identified as potentially noncompliant, and the audit of those cases would correctly find and remedy 644 noncompliant cases. In other words, in this hypothetical example, the technique described in the current paper would identify 2.58 times the number of noncompliant cases (644 vs. 250) while requiring 15.1% fewer auditing resources (25,000 – 21,236 = 3,764 fewer cases investigated).<sup>14</sup>

We expect that including additional mouse movement metrics not considered in the current study could further improve accuracy [e.g., 54]. For instance, in the present study, we used a single metric: additional distance beyond the idealized response trajectory. It is likely that additional metrics, such as those shown in Figure 6 [54], could aid in improving the accuracy by capturing additional unexplained variance or capturing additional types of interaction data, such as facial expressions using video or electrodermal data when a respondent is completing the survey [see 33]. Given that noncompliance by even a few entry-level employees can have significant costs to firms (as discussed previously), our observed level of classification accuracy suggests that the benefits to firms (i.e., reduction of the costs of noncompliance) are likely to outweigh the costs of using online questionnaires and mouse cursor tracking to help assess noncompliance risk.

## DISCUSSION

We found that noncompliant individuals chose greater leniency in defining compliance and in evaluating the potential consequences of noncompliance. Additionally, noncompliant individuals had greater deviations in mouse movements when answering questions about what constitutes compliance. These results suggest that noncompliance leads to cognitive dissonance and, subsequently, changes in responses to questions about compliance and consequences and related mouse cursor movements for those questions about compliance. We have provided evidence that this technology provides organizations with a cost-efective method to aid in assessing the risk of noncompliant behavior—especially when the potential costs of noncompliance are high.

## Contribution to Research and Practice

Our work adds to the research linking mouse movements to emotional and cognitive processes [17, 20, 22]. What is particularly novel is that we examined the joint efects of noncompliance on both responses to questions about compliance and consequences and on mouse movements measured within an information technology usage context. Our work suggests that analyzing mouse cursor movements as an actual (rather than perceptual) measure of usage could enrich other areas of Information Systems research (e.g., technology acceptance, eficacy, fear, etc.), in which the perceptions of states and behavior are measured post hoc regarding interaction [3, 20, 22, 27, 28, 29]. We believe that this approach suggests that mouse tracking is a powerful research method that can provide detailed observational data reflecting participant cognition on a large scale.

Additionally, our study makes several direct contributions to the blossoming mouse tracking literature. First, our study suggests that tracking mouse cursor movements can provide insights into the phenomenon of cognitive dissonance. Specifically, we created a context of probable cognitive dissonance by asking the participants to answer questions about what constitutes compliance and the consequences of noncompliance after performing a compliant or noncompliant action. The compliant group was predicted as having less cognitive dissonance than the noncompliant group because the latter group must reconcile their prior noncompliant behavior with their answers to the questions about compliance and consequences. Our results show that there is a significant diference in how compliant and noncompliant people answered the questions about what constitutes compliance and that the mouse cursor movements of compliant people are diferent from those of noncompliant people.

Second, our research further contributes to the mouse cursor movement literature by suggesting the eficacy of combining not only how a person moved the computer mouse, but also their answers to questions about compliance and consequences. Other than a few exceptions [22], what people answered and how they answered have been considered independently in past research. Our results show that noncompliant people simultaneously answered more leniently and also had greater deviations in mouse cursor movements when answering questions about what constitutes compliance. Using both signals of compliance may help provide a more accurate assessment of noncompliance risk; this contribution is supported in the research by Jenkins et al. [28], which showed that using mouse cursor tracking to moderate the efect of an answer on the desired outcome nearly doubles the explained variance in the model. Our research suggests that similar increases in the explained variance could be realized in a compliance scenario.

Third, the current study provides the first known empirical support to validate a claim made by organizations that train professionals in interviewing methodology. In particular, widely used interviewing techniques suggest that when individuals are asked to describe appropriate consequences for someone who is guilty of noncompliance, those who are guilty of noncompliance are more likely to espouse leniency relative to innocent people [45]. Our data provide initial empirical support for this assertion. Although this in and of itself is of interest, our mouse cursor findings provide additional insights into the underlying cognitive mechanism. That is, our data suggest that these leniency results are not driven solely by individuals with traits that make them more likely to simultaneously be noncompliant and provide more lenient responses (e.g., sociopaths). Future research may investigate how extremes in rationalization (e.g., driven by personality traits such as psychopathy) influence the level of cognitive dissonance and, therefore, influence mouse cursor movement while also considering alternative methods of detecting noncompliance from such individuals.

Fourth, our research provides a new line of inquiry into the methods to aid in discovering various forms of workplace deviance. Increasingly, organizations are using annual climate surveys to assess the aspects of the organizational climate, including issues of inclusion, sexual harassment, and other inequalities [51]. Adding mouse tracking technology to such surveys has the potential to aid in revealing those engaging in such deviant behavior, as well as aid in identifying reluctant victims, especially given the power distance diferences that are often evident when such abuse occurs [4748]. Relatedly, this methodology can also be used to improve response quality by improving the identification of low-quality responses because of satisficing, extreme responding, and other response biases [22, 34].

Lastly, our study provides a path for large organizations to design a fairly economical way to better monitor the risk of noncompliance. By surveying employees about compliance and the consequences of noncompliance, organizations can gauge the potential risk that groups of employees may be noncompliant. Given the enormous costs of not complying with laws such as anti-money laundering statutes, low-cost mechanisms to assess risk in these areas will likely come with cost savings, here assuming that organizations can respond efectively to mitigate higher risk. For example, if the employees of a branch of a large and global financial institution show signs of cognitive dissonance when answering questions about the consequences of noncompliant behavior (both in their mouse cursor movements and in the leniency of their answers), the financial institution could implement training or increased monitoring (e.g., internal auditing) for that branch of the firm. We recognize that the widespread adoption of such a tool may lead to awareness of its purposes, subsequently influencing the eficacy of the tool. Additionally, it could also lead to a lower frequency of noncompliance because efective tools for detecting noncompliant behavior may serve to deter noncompliant behavior. Future research could explore these potential impacts of awareness.

## CONCLUSION

Better understanding of noncompliance can help organizations avoid costly fines, reputation damage, and lost business. In the current paper, we propose a novel method for improving an organization’s understanding of noncompliance risk by jointly analyzing what people answered and how they moved the computer mouse when picking a response for questions about compliance and consequences. We found that relative to compliant individuals, noncompliant individuals chose greater leniency in defining what constitutes compliance and in stating the appropriate consequences for noncompliance. Importantly, these individuals also had mouse movements that difered from those of compliant individuals when answering questions regarding their noncompliant behavior. The results provide foundational research for using people’s answers, along with their mouse cursor movements in online questionnaires, to provide a low-cost, scalable tool to help assess noncompliance risk.
