# Do Social Dominance-Based Faultlines Help or Hurt Team Performance in Crowdsourcing Tournaments?

Fang Cao<sup>a,b</sup>, Weiquan Wang <sup>b</sup>, Eric Lim<sup>c</sup>, Xinmei Liu<sup>a</sup>, and Chee-Wee Tan<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Management, Xi’an Jiaotong University, Xi’an, Shanxi, China; <sup>b</sup>College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong SAR, China; <sup>c</sup>School of Information Systems and Technology Management, UNSW Sydney, NSW, Australia; <sup>d</sup>Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School, Frederiksberg, Denmark

## ABSTRACT

Firms organize tournaments on online crowdsourcing platforms to outsource complex business problems to external solvers. Participants on these platforms often self-organize into ad-hoc virtual teams to compete in such tournaments. Social dominance-based faultlines, which originate from the alignment of members based on ITenabled social dominance attributes (e.g., rank and tier), have emerged as a novel type of faultline in crowdsourcing teams. Building on the Categorization-Elaboration Model (CEM), we investigate the contingent efects of team ability and team efort on the relationship between social dominance-based faultlines and team performance in crowdsourcing tournaments. We collected data of 265 virtual teams from Kaggle.com. We discovered that IT-enabled social dominance-based faultlines positively influence the performance of teams with low ability and high efort, whereas the efect becomes negative for teams with high ability and low efort. Our study yields theoretical implications by advancing a novel type of social dominance-based faultline and extending the CEM with two contingent factors (i.e., team efort and team ability) pertinent to team performance on crowdsourcing tournaments. We also ofer practical guidelines for team formation in crowdsourcing tournaments and for the design of crowdsourcing platforms.

Crowdsourcing tournament; social dominance-based faultline; team ability; team efort; categorizationelaboration model; virtua teams; crowdsourcing

## Introduction

Harnessing the wisdom of online crowds is becoming the de facto practice for firms to tackle complex business problems [26]. Firms are gravitating toward online crowdsourcing platforms in the likes of IdeaStorm.com to generate innovative business ideas or Kaggle.com to seek solutions demanding predictive modeling skills [19, 27, 42]. Crowdsourcing is the act of outsourcing a task, in the form of an open call, to an undefined, generally large collective of people instead of to an individual, a designated team, or a corporate entity [1, 20]. Firms often initiate tournaments and reward the best solver with tangible incentives to motivate participants of crowdsourcing platforms to deliver optimal solutions for complex problems [41, 50, 90]. Participants selforganize into ad-hoc virtual teams to collaborate and compete for the rewards [27, 55].

The ad-hoc and self-organizing nature of spontaneous virtual teams on competitive crowdsourcing platforms<sup>1</sup> gives rise to IT-enabled social dominance-based faultlines (hereafter referred to as SD-based faultlines)[16, 17, 27, 48]. SD-based faultlines are hypothetical lines of division that divide a team into subgroups based on the alignment of IT-enabled SD attributes (or SD attributes for short) among members [16, 48]. Gamified technological features are widely available on crowdsourcing platforms to display and refresh the relative activeness and capability of contestants after the conclusion of each tournament. For example, Kaggle displays rank and tier for each participant. Rank is a dynamic attribute that captures the relative standing of a participant’s activeness as compared to other participants. In contrast, tier is a comparatively stable attribute that mirrors the social status of the participant’s abilities based on his/her past best performance on the platform. In this case, rank and tier are enabled by the technological capabilities of the Kaggle platform to update these attributes continuously. They are conceived as SD attributes in that they encapsulate relative performance metrics reflecting the extent to which one’s competitive capability and activeness overshadows those of their peers [51].

To optimize team composition for competing in crowdsourcing tournaments, participants are enticed to congregate by assessing one another’s SD attributes. Some participants may choose to cooperate with collaborators who possess a higher level of SD attributes than themselves to boost the competitiveness of the entire team and learn from these overachievers. In contrast, others may opt to work with collaborators who either (1) possess a lower level of SD attributes than themselves to facilitate the delegation of routine tasks to these underperformers, or (2) share similar SD attributes with themselves to guarantee smoother communication within the team because of greater commonality among members. Because discrepancy exists in how crowdsourcing teams are constituted from distinct configurations of SD attributes, discrete subgroups comprising members with diferent SD attributes may manifest within these teams, thereby culminating in SD-based faultlines [16, 17].

Yet, despite the predominance of SD-based faultlines in crowdsourcing teams, there is a dearth of research that examines the efects of such faultlines on team performance. Past studies in ofline contexts have alluded to the adverse impact of demographic faultlines (e.g., age, education, ethnicity, and gender) on collective outcomes in the likes of team creativity [65], performance [59, 86], and satisfaction [81]. We contend that the efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance may difer from those of their demographic counterpart due to the following two major distinctions between these two types of faultlines.

First, demographic attributes are intrinsic to an individual and remain invariant over time. In contrast, SD attributes of participants on crowdsourcing platforms are communal and dynamic. After each tournament, the SD attributes of each contestant are updated. As such, SD attributes fluctuate after each tournament. Participants can boost their dynamic SD attributes with better performance in crowdsourcing tournaments [15]. Team members are hence incentivized to collaborate with one another, harnessing diverse knowledge and opinions from other SD subgroups of a team to enhance team performance. Second, unlike demographic faultlines that divide a team into horizontal subgroups without clear superiority among them, SD-based faultlines often produce hierarchical subgroups within a team [17]. SD attributes of participants are publicly accessible on crowdsourcing platforms [28]; thus, team members may become acutely aware of whether they belong to higher or lower SD subgroups. In turn, it is not uncommon for members belonging to a higher SD subgroup within a crowdsourcing team to acquire an elevated social status than those belonging to a lower

SD subgroup. Such hierarchical configurations may erode team coordination and functioning by provoking conflicts and rivalries among team members [56]. Together, these two characteristics of SD-based faultlines may exert countervailing efects on team performance.

We attempt to uncover the conditions under which SD-based faultlines would augment or derail team performance in crowdsourcing tournaments. We draw on the Categorization-Elaboration Model (CEM) to decipher the relationship between SD-based faultlines and team performance [87]. CEM assimilates information elaboration and social categorization processes of teamwork. Whereas social categorization arising from faultlines could culminate in intergroup biases and disrupt the elaboration of task-relevant knowledge, faultlines could also potentially boost team performance through information elaboration [40, 87]. For this reason, CEM holds that the efects of faultlines are contingent on contextual factors associated with team characteristics [87]. Guided by the CEM framework, we endeavor to identify contingency factors that would moderate the efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance in crowdsourcing tournaments. In so doing, we provide an answer to the following research question: do SD-based faultlines help or hurt team performance in crowdsourcing tournaments?

We collected data of 265 virtual teams from one of the largest and most popular competitive crowdsourcing platforms, Kaggle.com. Building on the CEM, we contribute to faultline research in the context of crowdsourcing platforms by revealing two contingency factors, namely, team ability (i.e., the average level of members’ task-related knowledge and skills) [46, 47] and team efort (i.e., extent to which team members expend efort to incessantly improve their solution for a tournament) [28, 63], that moderate the efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance. By unraveling the conditions under which these faultlines could exert countervailing efects on team performance in crowdsourcing tournaments, we respond to scholarly appeals for research on faultlines that go beyond demographic attributes in ofline environments [81]. Our findings also bear implications for various stakeholders of crowdsourcing platforms. Particularly, we deliver guidelines on how teams can be formed along SD-based faultlines to achieve good performance in crowdsourcing tournaments. On the flip side, crowdsourcing platforms can benefit from the awareness of how public display of SD attributes afects team performance.

## Background and Theoretical Foundation

A crowdsourcing tournament involves three entities: seekers, solvers, and a crowdsourcing platform. Seekers denote entities who pose problems on crowdsourcing platforms in search of solutions. Solvers are participants of crowdsourcing platforms who compete among themselves to ofer solutions to the problems confronting seekers. To elicit solutions from solvers through tournaments, seekers upload problem specifications and outline the reward scheme for winning solutions. A crowdsourcing platform is an intermediary that streamlines interactions between seekers and solvers within the confines of predefined rules and procedures [9].

## Spontaneous Virtual Teams in Crowdsourcing Tournaments

Teams in crowdsourcing tournaments are formed spontaneously by groups of contestants who may not be geographically co-located but self-select to collaborate on a temporal basis to attain a common goal (i.e., winning a crowdsourcing tournament) [27, 83, 91]. Such spontaneous virtual teams are popular in contexts such as crowdsourcing, emergency telemedicine, online gaming, and avant technology firms [91]. For example, individual players of massive multiplayer online games often form spontaneous virtual teams to take on collective missions [25]. In the same vein, avant technology firms (e.g., Google and Twitter) have also embraced spontaneous virtual teams by allowing employees to selforganize and collaborate online on communal tasks [91].

Spontaneous virtual teams<sup>2</sup> difer from conventional teams in ofline contexts. Unlike the latter, spontaneous virtual teams are often self-managed. Because members are not bound by formal contractual relationships in crowdsourcing teams, they tend to work independently while simultaneously collaborating with other members. Additionally, spontaneous virtual teams are formed to accomplish ad hoc tasks with short cycle times and demanding deadlines [45]. For this reason, team members have to collaborate intensely for the duration of a crowdsourcing tournament and rely extensively on electronic means of communication (e.g., online chats and video conferencing) to coordinate tasks among one another [10, 91].

Due to the self-management of crowdsourcing teams, the responsibility of searching for team members rests primarily with the team leader, who is often the initiator of the group. To assemble competitive teams with the requisite competency to win in crowdsourcing tournaments, team leaders will carefully scrutinize participants on crowdsourcing platforms [15]. To aid in member selection during team formation, crowdsourcing platforms typically embody gamified technological features (e.g., ranking leaderboards) from which team leaders can directly access the SD attributes of potential members.

## Social Dominance Attributes in Crowdsourcing Tournaments

Many crowdsourcing platforms (e.g., 99design, Datacastle, Ideastorm, Innocentive, and Kaggle) have introduced gamified technological reward features in the likes of point (score), medal (badge, trophy), rank (leaderboard, scoreboard), and tier (level) to motivate participants to perform well in tasks [12, 72]. For instance, crowdsourcing platforms typically contain ranking leaderboards to display each participant’s rank and tier, indicating their relative performance on the platform (see a screenshot from Kaggle.com shown in Figure B-1 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B) [12]. Many crowdsourcing platforms rank users according to their accumulated overall performance, and this rank reflects the relative standing of participants’ cumulative overall tournament performance compared with peers [72]. The rank may decay over time to encourage active participation in tournaments. Achieving high performance in one tournament cannot guarantee a high rank on a platform. To attain a high rank on crowdsourcing platforms, participants have to be proactive in completing various tasks. Rank is thus indicative of the activeness of participants on a crowdsourcing platform.

Tier is another commonly employed SD attribute to showcase the social status of participants on crowdsourcing platforms. Unlike rank, which is a continuous and dynamic attribute, tier is a relatively stable attribute and is often classified into several categories from low to high status. The tier of a participant does not decay over time. Diferent crowdsourcing platforms may employ separate criteria to classify tiers. Whereas certain platforms classify participants into separate tiers based on their accumulated overall performance (e.g., earned points), others do so according to participants’ accumulated exceptional performance (e.g., earned medals) [12].

Rank and tier are typical SD attributes enabled by technologies on crowdsourcing platforms. Many crowdsourcing platforms contain competition leaderboards to update and display the quality score of each solution submitted by crowdsourcing teams participating in a tournament (see a screenshot from Kaggle.com shown Figure B-2 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B). Team members can track the quality of their submitted solutions in real-time. They can then build on the feedback to further improve their solution and resubmit a revised version. The relative performance metrics of all contestants will be updated on the ranking leaderboard based on the eventual quality score of their respective teams when the deadline of a tournament has lapsed [27]. These performance metrics are displayed publicly. The transparency of SD attributes on crowdsourcing platforms facilitates the formation of SD-based faultlines in a team. That is, members with comparable SD attributes are likely to form subgroups due to commonality among one another [16, 17].

For instance, participants on Kaggle.com are ranked according to their cumulative competition points. Competition points earned in each tournament are calculated according to how well a team performed in a tournament and decay over time (see Formula 3 in Section ‘Measurement’). Conversely, tiers are awarded based on medals earned by participants. Medals are permanent accolades bestowed on participants for exceptional tournament achievements in the past. Each medal corresponds to a particular outstanding tournament result (e.g., participants ranked within the top 10, 20, and 40 percent are awarded gold, silver, and bronze medals, respectively). Depending on the number and type of medals earned, participants on Kaggle.com are classified into five tiers (i.e., Novice, Contributor, Expert, Master, and Grandmaster) in ascending order.

## SD-Based Faultlines in Crowdsourcing Tournaments

The concept of faultlines originates from theories of self-categorization [84, 85] and social identity [77]. Social identity theory holds that team members tend to congregate and form subgroups with whom they share similar attributes [37, 53]. This resonates with the selfcategorization perspective, which claims that self-definitions contribute to the categorization of the self and others into in-subgroup members (i.e., those included in the category) and out-subgroup members (i.e., those outside the category). Categorization into insubgroups and out-subgroups thus arises when team members detect both high intrasubgroup commonalities and high inter-subgroup diferences [30, 38]. Because gamified technological features (e.g., ranking leaderboards) on crowdsourcing platforms draw attention to the SD attributes of participants, it is not uncommon for members in crowdsourcing teams to treat peers who share SD attributes similar to themselves as in-subgroup members and those whose SD attributes are dissimilar to themselves as out-subgroup members.

Although SD-based faultlines bear resemblance to the concept of diversity within online communities, they are conceptually orthogonal. Diversity accentuates variations among individual members within a team. For this reason, diversity research has sought to either capture the level of diversity within a team according to individual traits of members [5, 69, 70] or unveil the myriad of roles that can be assumed by team members [4, 64] and the power associated with these roles [24, 68]. In contrast, SD-based faultlines are rooted in the manifestation of subgroups within a team based on the configuration of SD attributes. Because SD-based faultlines stem from the distribution of commonalities and diferences in SD attributes among team members, teams with SD-based faultlines are often divided into discrete but relatively homogeneous subgroups. In this sense, research into SD-based faultlines is centered on members’ interaction at the subgroup level, focusing on concepts of subgroup, intra-subgroup support, as well as inter-subgroup bias and roles [48, 86, 87].

## Categorization-Elaboration Model (CEM)

CEM synthesizes information elaboration and social categorization perspectives pertaining to the efects of group faultlines on team performance [87]. The manifestation of subgroups in a team generally implies that members possess a broad range of knowledge and skillsets as well as contradictory opinions from time to time. The information elaboration perspective holds that this broad range of knowledge and skillsets could benefit learning and task division if members adopt an attitude of positive mutual afirmation (i.e., valuing knowledge diversity) [5, 23]. Conversely, the existence of contradictory opinions may encourage team members to exchange knowledge, deliberate on divergent ideas thoroughly, and assimilate insights relevant to the task at hand (i.e., information elaboration processes) [73, 87]. In turn, these actions prevent the team from defaulting to a suboptimal but convenient outcome based on consensus [75]. Espousing the information elaboration lens, a handful of studies have attested to the positive efects of faultlines on team performance [30, 33].

Contrary to the information elaboration perspective, its social categorization counterpart postulates that group faultlines may decrease team cohesion and collaboration due to the adverse impact of inter-subgroup biases [89]. In a team setting, members tend to categorize peers similar to themselves into the same subgroup and regard dissimilar others as belonging to out-subgroups. Studies have demonstrated that team members trust and interact more with their in-subgroup peers because of commonalities among them [14, 15, 77]. When subgroups are confronted with multiple opinions, team members are inclined to support in-subgroup peers and reject out-subgroup others as threats [58]. This “us versus them” mentality toward out-subgroups gives rise to estranged inter-subgroup relations [54, 87] that decrease team cohesion and increase relational conflicts among subgroups [5, 79]. Likewise, past studies have supplied empirical evidence that testifies to the negative efects of group faultlines caused by social categorization [39, 66, 71, 81].

Given the contrasting stance advocated by the information elaboration and social categorization perspectives, CEM alleges that the eventual impact of faultlines on team performance is contingent on factors responsible for engendering information elaboration or social categorization processes in the likes of task attributes and team characteristics [87]. While both information elaboration and social categorization processes may occur during teamwork, these contingent factors would dictate which of the two processes dominate [30, 40]. Extrapolating CEM to the context of crowdsourcing tournaments, we will identify these contingent factors and theorize their efects in the next section.

## Hypothesis Formulation

Figure 1 depicts our research model. Crowdsourcing tasks often assume the form of complex problems. Consequently, to win in crowdsourcing tournaments, not only should team members have the corresponding capability (i.e., task-related knowledge and skillsets) to tackle and solve complex problems, they also need to devote efort to continuously improve their solutions [62, 76, 92]. Applying CEM to the context of this study, we posit that the efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance are contingent on team ability and team efort that trigger faultlines to engender social categorization and information elaboration processes [87].

In crowdsourcing tournaments, team ability encapsulates the average level of members’ knowledge and skillsets for solving problems in a given tournament [46, 47]. Extant literature on team performance has afirmed the instrumental role of team ability. Specifically, in software development teams, members with high task-related knowledge and skillsets play critical roles in the success of information systems projects [35]. Studies conducted in the context of open innovation contests have shown that solvers’ ability accumulated from previous tournaments significantly influences their performance in subsequent tournaments [78].

In crowdsourcing tournaments, team efort refers to the extent to which team members devote efort to continuously improve their solutions in the hope of winning a tournament [28, 63]. On many competitive crowdsourcing platforms (e.g., Kaggle and Datacastle), team members are permitted to continuously refine and resubmit their solutions throughout a tournament [27, 28, 63]. The crowdsourcing platform or seekers will evaluate the performance of each submission in real-time and ofer immediate feedback [27]. Such performance feedback allows team members to monitor whether they are making progress in the right direction and ascertain how they can further improve their solutions for the crowdsourcing task [7].

## Contingent Role of Team Ability

We conjecture that the efects of IT-enabled SD-based faultlines on team performance will difer for varying levels of team ability in a crowdsourcing tournament. For teams with high ability, SD-based faultlines are detrimental because these faultlines are likely to engender a destructive social categorization process by inducing inter-subgroup biases. On crowdsourcing platforms, ranking leaderboards can display and update the attributes (i.e., cumulative competition points) of each member to quantify their competitive ability. These attributes sensitize team members to the competitive ability of participants on the crowdsourcing platform. Members of a team with high ability tend to be confident, guarded, and territorial about their competencies and remain vigilant against being accorded a lower status within the team [34, 43, 61]. Under this scenario, members from diferent SD subgroups of a team are likely to congregate in and protect their own subgroup. Members in higher SD subgroups are likely to subjugate their peers from lower SD subgroups [29, 67]. In response, members in lower SD subgroups may be unwilling to put up with being treated in this manner because they might deem themselves to possess the requisite ability to contribute at the same level in crowdsourcing tournaments as well.

Members belonging to diferent SD subgroups of a team may come up with divergent solutions. Nevertheless, for a team with high ability, diferent SD subgroups may not agree with each other and are inclined to defend their own solutions. Members tend to support insubgroup peers and treat opposing opinions from out-subgroups as threats [74]. In turn, the parochial focus on intra-subgroup favoritism and inter-subgroup hostility could degenerate into team conflicts [77]. Through perpetuating an us versus them mentality, members in diferent SD coalitions would accelerate team fragmentation, thereby hindering collaboration and coordination [21, 52]. Social categorization will probably induce inter-subgroup biases for teams with high ability, given the increased aggressiveness among members in preserving their own standing. Information elaboration processes may exist for such teams. Still, the chances for SD-based faultlines to engender such processes for enhancing team performance (e.g., access to diverse resources, inter-subgroup interactions, and information elaboration) are likely to be limited [49], with social categorization processes dominating teamwork.

Conversely, for a team with low ability, SD-based faultlines could potentially benefit team performance because the mechanism of information elaboration is likely to be dominant in this situation [87, 89]. For such a team, members are acutely aware of their team’s inadequacies through the ranking leaderboard. Members may realize that solving the focal challenging task by relying solely on their individual abilities would be dificult and that working together to complement each other is the way forward. For this reason, members are not pressured to safeguard their own standing [43]. Instead, they realize that cooperating with one another constitutes the most efective means of maximizing overall team performance.

Additionally, for teams with low ability, members in higher SD subgroups will be elevated as experts and enjoy the support of all of their peers. These members are likely to take the lead in dealing with the challenging aspects of a task and allocate routine tasks to their peers in lower SD subgroups [27]. Members in a lower SD subgroup may be unable to solve challenging tasks by themselves. They are likely to be receptive to handling routine jobs and consider working with team members from a higher SD subgroup as learning opportunities. Therefore, for teams with low ability, it is unlikely for SD-based faultlines to engender social categorization processes and activate inter-subgroup biases. Rather, these faultlines may culminate in good performance during crowdsourcing tournaments through engendering information elaboration processes [32, 36].

Given the diferential efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance for teams with varying levels of team ability, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): The efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance in crowdsourcing tournaments are contingent on team ability. When team ability is high, SD-based faultlines negatively afect team performance. By contrast, when team ability is low, these faultlines positively afect team performance.

## Contingent Role of Team Efort

The efect of SD-based faultlines on team performance also depends on the efort level of a team. When the efort level is high, we posit that these faultlines will positively afect team performance because information elaboration processes are likely to dominate. Teams with high efort imply that members may expend efort in continuously improving their solutions in the hope of winning the tournament. For instance, team members may devote extensive efort to refine and resubmit their solution multiple times to solicit performance feedback [28]. In so doing, they can be notified of the discrepancy between the team’s performance and that of other teams and learn whether their progress in the tournament is in the right direction [3, 7]. Under such circumstances, members will engage in constructive and meaningful interactions to reduce the discrepancy. Such interactions in turn act as an impetus for diferent SD subgroups to get acquainted with one another [6]. Although great team efort may also result in social categorization and give rise to heated debates among members belonging to diferent subgroups, information elaboration processes may be dominant, given members’ high motivation to win a tournament. Such interactions will serve as a form of “social glue” in preserving team integrity [44, 88].

In addition, participants on crowdsourcing platforms may embrace diverse knowledge and opinions as the platforms do not place constraints on their educational background or skillsets to qualify for tournaments [31]. Members in teams with high efort are likely to identify divergent but invaluable knowledge and opinions, be it from their own or other subgroups, given that they are united in wanting to improve their solutions. In this sense, diferent SD subgroups of a team will actively exchange and assimilate diverse knowledge and opinions within the team (i.e., information elaboration processes) [87], thereby enhancing team performance.

When team efort is low, we posit that SD-based faultlines will negatively influence team performance. For such teams, social categorization processes are likely to dominate the teamwork. Members belonging to these teams may not expend much efort in continuously revising their solutions. They are usually uninterested in keeping track of the discrepancy between their own team performance and those of their peers [3, 6]. Under this scenario, diferent SD subgroups may not put in extra efort to exchange and assimilate diverse knowledge and opinions with one another. Deprived of the drive to continuously improve, the us versus them mentality inherent in SD-based faultlines is reinforced for teams with low efort, which in turn compromises collaboration and coordination among members [14, 77]. When divergent opinions are presented by diferent SD subgroups, information elaboration processes are unlikely to occur for teams with low efort because such processes consume tremendous efort. Instead, members are inclined to support in-subgroup peers while rejecting their outsubgroup counterparts, leading to inter-subgroup biases. Such biases will impede the sharing and acceptance of diverse knowledge and opinions among SD subgroups and harm team performance [89].

From the aforementioned discussion, we hypothesize the following moderation hypothesis regarding the efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): The efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance in crowdsourcing tournaments are contingent on team efort. When team efort is high, SD-based faultlines positively afect team performance. By contrast, when team efort is low, these faultlines negatively afect team performance

## Methodology

To validate our hypotheses, we extracted publicly accessible data from Kaggle.com, a popular team-based data science crowdsourcing platform for predictive modeling and analytics tournaments [42]. The majority of participants on Kaggle.com are computer scientists, mathematicians, physicists, and statisticians from various universities around the world. Many renowned companies (e.g., General Electric and Facebook) have previously been seekers of Kaggle.com to solicit solutions for complex business problems. Seekers supply problem descriptions, rewards, and relevant datasets that are publicly accessible from the crowdsourcing platform [26]. Kaggle.com then initiates tournaments for the crowdsourcing community. Participants, in turn, self-select collaborators to compete in these tournaments. Each participant or team can submit multiple solutions before the tournament deadline. Kaggle.com evaluates all submissions in real-time and ofers instant feedback to participants.

We gathered data on the tournaments that began and concluded between April 22, 2020, and September 18, 2020. We checked the discussion forums of Kaggle.com and learned that participants in these tournaments considered others’ rank and tier when inviting others to join their teams or when considering such invitations from others.<sup>3</sup> This ensured that SD attributes were relevant to the formation of teams in our research context.

Tournament-level (i.e., tournament duration and reward) and team-level (i.e., number of members for each team, number of submissions for each team, and final team rank) data were extracted from the competitions page (see Figure B-3 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B) and the competition leaderboards (see Figure B-2 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B) at the conclusion of each tournament. Attributes (i.e., competition points, rank, and tier, as well as their self-disclosed geographic and occupational information) of each team member on the crowdsourcing platform in combination with the previous collaboration experience of each team were extracted from the ranking leaderboards (see Figure B-1 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B) and the profile page (see Figure B-4 in the Online Supplemental Appendix B) at the beginning of each tournament. Definitions of focal variables are summarized in Table A-1 of the Online Supplemental Appendix A.

We collected data from tournaments with four or more members. Faultlines would only exist with at least two subgroups, and each subgroup should include at least two members. Thus, the concept of faultlines can only be applied to teams with a minimum size of four members [80]. This is also consistent with past studies of faultlines in small teams [22, 30, 80]. Furthermore, we excluded tournaments without monetary rewards because these tournaments turn to other forms of incentives such as “Jobs,” “Knowledge,” “Kudos,” or “Swag” to motivate participants to join tournaments and converting these incentives to equivalent monetary rewards would be impossible. In total, our data sample comprises 265 teams with 1,200 participants.

## Measurement

## Team Performance

We employed team rank of a tournament as a measure of team performance. Team rank denotes the relative ordinal position of a team in terms of their submission in relation to the submission with the best performance upon the conclusion of a tournament [27]. We applied a relative measurement (i.e., team rank/the number of teams participating in the tournament) [57], instead of the absolute final rank, because the number of participating teams across tournaments fluctuates extensively. The same absolute rank could mean diferent performance levels across tournaments with a diferent number of teams. The formula is shown as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { Team   Performance } = (1 - \text { Team   rank } / \\ \text { No.   of   participating   teams   in   a   tournament }) \times 1 0 0 \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

## SD-Based Faultline Strength

We calculated the strength of SD-based faultline using the algorithm by Thatcher et al. [80]. This algorithm has been employed extensively in faultline research [11, 22, 59]. It assesses the extent to which the alignment of individual attributes divides a team into diferent subgroups via a multivariate clustering analysis. More specifically, based on the largest split for a team, the algorithm computes the ratio of the between-group sum of squares to the overall sum of squares. The higher the ratio of the between-group sum of squares to the total sum of squares, the stronger the faultline. Consider a team containing n members who are measured on $\boldsymbol { p }$ attributes. A faultline can split this team into two subgroups via a total of ${ \mathsf S } = 2 ^ { { \mathrm n } - 1 } - 1$ ways. The permutation of plausible splits $F a u _ { g } , \mathbf { g } = 1 , \ 2 , \ldots , \ \mathrm { S } .$ , across subgroups with at least two members is calculated as:

$$
F a u _ {g} = \frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {2} n _ {k} ^ {g} \left(\bar {x} _ {. j k} - \bar {x} _ {. j}\right) ^ {2} 1 m u g = 1 , 2 , . . . , S ,}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {2} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {k} ^ {g}} \left(\bar {x} _ {. i j k} - \bar {x} _ {. j}\right) ^ {2}}\tag{2}
$$

where $x _ { i j k }$ denotes the value of the $j ^ { t h }$ attribute of the $i ^ { t h }$ member of subgroup $k , \bar { x } _ { . j }$ denotes the overall group mean of attribute $j , \ \bar { x } _ { \cdot { j k } }$ denotes the mean of attribute j in subgroup $k ,$ and $n _ { k } ^ { g }$ denotes the number of members of the $k ^ { t h }$ subgroup $\left( { k = 1 , \ 2 } \right)$ under split $g .$ The faultline strength, Fau, is identified as the maximum value among all possible values of $F a u _ { g }$ over all possible splits $( g = 1 , 2 \dots S )$ . Fau takes on values between 0 and 1, with a larger value indicating a stronger faultline.

Employing the preceding algorithm, we measured the strength of SD-based faultline based on team members’ rank and tier attributes. On Kaggle.com, rank is a continuous variable while tier is a categorical variable with five values: Novice (N), Contributor(C), Expert (E), Master (M), and Grandmaster (G). Given that Fau is calculated from the Euclidean distance between members, we performed rescaling in line with Thatcher et al. [80] such that Fau can be derived from both categorical and continuous variables. The rescaling involved the following steps:

In the first step, we recoded the categorical variable (i.e., tier) into five dummy variables. For instance, Novice was coded as $N = 1 , C = 0 , E = 0 , \mathrm { { M } = 0 }$ and ${ \mathrm { ~ G ~ } } = 0 .$ and Grandmaster was coded as $N = 0 , \mathrm { ~ C ~ } = 0 , \mathrm { ~ E ~ } = 0 , \mathrm { ~ M ~ } = \mathrm { ~ 0 ~ }$ and $\mathrm { ~ G ~ } = \mathrm { ~ 1 ~ }$ . Then, the Euclidean distance between a Novice and a Grandmaster was computed as $\sqrt { \left( 1 - 0 \right) ^ { 2 } + \left( 0 - 0 \right) ^ { 2 } + \left( 0 - 0 \right) ^ { 2 } + \left( 0 - 0 \right) ^ { 2 } + \left( 0 - 1 \right) ^ { 2 } } = \sqrt { 2 } .$ Next, we rescaled the continuous variable (i.e., rank) and the dummy variable (i.e., tier) such that these two attributes can be combined into one distance measure. We rescaled the dummy variable by dividing it by $\sqrt { 2 }$ such that the tier value equals $1 / \sqrt { 2 }$ rather than 1 when a member belongs to a particular tier and 0 otherwise. Additionally, we rescaled the continuous variable by dividing all ranks by 1,000 because a tier disparity contributes as much to the distance between two members as a separation of 1000 ranks according to data distribution [80].

Participants can gauge each other’s performance along three dimensions (i.e., competition points, rank, and tier) on Kaggle.com. We have opted for rank and tier, but not competition points, as indicators for calculating the strength of SD-based faultlines for two reasons. First, rank and tier have social connotations: rank directly captures the relative standing of the participants’ cumulative overall tournament performance, and tier reflects the communal status of participants’ cumulative exceptional tournament achievements. Rank and tier enable participants to easily appraise the relative performance of their peers within the crowdsourcing community. Conversely, competition point, as an absolute measure of participants’ performance in previous tournaments, lacks such relative performance signals. Second, participants’ rank is evaluated according to their competition points on the crowdsourcing platform. That is, the inclusion of rank in calculating SD-based faultlines has already considered competition points. Consequently, we decided to omit competition points from our calculation of faultline strength.

## Team Ability

We relied on cumulative competition points of participants to assess their competence levels on crowdsourcing platforms. Members with higher ability can generally attain good performance and amass cumulative competition points on crowdsourcing platforms. Thus, we adopted the average competition points of all team members as an indicator of a team’s overall ability (i.e., team ability) [8]. Operationalizing team ability as competition points on Kaggle.com is in line with Dissanayake et al. [27, 28]. Kaggle.com relies on the following formula (3) to arrive at each participant’s competition points based on their performance in previous tournaments:

$$
\text { Competition   Point } = \left[ \frac {1 0 0 0 0 0}{\sqrt {N _ {\text { teammates }}}} \right] \left[ \operatorname{Rank} ^ {- 0. 7 5} \right] \left[ \log_ {1 0} \left(1 + \log_ {1 0} (N _ {\text { teams }})\right) \right] \left[ e ^ {- t / 5 0 0} \right]\tag{3}
$$

where $N _ { \mathrm { t e a m m a t e s } }$ is the number of teammates of a team, rank is the final rank of the team in the tournament, $N _ { \mathrm { t e a m s } }$ is the number of teams participating in the tournament and t is the number of days elapsed since the tournament deadline. In this formula, competition points decay over time. This design renders competition points an appropriate indicator of participants’ current ability because performance in more recent crowdsourcing tournaments is weighted more. To reduce data skewness for team ability, we performed a log transformation for team ability to make its distribution approximately conform to normal distribution [28].

## Team Efort

Following Dissanayake et al. [28] and O’Leary [63], we opted for the total number of submissions of each team in a tournament as a proxy for the team efort. As previously mentioned, when a team submits its solution, the platform evaluates the performance of the submission in real-time and ofers immediate feedback. Teams need to expend efort in improving their solution according to the feedback received and then resubmit their revised solution. Hence, the number of times each team submits its solutions during a crowdsourcing tournament is reflective of the amount of efort devoted by each team in the tournament [28, 63].

We also controlled for the following variables.

## Tournament Characteristics

We controlled for tournament reward and duration that may afect team performance [28, 63]. A tournament with a higher reward will motivate team members to work harder. Additionally, a longer tournament duration accords more time to team members to develop a better solution [28, 63].

## Team Size

Larger teams may possess more diverse informational resources and afect team performance [22]. Thus, we controlled for the number of members in a team to rule out the efect of team size on team performance [22, 28].

## Previous Collaboration Experience

We controlled for previous collaboration experience to rule out its efect on team performance [27]. Members who collaborated before might know each other better and continue to form a team if they have had a congenial collaborative relationship previously. Such collaboration experience may afect team performance. We verified whether any two members belonging to the same team had collaborated before in the same previous tournament. Every time we observed that any two members belonging to the same team had collaborated in a tournament, it is recorded as one previous collaboration experience of the team in line with extant literature [13].

## Contribution

To unravel the controversial efects of IT-enabled SD-based faultlines on team performance, we investigate the moderating efects of team ability and team efort in the context of crowdsourcing tournaments. Based on data collected from Kaggle.com, we uncovered empirical support for our postulations that SD-based faultlines exert countervailing efects on team performance for teams with varying levels of team ability and team efort. Particularly, we observed that the efect of SD-based faultlines on team performance is positive for teams with low ability and high efort and that the reverse becomes true for teams with high ability and low efort.

## Implications for Theory

This study contributes to theory on three fronts. First, we expand on extant literature by contextualizing the concept of faultline to crowdsourcing tournaments and drawing attention to a novel type of IT-enabled faultline (i.e., SD-based faultline) unique to such tournaments. Although research on faultlines has a long history, past studies are centered on demographic faultlines in traditional face-to-face teams [79, 81]. In contrast, virtual teams in crowdsourcing tournaments consist of geographically dispersed participants who self-organized into ad-hoc teams [55]. Unlike traditional face-to-face teams, demographic attributes of members (e.g., age, gender, and educational background) are less visible in ad-hoc virtual teams. In comparison, SD attributes of team members are rendered transparent on crowdsourcing platforms due to gamified technological features (e.g., ranking leaderboards). Given the competitive and dynamic nature of crowdsourcing tournaments, team members are inclined to place greater emphasis on such ITenabled SD attributes to facilitate better team collaboration. This study hence departs from prior research by corroborating the efects of SD-based faultlines in crowdsourcing tournaments as opposed to that of demographic faultlines in traditional face-to-face teams. In so doing, we respond to calls for further research into faultlines within virtual teams [81] by considering how contextual characteristics underlying virtual team formation could activate certain types of faultlines.

Second, rather than conceptualizing faultlines as having a universally dichotomous impact on team performance, be it positive or negative, we alluded to the presence of contingent factors that moderate the efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance [22, 87]. We extend the CEM by bringing forth two contingent factors that govern the relationship between faultlines and team performance, namely team efort and team ability. We advance team efort as a novel contingent factor that moderates the relationship between SD-faultlines and team performance. In crowdsourcing tournaments, team members can submit their solutions multiple times and solicit real-time feedback for each submission. We observed that teams, which devote more efort to continuously seek feedback on their submissions to refine and improve their solutions, are often able to exploit SD-based faultlines to their advantage. For teams with higher efort levels, members in diferent SD subgroups tend to show greater eagerness in collaborating with one another to improve their solutions.

Contrary to team efort as a novel contingent factor in faultlines research, we found distinct contingent efects of team ability whose efect has been theorized in prior faultlines research. Van Knippenberg et al. [87] asserted that faultlines are more likely to engender information elaboration processes and enhance team performance for teams with high ability, but not for those with low ability. Contrary to the aforementioned assertion, our study reveals that SD-based faultlines are more likely to engender information elaboration processes for teams with low rather than high ability in crowdsourcing tournaments. This discrepancy in the moderating efect of team ability could be attributed to our emphasis on SD-based faultline, a novel type of faultline unique to crowdsourcing tournaments.

Extant literature on CEM mainly revolves around faultlines that are founded on demographic attributes inherent to individuals [87]. Teams were divided into horizontal subgroups by demographic faultlines (e.g., diferent subgroups with members sharing similar physiological characteristics) [11, 16, 65]. In such cases, subgroups are not prompted to compete for social status within the team. In turn, it is likely that teams with higher, rather than lower ability, would be more capable of dealing with intersubgroup biases and conflicts such that diverse knowledge and opinions from members in diferent subgroups can be harnessed to facilitate information elaboration. In actuality, however, SD-based faultlines permeate crowdsourcing tournaments and divide teams into hierarchical subgroups [82]. On many crowdsourcing platforms, IT-enabled visualization of the dispersion of SD attributes renders the hierarchical structure transparent to all team members. Under such circumstances, members belonging to teams with high ability are acutely aware of their superior ability as compared to other participants on the platform. They tend to be confident about their own competencies and resist being accorded a lower status within the team [34, 43, 61]. The hierarchical structure between subgroups incites members to compete for social status within the team, which in turn engender social categorization processes. The ensuing inter-subgroup biases may impede team performance. Conversely, for teams with low ability, members are conscientious of their own inadequacies, often exhibiting a greater willingness to leverage on the abilities of all team members and engage in information elaboration in a bid to maximize team performance [32, 36]. In this sense, the moderating efect of team ability culminates in diferential efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance from those of demographics-based faultlines in traditional face-to-face teams.

## Implications for Practice

This study illustrates that faultlines may not necessarily be dysfunctional and could, in reality, enhance team performance when members coalesce into subgroups along with SD attributes for teams with low ability and high efort. This finding yields practical implications for team leaders on crowdsourcing platforms and the design of crowdsourcing platforms in three ways. First, when team leaders recruit collaborators to form teams to compete in crowdsourcing tournaments, they should reference the ranking leaderboards to track the SD attributes of potential candidates. Specifically, team leaders should not only be cognizant of how the collaborators could contribute to the teams’ overall ability, but they should also be vigilant about how skillsets and ability are distributed among team members. For teams with low ability, leaders should enlist members with disparate SD attributes to form teams with strong SD-based faultlines. This is because the existence of SD subgroups would facilitate information elaboration for teams with low ability, which leads to better performance. Conversely, for teams with high ability, leaders should target collaborators with comparable SD attributes to diminish the likelihood of SD-based faultlines emerging within the team.

Second, our study bears implications for the design of crowdsourcing platforms. Crowdsourcing platforms can prescribe guidelines that sensitize leaders to the need for considering a team’s overall ability and its distribution among team members during team formation. In fact, crowdsourcing platforms could incorporate a recommender system for team members that takes into account participants’ SD attributes on these platforms. Because SD-based faultlines exert positive efects on team performance for teams with low ability and negative efects for teams with high ability, the recommender system should recommend participants with low ability to form teams that will give rise to strong SD-based faultlines while preventing participants with high ability to form teams with strong SD-based faultlines.

Third, for teams with strong SD-based faultlines, leaders should motivate members in diferent SD subgroups to devote efort to continuously improve their solutions in crowdsourcing tournaments. For instance, team leaders could encourage members in diferent SD subgroups to partake in constructive and meaningful dialogues with one another that leads to further refinement and resubmission of their solutions. Doing so could minimize the adverse impact of SD-based faultlines on team performance for teams with low efort.

## Limitations and Future Research

Four caveats exist in our study from which future avenues of research could spawn. First, our study revolves around a single data science crowdsourcing platform, namely Kaggle. com. Participants on Kaggle.com are enticed by SD attributes because these attributes are crucial to winning crowdsourcing tournaments. The role of SD attributes for other crowdsourcing platforms remains unexplored. Participants of non-competitive or non-profit crowdsourcing platforms may not value SD attributes highly because their participation on these platforms often stems from altruistic or personal reasons. Future research can thus replicate our study on non-competitive or non-profit crowdsourcing platforms to discern whether our findings still hold for these platforms. Furthermore, the boundary of our study is restricted to virtual teams in crowdsourcing tournaments. Future research can explore other types of tournament-based virtual teams that are not formed in the context of crowdsourcing (e.g., e-sports).

Second, we did not collect data on any intervening process variables (e.g., communication, conflicts, and coordination) to ofer direct evidence about the underlying information elaboration and social categorization mechanisms alleged by CEM. We only extracted publicly accessible data from recently concluded tournaments on Kaggle.com. Nonetheless, we have interviewed several participants on Kaggle.com about their previous experience with team formation and collaboration. Through the interviews, we obtained supporting evidence for the existence of information elaboration and social categorization processes in crowdsourcing tournaments. Participants mentioned that in teams with members possessing diferent SD attributes, members with higher SD attributes were more willing to communicate and interact with peers who also possess comparable SD attributes. In comparison, these members with higher SD attributes would have less interaction with members with lower SD attributes. Such a phenomenon exemplifies the existence of social categorization processes for teams with SDbased faultlines. Moreover, we have also learned from the ‘Kaggle Winner’s Blog’ that some participants prefer other team members to introduce diverse knowledge and skillsets to the final solution<sup>4</sup>. Members in diferent SD subgroups can bring diverse knowledge and opinions that engender information elaboration within crowdsourcing teams. Such views point to the existence of information elaboration processes for teams with SD-based faultlines. Future research could conduct field experiments or surveys to secure direct evidence for the mechanisms underlying the efects of SD-based faultlines on team performance.

Third, in line with the work of Dissanayake et al. [27], we employed participants’ competition points to compute team ability. This measure is calculated based on the cumulative earned points of members in previous tournaments. It may not perfectly mirror participants’ task-related knowledge and skillsets for the current tournament. Nevertheless, we deem this measure suitable because participants’ knowledge and skillsets accumulated from earlier crowdsourcing tournaments are reasonable indicators of their ability to resolve contemporary problems. For instance, in the case of Kaggle.com, team members’ prior knowledge reflects their cumulative experience and proficiency in data science, both of which are vital for tackling problems posed by seekers on the crowdsourcing platform regardless of the task domain. Future research can identify alternative measures for team ability before re-examining our research model.

Last, but not least, despite focusing exclusively on SD-based faultlines in this study, we do not discount the presence of other types of faultlines that could equally exist in the context of crowdsourcing tournaments. Even though we deem SD attributes to be key parameters for winning crowdsourcing tournaments in the case of Kaggle.com due to these attributes being publicly accessible to all participants when forming teams on the crowdsourcing platform, it does not preclude participants from disclosing other personally identifiable attributes (e.g., geographic or occupational information). We have checked the public profile pages of team members on Kaggle.com and found that few participants have disclosed their personal information publicly. Among our collected 265 teams, only 21.13 percent of the teams (i.e., 56 teams) had members who had all disclosed their geographic information, and only 17.36 percent of the teams (i.e., 46 teams) had members who had all disclosed their occupation. For this reason, we did not consider other types of faultlines in this study. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that there may be distinctive attributes that are publicly accessible on other crowdsourcing platforms and could give rise to alternative types of faultlines within crowdsourcing teams. Future research could identify whether alternative types of faultlines manifest on other crowdsourcing platforms and explore their efects on team performance.

## Conclusion

Deviating from previous work on demographic faultlines, this study unveils the conditions under which IT-enabled SD-based faultlines exert countervailing efects on team performance in crowdsourcing tournaments. Grounded in CEM, we investigate the contingent efects of team ability and team efort on the relationship between SD-based faultlines and team performance. Findings indicate that the impact of SD-based faultlines on team performance depends on teams’ overall ability and efort. Armed with a deeper understanding of the role of SD-based faultlines in crowdsourcing tournaments, team leaders can exploit these faultlines to enhance team performance while avoiding their downsides. Team leaders should not only consider members’ average ability and ability distribution during the recruitment phase, but they should also encourage members in diferent SD subgroups to partake in constructive and meaningful dialogues with one another to continuously improve on their solutions.
