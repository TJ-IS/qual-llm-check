# Deriving Execution Efectiveness of Crowdfunding Projects from the Fundraiser Network

Ta-Wei (Daniel) Kao<sup>a</sup>, Shih-Hui (Steven) Hsiao<sup>b</sup>, Hung-Chung Su<sup>a</sup>, and Chih-Hao (Justin) Ku<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management Studies, College of Business, University of Michigan – Dearborn, Dearborn, MI, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Marketing & Business Information Systems, Rohrer College of Business, Rowan University, Glassboro, NJ, USA; <sup>c</sup>Department of Information Systems, Monte Ahuja College of Business, Cleveland State University, Cleveland, OH, USA

## ABSTRACT

Reward-based crowdfunding has become a popular fundraising marketspace for entrepreneurs. However, few studies take a process perspective and examine how fundraiser network influences the execution efectiveness of crowdfunding projects. Collecting the funding activities of all fundraisers on Kickstarter in 2017, we construct a fundraiser network and examine the efects of fundraiser network structural characteristics on project execution efectiveness. Employing endogenous stochastic frontier analysis, we show that reciprocity and accessibility in the fundraiser network increase project execution efectiveness. We also demonstrate that gaining network status by virtue of being an authoritative figure in the fundraiser network increases project execution efectiveness. In sum, our study identifies the efects of the fundraiser network characteristics on project execution efectiveness and introduces a process perspective for evaluating crowdfunding campaigns.

## KEYWORDS

Crowdfunding; social network; fundraiser network; crowdfunding efectiveness; stochastic frontier analysis; reward-based crowdfunding

## Introduction

Reward-based crowdfunding has become a mainstream fundraising marketspace for entrepreneurs [26]. Crowdfunding sites such as Kickstarter.com (hereafter Kickstarter) provide web-based platforms that allow both fundraisers (i.e., project creators) and interested funders (i.e., backers) to participate in, interact with, and facilitate fundraising activities. Funders in reward-based crowdfunding invest their money in exchange for tangible or intangible rewards instead of financial incentives, returns, or repayment [13, 34, 46]. On Kickstarter, for example, funders who invest (i.e., back) projects may receive actual products or creative experiences (e.g., visits to a film set) and are often treated as early customers and participate in pre-selling events for the products [37].

To set up crowdfunding projects and attract potential funders, fundraisers commit resources such as their own capital and labor (the inputs for creating a project) in exchange for funders’ investments in their projects (the project output). In a crowdfunding setting, fundraisers often have the daunting task of generating funds with limited resources. Crowdfunding projects are often small in scale compared to projects that are conducted by businesses, and fundraisers typically face a variety of challenges, such as fewer resources at their disposal, a limited time commitment, and high uncertainties regarding demand [17].

Still, some crowdfunding projects are more efective than others in the sense that they generate more funds with comparable levels of resource inputs during the fundraising period; that is, the execution of some projects is more efective than that of others in terms of transforming inputs into an output, which raises an underlying question: As project execution proceeds, why are some projects more efective than their comparable peers’ projects in realizing this transformation? To date, the execution efectiveness of a crowdfunding project (project execution efectiveness, hereafter) remains an understudied concept in the information systems (IS) literature.

Project execution efectiveness captures the performance of projects in terms of transforming inputs into the desired output during the project period (cf. Farrell [21]) and enables an analysis that is “deeper than a mere comparison of profitability and financial returns” [15] p.20. In this study, we define the project execution efectiveness as the extent to which project inputs are transformed into the desired project output during the fundraising period, following Mishra et al. [36].

We posit that project execution efectiveness can be improved by utilizing internal social capital embedded in the fundraiser network, a social network consisting of funding relationships between Kickstarter fundraisers. Figure 1 illustrates a funding relationship between two fundraisers on Kickstarter. When fundraiser “Lucky Duck Games” invested a project created by another fundraiser, “PenTree Games,” the funding relationship was established and shown on the “Lucky Duck Games” profile page as a directional linkage (i.e., a tie) from “Lucky Duck Games” to “PenTree Games.”

The funding relationships among multiple fundraisers constitute a fundraiser network that connects fundraisers through social interactions [16]. The social relationships embedded in the fundraiser network thereby facilitate social cohesion and become the foundation of internal social capital for participating fundraisers in the crowdfunding community.

The value of the internal social capital accrued from a fundraiser network has rarely been discussed in the IS literature as a potential resource for fundraisers. To better capture the efect of internal social capital on project execution efectiveness, we ask the following research question: How do fundraiser network structural characteristics afect project execution efectiveness? Specifically, we examine the impact of three structural forms of internal social capital on project execution efectiveness: (1) reciprocity in the fundraiser network, (2) accessibility of a fundraiser’s project to potential funders through the fundraiser network, and (3) a fundraiser’s network status that emerges from the fundraiser network [1].

For this study, we collect data from Kickstarter for crowdfunding projects undertaken in 2017 and use the fundraisers’ funding relationships to construct the fundraiser network. We consider the project-related resources that require direct labor or capital investments for setting up the project as inputs and the final money that is raised as the project output, following prior production economics research (e.g., [3]). We then utilize stochastic frontier analysis [5] to estimate the efects of the fundraiser network structural characteristics on project execution efectiveness.

Figure 1. Funding relationship between two fundraisers on Kickstarter.

The results show that funding a greater number of other fundraisers (i.e., a high level of reciprocity) helps fundraisers execute projects more efectively, given the project inputs. Increasing accessibility of a fundraiser’s projects in the fundraiser network also has a positive efect on project execution efectiveness. In addition, we find that fundraisers gaining network status by virtue of being an authoritative figure (i.e., receiving funding from other key fundraisers) in the network can increase their project execution efectiveness. In contrast, we find no benefit for a fundraiser to play a hub role (i.e., funding many other authoritative fundraisers) in the fundraiser network.

This study makes two theoretical contributions to the existing crowdfunding literature. First, we introduce project execution efectiveness as a metric for crowdfunding project performance, which has not been discussed in prior studies. Second, our study demonstrates that the beneficial structural characteristics of the fundraiser network: reciprocity, accessibility, and network status are manifestations of internal social capital for better project execution efectiveness. The study’s results also have important implications for entrepreneurs in a crowdfunding setting. We point out that project creators with limited resources can leverage the internal social capital embedded in the fundraiser network during the fundraising period to better execute their crowdfunding projects.

## Conceptual Background

## Reward-based Crowdfunding

Crowdfunding has become a popular way for entrepreneurs to raise initial funds from individuals to support a specific idea, task, or product [12, 13]. Crowdfunding has been defined as “an open call, mostly through the Internet, for the provision of financial resources either in the form of donation or in exchange for the future product or some form of reward to support initiatives for specific purposes” [6] p.588. Crowdfunding sites integrate social media features with an E-commerce element that allows users to exchange tangibles and intangibles based on the social connections between users. Thus, crowdfunding provides users with a platform that facilitates E-commence transactions. For instance, sites like Kickstarter support fundraisers in building social interactions with their current and potential customers and in providing a product or service in exchange for funds from the customers via web-based solicitation [23]. Agrawal et al. [2] identify four major types of crowdfunding: rewardbased, loan-based, equity-based, and donation-based crowdfunding. Currently, rewardbased crowdfunding attracts much more attention than the other types, due to the popular marketspace on the Internet [26].

The stream of the existing crowdfunding literature investigates the factors that facilitate project success. Using datasets from two reward-based crowdfunding sites in China, Yuan et al. [54] have designed a text analytics framework for analyzing the textual information in crowdfunding projects in order to determine the projects chances of success. Burtch et al. [11] focus on privacy control in reward-based crowdfunding sites; through a randomized field experiment, the authors show that hiding personal information on a crowdfunding site increases people’s willingness to fund a project but reduces the amount of the average contribution. In a similar study, Burtch et al. [12] further emphasize the importance of social norms, or individuals’ beliefs about what other people commonly do; the authors argue that when previous funders hide their personal information on a reward-based crowdfunding site, this actually has a negative impact on future fundraising. Based on the unimodal theory of persuasion, Zhou et al. [56] propose a predictive model to demonstrate the importance of the project description and the fundraiser’s previous experience for predicting funding success. In sum, prior studies devote much attention to the relationship between project-based factors and project success. However, few studies focus on how the fundraiser network’s structural characteristics shape the execution efectiveness of a crowdfunding project.

## Project Execution Efectiveness

Crowdfunding project execution involves transforming the input resources into the desired output. The typical inputs in a project include labor, capital, time, and knowledge about the project. The output of a crowdfunding project is typically the amount of fund raised. The notion of project execution efectiveness is similar to the concept of deviation from production frontier discussed in the production literature (e.g., [5]). A firm’s production frontier “expresses the maximum output obtainable by the firm from a given combination of factors during the (assumed) short period of time required to produce this output” [4] p.827 to 828. Similarly, from a project perspective, there is a theoretical maximum output level that a project creator can produce with a given set of inputs. Deviations from the frontier are caused by (1) random shocks in the environment that create heterogeneity in the output level across projects and (2) project execution inefectiveness in the project execution transformation process. Identifying factors that reduce project execution inefectiveness in the project execution process can help project creators increase the efectiveness of the project execution process and move their crowdfunding projects closer to the frontier (i.e., more output, holding inputs constant). Figure 2 illustrates the notion of the frontier with one input (X on the horizontal axis) and one output (Y on the vertical axis). As illustrated, project A uses input X to produce the observed output $\mathrm { Y } _ { \mathrm { A } }$ . If the creator of project A is fully efective in utilizing the input resource during the transformation process (i.e., there is zero project execution inefectiveness: u = 0), then project A should achieve the ideal output $\mathrm { Y _ { A } } ^ { * }$ and be on the frontier (assuming no random error). The relative distance to the frontier then represents the extent of project execution inefectiveness (u). This study focuses on examining potential factors that reduce the project execution inefectiveness (u) or, in other words, increase the project execution efectiveness in the process of project execution.

## Internal Social Capital

Social capital is the sum of the actual or potential resources embedded within social relationships that facilitate social interaction [16, 33]. Prior studies have discussed the advantages of social relationships in crowdfunding settings. For instance, social market funders tend to rely more on social relationships than competency-based criteria when making investment decisions [44]. Funders in a crowdfunding environment often face uncertainties regarding the product quality or have insuficient knowledge of the fundraiser’s background when making investment decisions [17]. Social relationships in a crowdfunding community thus allow potential funders to obtain more personal information about the fundraisers to reduce their perception of uncertainty and information asymmetry [26, 37, 44]. By sharing information through established social relationships, fundraisers can enhance public recognition of their experience and knowledge [47]. The dissemination of information through social relationships also increases awareness of fundraisers’ products and attracts potential funders [32].

Bourdieu and Wacquant [10] emphasize the structural dimension and state that social capital comprises the resources resulting from a social structure that provides advantages. Colombo et al. [16] categorize social capital into internal versus external social capital. Internal social capital refers to the internal resources embedded within a community’s social structure. External social capital refers to the resources obtaining from outside the community through external contacts [33]. Previous crowdfunding research has mainly focused on the benefits of external social capital for crowdfunding campaign success. For example, in an exploratory study, Mollick [37] shows that the number of a fundraiser’s Facebook friends and the success of the crowdfunding campaign are correlated. Lukkarinen et al. [35] explain that fundraisers’ external social relationships can help their fundraising campaigns gain exposure and attract early contributions. Also focusing on external social capital, Thies et al. [52] identify the diferent efects of action-based (i.e., the number of previous funders) and opinion-based (i.e., electronic word-of-mouth) social interactions on fundraising events and explain that action-based social interaction is more efective. Similarly, Xiao et al. [53] illustrates how action-based social interaction can promote herding behavior and attract more potential funders, while Hong et al. [26] demonstrate the use of social media in promoting crowdfunding campaigns, noting that external social interactions afect funding performance.

Although previous studies have confirmed the positive impact of external social capital on the success of crowdfunding projects, there has been relatively little discussion of internal social capital in the crowdfunding literature. Colombo et al. [16] emphasize that crowdfunding platforms provide fundraisers with the opportunity to leverage internal social capital within the crowdfunding community and call for investigating “the network structure generated by a focal proponent’s contacts” (p. 22). Answering their call, this study focuses on the internal social capital embedded in the social relationships that constitute a fundraiser network. We contribute to the literature by examining the efect of internal social capital, manifesting as the fundraiser network’s structural characteristics, including reciprocity, accessibility, and network status, on project execution efectiveness.

## Reciprocity

Researchers have long suggested that the reciprocity principle, or the practice of exchanging for mutual benefit, serves as a source of social capital. The act of reciprocity transforms selfseeking, egocentric individuals into community members with a sense of obligation to others, shared interests, and a commitment to the common good [1, 16]. The feelings of mutual identification and the norms of specific and generalized reciprocity help build social capital relations among community members, leading them to support other members [16].

Using data from LiveJournal, Gaudeul and Giannetti [22] find that bloggers’ social capital (in this case, the number of readers) is influenced by how much attention they give to other bloggers. Studying the interaction patterns of virtual communities, Chan and Li [14] find that reciprocity enhances consumers’ commitment to the community and their intention to co-shop with others within the community. Reciprocity has been recognized as a determinant for project success in crowdfunding studies. For instance, Zvilichovsky et al. [57] find that a project fundraiser’s funding history has a significant efect on the likelihood of success. Likewise, Colombo et al. [16] find that reciprocity positively influences the early support of crowdfunding campaigns. While researchers have identified the importance of reciprocity, few studies investigate how reciprocity afects project execution efectiveness.

## Accessibility

Accessibility in a social network refers to the ease of retrieving information and indicates the cost of searching for relevant information in the network. In a social network, an individual’s accessibility facilitates social engagement and influences other people’s decisions, thereby increasing individual’s chances of receiving social value from one another [8].

Management scholars have shown the economic value implications of network accessibility. Focusing on the social commerce network, Stephen and Toubia [48] find that high accessibility of online shops facilitates customer browsing and positively afects sellers’ commission revenues. In a study of open source project-developer networks, Grewal et al. [24] find that high accessibility of a project positively afects its technical success. Sutanto et al. [51] study the connectedness of open source software (OSS) project and find that high accessibility of an OSS project improves the diversity of information flow and promotes the creation of knowledge in the development stage. Investigating an information technology outsourcing network, Ravindran et al. [40] show that the accessibility of clients and vendors is positively associated with contract duration.

Recently, crowdfunding researchers highlight the importance of accessibility of a project established through external social ties (e.g., Facebook connections and LinkedIn contacts). For instance, Mollick [37] has shown a positive relationship between Facebook connections and project success. Similarly, Colombo et al. [16] find that having more LinkedIn contacts helps attract early support for a project. Roma et al. [41], on the other hand, find that a fundraiser’s LinkedIn contacts could positively moderate the relationship between the amount of money pledged in the project and the likelihood of securing subsequent funding.

While network perspective permeates crowdfunding research, prior studies appear to highlight the importance of social ties outside of a crowdfunding community, therefore overlooking the value embedded within the network of fundraisers. As crowdfunding communities become loci of social connections, we argue that accessibility of a project established through internal social ties (i.e., funding relationships) can help improve performance in the crowdfunding campaign.

## Network Status

The structural characteristics of a network can reflect the network status of an entity embedded within the network [39]. The concept of network status is closely linked to acts of deference [7], and the form of deference often difers by context. Management scholars have shown that market participants rely on an actor’s status to make inferences about the actor’s capabilities and the quality of the products the actor produces [7]. For instance, in a study of the California wine industry, Benjamin and Podolny [7] find that a winery with a high network status can command higher prices for its wines and is more likely to subsequently produce high-quality products. In the context of academia occupational outcomes, Hadani et al. [25] find that management PhDs graduating from high-status departments are more likely to obtain academic jobs in other high-status departments and to have early quality publications. Analyzing the interbank network in the UK, Shipilov and Li [45] find a positive and significant association between a bank’s status and its market performance.

In a fundraiser network, the funding activities among fundraisers constitute the salient acts of deference that create the status hierarchy in the network. Simply put, the more the fundraisers fund a focal fundraiser’s projects, thereby showing deference to the focal fundraiser, the higher the focal fundraiser’s status in the eyes of other crowdfunding participants. While the IS literature has recognized the importance of network status in information technology outsourcing [40], few studies consider the efect of fundraiser network status on project execution efectiveness.

## Hypothesis Development

## Reciprocity

A fundraiser who has supported numerous projects earns a reputation as a giver in the crowdfunding community and benefits from both specific and generalized reciprocity [16]. On the one hand, specific reciprocity occurs when other fundraisers who receive money from a focal fundraiser feel obliged to help by giving back. Generalized reciprocity, on the other hand, occurs when other participants who notice a focal fundraiser’s funding behavior feel obliged to support the focal fundraiser because they have received support from others in the past.

We expect the reciprocity principles to manifest in the fundraiser network and the potential funders will be more willing to support fundraisers who have themselves also funded numerous other projects [16]. Furthermore, reciprocity makes potential funders feel obligated to support projects based on social norms rather than based solely on the amount or quality of the resource inputs to the project. As these funding relationships are publicly accessible, a fundraiser’s funding behavior may trigger the norm of reciprocity embedded in the community and thus increase potential funders’ willingness to fund [16]. As a result, high reciprocity can increase project execution efectiveness by enabling projects to generate more output (due to the social obligation of supporting good members of the crowdfunding community) with the same level of inputs. Based on the reciprocity principles, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): Reciprocity in a fundraiser network is positively associated with project execution efectiveness.

## Accessibility

The accessibility of a crowdfunding project in a fundraiser network directly influences the cost of searching for potential funders. Potential funders can easily find highly accessible projects while using the crowdfunding platform and thus incur lower search costs. The typical journey of potential funders involves multiple activities that incur a variety of costs, such as searching for projects that interest them, evaluating the projects’ inputs, such as the contents, look, and feel of the project, and assessing the uncertainty associated with the project. Reducing search costs reduces some of the costs involved in the decision-making process for potential funders, which increases project execution efectiveness.

Accessibility in a fundraiser network is related to the fundraisers’ connectedness with other fundraisers in the crowdfunding community. Connections among fundraisers in the network act as conduits that afect the number of visitors to any project. Once fundraisers are connected in the network, potential funders can follow their favorite fundraisers on Kickstarter and receive notifications about all of the projects these fundraisers have supported, which alleviates the cost of information retrieval and increases project execution efectiveness. Potential funders will follow the fundraiser’s updates and visit the fundraiser’s page more frequently, thereby increasing website trafic to the fundraiser’s projects, which in turn enhances the visibility of these projects and hence the likelihood of receiving support from funders.

In this research, we consider the accessibility of crowdfunding projects in a fundraiser network to be their fundraisers’ PageRank centrality. PageRank centrality evaluates the connectedness of a project by considering the focal fundraiser’s direct and indirect funders in the fundraiser network. High PageRank centrality means that the focal fundraiser receives funding from many other fundraisers (direct funders to the focal fundraiser) who are themselves supported by many other fundraisers (indirect funders). As such, projects created by high PageRank centrality fundraisers have high accessibility since potential funders can trace the connections and access those projects with less efort. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Accessibility in a fundraiser network is positively associated with project execution efectiveness.

## Network Status

As previously mentioned, an entity’s network status in a social network is determined by its network structure [39]. Occupying a prominent network position that enables high network status afects how other participants in the network perceive the entity’s capacity, products, or services [39]. In the crowdfunding context, projects created by high network status fundraisers can be perceived by potential funders as having higher quality [7]. It is then easier for potential funders to make decisions about funding those projects because network status provides an assurance that hedges against the transaction risks involved in funding the project. Potential funders are more willing to commit to the project because they are confident about the projects created by high status fundraisers. Therefore, with comparable levels of project inputs, the projects created by a high-status fundraiser are likely to generate more funding than the projects created by relatively low-status fundraisers.

We consider two related types of network status: hub and authority status. Hub and authority status are built on the idea that the directional links in the network indicate acts of deference between fundraisers. A fundraiser earns hub status by linking to (i.e., showing deference to) other fundraisers who contribute popular, authoritative projects. Meanwhile, a fundraiser obtains authority status by being linked from (i.e., receiving deference from) other fundraisers who direct potential funders to authoritative fundraisers’ projects [48]. As a result, we maintain that projects created by high network status fundraisers will be perceived as high-quality projects and that potential funders will be more willing to support these projects since high network status reduces the funders’ perceived uncertainty regarding project quality. As a result, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Hub status in a fundraiser network is positively associated with project execution efectiveness.

Hypothesis 4 (H4): Authority status in a fundraiser network is positively associated with project execution efectiveness.

## Research Methods

## Data Source and Variable Description

We select Kickstarter as our target site because it is one of the largest reward-based crowdfunding sites. Kickstarter has collected more than 4 billion dollars to fund more than 16 million projects since 2009 [30]. Furthermore, extensive crowdfunding-related studies have examined diferent issues in the context of Kickstarter (e.g., [56]), and this allows us to present comparable results to demonstrate our contribution. In Kickstarter, fundraisers (project creators) create their crowdfunding projects with product information, visuals, demo videos, incentives, and so forth (the projects’ inputs) to attract funders. Social media features such as following and commenting provide Kickstarter with the capability to form a huge social network with diferent communication channels [55].

We design an online crawler to collect project-level data for all projects launched during our sample collection period (January 1 to December 31, 2017). The initial dataset consists of 27,949 projects. After removing non-U.S. projects, we retain 17,839 projects. We then exclude 20 ongoing projects to ensure projects included have reached their full funding potential (i.e., executed through a complete fundraising cycle). To preclude suspicious or fake projects, we remove canceled and suspended projects, resulting in 16,429 projects. We also removed those projects with funding goals greater than \$10 million or less than \$100 [37]. These procedures result in 16,055 projects that have completed the fundraising cycle (i.e., both successful and failed projects). Lastly, we examine the fundraiser profile data and remove those projects whose fundraisers have no funding relationships with others. The final dataset consists of 5,164 projects. Figure 3 illustrates our data cleaning process.

To construct the fundraiser network, this study defines fundraisers as receiving an incoming tie in the network when their projects are funded by another fundraiser. ## Stochastic Frontier Model Specification

We use stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) to model project execution efectiveness and examine the hypotheses. In production economics, SFA infers an entity’s transformation efectiveness as its deviation relative to a production frontier composed of entities that convert inputs into output in the most efective manner [5]. SFA explicitly models the extent of an entity’s efectiveness in transforming inputs into output without assuming that entities are always efective in their use of inputs [15]. As a result, SFA allows researchers to infer an objective measure of the transformation efectiveness rather than assessing it in a subjective manner [15, 50] or measuring it as the ratio of output to input [49].

In our context, SFA decomposes the observed actual project output into three elements: the ideal project output determined by a set of project inputs, the random error term, and the project execution inefectiveness term [3]. The basic form of the stochastic frontier model is formulated as follows:

$$
Y _ {i} = f (X _ {i}; \beta) + v _ {i} - u _ {i}\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { i }$ is the actual output; $f ( X _ { i } ; \beta )$ is the production function, which represents the idealized project frontier—the maximum expected output given inputs $X _ { i } ,$ which is common to all sample units—by defining the ideal output as a function of a set of inputs $X _ { i }$ with unknown coeficients $\beta ; V _ { i }$ captures the random errors afecting the output due to unobserved inputs or measurement errors in the data; and $u _ { i }$ denotes the relative distance to the project frontier, that is, the project execution inefectiveness of the individual unit i.

Following prior studies [49, 50], this study uses the Cobb-Douglas production function to specify the relationship between multiple project inputs and project output as follows:

$$
\ln Y _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \sum_ {j = 1} \beta_ {j} \ln X _ {j i} + v _ {i} - u _ {i}\tag{2}
$$

where $Y _ { i }$ is the project output; $X _ { j i }$ denotes the project inputs; $\nu _ { i }$ is the random error term, which is i:i:d: with probability distribution $N { \left( 0 , \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } \right) }$ ; and $u _ { i }$ represents the project execution inefectiveness.

The main contrast between SFA and the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis is that the OLS analysis attributes the deviation from the eficient frontier to the random error $\nu _ { i }$ alone, while SFA recognizes entity-specific project execution inefectiveness $u _ { i }$ as a potential cause of the deviation and allows factors to afect the extent of the project execution inefectiveness $u _ { i }$

In this study, we follow Karakaplan [29] to model project execution inefectiveness $u _ { i }$ as a function of external factors (e.g., network structural characteristics), which allows heteroscedasticity in its distribution. Equation (3) specifies the model of project execution inefectiveness $u _ { i } { : }$

$$
u _ {i} = h \left(x _ {2 i} ^ {\prime} \gamma\right) \cdot u _ {i} ^ {*}\tag{3}
$$

where $h \left( x _ { 2 i } ^ { \prime } \gamma \right)$ is a non-negative scaling function, $x _ { 2 i } ^ { \prime }$ is a vector including the hypothesized factors $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , network characteristics) that explain project execution inefectiveness $u _ { i } , \gamma$ is the corresponding coeficient vector, and $\boldsymbol { u } _ { i } ^ { * }$ is a random variable that follows a halfnormal distribution. In Eq. (3), a positive value of the coeficient γ indicates that as the level of $u _ { i }$ increases, so does the project execution inefectiveness $( u _ { i } ) ;$ on the other hand, a negative value of $\gamma$ indicates a reduction in project execution inefectiveness $\left( u _ { i } \right)$ . We adopt a one-step approach to simultaneously estimate Eq. (2) and Eq. (3) [15, 50].

## Endogenous Stochastic Frontier Model

Productive fundraisers might develop specific fundraiser network structures because they are more likely than their unproductive peers to be supported by others within the crowdfunding community. In this way, a fundraiser’s fundraising performance could afect the fundraiser’s network position. This reasoning suggests that fundraiser network structural characteristics could be endogenous and cause a correlation between the project execution inefectiveness term $\left( u _ { i } \right)$ and the error term $\left( \nu _ { i } \right)$ , which would lead to inconsistent and biased parameter estimates.

To account for potential issues of endogeneity, we employ the latest development of the endogenous stochastic frontier model [29]:

$$
y _ {i} = x _ {1 i} ^ {\prime} \beta + v _ {i} - u _ {i}\tag{4}
$$

$$
x _ {i} = Z _ {i} \delta + \varepsilon_ {i}\tag{5}
$$

$$
u _ {i} = h (x _ {2 i} ^ {\prime} \gamma) \cdot u _ {i} ^ {*}\tag{6}
$$

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} \tilde {\varepsilon} _ {i} \\ \nu_ {i} \end{array} \right] \equiv \left[ \begin{array}{c} \Omega^ {- \frac {1}{2}} \varepsilon_ {i} \\ \nu_ {i} \end{array} \right] \sim N \Bigg (\left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 0 \end{array} \right], \left[ \begin{array}{c c} I _ {p} & \sigma_ {\nu} \rho \\ \sigma_ {\nu} \rho^ {\prime} & \sigma_ {\nu} ^ {2} \end{array} \right] \Bigg)\tag{7}
$$

where $\gamma _ { i }$ is the logarithm of the output; $x _ { 1 i }$ is a vector containing the production factors and $\beta$ is the corresponding coeficient vector; $\nu _ { i }$ and $\varepsilon _ { i }$ are two-sided error terms; $x _ { i }$ is a $\boldsymbol { p } \times \boldsymbol { 1 }$ vector of all endogenous variables; and $Z _ { i } = I _ { p } \otimes z ^ { \prime } { } _ { i }$ , with representing $z _ { i } \mathrm { ~ a ~ } q \mathrm { ~ \times ~ } 1$ vector of all exogenous variables, including instrumental variables. Similar to Eq. (3), the term $u _ { i }$ measures the output loss due to project execution inefectiveness and is jointly determined by the half-normal distribution $u _ { i } ^ { * }$ and a non-negative scaling function $h ( x ^ { \prime } { } _ { 2 i } \gamma )$ , where $x ^ { \prime } { _ { 2 i } }$ is a vector that includes potential endogenous variables $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , fundraiser network characteristics) that explain the project execution inefectiveness and $\gamma$ is the corresponding coeficient vector. $\varOmega$ denotes the variance-covariance matrix of $\varepsilon _ { i } ; \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 }$ is the variance of $\nu _ { i }$ , and $\rho$ is the vector of correlation between $\tilde { \varepsilon } _ { i }$ and $\nu _ { i }$

In this model, a variable is endogenous if it is not independent from $\nu _ { i }$ . Endogenous variables $x _ { i }$ can be correlated with $\nu _ { i }$ and $u _ { i }$ , which are conditionally independent given $x _ { i }$ and $z _ { i }$ . In addition, $\varepsilon _ { i }$ and $u _ { i }$ are conditionally independent given $x _ { i }$ and $z _ { i }$ . By a Cholesky decomposition of the variance-covariance matrix of $( \tilde { \varepsilon } _ { i } ^ { \prime } , \nu ) ^ { \prime }$ , the matrix can be represented as follows:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} \tilde {\varepsilon} _ {i} \\ v _ {i} \end{array} \right] \equiv \left[ \begin{array}{c c} I _ {p} & 0 \\ \sigma_ {v} \rho^ {\prime} & \sigma_ {v} \sqrt {1 - \rho^ {\prime} \rho} \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} \tilde {\varepsilon} _ {i} \\ \tilde {w} _ {i} \end{array} \right]\tag{8}
$$

where $\tilde { \varepsilon } _ { i }$ and $\tilde { \boldsymbol { w } } _ { i }$ are independent and each follows a normal distribution with parameters (0,1). The frontier Eq. (4) can then be specified as follows:

$$
y _ {i} = x _ {1 i} ^ {\prime} \beta + (x _ {i} - Z _ {i} \delta) ^ {\prime} \eta + e _ {i}\tag{9}
$$

where $\eta = \sigma _ { w } \Omega ^ { - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } \rho / \sqrt { 1 - \rho ^ { \prime } \rho }$ and $e _ { i } = w _ { i } - u _ { i }$ , with $w _ { i } = \sigma _ { \nu } \sqrt { 1 - \rho ^ { \prime } \rho } \tilde { w } _ { i }$ . Here $e _ { i }$ is conditionally independent of the regressors, given $x _ { i }$ and $z _ { i }$ . It should be noted that $( x _ { i } - Z _ { i } \delta ) ^ { \prime } \eta$ is a correction term for endogeneity bias, and its inclusion solves the problem of inconsistent parameter estimates due to the endogenous variables $x _ { 2 i }$ that explain the project execution inefectiveness. Karakaplan [29] develops an endogeneity test based on the joint significance of the components $\eta$ . If the components are jointly significant, it can be concluded that there is endogeneity in the model. Otherwise, it is not necessary to include the bias correction term and estimation can be conducted with the exogenous stochastic frontier model.

## Variables in the Production Function

Prior studies have investigated a variety of project-related inputs that relate to creating a successful crowdfunding project [12, 26, 35, 52, 56]. For example, researchers have pointed out that when creating crowdfunding projects, fundraisers should invest in factors associated with consumer decision-making [53], such as videos, images that will attract potential funders’ attention [56], project content (e.g., descriptions), and sentiments that will afect funders’ evaluation of the project [35, 52, 56].

In this study, the output variable of the production function $( Y _ { i } )$ is the final money raised to a fundraiser’s project (Final Money Raised). We consider factors that require the fundraiser’s labor and capital investments in the project as the project inputs in the production function (2), which include project updates (Updates) [37], the numbers of images and videos (Images and Videos) [16, 37, 56], the number of frequently asked questions (FAQs), the number of reward levels (Rewards) [37, 56], the length of the project title (Title Length), the length of the blurb (Blurb Length), and the length of the project description (Description Length). Additionally, we perform sentiment analysis for the project title, blurb, and descriptions and include the project content sentiments (Title Sentiment, Blurb Sentiment, and Description Sentiment). Finally, we consider the number of days on which the project accepted funding (Project Duration), project category dummies, and location dummies for the top five states by the number of projects (i.e., California, New York, Texas, Florida, and Illinois) to control time, category, and geographic efects related to the final money raised [16, 37, 56]. Overall, the production function is shown in Eq. (10), with $u _ { i }$ representing the extent of project execution inefectiveness:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln (F i n a l M o n e y R a i s e d _ {i}) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \ln (U p d a t e s _ {i}) + \beta_ {2} \ln (I m a g e s _ {i}) + \beta_ {3} \ln (V i d e o s _ {i}) + \beta_ {4} \ln (F A Q s _ {i}) \\ + \beta_ {5} \ln (R e w a r d s _ {i}) + \beta_ {6} \ln (T i t l e L e n g t h _ {i}) + \beta_ {7} \ln (B l u r b L e n g t h _ {i}) + \beta_ {8} \ln (D e s c r i p t i o n L e n g t h _ {i}) \\ + \beta_ {9} \ln (T i t l e S e n t i m e n t _ {i}) + \beta_ {1 0} \ln (B l u r b S e n t i m e n t _ {i}) + \beta_ {1 1} \ln (D e s c r i p t i o n S e n t i m e n t _ {i}) \\ + \beta_ {1 2} \ln (P r o j e c t D u r a t i o n _ {i}) + \sum_ {j} \beta_ {j} C a t e g o r y D u m m y _ {j i} + \sum_ {k} \beta_ {k} L o c a t i o n D u m m y _ {k i} + v _ {i} - u _ {i} \end{array}\tag{10}
$$

## Variables in the Project Execution Inefectiveness Function

## Reciprocity

We evaluate reciprocity (Reciprocity) as the number of projects the fundraiser has supported on Kickstarter. Because Kickstarter recognizes the importance of reciprocity and displays the number of projects fundraisers have supported on their project pages, it is possible to learn whether fundraisers actively participate in supporting others in the Kickstarter community.

## Accessibility

We evaluate the accessibility of a fundraiser’s projects (Accessibility) by calculating the fundraiser’s PageRank centrality. We use Google’s PageRank algorithm, which is a popular approach that is used to identify social influencers. Rooted in a search engine mechanism, the PageRank algorithm was proposed by Page et al. [38] and has been adapted in multiple research fields (e.g., information science, communication, social sciences). On the Internet, each page provides hyperlinks that redirect to other pages. Based on these links (ties), webpages (nodes) connect to each other as a network. The PageRank algorithm calculates these links recursively to measure the accessibility of each page (node). When the number of incoming links (ties) is high, the page is more accessible in the network and has a higher PageRank. In our context, fundraiser $i \gamma _ { s }$ PageRank centrality $( \mathrm { i . e . , } P R ( i ) )$ is calculated recursively as follows:

$$
P R (i) = c \sum_ {j \in B _ {i}} \frac {P R (j)}{N _ {j}}\tag{11}
$$

where $B _ { i }$ is the set of profile pages that point to fundraiser i’s profile page, c is a factor used for normalization to ensure that the PageRank value of each page is constant, $P R ( j )$ is the PageRank value for fundraiser $j \dag s$ profile page, and $N _ { j }$ is the total number of outgoing funding relationships developed by fundraiser j. The algorithm continues its iterations until the PageRank score of each page reaches a state of convergence.

## Network Status

We evaluate fundraisers’ network status by calculating their hub and authority centrality measures using the Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search (HITS) algorithm. HITS has been used to identify the most authoritative pages depending on the linkage structure in a specific topic space [31], and it has also been adapted to measure the reputation scores of nodes in a network setting [43]. The original HITS algorithm [31] was designed to analyze the linkage structure of the Internet. In our context, fundraiser i’s hub and authority centrality scores are recursively calculated as follows:

$$
h _ {i} = \sum_ {j: i \rightarrow j} a _ {j}\tag{12}
$$

$$
a _ {i} = \sum_ {j: j \rightarrow i} h _ {j}\tag{13}
$$

where $h _ { i } \left( h _ { j } \right)$ denotes fundraiser $i \gamma _ { s }$ (fundraiser $j \dag$ hub centrality; $a _ { i } \left( a _ { j } \right)$ denotes fundraiser $i \varsigma$ (fundraiser $j \dag$ authority centrality. Each fundraiser’s profile page is initially given a nonnegative authority centrality score as its in-degree, and a non-negative hub centrality score as its out-degree. During the iterative process, the authority centrality and the hub centrality of each page are maintained and updated. Profile pages with the highest hub centrality $\left( { { h _ { i } } } \right)$ indicate those fundraisers who connect to the greatest number of other popular fundraisers $( a _ { j } )$ , and profile pages with the highest authority centrality $\left( { a _ { i } } \right)$ indicate those fundraisers who receive the greatest number of referrals from other fundraisers playing the role of hubs in the network $( h _ { j } )$ [9, 27].

## Control Variables

We include several control variables in the project execution inefectiveness function. First, we consider the number of a fundraiser’s social media pages (Social Media), as this indicates the fundraiser’s eforts to promote their crowdfunding projects. The fundraiser’s crowdfunding experience is captured by the number of projects that the fundraiser has previously created (Previous Projects) [28] and the time elapsed since the fundraiser created the fundraiser’s user account on Kickstarter (Tenure). Additionally, we also consider whether the project is featured by Kickstarter on its homepage (Staf Pick) and the amount that the fundraiser seeks to raise (Goal), both of which influence potential funders’ willingness to support the project [16,37]. The project execution inefectiveness function following equation (3), which connects project execution inefectiveness $\left( u _ { i } \right)$ to the fundraiser network factors and control variables, is shown in equations (14) and (15) [29].

$$
\begin{array}{l} u _ {i} = h (\gamma_ {1} \text {Reciprocity} _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} \text {Accessibility} _ {i} + \gamma_ {3} \text {Hub Status} _ {i} + \gamma_ {4} \text {Authority Status} _ {i} \\ + \gamma_ {5} \text {Social Media} _ {i} + \gamma_ {6} \text {Previous Project} _ {i} + \gamma_ {7} \text {Tenure} _ {i} + \gamma_ {8} \text {Staff Pick} _ {i} + \gamma_ {9} \text {Goal}) \cdot u _ {i} ^ {*} \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

$$
u _ {i} * \sim | N (0, 1) |\tag{15}
$$

## Results

To justify the use of the stochastic frontier analysis, we first perform a generalized likelihood ratio (LR) test of the null hypothesis that no project execution inefectiveness term $( u _ { i } )$ exists in the stochastic frontier model. Specifically, the LR test is based on the log-likelihood values of the OLS and the stochastic frontier models. The resulting LR test result $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 8 5 . 3 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ rejects the null hypothesis of no project execution inefectiveness term (see Table A1 in the Online Supplemental Appendix).

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics and simple correlations for all variables. Before conducting the data analysis, we winsorize each variable at the 1 and 99 percent levels to reduce the potential bias caused by extreme values. We standardize all hypothesized factors to facilitate comparisons of their impact on project execution efectiveness.

Following the institutional theory [19], we instrument the suspected endogenous variables (i.e., fundraiser network structural characteristics) using their location medians based on fundraisers’ geographic information and the category medians of fundraisers’ network contacts [42]. Since fundraisers within the same geographic location or project category often mimic their peers [18], these median variables would therefore relate to the suspected endogenous variables and satisfy the relevance condition. In addition, location-level or category-level medians are often afected by range of factors outside of the fundraisers’ direct control and may not be directly related to the focal fundraiser’s fundraising performance [42], thereby fulfilling the exclusion condition.

To ensure the validity of the instruments, we apply the two-stage least squares approach to carry out instrumental variable tests. Specifically, using the suspected endogenous variables and project output variable as dependent variables in the first and second stage models, respectively, we perform Kleibergen-Paap LM test and Hansen J test to examine relevance and exclusion conditions for the IVs. Kleibergen-Paap LM test statistic $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 8 . 1 5 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ rejects the null hypothesis that instrumental variables are irrelevant. Meanwhile, Hansen J test result $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 . 2 0 7 , p = 0 . 2 7 1 9 )$ does not reject the null hypothesis that the instrumental variables are exogenous and uncorrelated with two-sided error term $\left( \nu _ { i } \right)$ . Taken together, these results demonstrate the validity of the instrument variables.

Table 2 summarizes the estimation results. Model EX shows the results treating the hypothesized factors as exogenous, while Model EN treats the hypothesized factors as endogenous and uses instrumental variables and bias correction terms to address endogeneity. Table 2 also presents the results of the endogeneity test. The test results confirm that the hypothesized variables are endogenous; thus, correction for endogeneity regarding the fundraiser network measures is required $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 4 . 9 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . As such, the examination of our research hypotheses is based on the results from Model EN.

## Robustness Check

To ensure the robustness of our main findings, we consider two alternative project output measures often discussed in the literature (1) the number of funders and (2) the percentage of funding goal achieved (i.e., final money raised divided by funding goal). Table A2 in the Online

Supplemental Appendix presents the results with the number of funders as the project output. The overall results are consistent with our main findings indicating reciprocity, accessibility, and authority status have similar efects on project execution efectiveness with the number of funders as the project output.

Table A3 in the Online Supplemental Appendix demonstrates the results with the percentage of funding goal achieved as the project output. We find similar results for reciprocity and accessibility, but the efect of authority status is not significant. That is, authority status does not afect project execution efectiveness if the ratio of final money raised and funding goal is used as project output. One possible explanation is that evaluating project execution efectiveness using the percentage of funding goal achieved is diferent from using the final money raised. For example, a project with a certain amount of final money raised can have a low or high percentage of funding goal achieved depending on the level of funding goal. As a result, how fundraisers determine the level of funding goal could influence the evaluation of project execution efectiveness and leads to diferent estimated efects of the network factors. This finding also suggests future research opportunities to pursue.

## Post Hoc Analysis

Prior studies highlight the role of external social capital in project outcomes (e.g., [16, 37]). In our post-hoc analysis, we compare the relative importance of internal versus external social capital in determining project execution efectiveness. Following Mollick [37] and Colombo et al. [16], we use the number of Facebook friends (Facebook Friends) to evaluate a fundraiser’s external social capital represented by family, friends, and personal acquaintances [16, 37]. To facilitate our comparison of the efectiveness of internal versus external social capital, we standardize Facebook Friends to put it on the same scale as our independent variables (i.e., Reciprocity, Accessibility, Hub Status, and Authority Status) and run both exogenous and endogenous SFA models. Table A4 in the Online Supplemental Appendix presents the results. Table A4: Model EN shows that the efects of Accessibility and Authority Status are greater than the efect of Facebook Friends, thus suggesting that internal social capital could exert a greater impact than external social capital on project execution efectiveness.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Theoretical Contributions

Existing crowdfunding studies provide a detailed understanding of the relationship between project-related inputs (product description, images, etc.) and successful fundraising, but only limited attention has been paid to fundraiser network and a fundraiser’s capability to raise funds during project execution. Applying stochastic frontier analysis, we capture this capability by introducing the concept of project execution efectiveness, which provides fundraisers an alternative perspective on how to evaluate their capability toward crowdfunding success. Existing crowdfunding literature implicitly views such capability from an outcome-oriented perspective (success, failure, etc.); namely, fundraisers who create successful projects are often considered capable of managing crowdfunding projects. However, such a perspective neglects the reality that fundraisers, especially in a crowdfunding context, generate the output with a set of limited inputs through a transformation process. In this study, we take a process-oriented perspective, view a crowdfunding project as a process of converting project inputs to output, and use SFA to study how fundraiser network factors influence project execution efectiveness [20].

More importantly, our study extends Colombo et al. [16] by illustrating that the internal social capital embedded in the fundraiser network is a driver for project execution efectiveness. The structural dimensions of the internal social capital—reciprocity, accessibility, and authority status—help explain the diferences in execution efectiveness across crowdfunding projects.

The finding of reciprocity supports the notion that a fundraiser’s funding activity triggers reciprocity by arousing others’ feelings of obligation in the crowdfunding community [16, 28]. Interestingly, Stephen and Toubia [48] find that online shops with more links that go to other shops perform poorly, as these provide more opportunities for customers to leave these shops. We do not observe such a negative influence in the crowdfunding community, even though fundraisers supporting numerous other projects run the similar risk of diverting potential funders’ attention away from their own projects. Thus, our finding suggests that, compared to the social shopping community, the crowdfunding community has developed an environment that favors reciprocity. This is similar to an argument of Zvilichovsky et al. [57], which states that people in the social commerce marketplace favor those sellers who also contribute back to the community and encourage other entrepreneurs.

Our study confirms that fundraisers with higher accessibility (i.e., a higher PageRank centrality) in the network have greater project execution efectiveness. This finding uncovers a similarity between the crowdfunding community and the social shopping community that connects sellers in online marketplaces. Studies on the social shopping community suggest that online shops with more incoming ties from other shops are more likely to be found and accessed by browsing customers [48]. Our study shows that such accessibility also plays a role in facilitating project execution efectiveness in the crowdfunding community. Since the use of PageRank centrality considers a fundraiser’s indirect ties (e.g., supporters of supporters), this finding also suggests that indirect ties play a role in improving project execution efectiveness by building the channels through which potential funders can reach the focal fundraiser’s projects.

Additionally, our study supports the view that “market exchanges often involve not only the manifest transfer of goods and resources, but the latent transfer of status” [39] p.453. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to diferentiate between two types of fundraiser network status (hub and authority status) and to relate both status measures to project execution efectiveness. We show that playing an authority role within the crowdfunding community helps fundraisers improve their project execution efectiveness. Conversely, our study does not find a significant relationship between hub status and project execution efectiveness. One explanation might lie in the fact that potential funders may view fundraisers with high hub status as having a high network status in terms of funding other high-quality projects rather than creating high-quality projects, and thus, hub status has no efect on project execution efectiveness.

Our post hoc analysis highlights the role of internal social capital in determining project execution efectiveness. The efects of reciprocity, accessibility, and authority status are consistent after we add Facebook friends to the model (see Table A4 in the Online Supplemental Appendix), and the efect sizes of accessibility and authority are greater than that of Facebook friends. The results demonstrate that both external and internal social capital are imperative for project execution efectiveness. While external social capital can promote a fundraiser’s project to potential funders [16], internal social capital accrued from the fundraiser network helps promote reciprocal norms, signal project quality, and reduce information asymmetries between fundraisers and potential funders.

## Managerial Implications

Our study has several practical implications for fundraisers and, more broadly, for crowdfunding platform administrators. First, fundraisers should recognize that their funding activities are the basis for internal social capital that can influence their project execution efectiveness. Before launching crowdfunding projects and during project execution, fundraisers should consider supporting other creators’ projects to trigger the reciprocity that makes potential funders more willing to invest their money due to feelings of obligation. Our study results also indicate that the funding relationships initiated by other fundraisers enhance the accessibility of the focal fundraiser’s projects. As such, when choosing which projects to support, fundraisers should choose individuals with many followers in order to receive reciprocal support and also enhance their accessibility in the fundraiser network. Furthermore, fundraisers can support individuals who play the role of hubs in the crowdfunding community to forge their reputation as authority figures in the fundraiser network to improve their project execution efectiveness.

Second, the estimated coeficients of the project inputs in the production function largely corroborate with prior studies. Fundraisers in a reward-based crowdfunding platform can consider investing in important production inputs such as the number of images and videos, and the descriptions of their projects. In addition, contrary to common belief, the sentiments of the project title, blurb, or description turn out to be insignificant project inputs for reward-based crowdfunding. The potential funders in reward-based crowdfunding seem to focus more on the objective content of the project such as text descriptions, number of images and videos. Future research may wish to evaluate the same project inputs in a donation-based crowdfunding environment, where we expect the sentiments of the project content may play a bigger role.

Third, before launching a project, the fundraiser could utilize the estimated production function as a tool to predict the funds that the project can potentially raise. To illustrate, we apply the estimated production function on a crowdfunding project with a \$20,000 funding goal (see Figure 4) in the year 2018. Our prediction indicates the project could potentially raise to \$49,513 instead of \$20,000 based on the project inputs. The final amount raised by the project is \$53,649, which is close to the predicted value of \$49,513. This example demonstrates the applicability of the estimated production function in assessing the funding potential of fundraisers’ projects.

Finally, for crowdfunding platform administrators, we suggest improving crowdfunding platform services to facilitate interactions among fundraisers. One feasible way to do this is to create a chat room or discussion forum for fundraisers to share ideas or product information. Building such a virtual environment will not only enable fundraisers to share ideas but also help increase the accessibility of their crowdfunding projects. Furthermore, crowdfunding platform administrators could create billboards that display those fundraisers who frequently support other projects to reinforce the norm of reciprocity within the crowdfunding community. This will encourage crowdfunding participants to support each other, enhancing the number of successfully funded projects and increasing revenue for the platform.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

In closing, we discuss the limitations of this study and highlight avenues for future research. First, we only focus on a reward-based crowdfunding platform. The results of this study may not apply to other types of crowdfunding platforms (loan-based, equity-based, and donation-based crowdfunding platforms). Future research examining the generalizability of our findings to other crowdfunding platforms can enhance the external validity of this study. Second, we did not consider the impact of category diferences in this study. The networks constituted from each category in the dataset tend to be small and fragmented, which would result in unreliable network measures and SFA estimations. We suggest that future research can apply a longer data collection horizon and compare the diferences in the efects caused by diferent project categories. Third, this study considers the number of reward levels, not the price of each reward level, which suggests opportunities for future studies. Finally, this study only considers the final money raised and two alternative project output measures; future studies can utilize the stochastic frontier approach to examine other types of project output to help understand diferent aspects of project execution efectiveness.

## Conclusion

In this study, we consider relationships among fundraisers as a source of internal social capital to examine how fundraisers’ structural characteristics in the fundraiser network afect the execution efectiveness of crowdfunding projects. Our findings suggest that internal social relationships within the crowdfunding community determine the accessibility of crowdfunding projects, trigger the norms of reciprocity, and establish fundraisers’ network status, all of which influence fundraisers’ capability to manage the input-output transformation process during project execution. We hope that our work encourages researchers to investigate internal social capital issues from a process-oriented perspective, thereby shedding light on how to improve the project execution efectiveness through nurturing social connections within the crowdfunding community.
