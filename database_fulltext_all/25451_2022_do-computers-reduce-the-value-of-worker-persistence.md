# Do Computers Reduce the Value of Worker Persistence?

Erik Brynjolfsson<sup>a</sup>, Meng Liu<sup>b</sup>, and George Westerman<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Digital Economy Laboratory, Stanford University, Stanford, CA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Washington University in St. Louis, St. Louis, MO, USA; <sup>c</sup>MIT Sloan, Cambridge, MA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Worker persistence—the ability to focus on a task for long periods of time—is often highlighted as essential to success. However, computers are extraordinarily persistent, particularly for routine, repetitive work. This potentially reduces the value of human persistence in occupations that are computerized. Using a well-defined measure of worker persistence across a nationally-representative 16-year sample of 4,239 individuals, we investigate the extent to which occupations value worker persistence in the presence of computers. We find that the labor market does indeed value persistence. Nonetheless, we find that in routine jobs, the wage premium of human persistence diminishes with the degree of workplace computerization. Yet, this substitution does not occur in non-routine jobs. These findings deepen our understanding of the efect of workplace computerization on the future of work and workers, and they also warrant imlications on government job training programs, organizational talent management, as well as the redesign of the K-12 curriculum.

## KEYWORDS

Workplace computerization; routine-biased technological change; wage premium; worker grit; labor economics; worker persistence; computerization efects

## Introduction

ersistence describes an individual’s ability to perform tasks with constant dedication, stay the course, and keep focus for a long period of time [25, 26]. Ever since the introduction of Ford’s assembly line, persistence has been viewed as a valuable trait for workers. The ability to keep focus and stay on task is not universal, and it is important for delivering high eficiency and consistent quality of work. Employers should reward persistent workers because they can deliver higher productivity with less oversight.

While persistence can be a complement for many technologies, computers can increasingly do repetitive work. Workplace computers designed for automation are most efective when they carry out work that can be codified and replicated [14]. As a result, workplace computerization has disproportionately been replacing human labor in codifiable, routine tasks [1, 8, 15, 46]. This routine-biased technological change (RBTC) has been demonstrated to have important implications on how various types of worker skills are valued in the labor market.<sup>1</sup> Could the persistence of computers reduce the value of human persistence? If so, where does human persistence still matter in the twenty-first century labor market?

Our longitudinal analysis of 16 years of data for a nationally-representative sample of 4,239 workers finds that persistence is rewarded with higher wages. However, the wage premium of worker persistence in the face of computerization varies systematically across diferent types of occupations. In routine jobs, the wage premium of worker persistence decreases as the occupation is more computerized.

Specifically, the wage premium for persistence decreases to zero among routine jobs that are computerized the most. Meanwhile, for routine jobs that are not computerized, a worker whose persistence is two standard deviations above average is expected to earn 20 percent more than one with persistence two standard deviations below average, all else equal. The substitution efect of computers is not seen in non-routine jobs, where we find a wage premium of persistence but no decrease in computerized occupations.

These empirical findings extend our knowledge of the labor implications of workplace computerization. Previously researchers have primarily focused on the broad employment replacement efect of workplace computerization, but often did not develop micro perspectives on how the substitution manifests itself in diferent segments of the labor market. This study builds a micro-level perspective on the nature of persistence that yields equivocal theoretical predictions for the potential for computers to substitute for human persistence in routine and non-routine occupations. It then tests the predictions empirically to generate clear findings that are more conceptually nuanced than prior research on computerization in routine and non-routine occupations.

These findings provide important additions to the RBTC literature in two ways. By decomposing persistence from a worker’s skill set, the nature of computer-human substitution can be explained to a better extent. Second, to the extent that wage is a sensitive signal of labor productivity, a study of computerization’s wage efect across the distribution of worker persistence provides a great opportunity for us to observe how computer-human substitution takes its course.

This study also relates to the information systems literature on worker traits that afect productivity. Examples of such productivity-enhancing traits include personality characteristics [20, 43], worker intrinsic motivation [35, 41, 42], and practical intelligence [34, 37]. Our findings add to this literature by conceptualizing and testing the importance of human persistence in diferent segments of the increasingly-computerized workplace.

Our work also adds to the growing psychology literature on grit, which tends to argue for a universal return to grit “no matter the domain” [23–26, 29, 47, 49]. By examining longitudinal employment histories of a large number of individuals, we find a more nuanced efect. In particular, we find that the persistence aspect of grit does not lead to higher wages in highly computerized routine occupations, although it is still valuable in other jobs. Particularly, persistence adds the most value (in percentage terms) in the leastcomputerized routine jobs, which tend to be low-skill and low-pay jobs, and this can be an important addition to the literature that focuses more on high-skill jobs. As more and more occupations become increasingly computerized, we expect the relationships we have uncovered to have increasing economic importance.

Our findings have several policy and managerial implications. First, from a public policy perspective, adding individual persistence evaluation in various job-matching programs for the disadvantaged could lead to better matching of workers to jobs and more eficient public spending on workforce training and placement. The same logic should apply to private sector hiring as well, where human resource managers can incorporate persistence assessment to improve the hiring process. Managers throughout the firm could benefit by considering employee persistence in team building, task assignment, and promotion. Our results also suggest a redesign of the K-12 curriculum and culture. A move from traditional methods focused on memorization, rule following, and individual cognitive attainment to modern methods such as active learning and project based learning can foster persistence and other traits needed for twenty-first century occupations.

## Theoretical Background and Hypotheses

The labor market in recent decades has gone through rapid routinization, such as Acemoglu and Autor, Autor et al., Orlikowski and Iacono, and Restrepo [1, 8, 45, 46], among others.<sup>2</sup> As discussed in the seminal work of Autor et al. [8], routine tasks are repetitive work that can be “accomplished by machines following explicit programmed rules.” Routine tasks are relatively well structured, although they may vary in context. For example, call center workers or insurance claims representatives tend to do the same tasks over and over again, although customers difer. They know where and how to get the information they need. The sources and types of information can vary from task to task, but they tend to be very similar. These jobs often entail repeating the same tasks multiple times within a day, with relatively few tasks extending to multiple days or weeks. In contrast, non-routine tasks vary more extensively from iteration to iteration. They may also have tasks that are longer term than a single day, or goal that require stringing together several tasks across days or weeks. They may draw from more diverse or variable data sources, and require more diverse performance repertoires to complete each task. Occupations are a mix of routine and nonroutine tasks, but routine occupations have a higher proportion of standardized tasks than non-routine occupations do, and vice versa.

Persistence is a component of the widely-known personality trait grit. Grit is defined as “perseverance and passion for long-term goals” [25], and it consists of two related yet distinct abilities: persistence and diligence [25, 26]. Persistence describes an individual’s ability to perform tasks with constant dedication, stay the course, and keep focus for a long period of time. Diligence emphasizes how hardworking an individual is, or their efort.

While grit—the combination of persistence and diligence—is important for many purposes, persistence is the trait that most accurately measures the ability to focus and excel on tasks over the long term. We expect persistence to provide three sources of labor market value:

(1) Execution Persistence: This type of persistence helps the worker to do tasks as defined, executing them quickly and without variation for a full shift of work. Workers with less Execution Persistence would lag in speed or quality.

(2) Integration Persistence: Within a single task execution, Integration Persistence helps to manage across and integrate diverse information sources or adjust to unexpected variations. Keeping the goal in mind, these workers are able to adjust to non-standardized parts of a task better than others.

(3) Cross-task Persistence: Across task executions, Cross-task Persistence helps workers to stay focused on a long-term goal by stringing together multiple types of tasks. This is especially important for tasks that take longer to perform and depend on integrating across sequences of tasks, sometimes lasting days or weeks, to achieve a goal.

We would expect to see a premium for persistence in all occupations, whether routine or non-routine. Both routine and non-routine jobs have some element of the need to execute well within a task and to execute well across tasks in support of a longer-term goal. They just vary in the mix of tasks they contain. Therefore, persistence should be valued in the labor market across all occupations, although the premium may be diferent across occupations, which we come back to in later theoretical reasoning. This expectation follows on past research that showed the value of diferent, but related, measures. For example, Malhotra and Galletta [41] find that internalization based on personal norms has a sustained positive influence on user attention and commitment. Similarly, Malhotra et al. [42] demonstrate the importance of endogenous motivation in user adoption of, and engagement with, information technologies. Ke et al. [35] show that worker intrinsic motivation plays an important role in driving workers’ exploration of enterprise systems. Although not directly speaking to worker persistence, these studies shed light on the importance of worker commitment and the need to investigate various factors that drive such commitment. Thus, we formulate and test the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 1 (H1): There is a positive labor market return to worker persistence.

As a result, how do computers afect the value of worker persistence in diferent types of occupations? Workplace computerization has significantly shaped the nature of work [2, 9, 10, 22, 27, 31, 48]. Occupations with high degree of codifiable routine tasks became the first place for workplace computer adoption, and the adoption accelerated as the cost of computers dropped [8]. Table 1 lists example jobs in diferent levels of routineness and computerization.<sup>3</sup> Computers can easily outperform human labor at routine tasks because these tasks are repetitive, specialized, and low-variance in nature.

However, adding computers in an occupation is not just a pure task substitution. It also involves reconfiguring the tasks that computers are not designed to do. We make a case for four potential impacts on the value of persistence in the presence of computers.

First, computers are good at well-codified routine tasks. Therefore, we would expect them to reduce the value of Execution Persistence by being just as persistent as people when it comes to executing well-defined tasks quickly and consistently. We refer to this as Condition 1.

Second, computers require standardized data so that they can execute their repeated instructions easily. Computers do not work as well when data is inconsistently defined or is sparsely supplied from diferent data sources. Therefore, computerized environments would imply an information context that is more standardized and has less unexpected variation. This low-variation environment would give less value to Integration Persistence, since there would be less unexpected variation in the task. We refer to this as Condition 2a.

Conversely, in computerized environments, computers may do the most standardized tasks or cases from the workload, and leave out the non-standardized exceptions for humans to handle. In that case, the value of persistence would go up in computerized environments, since the exceptions would be more variable and less routine. In this case, Integration Persistence would dominate, giving more value to persistence in the presence of computers (Condition 2b).

Instead of substituting, computers may also serve complementary roles such as screening for anomalies, conducting complex calculations, or reporting information in easilyunderstood ways. This complementary role is particularly important in non-routine jobs where productivity requires integrating various tasks over a relatively long period of time. It is unclear what efect this would have on the value of Cross-task Persistence. On the one hand, it could raise the value of Cross-task Persistence since persistent workers can be even more productive than less-persistent ones in the presence of computers (Condition 3a). On the other hand, it could reduce the value of Cross-task Persistence if it makes the nonpersistent workers more productive with the help of computers, which we call Condition 3b. We summarize these points in Table 2.

As one can see, the efect of computers on the value of worker persistence is highly complex, depending how the specific work environment is characterized by the theoretical conditions above. For example, if an occupation is predominantly characterized by Conditions 1, 2a, and 3b and bears little relation to 2b and 3a, then computers in that occupation are likely to reduce the premium of persistence.

Since occupations are combinations of the five theoretical conditions, theory alone does not pin down whether the wage premium of persistence is higher or lower in the presence of computers. Therefore, the computer-persistence interaction across occupations is an empirical question, and we test the following competing hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2a (H2a): In routine occupations, the labor market return of worker persistence is smaller in more computerized occupations.

Hypothesis 2b (H2b): In routine occupations, the labor market return of worker persistence is larger in more computerized occupations.

Hypothesis 3a (H3a): In non-routine occupations, the labor market return of worker persistence is smaller in more computerized occupations.

Hypothesis 3b (H3b): In non-routine occupations, the labor market return of worker persistence is larger in more computerized occupations.

## Data

To test the hypotheses, we draw individual-level worker data from the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1997 (NLSY97). NLSY97 is a longitudinal study that surveys a nationally-representative sample of individuals who were born between 1980 and 1984, meaning that the youths in the sample were 12 to 16 years old at the end of 1996. The survey was conducted annually from 1997 to 2011 and biennially since then. Over the years, NLSY97 has tracked the respondents longitudinally across various aspects of their life such as education, employment, family relationships and marriage, health, and income.

## Measuring Persistence

Our measure of persistence is from the 2013 cross-section, which includes individual-level measures of grit. Respondents were asked to give ratings on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (Disagree strongly) to 5 (Agree strongly), for the eight statements shown in Table 3. This measure, named Grit-S by Duckworth and Quinn [26], is a shortened version of the original 12-item GRIT-O construct developed in Duckworth et al. [25].

Using various samples, Duckworth and Quinn [26] show that Grit-S exhibits a twofactor structure, one of which represents “consistency of interest” and the other “perseverance of efort.” Our analysis confirms that the same two-factor structure of Grit-S exists in our sample (Table 3). First, we perform an exploratory principal component factor analysis (PCA), which shows that the first four items load well onto the first factor, and the other four items load reasonably well onto the second factor. Then, we conduct a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) that confirms that all items load to their respective factors, as suggested by the PCA, with statistical significance. The fit indexes suggest a good fit in NLSY97 $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 9 , \mathrm { N } = 4 , 8 7 9 ) = 5 2 1 . 2 1 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ for the CFA. The first four items describe how well a person persists on tasks for the long term, whereas the last four describe how hardworking a person is. Therefore, we name them Persistence and Diligence, respectively, and we standardize both measures to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 across the individuals who have non-missing values of these measures in the 2013 cross section.

As previously described, we focused on Persistence, since it more accurately describes the extent to which a person stays the course for a long period of time, and thus it is likely to be a better measure of worker ability on performing routine tasks than Diligence. Therefore, we focused our analysis on Persistence. While grit is a “stable individual diference” like other personality traits, it has been shown that grit may increase monotonically with age [25].<sup>6</sup> Since our sample includes only young adults (with the oldest being 33 years old in 2013), we treat persistence in our analysis as a personal time-invariant endowment to one’s labor market experience. Later, we will show robustness tests that restrict the sample to person-years where the individuals are older than various age thresholds in order to alleviate the concern that persistence could be developed and thus endogenous in very young individuals.

## Routineness, Computerization, and Other Occupational Characteristics

NLSY97 tracks all current and previously held jobs for individuals in the sample. Key variables include occupation, job search, tenure, hours worked, and wages, as well as other job-related characteristics. Our outcome variable is logged real hourly wage in 2013 dollars. We follow Altonji et al. [4] to truncate real hourly wage for values under 3 dollars and above 200 dollars.

We augment the data set with occupational characteristics from The Occupational Information Network (O\*NET), which is a comprehensive database developed by the U.S. Department of Labor that measures skill and task requirements of approximately 900 occupations in the US economy. O\*NET is used frequently in studies of labor economics and RBTC (e.g., [1, 5, 19]). O\*NET is not updated annually for all occupations; instead, only a small portion of occupations have their characteristics updated every year. Given that the vast majority of individuals in our sample entered the job market in the 2000s, we chose to use O\*NET 2008 where all occupations had been updated by that year since the initial roll-out. This way the measures can best reflect the overall occupational characteristics in the early 2000s. Table 4 lists the O\*NET items we use to construct each of the occupational skill requirement measures used in our analysis.

For example, to measure the routineness of a given occupation, we first identify the O\*NET score of each of its constituent items, namely “degree of automation” and “importance of repeating same tasks” [19]. One complication is that occupations in NLSY97 are in Census 2002 classification while O\*NET occupations are in Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) 2000. We use the appropriate crosswalk<sup>7</sup> to link these two classifications and then collapse the item scores from SOC to Census occupations, using employment weights from Occupational Employment Statistics (OES).<sup>8</sup> For example, if two SOC occupations correspond to the same Census occupation with equal employment size, we compute the item score of that Census occupation by applying a weight of 0.5 to item scores of the two SOC occupations. Then we standardize each item score to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one, weighted by employment size of their Census occupations. After that, we take the simple average of the item scores that are already standardized. Finally, we standardize the average with the same employment weights to generate the measure of occupational routineness. By this procedure, all occupational characteristics should be continuous standardized measures. An exception is “routineness” (we name it “routine” throughout the analysis), which we discretize to be a dummy that equals one when the occupation is above median in routineness and zero otherwise. This discretization enables us to split our main analyses into routine occupations and non-routine occupations.

## Empirical Analysis and Main Results

In this section, we discuss our empirical strategies and report the empirical findings. We start by studying the overall wage efect of persistence as a test of H1 via a standard wage equation. In addition, we address potential endogeneity issues such as worker fixed efects, job selection, occupation fixed efects, and the possible time-varying nature of worker persistence. ## Subsampling with Various Age Thresholds

Another potential endogeneity concern is that worker persistence may be time-varying, instead of an exogenous ability with respect to their labor market experience. Although the psychology literature generally argues for the stability of personality traits such as persistence, we cannot rule out the possibility that workers develop or lose persistence over time, especially for very young adults or teenagers.

To alleviate this concern, we leverage the fact that the persistence measure in our sample was collected from the last cross section. Persistence can be viewed as more exogenous when we study a shorter time span of the same individuals between the start year of any subsample and the last year of the sample. Therefore, instead of focusing on the whole 16- year span, we successively limit the sample to shorter time periods and examine the consistency of the results.

Given that the persistence measure in our sample is collected from the last cross section when the individuals were about 29-33 years old, we remove early years of the same individuals from the sample, by various age thresholds, that is, 20, 22, 24, 26. ## Removing the Largest Occupations

We perform another set of robustness checks by estimating our main empirical models of persistence, but leaving out the largest occupation in routine jobs or non-routine jobs. The idea behind these robustness checks is that the observed wage efects might be driven by a particular occupation, instead of an overall pattern in the labor market. In Specification (1) of Table 15, we leave out cashiers—the largest routine occupation, comprising 9.4 percent of all person-year observations in the routine subsample—and find that the wage efects of persistence, computer, and their interaction are largely unchanged. Similarly, for non-routine jobs, we leave out salespeople (5.7 percent of all person-year observations in the non-routine subsample) in Specification (2) and find no material change in estimation results. Therefore, we can conclude that the main empirical results are not driven by large occupations.

## Conclusion

Persistence in work is important; therefore, persistent workers should be valued. Using a new measure of personal persistence, and a nationally-representative longitudinal sample of workers, we find important evidence that the labor market does indeed value persistence. Each standard deviation of persistence in the population earns a wage premium averaging of 2.3 percent for routine occupations and 1.6 percent for non-routine ones.

However, computers can deliver super-human persistence, and they do not ask for an hourly wage. They carry out their programmed tasks consistently for long durations, without calling in sick, taking breaks, or losing quality as they work longer, which is not the case for humans. For example, both worker productivity and safety tend to be compromised during night shifts [30].

Under what conditions do persistent computers afect the value of human persistence? Our empirical models find that computerization substitutes for persistence in routine jobs. Specifically, while the wage premium of persistence in the least computerized routine jobs is estimated at 5 percent, it diminishes to zero in the most computerized ones. In the least computerized jobs, an otherwise-similar worker with persistence two standard deviations above average is expected to earn 20 percent more than one with persistence two standard deviations below average. Meanwhile, the same highly-persistent worker in the most computerized routine jobs would earn no premium at all.

However, this substitution efect is not present in non-routine occupations. Our findings add empirically-grounded nuance to the conversation on how human labor is afected by, and how workers cope with, changing technologies. The findings for persistence are even more interesting when compared with the relation between cognitive ability and computerization. Unlike computers substituting for persistence in routine jobs, our analysis shows a strong complementary efect for cognitive ability in the presence of computerization. Specifically, the wage premium of cognitive ability increases by 2.8 percent when the routine job scores one standard deviation higher in computerization.

Our study has limitations. First, we are cautious about any causal interpretation of our results because our data lack random assignment of worker persistence or job characteristics. Without the luxury of an experimental ideal, we implement a set of econometric tests to provide robustness to our results. Second, our focal personality trait—persistence—is only observed once for all individuals in our sample. While persistence tends to be relatively stable across years of the same individual, especially for the time span of our sample, it would certainly be helpful if we could observe any changes in persistence over time for the same individual. Lastly, our sample focuses on relatively young adults who entered the labor market mostly after 2000. While this is an interesting and representative sample to study, additional research is needed to uncover the persistence-computer interactions for other age cohorts and for more recent technologies such as the emerging adoption of artificial intelligence.

Our findings have important implications for both the public sector and the private sector as jobs are rapidly digitized. Various public job-matching programs and job-training programs should incorporate measures of persistence. Recognizing and taking advantage of the uneven returns of persistence across jobs can yield more eficient public investment and better job market outcomes for afected individuals. Another implication is finding work opportunities for people who do not have persistence. In some occupations, there is little to no premium for persistence. These occupations could be good (if possibly temporary) targets for employing people who do not have persistence, since their lack of persistence will not be penalized as highly as in other occupations. Perhaps, in these cases the computers manage the types of persistence required, allowing the worker to still gain value from performing non-persistence-requiring tasks that the computers cannot. For example, software like Salesforce often helps keep people on track with sales calls who might not have had the persistence to do well in those jobs otherwise, even if they had all the other traits needed.

At the firm level, the findings of the study can help managers allocate human resources more efectively in the form of task assignments, team building, promotion, and so on. Employees who score high on persistence should re-evaluate their occupational choices since their relative advantage may start to erode if their routine jobs become more computerized. Similarly, job market candidates should take into account the relationship between their persistence and job characteristics when preparing to launch a career.

Our results also suggest that a redesign of the K-12 curriculum and culture may be in order. Many schools have traditionally used pedagogical approaches that too often emphasized route memorization, careful instruction-following, and obedient attention from children in neat rows of desks. Training persistence in this way might have been important for helping twenty century workers perform routine tasks. However, in the twenty-first century, as routine tasks become increasingly automated, curriculum should help students to build persistence for tasks that are less routine by incorporating active learning, project based learning, peer learning and other modern approaches.
