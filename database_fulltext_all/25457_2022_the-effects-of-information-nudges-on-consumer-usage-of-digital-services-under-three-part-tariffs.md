# The Efects of Information Nudges on Consumer Usage of Digital Services under Three-Part Tarifs

Ping Xiao<sup>a</sup>, Yuanyuan Chen<sup>b</sup>, Anandhi Bharadwaj<sup>c</sup>, and Weining Bao<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Melbourne Business School, University of Melbourne, Carlton, VIC, Australia; <sup>b</sup>Culverhouse College of Business, The University of Alabama, Tuscaloosa, AL, USA; <sup>c</sup>Goizueta Business School, Emory University, Atlanta, GA, USA; <sup>d</sup>Department of Marketing, School of Business, University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT, USA

## ABSTRACT

We develop a dynamic model to examine how information nudges influence digital services’ consumer usage behavior and welfare under a three-part tarif structure. We study two types of information nudging: nudging through full and nudging through partial information provision. In the former, information nudges are provided to inform consumers of their usage status at every decision point in a billing cycle. In contrast, in the latter, consumers are nudged at one or more decision points within a billing cycle but not throughout the billing cycle. Our model considers two dimensions of consumer heterogeneity: inattentiveness and preference. Furthermore, our model investigates an important but under-investigated design element of information nudges, namely, the timing of the information nudges. We find that (1) nudging through information provision influences inattentive consumers’ usage decisions and improves consumption eficiency, (2) consumers’ welfare gains from full information nudging depict an inverted-U shape contingent on consumers’ preference heterogeneity, and (3) the timing of nudging matters. Our findings provide managerial implications for the design of information nudging strategies and procedures. Finally, we empirically illustrate the analytical results in the context of consumers’ mobile data usage behavior.

Digital nudge; behaviora economics; limited attention; preference heterogeneity; service tarifs; three-part tarif; digital services; information nudge

## Introduction

In the digital era, information is ubiquitous and grows exponentially. Empowered by information technology, consumers can utilize diferent platforms or apps to obtain notifications for diferent products and services, compare product attributes and prices, and track and trace products from the manufacturer to the end consumers, among others. As attention is required to encode environmental stimuli and process information in conscious thought, people may fail to process information properly or ignore valuable information signals due to their limited attention [2, 4, 9]. Ultimately, limited attention leads people to make sub-optimal decisions, reducing their consumer welfare [17, 48].

For consumers using digital services under a three-part tarif structure, the inability to be always attentive to their usage status is a critical problem. In a three-part tarif, consumers pay a regular, often monthly, fixed fee to obtain a specific usage (i.e., a contract allowance) with an over-usage price per unit if they exceed that allowance within a billing cycle. Such three-part tarif pricing structures are prevalent in product and services markets (e.g., cloud computing, internet services, mobile data, and financial services). Because consumers can use services across multiple disaggregated activities without salient consumption information for each activity, it is cumbersome for them to track usage status (i.e., how much they have used at diferent “decision points” within a billing cycle). Consumers are often unaware of their usage status because they have limited attention and cognitive constraints [18, 34]. Consequently, they are likely to either experience “bill shocks” due to over-usage or end up leaving money on the table by underutilizing their contract allowance (i.e., underusage) [16, 30, 34].

Firms are keen to explore possible intervention designs that influence consumers’ behavior, thereby mitigating the welfare loss due to limited attention. Consequently, there has been a steady increase in the use of nudging interventions which refer to “any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people’s behavior predictably without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives” [48, p. 6]. Behavioral economics literature posits that a well-designed nudge can influence how people allocate their attention and conduct a more efective and conscious reasoning process [48]. An information nudge informing consumers of their usage status may help increase the salience of the actual costs of future consumption under three-part tarif pricing, thereby allowing them to consume the service more eficiently. Eficacy of nudging tactics depends on the design elements of the choice architecture (e.g., timing, contents, framing, etc.), and the contexts they work in [10, 15, 26, 28, 50]. In this paper, we aim to examine how information nudges, a popular nudging tactic that draws individuals’ attention and provides information on their usage status, afect consumer behavior under three-part tarifs from a theoretical and empirical perspective.

Firms have implemented many types of information nudges to alert consumers [8, 10, 11, 20, 21, 28, 36, 38, 39, 40, 56]. Such nudges have been shown to provide various benefits, such as increasing customer lifetime value, reducing demand uncertainty, and achieving high utilization of resources and capacity [3, 10, 21, 27, 28, 34, 38, 53, 56]. Despite the significance of information nudges under three-part tarif structures, scant theoretical or empirical work has examined how consumers respond to a nudge that draws their attention and informs them of their usage status. The few empirical studies that explore the efects of information nudges on consumer behavior mainly focus on electricity services, which implements “increasing block pricing” [2, 4, 49]. The pricing scheme is diferent from three-part tarifs in that, in the latter pricing structure, consumers pay a monthly fixed fee for usage allowance while the former does not have this feature. This feature leads to significant diferences between these two pricing structures, which causes fundamental diferences in consumer usage behavior. We further elaborate on how our study difers from related prior work in the literature review.

We first develop an analytical model to investigate the impact of information nudges on consumers’ usage decisions and subsequent consumer welfare implications. We examine two types of nudging: nudging through full information provision and nudging through partial information provision. In the former, consumers are informed of their usage status at each decision point within a billing cycle. In the latter, consumers are informed of their usage status at one or more—but not every—decision point within a billing cycle; that is, nudging may happen in the early or late period of a billing cycle. For the sake of brevity, we name these two types as nudging through full information and nudging through partial information.

Our model considers four consumer segments to account for consumers’ heterogeneous attentiveness to their usage activities. One segment is always attentive to their usage, and the other three are inattentive segments: (a) being inattentive only in the early period of a billing cycle; (b) being inattentive only in the late period of a billing cycle; and (c) always inattentive. Such heterogeneity helps our model incorporate the possibility that some consumers may take initiatives (e.g., utilize usage management tools) to keep attentive to their consumption. Our model also accounts for the impact of consumer preference heterogeneity, that is, the heterogeneity (variability) in the consumers’ utility from completing an activity.

Several important findings emerge from the analytical model. First, both nudging through full and partial information influence inattentive consumers’ usage decisions and improve consumption eficiency. In particular, upon receiving an information nudge, an inattentive consumer will slow down consumption if her allowance balance is less than a threshold value; she will speed up usage if the balance is greater than the threshold. Second, inattentive consumers’ welfare gains from full information nudging are not monotonic. It depicts an inverted-U shape contingent on consumers’ preference heterogeneity. Surprisingly, consumers with middle-range preference heterogeneity achieve the highest welfare gain. Among consumers of the same preference heterogeneity, those with a higher monthly allowance gain more than those with lower allowances. Third, when only nudging through partial information is available, the timing of partial information nudging matters. Nudging in the late period of a billing cycle maximizes consumer welfare for those who are always inattentive. If the sizes of the three inattentive consumer segments are equal, nudging in the late period also maximizes the inattentive segments’ total consumer welfare. Our analytical findings help inform the design of efective nudging strategies.

We provide an empirical illustration of the analytical model using data from China’s largest mobile service provider. We are particularly interested in empirically testing the impact of information nudges on consumer usage behavior.<sup>1</sup> Herein, the service provider used a nudging through partial information mechanism; the firm sent consumers text messages on two fixed dates in the billing cycle (the 11th and 21st of the month). The fixed-date nudging messages (FDNs) informed users of their accumulated data usage and allowance balances as of the message sent-date. However, without prior notification, the service provider abruptly canceled the FDNs. The abrupt cancelation provided a quasiexperiment to examine the diferences in consumer mobile data usage behavior, with and without the nudges. Comparing consumer usage behavior before and after the FDNs were removed, we find that consumers increase subsequent data usage if they have used less than 18.4 percent of their allowances upon receiving an FDN on the 11th of a month and decrease usage otherwise. We find a qualitatively similar efect of the FDN sent on the 21st of a month. Therefore, the empirical finding supports our related analytical finding— an information nudge prompts consumers to adjust usage behavior and improve consumption eficiency.

Our study makes several theoretical and empirical contributions to the information systems (IS) and behavioral economics research. First, to the best of our knowledge, we fill a research gap by examining how consumers respond to diferent information nudges under three-part tarif structures from theoretical and empirical perspectives. Our analytical model considers two dimensions of consumer heterogeneity: inattentiveness and preference. Our model is general and can be applied broadly to the markets under three-part tarifs (e.g., internet services, cloud computing, mobile data, credit card services, retail banking, etc.). The model illustrates how nudging through partial information slows down or speeds up mobile data consumption, supported by our empirical findings. Second, our model investigates a critical but under-investigated design element of information nudges—their timing, contributing theoretically to the behavioral economic literature and the nudging design research. The optimal timing (i.e., early versus late period) has longbeen an essential element in designing nudging mechanisms, the importance of which, although highlighted in previous studies [34, 35, 37], has never been thoroughly examined. Our study fills this important research gap. Lastly, our model provides insights into how consumers’ welfare gains could vary with preference heterogeneity, contract allowances, and the extent of consumer inattentiveness in diferent nudging scenarios. We propose a nudging design procedure that can be widely applied to many business contexts, including IT services, digital products and services, and financial services. We elaborate on the managerial implications in the discussion section.

## Theoretical Framework

## Brief Review of the Related Literature

Our study is tied to the research examining the efect of information nudges on consumer behavior. In the literature on the impact of information nudges under diferent pricing arrangements, several studies have looked at the influence of providing real-time usage status through smart meters in the energy and electricity markets [24, 36, 49]. Where our study difers is that those findings are not generalizable to services that operate under a three-part tarif because electricity is priced under an increasing block structure. Though both structures charge higher marginal prices when usage exceeds a pre-determined level, an important feature of three-part tarifs is that consumers obtain a certain free allowance upon paying a fixed fee. As this feature does not exist in increasing block pricing, there are no over-usage/under-usage concerns for consumers. Hence, the impact of information nudges on consumer behavior is diferent under these two pricing structures.

Our study also relates to the emerging literature on digital nudging, which is defined as “the use of user-interface design elements to guide people’s behavior in digital choice environments” [52]. Many IS scholars have examined the efects of digital nudging in online shopping contexts [10, 11, 21, 22, 40]. For example, Dennis et al. [10] studied two digital nudging mechanisms: numeric priming and semantic priming. They found that both strategies are efective in nudging consumers to pay more for products whose value is unclear. Ho and Lim [21] looked at the impact of taste-and-need-matched recommendations on unplanned mobile shopping behaviors and found that recommendations were efective in motivating consumers to buy during negative moods. Hong et al. [22] studied whether a match between the information format and the shopping task could nudge consumers to search the product information more eficiently. They found that people paid more attention to product information conditional on a match.

Finally, our study is also informed by the literature on the eficacy of other types of information nudges on consumer behavior. The information provided in nudges may include, for example, other people’s decisions and actions [7, 19, 32, 33, 42, 51], useful or educational information about product and action [1, 2, 6, 20, 27, 29, 57], payment reminders [37, 46, 47], over-usage alerts or notifications [18, 25], and event or activity reminders [23, 38, 45, 55, 56]. Even though nudging consumers through the provision of information about usage status is a popular business practice, to the best of our knowledge, no prior study has examined how usage behaviors are influenced by information nudges in markets that operate under a three-part tarif structure. In sum, then, our review of the related literature points to a lack of generalizable insights into how information nudges on usage status afect consumer behavior and welfare under three-part tarifs. With this research gap in mind, we have built the analytical model that follows.

## Analytical Model

The behavioral assumption underlying our analytical framework is that some consumers have “limited attention” [8]. Prior research shows that people are inattentive to marginal prices [30] and past consumption levels [16, 17, 25]. Consumers with limited attention to their past consumption are likely to be uncertain about the efect of upcoming usage activity and whether it will fall within allowable limits or lead to over-usage expenses [17].

We consider the case that a user purchases services under a three-part tarif contract, which consists of a monthly fixed fee A, an included free usage allowance n, and a unit price $\boldsymbol { p }$ for any usage exceeding the allowance. The contract allowance is reset at the beginning of each billing cycle (e.g., one calendar month). The unused allowance cannot be carried over into the next billing cycle. Without loss of generalizability, we divide a billing cycle into three equally divided decision periods $( y = 1 , 2 , 3 )$ .<sup>2</sup> At each period $y ,$ a user makes a binary choice $( s _ { y } )$ and decides whether to complete an activity (e.g., conducting a video-conference call), which will cost her one-unit allowance.<sup>3</sup> Such a discrete demand assumption under three-part tarifs has been adopted in prior literature [5, 17]. We assume that the user has no capability constraints, meaning that she can complete any activity she wants to. The user’s per period utility is $s _ { y } \cdot x _ { y }$ . This implies that the user will receive utility $x _ { y }$ upon completion $( s _ { y } = 1 )$ , and none if she decides not to act $( s _ { y } = 0 )$ . We assume that utility $x _ { y }$ is i.i.d uniformly distributed over $[ u - \in , u + \in ]$ , where u is the mean utility of completing an activity, and $\in$ represents preference heterogeneity, that is, the heterogeneity (variability) in the user’s utility from completing an activity, $u , \in > 0$ . It could also imply that consumers may get diferent utilities across time (diferent occasions) when undertaking the same activity, and/ or that diferent consumers may get diferent utilities from taking the same action. A larger value for $\in$ is more likely to occur with users who use the service for a wider variety of activities. It could also occur more frequently for users who use the service for a small set of activities but on diferent occasions. For this paper, we focus on the case in which activities are goods or services (i.e., always yields a positive utility) that are reasonably heterogeneous.<sup>4</sup> We assume that the unit price p falls between $u - \in$ and $u + \in \mathsf { \Gamma } ( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ $u - \in \Lt < p < u + \in \Lt$ . ## Analysis

To solve the model, we need to find the user’s usage decision in each period and a set of user’s beliefs about their allowance balance such that (i) the user’s usage decision is optimal given her beliefs about her allowance balance, and (ii) the user’s beliefs are consistent with her optimal usage decisions. We first solve the user’s optimal usage decisions given her beliefs and then derive the beliefs at the optimal consistent with these optimal usage decisions. ## Implications of Propositions

Our propositions have important implications for information nudging strategies under three-part tarif pricing structures. In particular, we propose a procedure for designing firms’ information nudging strategies under diferent nudging scenarios. Service providers can take the following steps to design a nudging through full information strategy; first, firms identify which inattentive customer segments to target. They should target users who may obtain relatively high values from nudging services. Proposition 2A suggests aiming at inattentive customers whose preference heterogeneity (i.e., variant of usage uncertainties) is in the middle range. Proposition 2B further implies that customers under high-allowance contracts are the top priority for nudging among consumers with the same preference heterogeneity. Ofering the nudging service to the targeted segments helps strengthen their loyalty to service providers. Second, conditional on targeting and following Proposition 1, firms can tailor or personalize nudging messages to these targeted customers based on whether they reach a predetermined threshold value upon the date of nudging. The threshold value can be calculated and revised based on the user’s consumption history and pattern. For example, mobile data service providers can include promotional add-on services and digital content such as games and videos in the nudges sent to users with allowance balances higher than the predetermined threshold values. Such targeted nudging strategies may help to create additional revenue and increase customer loyalty.

When designing a strategy for nudging through partial information, first, firms can identify which consumer segments to target by referring to Corollary 1, which suggests that nudging through partial information exerts difering benefits on diferent inattentive segments. Second, conditional on which segments to target, firms can decide the timing of nudging. For example, if targeting users who are only inattentive in the early period, firms should nudge them in the early period. If targeting always-inattentive users, firms should nudge them in the late period following Proposition 4. This may help customer loyalty and attract new customers. Third, after deciding on the targeting and timing, firms can further tailor or personalize information nudges based on a pre-determined threshold value following Proposition 3.

## Empirical Demonstration

This section analyzes a real-world practice of nudging through partial information in the mobile data service sector and empirically illustrates how sending messages about usage status afects consumers’ usage decisions, as shown in Proposition 3. As nudging through full information can be considered a special and extreme case of nudging through partial information, our empirical illustration indirectly provides empirical validity for Proposition 1.<sup>9</sup>

Three-part tarif pricing structures are common in the mobile data service market. Nowadays, mobile service providers in many countries ofer unlimited data plans and limited data plans without excess charge. However, unlimited data plans are not truly “unlimited” because such plans usually come with a catch. Service carriers will throttle the data once a consumer on an unlimited data plan hits a certain usage threshold. This makes the “unlimited” plan equivalent to the “limited” plans with normal data transmission speed. In a limited data plan without excess charges, service providers will significantly reduce the data transmission speed once a consumer reaches a usage threshold. Consumers then have to pay additional fees to boost the speed to normal. This makes the limited data plans without excess charges equivalent to the “limited” plans under normal data transmission speed with extra charges. Moreover, many consumers (especially in developing countries such as China and India) continue to subscribe to three-part tarif data plans with a limited free-usage allowance. Even in countries where unlimited data plans are popular, mobile carriers still ofer data plans with limited usage allowances for price-conscious customers. For example, mobile service carriers in Canada, the UK, and France ofer their customers multiple limited data plans that include a limited amount of data allowance, calling time, free text message, and voicemail.

Mobile service consumers may find it dificult to track hidden apps running in the background (which can quickly consume their usage allowance). It can be challenging to calculate the exact amount of usage across all apps. Mobile data users are largely inattentive to their usage status [17, 18]. Without information nudges, inattentive consumers are more likely to experience over- or under-usage. As mobile data service has become an integral part of people’s daily lives worldwide, it is essential to understand how to nudge consumers to utilize their mobile services eficiently.

Although our analytical model provides useful insights into the efect of information nudges (usage status information) on consumer behavior, empirical results demonstrating how consumers respond to nudging under three-part tarifs is still lacking. Consumers may not be responsive to information nudges, or nudges may purely alert some consumers without spurring them to use the information strategically, or consumers may not only be alerted by nudges but also adjust their consumption by utilizing their usage status information. Despite the popularity of usage-monitoring apps, many mobile service providers worldwide still send text messages to inform consumers of their usage status, either voluntarily or as required by law. Our empirical illustration examines the nudging’s efects delivered by a Chinese mobile service provider.

## Institutional Background and Data

The Chinese service provider represents more than half of the Chinese mobile service market and ofers many three-part tarif plans. For example, a Chinese consumer can subscribe to a data plan equipped with a 500-megabyte (MB) allowance for CNY 30/ month (USD 4.30) and pay about CNY 0.29 (USD 0.04) for every MB used over that. The billing cycle for all three-part tarif plans starts on the first day and ends on the last day of every calendar month. In January 2008, the Chinese mobile service provider voluntarily started an FDN service for its customers without government regulation or intervention. They provided customers with data usage messages free of charge in every billing cycle. Consumers on three-part tarif plans received two FDN messages (on the 11th (FDN1) and the 21st of the month (FDN2), respectively, which informed consumers of their accumulated data usage and allowance balance as of the date of FDNs. This constitutes a nudging through partial information scenario as the information was not sent by nudging message at every decision point during the billing cycle. If a user exceeded the data allowance, she would receive a threshold nudging (TSN) message informing her of the allowance’s depletion.

On May 21, 2014, the provider canceled the FDN services without notifying consumers but retained the TSN service. As smartphones were not popular in China at that time, consumers did not have access to mobile usage monitoring apps. Without FDN services, it was dificult for consumers to track their usage status, resulting in both under- and overusage. The cancelation of FDN was due to the provider’s operating costs and exogenous to consumer behavior; this context provides an ideal setting to examine how consumers’ usage behavior changed when the FDNs were canceled.<sup>10</sup>

The data comes from a random sample of 22,618 customers over six months, starting from February 1, 2014, and ending July 31, 2014. It includes four months of data usage information in the pre-FDN cancelation period (i.e., February 1, 2014–May 21, 2014, before the FDN service was canceled) and about two months in the post-FDN cancelation period (May 22, 2014–July 31, 2014). There were no other price changes for the service during the study period, no consumer dropped out, and only 4.6 percent of consumers switched service plans with the same provider. It is worth noting that our experimental setting is diferent from the previous studies of bill-shock notifications [16, 19], where cellular phone companies are assumed to change prices as a reaction to bill-shock regulations.

The dataset comprises more than 4 million user-daily observations (135,708 usermonthly). It contains information on consumers’ daily usage of data, voice, SMS text services, and nudges types and dates. Among all user-monthly observations, over-usages account for 15 percent of all observations. About 62 percent of the over-usages occurred after the 21st of a month, 28 percent occurred between the 11th and 21st, and only 10 percent occurred before the 11th of a month. On average, consumers spent thirteen minutes talking on the phone each day and sent two text messages to their contacts. We present additional statistics and discussions on daily usage and monthly allowances of the sample data in Table A-1 of the Online Supplemental Appendix A.

We also include the log-transformed allowance as a control, accounting for the efect of the size of the contract data allowance.

## Identification Strategy

In the classical diference-in-diference approach, treatment and control groups temporally exist in the same period. The participants in the treatment group are diferent entities from those in the control group. Our case, however, does not have users who do not receive any nudging messages across the entire sample period (pre-FDN and post-FDN cancelation). The efects of the FDNs are identified by comparing the daily usage behavior of consumers between the post-FDN cancelation and the pre-FDN cancelation period. It is useful to think of consumers’ behavior in the pre-FDN cancelation period (when the FDNs were sent) as the “treatment” group proxy and their behavior in the post-FDN cancelation period (after the FDN service was canceled) as the “control” (base) group proxy. In other words, the “treatment” group and the “control” group are temporally separated in our identification strategy. The diference between our “treatment” and “control” groups is that consumers in the treatment group were informed of their data usage up through the two FDNs, while consumers in the control group did not receive any nudges, as the two FDNs were canceled.

In the pre-FDN cancelation period, consumer behavior was subject to both the impact of FDN1 and the time trend $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , consumer’s natural changes in usage across days in a month) after receiving the nudge. The combined efect is measured by α 11 as day $. 1 1 \mathrm { - a f t e r } _ { d m } = 0$ In the post-FDN cancelation period, consumer behavior after the $1 1 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ of the month was only conditioned on the time trend as the FDN service ceased, $\mathrm { i . e . , d a y . 1 1 . a f t e r } _ { d m } = 1$ . The time trend efect is measured as $\alpha _ { - } 1 1 { + } \alpha _ { - } ^ { \prime } 1 1$ . Therefore, the pure efect of FDN1 on consumer daily data usage can be derived through $\alpha _ { - } 1 1 - \left( \alpha _ { - } 1 1 + \alpha ^ { \prime } _ { - } 1 1 \right) = - \alpha ^ { \prime } _ { - } 1 1$ . In the same vein, we derive the pure efect of FDN2 as

$$
\alpha_ {2} 1 - \left(\alpha_ {2} 1 + \alpha^ {\prime} _ {2} 1\right) = - \alpha^ {\prime} _ {2} 1.
$$

This identification strategy assumes that any unobserved time-varying efects are similar across both “treatment” and “control” groups, implying that natural changes in consumers intrinsic behavior will be consistent in both groups. We conducted an extensive set of additional checks to ensure that this assumption is valid. We also checked that our results are less likely to be confounded by unobservable factors such as external events, adoption of streaming services, adoption of data usage management tools, and systematic changes on main specifications of users’ mobile phones. It is worth highlighting the issue of the adoption of data management tools. According to our data provider, the adoption rate of phones with embedded data monitoring tools and third-party management tools is low in our sample period. The efects of third-party tools may be limited due to the following: (1) many users may not use the apps due to factors such as ignorance, forgetting, and laziness. (2) even if consumers make eforts to set up management tools in their smartphone settings, it is still dificult to track data usage, especially for people who use their mobile phones for everyday tasks or who have multi-device data plans. To save space, we provided a detailed argument and additional analyses in the Online Supplemental Appendix B to support that the use of data management tools is unlikely to bias our results significantly. However, we note that we might underestimate the efects of nudging if a non-negligible proportion of users were using the data management tools.

## Discussion and Conclusion

There is a growing interest in examining how firms can influence consumer behavior using non-marketing instruments such as information nudges. For digital services under threepart tarifs, consumers face demand uncertainty and may be unaware of their usage status. Such a decision-making environment will likely foment strong behavioral biases, resulting in ineficient use of free allowances and consumer welfare loss. Information nudges that inform consumers of usage status may influence usage behavior and reduce behavioral biases. However, the literature lacks insights on information nudges that influence consumers’ usage behavior under three-part tarifs. The present study develops an analytical model to address this research gap.

Our analytical model draws from the literature on limited attention and nudges and examines two types of information nudges for digital services: full information provision and partial information provision of usage status. In particular, the model incorporates the case that some consumers take initiatives to stay attentive and become aware of their usage status. Findings from the study demonstrate that nudging by full or partial information provision afects consumers’ usage behavior of digital services. In addition, our results show a non-monotonous relationship (an inverted U-shape) between consumers’ welfare gain from nudging and their preference heterogeneity. Furthermore, the analytical result demonstrates that the timing of information nudging matters for consumers’ welfare gain. Finally, we provide an empirical illustration of the analytical model using the data from a mobile data service provider.

Findings from the study make several theoretical contributions to the IS and behavioral economics literature. First, the research shows that nudging through full information and nudging through partial information can enable consumers to make more eficient usage decisions. Consumers adjust their consumption speed depending on whether their allowance balance exceeds a threshold value. Second, the gain in consumer welfare is contingent on consumer preference heterogeneity and allowance levels. Interestingly, the relationship between gains in consumer welfare from nudging through full information provision and preference heterogeneity is not monotonic. Instead, it depicts an inverted-U shape, which means that consumers with preference heterogeneity in the middle range achieve the highest gain in consumer welfare. At the same level of preference heterogeneity, those on a high usage allowance contract benefit more than those on a low usage allowance contract. In the scenario of nudging through partial information, users who are always inattentive obtained the largest welfare gain relative to other user segments. The timing of nudging (i.e., the early period or late period) matters. Information nudging in the late period of a billing cycle maximizes consumer welfare of the consumer segment that is always inattentive. It also maximizes the total consumer welfare of the three equal-sized inattentive segments.

This research has several managerial implications for designing information nudges. Prior work, especially in the context of digital goods, has identified price transparency [12, 33, 44, 56] and product transparency [14, 41, 54] as important dimensions that contribute towards greater consumer welfare [13, 31]. Our work highlights the importance of nudging users with information about usage status, especially for digital products sold under threepart tarifs with significant opacity about overall usage and over-usage charges. It is challenging for users to track how much data and bandwidth they consume on a day-today basis. Consumers worldwide have sufered the shock of outrageously high bills for services such as cloud computing, internet services, and mobile data. Firms would do well to determine the optimal mechanisms and appropriate timing for nudging. Lacking such proactive initiatives from service providers, stringent regulations to protect consumer interests are likely to be enacted. Information nudges may also help firms in usagesmoothing and capacity utilization from the perspective of operations and resource management.

This study’s findings suggest several factors that can be used in designing information nudging strategies: consumer inattentiveness heterogeneity, consumer preference heterogeneity, contract allowance level, allowance balance threshold, and timing of nudging. In the first step of our proposed nudging procedure, service providers can use consumer inattentiveness heterogeneity, consumer preference heterogeneity, and contract allowance levels as additional lenses to micro-segment customers under three-part tarifs to see which sub-segment can be targeted for nudging services. For example, when preference heterogeneities are non-trivial, those on a high-allowance contract gain more from nudging than those on a low-allowance contract; this implies that service providers can sell value-added nudging services to consumers on a high-usage allowance contract. The benefits of doing this include reducing marketing expenditures, improving customer retention, and increasing customer lifetime value. Firms can decide the timing of nudging through partial information and further tailor the nudging messages to the targeted groups. For example, many information technologies (IT) nowadays enable firms to capture and analyze large amounts of customer data to understand day-to-day operations better and learn about their customers. Firms can quickly identify the threshold value of allowance balance and tailor nudging messages accordingly.

Our study suggested some interesting future research directions. Our empirical illustration supports Proposition 1 and 3 (i.e., the impact of information nudge on consumer usage decision), given the scope of the study and data availability. Future research can use richer data to test the relationship between consumer welfare and preference heterogeneity and the welfare implication of providing information in early versus late periods of a billing cycle. In addition, another interesting research direction would be to compare the eficacy of timebased and usage-based nudging in diferent business contexts.<sup>13</sup>

12. The focus of our empirical analysis is not to separate the impacts of the two FDN messages but to provide an empirical illustration of the overall impact of nudging on consumer usage behavior. The identified efect of FDN2 may include the carry-over efect from FDN1.

13. The analyses and results on how our analytical propositions may hold under usage-based nudging are available upon request.
