# Repeated IT Interruption: Habituation and Sensitization of User Responses

Christoph Weinert <sup>a</sup>, Christian Maier <sup>a</sup>, Sven Laumer <sup>b</sup>, and Tim Weitzel <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information Systems and Services, University of Bamberg, Bamberg, Germany; <sup>b</sup>Digitalization in Business and Society, Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, Nürnberg, Germany

## ABSTRACT

Information technology (IT) interruptions are IT-based events that capture users’ attention and interfere with other activities. This study focuses on repeated IT interruption and task performance. We draw on dual-process theory and suggest that users may get used to repeated IT interruption, known as habituation, or may become hypersensitive, known as sensitization. We validate the research model based on data from a laboratory experiment with 100 subjects by using a multivariate latent growth model (LGM). With subjective and objective measurement techniques, we show how users respond to repeated IT interruption with physiological arousal, psychological exhaustion, and behavioral task performance. Our results indicate that user responses follow diferent patterns over time, revealing time-dependent efects of arousal and exhaustion on task performance. We contribute to literature by providing evidence that repeated IT interruption results in unique habituation and sensitization user response patterns compared to a single IT interruption.

## KEYWORDS

IT interruption; user attention; dual-process theory; IS use; longitudinal research; latent growth modeling; arousal; exhaustion; task performance; skin conductance; continuance state sampling; NeuroIS

## Introduction

Interruptions in the workplace are a well-known cause of low job performance [19]. In the digital era, interruptions often involve information technology (IT) malfunctions or incoming emails that draw users’ attention away from their current task [2]. Recent research considers IT interruptions as IT-based events that capture users' cognitive attention and interfere with their current activities [1]. Extant information systems (IS) literature investigates single IT interruptions [1, 2, 80], especially their content and structure. For example, IS literature [1, 2] studies the efect of relevant and irrelevant content of single IT interruptions and their efect on the present task and develops taxonomies to classify such IT interruptions. Such studies demonstrate that single IT interruptions result in adverse responses for individuals and organizations. It has been shown that a single IT interruption leads to physical responses including increased heart rate [60], exhaustion, or mental overload [79, 80], as well as negative behavioral responses such as diminished job performance and work eficiency [2, 80].

However, many users encounter IT interruptions repeatedly. For example, computer breakdowns typically occur more than once. Computer systems that have crashed once have a 1 in 3.3 chance of crashing again [58]. Psychological literature finds that responses to repeated events difer from responses to single events [37]. There is evidence that task performance recuperates after repeated encounters with a specific interruption event [68]. With regard to IT interruptions, we only know that the encounter with a single IT interruption reduces task performance [79, 80]. Extant IS research does not provide guidance on how multiple repetitions of the same manner of IT interruption, which we refer to as “repeated IT interruption” in this paper, influence task performance and whether and how the efect of repeated IT interruption difers from the efect of a single IT interruption. We develop theory that explains how users respond to repeated IT interruption with habituation and sensitization user response patterns and how repeated IT interruption influences task performance over time. Thus, our overarching research question is:

## How does repeated IT interruption influence users’ responses and task performance over time?

We draw on dual-process theory (DPT) [37], which posits two diferent patterns in response to a repeated event: habituation and sensitization. Habituation<sup>1</sup> is when user responses decrease with each repeated encounter (response decrement), while sensitization is when user responses intensify with each repeated encounter (response increment) [37]. We investigate how these two patterns influence physiological, psychological and behavioral user responses. Considering all three responses holistically using methods to measure each individually, allows us to gain insights into both visible and conscious psychological responses as well as invisible and unconscious physiological responses [60, 79]. This approach also casts light on the time-dependent efects of repeated IT interruption on each of the three types of user responses (physiological arousal, psychological exhaustion, and behavioral task performance) individually as well as collectively and simultaneously. Building on DPT, we hypothesize that users respond to repeated IT interruption by experiencing habituation and sensitization and that the efect on task performance is time-dependent. To test our hypothesis, we conducted a laboratory experiment with 100 subjects, integrating four diferent data collection techniques: continuance state sampling, time-based performance, skin conductance, and questionnaires. The longitudinal design examines the time-dependent development (i.e., trajectories) of users’ physiological arousal, psychological exhaustion, and task performance as dependent variables in response to encountering repeated IT interruption. The study contributes to the literature by revealing how users respond to repeated IT interruption, identifying habituation and sensitization efects on user responses, and demonstrating that habituated and sensitized user responses influence task performance in a way that the deterioration decreases over time such that task performance recuperates.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. The theoretical background section introduces the dual-process theory and extant literature on IT interruptions. We then develop our research model and present our methodology and data analysis. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical contributions of our findings and identify potential avenues of future research.

## Theoretical Background

This section introduces the dual-process theory, IT interruptions, and the resulting user responses.

## Dual-Process Theory

To study changes in user responses caused by repeated IT interruption, we apply dualprocess theory (DPT) [37]. This theory posits that individuals show two diferent response patterns following a repeated stimulus: a decremental response called habituation and an incremental response called sensitization [32, 37]. Habituation and sensitization are separate mechanisms and constitute the simplest forms of learning. Both are non-associative learning behaviors that happen without positive (e.g., reward) or negative (e.g., punishment) reinforcement [36].<sup>2</sup>

Habituation is defined as a “response decrement that results from repeated stimulation” [65, p. 136]; it is thus a learning behavior that decreases the response to a repeated stimulus [85]. Habituation allows individuals to filter out irrelevant stimuli and focus on what’s important [65]. It occurs when a stimulus is repeated following the initial response to the once-novel stimulus. Specifically, the response decreases as the individual becomes habituated to it [36, 85]. Habituation develops in the stimulus-response (S-R) neural pathway [84]. The nervous system creates a model of the expected stimulus and compares further presentations of the stimulus to the model. If the stimulus complies with this model, the response decreases over time [74]. As depicted on the left in Figure 1, after the first encounter, the response decreases with each further encounter of the same stimulus. This indicates that the individual is becoming habituated. The efect of habituation is familiar to everyone: for example, one gets used to the ticking of a clock and eventually fails to perceive it, which depicts a positive efect of habituation. An IT-related example is the decreasing attention spent on repeatedly appearing security warnings [13, 89], demonstrating a negative efect of habituation as users are less alerted by security warnings.

Sensitization is an incremental response that results from repeated stimulation [37]. Sensitization develops in a separate-state system and not in the S-R neural pathway [84]. Individuals are sensitized to a stimulus when the stimulus is perceived as having a strong positive or negative value [38], making users more receptive to positive and negative stimuli. The encounter with a stimulus may lead to the perception of greater joyful anticipation if the stimulus is positive and greater perceived vulnerability if the stimulus is negative. Thus, the efect in either case is an increase in the level of response over time [35]. The response intensifies with each further repetition of the same stimulus, indicating that the individual is becoming sensitized (right part in Figure 1). An example is hospitalization, to which individual responses increase with each encounter because the first encounter sensitizes one to future vulnerability. Psychological responses such as tiredness or anxiety, which highly correlate [44], follow this pattern, in that individuals prioritize adverse thoughts and information resulting in intensive responses [17, 33, 88]. This pattern also has a positive efect when perceiving a significant stimulus (e.g., a security warning) and a negative efect when perceiving an irrelevant stimulus (e.g., a computer malfunction).

The literature indicates that, among others, physiological and psychological responses, even if triggered by the same repeated stimulus, can follow diferent response patterns [86]. One response might follow a habituation pattern, and a another response might follow a sensitization pattern, even if both responses are triggered by the same repeated stimulus [49]. The literature indicates that physiological responses (e.g., arousal) follow a habituation pattern [36], whereas psychological responses (e.g., exhaustion) follow a cognitiveemotional sensitization [17].

## IT Interruptions and User Responses

IT interruptions are IT-based events that capture a user’s cognitive attention and interfere with current activities [1]. Scholars diferentiate between two categories of interruptions [22]: disruptions and distractions. Disruptions are relevant stimuli (e.g., secondary task) that interfere with primary tasks. The primary task can be disrupted by a second task, causing a user to switch her attention rapidly between tasks [62], or by unavailable system resources, disrupting current work on the primary task [1]. Distractions are irrelevant stimuli that capture users’ attention and that should be ignored [22]. In this study, we focus on system intrusion IT interruption, hereafter “IT interruption,” which fall into the disruption category [1]. They involve “system property issues or lack of availability of system resources which disrupt an individual’s current flow of work” [1, p. 236]. For example, work is disrupted when system resources are unavailable because of system glitches or breakdowns that result when a system’s features are new or unexpected. IT interruptions lead to user responses, which we define [14, 15, 24, 64] as the set of reactions manifested among users that co-emerge as consequences of disturbing or threatening stimuli (e.g., IT interruptions, new IS, techno-stressors). We distinguish three types of user responses [24, 71].

Physiological user responses include bodily reactions associated with cardiovascular, biochemical and gastrointestinal symptoms [24]. For example, users’ heart rates may increase, their hormone levels may increase (e.g., alpha-amylase), or they may sweat more [34, 60]. Psychological user responses reflect the state of mind at a conscious level [79], for example, feelings of emotional exhaustion, mental overload, or negative computer-related thoughts (e.g., thinking about the IT interruption) [60, 80]. Behavioral user responses include how users behave in a given situation [24]. Examples include lower performance or specific IS usage behavior such as clicking on a permission button [2, 80, 89]. The influence of IT interruptions on behavioral user responses assumes that working memory—storage and manipulation of information—is compromised by shifting the user’s attention to the interruption [22]. In other words, IT interruptions divert the user’s attention away from the purposeful activity or task and towards the IT interruption. This disrupts the user’s current workflows and results in negative user responses [1]. For example, system failures such as a computer breakdown require attention and, in turn, prevent users from conducting their task. The users’ concentration on their primary task is disrupted when their attention turns to the computer breakdown, and their workflow is disrupted.

Previous literature has shown that IT interruptions trigger psychological [2, 21, 79, 80], physiological [34, 79], and behavioral user responses [2, 21, 80].

## Relevant Extant Research into IT Interruptions

Extant IT interruption research focuses primarily on disruptions and distractions [1, 22]. Ortiz de Guinea and Webster et al. [60] show that, in the context of IS usage, disruptions in the form of errors in text formatting in MS Word lead to psychological, physiological, or behavioral user responses as a proxy of IS usage. Their results show that IT interruptions lead to adverse emotional responses, increased negative computer-related thoughts, and more frequent adaptive behaviors, proving the existence of an automatic and adjusting IS usage pattern. Jenkins et al. [42] investigate security message interruptions during a primary task. Looking at high and low dual-task interference, they measure brain responses using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Chen and Karahanna [21] study the work and nonwork consequences of technology-mediated work-related interruptions during private or leisure time. To measure these interruptions, they use a latent construct. Their results showed both positive and negative influences on work and nonwork exhaustion and performance. A recent examination [78] investigates the efect of perceived interruption overload on IS usage, the mediating role of work-life conflict (WLC), and the moderation efect of control. The results show that control acts as an essential boundary mechanism of the spillover efect of interruptions through WLC on IS usage.

Extant research into interruptions caused by distraction, specifically email interruptions, distinguishes between incongruent interruptions, for example, containing information that is not relevant to the task, and congruent interruptions, for example, containing relevant information to the task, finding that incongruent interruptions indirectly reduce task performance, while congruent interruptions have an indirect positive efect on task performance [2]. A subsequent investigation examines how these incongruent and congruent interruptions influence the user and lead to impaired group outcomes, as explained by a theoretical model of how the personal efects of an interruption impact the group level [3]. Tams et al. [79] focus on reduced task performance following physiological and psychological user responses to IT interruptions. Their study looked at instant messages, comparing user responses to notifications every 90 seconds and user responses to notifications every 15 seconds. Their results show that IT interruptions lead to psychological and physiological user responses and, in turn, to lower task performance. A similar study shows that email interruptions lead to individual stress, such as increased exhaustion and reduced task performance [80]. Another study of highly and moderately demanding messages demonstrated that IT interruptions lead to perceptual strain and increases in alpha-amylase [34]. In the context of security, scholars have investigated whether repeated views of either static or polymorphic security warnings attract more or less attention as measured by eyetracking. Their results showed that polymorphic warnings attract more attention than static images [4, 5, 89].

In summary, we identified diferent types of IT interruptions (see Online Supplemental Appendix, Table A 1). Security research has analyzed repeated security warnings and especially their efects on users’ physiological responses. In other contexts in which IT interruptions have negative consequences, research has concentrated mostly on other characteristics of IT interruptions and has neglected the role of repeated IT interruptions. There is a minimal amount of research into disruption in general and no research considering the efect of repeated IT interruption caused by a disruption. Our review of related extant literature finds very few simultaneous investigations of psychological, physiological, and behavioral response patterns over time and a lack of theoretical and empirical examination of their dependences over time. To close these research gaps, we investigate user responses to a repeated IT interruption caused by disruption. We thus show how each user response is diferently influenced by repeated IT interruption and follows a unique pattern over time.

## Model Development

The present paper investigates two distinct user response patterns to repeated IT interruption: habituation and sensitization. We diferentiate between physiological, psychological, and behavioral user responses [60, 79] and collectively and simultaneously investigate the efect of conscious psychological and unconscious physiological user responses on behavioral user responses. To operationalize the various user responses, we draw on previously identified responses to IT interruptions (see the Online Supplemental Appendix, Table A 1): arousal as a physiological user response [34, 60, 79], exhaustion as a psychological user response [21, 80], and task performance as a behavioral user response [2, 60, 79, 80], as summarized in Table 1.

Physiological arousal, hereafter “arousal,” is one possible response of a user to stimuli, such as IT interruptions [60, 83], and is understood as the elevated state of bodily functions [30]. It encompasses the activation of the autonomic nervous system. It manifests as sweating, pupil dilation, faster heartbeat, and increased cortisol and alpha-amylase levels [34, 79]. Arousal is relatively unexplored in the context of IT interruptions but common in other contexts [55, 59, 60, 87, 96]. Arousal can be positive (e.g., excited, happy) or negative (e.g., distressed, frustrated) [60, 69]. When the stimulus is positively evaluated (e.g., goaloriented or satisfying), the arousal is positive. Correspondingly, when the stimulus is negatively evaluated in terms of hindering or threatening, the arousal is negative [30]. The present paper focuses on negative arousal as past literature indicates that disruptive IT interruptions (e.g., computer breakdowns) are negatively evaluated [51, 92] and hence result in negative arousal [93].

Psychological exhaustion, hereafter “exhaustion,” is defined as the feeling of tension and depletion of one’s emotional resources [54], especially negative emotions following encounters with an IT interruption [60, 79, 80]. Exhaustion is also associated with IS usage [11]. Most recent exhaustion research is anchored around research on burnout [27, 54, 56], job stress [56, 90], technostress [9, 79, 80], work demands [21], and IS usage [11].

Behavioral task performance, hereafter “task performance,” has been examined intensively as an outcome over the last decade [8, 31, 63, 76, 77, 81, 82]. Task performance studies the behaviors involved in completing a job and is understood as an outcome measured in terms of efectiveness. In other words, performance is the extent to which the task results fulfill the task’s goals [83]. This definition emphasizes the short-term character of task performance, which is common in extant research [18]. Consequently, focusing on shortterm task performance encompasses the efectiveness of an immediately performed task and neglects long-term performance over a more extended working phase. Task performance is one of the most studied consequences of IT interruption, albeit with a bias towards shortterm efects (Table 1).

We next develop a research model that explains how these user responses develop over time in response to repeated IT interruptions and the time-dependent dependencies among response patterns. Using the DPT as a theoretical lens, we hypothesize that various responses (e.g., arousal, exhaustion, task performance) follow diferent patterns —either habituation or sensitization—in response to a repeated IT interruption [49]. In alignment with existent literature demonstrating a decreasing efect of physiological response over time, we hypothesize that arousal follows a habituation pattern [65]. At the psychological level, we anticipate sensitization processes [33]. Following the lead of extant literature showing that psychological responses increase based on the cognitiveemotional sensitization pattern [17], we hypothesize that exhaustion follows a sensitization pattern. Finally, we propose that task performance follows a habituation pattern because task performance recuperates with repeated encounters of a specific event [68].

Our research design considers the longitudinal nature of repeated IT interruptions and the corresponding changing user responses. The paper takes a longitudinal latent growth modeling (LGM) approach (see Online Supplemental Appendix B for more details). LGM helps to measure the change in a latent variable over time and validates causal models to explain changes and evaluate the impact of changes on outcome variables within a single structural model [94]. It integrates the initial level and the trajectory of the variables. The initial level of the user responses represents user responses to the first IT interruption (see Figure 1, $1 ^ { \mathrm { s t } }$ EWS), and the trajectory of the responses represents the slope of the user responses developing through repeated encounters of the IT interruption (see Figure 1). The research model illustrated in Figure 2 considers the initial level and the trajectories for arousal, exhaustion, and task performance and theorizes their dependencies.

A “given stimulus elicits an HPA [hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal] response, repeated exposure to that stimulus will elicit a progressively reduced response” [36, p. 218]. It was demonstrated that physiological user responses (e.g., fMRI, eye tracking) to a repeated stimulus such as security warnings [5, 89] decrease over time because users are able to filter out irrelevant IT interruptions and focus on important ones [65]. ## Sensitization to Repeated IT Interruption Results in Increased Exhaustion

Encounters with a repeated IT interruption might lead to a perception of greater vulnerability and, hence, to an increase in psychological user responses [35]. This sensitization is grounded in the brain, where users begin to adapt to the process of repeated stimuli (i.e., a repeated IT interruption) in a way that makes them increasingly vulnerable to repetitions of the interruption [35]. The previous literature indicates that psychological responses follow a cognitive-emotional sensitization pattern [17, 33, 88]. This pattern is similar to an attentional bias where too much attention is focused on a problem [17]: “Perceiving or experiencing threat leads to the detection of even more threat” [17, p. 115]. A meta-study shows that anxiety, which follows a sensitization pattern [17], is positively correlated with exhaustion [44], implying that the latter follows the same pattern over time. In the context of exhaustion, the literature assumes that the efect of repeated interruptions accumulates with each interruption [10]. One explanation is that users aim at acquiring and maintaining resources [39]. As the user’s resources drain due to repeated IT interruptions, fewer and fewer resources are available, and recovery is absent, so the exhaustion of the users increases with each repetition. The loss of resources due to IT interruptions leads to high levels of exhaustion and even greater losses when the IT interruption is encountered again. The available resources cannot be regained such that with each IT interruption, fewer resources are available, resulting in increasing exhaustion with each repeated encounter of the IT interruption [39]. Encountering an IT interruption and the perception of tiredness that follows leads to identifying the interruption as a form of threat, leading to even more tiredness and fatigue. We so propose that exhaustion increases with each IT interruption encounter because each encounter sensitizes users to their vulnerability. After all, attention is biased, such that users get into a cycle in which each repetition of the IT interruption worsens the situation and hence requires more emotional resources. These resources cannot be regained and are further reduced with each IT interruption, resulting in increasing exhaustion. For example, a computer breakdown that occurs six times in a row lead to increased exhaustion with each repeated breakdown since each encounter drains emotional resources, leading the user to feel tired and exhausted. The hypothesis is:

Hypothesis $2 _ { S e n } ( H 2 _ { S e n } ) \colon$ Through a repeated encounter with an IT interruption, users become sensitized, such that the increased exhaustion caused by the first IT interruption intensifies with each repetition of the IT interruption.

## Habituation to Repeated IT Interruption Results in Recuperation of Task Performance

Past literature demonstrates that encountering of a single IT interruption leads to a reduction in task performance [79, 80]. In the context of repeated interruptions, psychological literature shows that task performance recuperates with repeated encounters of a specific event [68]. One rationale might be the non-associative learning of habituation. Habituation also has a behavioral efect and repeated stimuli lead to better behavioral outcomes [89]. Habituation literature assumes that when a stimulus is perceived repeatedly, the nervous system creates a model of the expected repetition. Further presentations of the repeated event are compared to the model. If the repeated event complies with the model, then the behavioral user responses decrease [74, 84]. The performance literature indicates a similar process by assuming that mental models guide users and reduce the error rate as well as the efort and time required to complete the task when information conforms to the user’s mental model [8]. Additionally, as IT interruptions require attention and disrupt the activity users are working on (e.g., primary task) [1], their working memory is compromised, resulting in the reduction of task performance [22]. As users get used to the repeated IT interruption, less attention is required for the repeated IT interruption over time. Therefore, more attention can be spent on the primary task, such that task performance recuperates again with encountering repeated IT interruptions. This assumes that users habituate to their repeated encounter of an IT interruption and therefore demonstrate recuperated task performance. For example, suppose the computer breakdown occurs six times in a row. In that case, task performance might deteriorate after the first encounter but recuperate with repeated encounters as users get used to such interruptions. Hence, the hypothesis is:

Hypothesis ${ 3 } _ { H a b } ( H 3 _ { H a b } ) \div$ : Through a repeated encounter with an IT interruption, users become habituated, such that the deteriorated task performance caused by the first IT interruption recuperates again with each repetition of the IT interruption.

## Arousal Influences Task Performance

Arousal influences task performance [79]. We so propose that in the context of a repeated IT interruption, the development of arousal (habituation efect) is responsible for changes in task performance. Therefore, we argue that the initial level (comparable with the encounter of a single IT interruption; $1 ^ { \mathrm { s t } }$ EWS in Figure 1) of arousal influences the initial level of task performance. By focusing on the efect of the initial level of arousal on the initial level of task performance, studies indicate an adverse efect on task performance caused by arousal [20, 79]. Studies also indicate that single IT interruptions, such as computer breakdowns, result in high arousal [60, 66, 67]. The literature suggests that performance decreases when arousal is high [45]. In addition, attentional mechanisms can explain the influences of arousal on task performance. In particular, high arousal levels have been found to disrupt information processing [70]. Increased arousal leads to a restriction of attention, inhibiting performance as attention is focused on the stimulus (in this case, the IT interruption) rather than on the primary task [42]. The feedback from the autonomic nervous system activity is highly salient, thus competing with other stimuli for limited attentional capacity. The attentional demands of internal stimuli that are characteristic of a high arousal state leave less capacity available for performing the task [52]. For example, users who are highly aroused (e.g., showing intense sweating of their hands) by the first encounter with a repeated IT interruption (or single IT interruption) may accomplish the first task slower than those who show lower arousal. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 4a (H4a): Users with a higher initial level of arousal will exhibit a worse initial level of task performance than users with a lower initial level of arousal.

In addition, we consider how the trajectory of arousal afects the trajectory of task performance. Users habituating faster to the repeated IT interruptions have decreasing arousal [5, 65, 89]. As strong arousal disrupts information processing [70], the decreased arousal will lead to better information processing and, in turn, better task performance. The decreased arousal enables users to draw their attention back to the task and thus have more available capacity for work on the task [42]. For example, when arousal and hence skin perspiration decrease as users become habituated to the IT interruption with repeated encounters, they are able to accomplish their work tasks faster. Users who have a higher decreasing trajectory of arousal when facing repeated IT interruption will experience a greater recuperation in task performance. The hypothesize is:

Hypothesis 4b (H4b): Users with a steeper downward trajectory of arousal will exhibit a stronger recuperation in task performance than users with a less steep downward trajectory of arousal.

## Exhaustion Influences Task Performance

Similar to psychological arousal, we suggest that changes in task performance are influenced by exhaustion. As the initial level represents the first encounter with the IT interruption, the efect of exhaustion would be similar to an encounter of a single IT interruption. In the context of IT interruptions, the extant literature indicates that exhaustion and performance are negatively correlated [25, 80, 95]. Users who are emotionally exhausted are tired and fatigued, such that they have fewer emotional resources [54, 56] and are emotionally overextended [95]. Consequently, they have poorer task performance because they have fewer mental resources left to accomplish their work task. As exhaustion is most likely to occur in situations where one’s resources are inadequate to meet the demands, and when users have insuficient mental resources [39], they are not able to efectively accomplish their work task. For example, users who are initially highly exhausted and have fewer mental resources might not be able to accomplish their working task in an appropriate manner. Hence, we assume that:

Hypothesis 5a (H5a): Users with a higher initial level of exhaustion will exhibit a worse initial level of task performance than users with a lower initial level of exhaustion.

We also consider the efect of the trajectory of exhaustion on the trajectory of task performance. Users are sensitized by exhaustion, which can lead to serious illness and burnout [54, 56]. The first encounter does not lead to immunity to future encounters [35]; instead, it sensitizes the user to potential future vulnerability [37]. In other words, the first encounter with an IT interruption costs the user mental resources, and as the user remembers this vulnerability over repeated encounters with the IT interruption, each encounter drains even more mental resources. The brain begins to adapt to the process of repeated IT interruptions in a way that makes it increasingly vulnerable to repetition [35], costing more mental resources and causing deterioration in task performance. Hence, we assume that:

Hypothesis 5b (H5b): Users with a steeper upward trajectory of exhaustion will exhibit a stronger deterioration in task performance than users with a less steep upward trajectory of exhaustion.

## Methodology

To test our hypotheses, we conducted an experiment. We next describe the sample of the study, the experimental design encompassing the experimental task and the manipulation, the multiple data collection techniques and the experimental procedure.

## Study Sample

Participants in the study were university students and staf members from a German university, and the laboratory study was conducted over a four-week period. Overall, 107 participants were recruited; the answers of seven participants were dropped because of problems with the objective measurements or inappropriate behavior during the experiment, such as switching of the computer. The paper follows a latent growth modeling (LGM) approach, which is eficient regarding sample size [73], as even small samples (N=40) can provide good statistical power [26, 57]. The appropriate and acceptable performance of the LGM is ensured with 100 participants [41]. The present sample shows a nested data structure, where the seven measurement time points that followed the repeated IT interruptions (level one) are nested within one participant (level two) (see Online Supplemental Appendix, Figure A 1). Hence, the sample size on level two contains 100 participants, which in turn encompasses 700 data points on level one, which we used for the intra-individual analyses.

The characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 2. The sample was split almost equally between men and women, and the majority of the participants were between 19 and 34 years old.

## Experiment Design

The experiment follows a within-subject design, encompassing the factors IT interruption (IT interruption, no IT interruption) and repetition (no encounter to six encounters) (see Online Supplemental Appendix, Table A 2). All participants encountered the treatments. The experiment contained a baseline to ensure that every subject acts as his or her own control and undergoes six treatments. During the baseline, participants did not encounter an IT interruption and consequently could not habituate or sensitize to the IT interruption. We used this baseline to establish control values for each subject in an unmanipulated situation. After the baseline, the subjects encountered the same IT interruption six times (see section Manipulation), i.e., a repeated IT interruption.

## Tasks and Technology

An enterprise content management (ECM) [47] system (MS SharePoint) was used to simulate a realistic organizational situation. Using a text-based scenario, participants slipped into an intern’s role and completed seven diferent tasks for a supervisor, all solvable using MS SharePoint (Online Supplemental Appendix, Table A 3). The tasks were approximately equally simple to solve without prior MS SharePoint experience, eliminating task complexity and familiarity with the software. A sample task was to use the system to search for a specific contact person in the HR department. To complete the task, the subject had to navigate from the main window to the correct list of employees and find the person. All experimental tasks are shown in Online Supplemental Appendix, Table A 4.

## Manipulation: Repeated IT Interruption

As the paper focuses on IT interruptions, which “include system property issues and the lack of availability of system resources” [1, p. 237], a staged computer breakdown was chosen to function as the repeated IT interruption [1, 66]. We simulated a 20-second system freeze during which input by mouse or keyboard was impossible. The facilitator manually but remotely activated this freeze. The facilitator and the subject were sitting in the same room, separated by a partition wall, and the facilitator’s screen was mirrored to the subject’s screen so the subjects’ activities could be tracked in real-time. To manipulate the repetition, subjects were exposed repeatedly to the same stimulus—computer breakdown—while trying to keep the working environment otherwise stable [65]. Subjects were exposed to the IT interruption six times, an appropriate number of stimuli to examine habituation and sensitization [29].

## Data Collection Techniques

We diferentiate between three user responses, so we used various data collection techniques. To capture arousal, an unconscious response, objectively, we used skin conductance. We applied the continuance state sampling method to measure the conscious state of mind in terms of exhaustion during the experiment and use a time-based measure for task performance.

## Skin Conductance Operationalizing Arousal

Skin conductance (SC) has been used in the context of NeuroIS [7, 91] to capture human responses by measuring electrical activity in the skin [16, 29]. Specifically, research shows that SC can be used to measure arousal [67, 83]. In our study, we used an exosomatic SC method that applies direct current to the skin. Two electrodes were installed on the palmar surface of participants’ non-dominant hand to measure the low-level voltage between the electrodes. Participants’ SC was measured once per second using a MentalBioScreen K3 device and recorded in microsiemens (µS). SC was divided into a tonic and a phasic component [16]. The tonic component is “the absolute level of [. . .] conductance at a given moment in the absence of a measurable phasic response” [29, p. 158]. In other words, tonic values represent SC over a longer period of time and are referred to as skin-conductance level (SCL). The phasic component takes the increases in conductance into consideration, which occurs in the tonic phase, typically triggered by diferent external or internal stimuli. Increases in conductance represent skinconductance responses (SCR) [16]. In line with past IS literature [67, 83], we used SCR to measure arousal after each manipulation, and in line with Teubner et al. [83], we measured the amplitude of the SCR (SCR.amp) to calculate the strength of each response [16]. SCR.amp acts as the proxy for the extent of instant arousal and reflects short bursts of sympathetic activity [28]. As SCR.amps are frequently skewed, a transformation algorithm was applied, as suggested by past literature [29, 83], to transform all the SCR.amp values by log(x+1). SCR.amp was measured after each repeated IT interruption and a latency (SCR.latency) of three seconds between each repeated IT interruption and response onset was considered [28].

## Continuance State Sampling Operationalizing Exhaustion

An experience sampling method (ESM) [46] called continuance state sampling (CSS) [72] was performed to measure exhaustion [12, 60]. The objective of this method is to capture the emotional state of the participants in everyday situations. In this method, the questions to measure emotional state are based on a specific scenario. Participants are asked to report their feelings at a specific moment rather than recall their feelings later in a post-experimental survey. The measurement can be initiated by a signal such as a beeping tone, an event such as a computer breakdown, or a time interval. In the present study, a text-based scenario was used, in which the subjects were questioned about their feelings of exhaustion after each task and manipulation. This enabled measurement of users’ exhaustion during the experimental task activities [60], and specifically after each encounter with the IT interruption (Figure 3) [9].

## Time-Based Measurement Operationalizing Task Performance

Task performance consists of behaviors carried out to complete a job. When studying task performance, measures must reflect the task under consideration. For the purpose of this empirical investigation, performance was assessed as an outcome because the user had complete control of her own task, and the output did not depend on other people [18]. In line with past investigations [60], the outcomes and completion times for all seven tasks were drawn from video analysis. This made it possible to take a time-based performance measurement approach [23, 61]. The completion time of each task was measured in seconds and accurate task completion was the desired outcome. Following the lead of prior research, a time penalty of one standard deviation among all task completion times was added to the time required for tasks completed inaccurately. In addition, each incorrect task resulted in a time penalty of one standard deviation from the times of all participants added to this task’s specific completion time [23].

In the pre-experimental stage, a questionnaire was used to collect demographic data, including age, gender, IT experience [6], IT self-eficacy [53], and computer breakdown selfeficacy [53]. In the post-experimental stage, a second questionnaire was used to measure perceived-threat appraisal [48] and techno-unreliability [9] (see the Online Supplemental Appendix, Table A 8).

## Experimental Procedure

The experiment took about 30 minutes and was divided into a pre-experimental, an experimental, and a post-experimental stage (see Figure 3). In the pre-experimental stage, participants were seated at individual, isolated desks in the laboratory. After hearing a short introduction to the experiment, subjects were fitted with the SC equipment and filled out the first survey.

In the experimental stage, the subjects read a text-based scenario asking them to imagine they were a new intern in a fictitious company called IT-share AG. The subjects were welcomed as new interns and provided information regarding the internship program. The supervisor explained that the company used MS SharePoint and that today’s task was to become familiar with the software. The supervisor explained how to access several lists and libraries in MS SharePoint, and told them that they would be expected to support the supervisor by drawing information from MS SharePoint. In addition, the supervisor informed the interns that they would be questioned frequently during these tasks to improve the quality of the internship program.

After the introduction, the subjects received their first task in a short message from the supervisor asking them to draw information from MS SharePoint (see section Tasks and Technology). There was no manipulation before the first task to obtain a baseline for subject user responses. Prior to the second task, the subjects encountered the first manipulation: the computer froze for 20 seconds. During that time, no keyboard or mouse input was possible. After the freeze, the subjects were able to continue working (see section Manipulation). As the subjects worked on tasks three to seven, they encountered the same computer freeze manipulation before each task.

During all seven tasks, SCR was measured shortly after the manipulation and the working time and the task results were captured as a proxy for task performance. After the subjects accomplished each task, the CSS method was used to capture the psychological user responses of the subjects (see the Data Collection Techniques section).

In the post-experimental stage, the subjects filling out the second survey, which contained several control variables (see section Data Collection Techniques).

The chi-square diference test is implemented by calculating the significance level for the diference in model chi-square values and the degrees of freedom for a pair of nested models (see the Online Supplemental Appendix, Table A 9).

## Control Variables

We also tested whether inter-individual diferences exist that afect arousal, exhaustion, and task performance. ## Discussion, Contributions, and Limitations

The paper’s fundamental goal is to provide a theory of changes in user responses over time in terms of arousal, exhaustion and task performance when users encounter repeated IT interruption. Past literature indicates that single IT interruptions influence user responses [2, 60, 79, 80]. However, the consequences of encountering multiple repetitions of the same manner of IT interruption, which we refer to as repeated IT interruption, for users vary substantially, and user responses depend on whether they encounter the interruption for the first time or have encountered it repeatedly, that is, their responses change with each repetition. We thus extend existing literature that concentrates only on a single IT interruption by taking a longitudinal view of repeated IT interruption. We consider physiological arousal, psychological exhaustion, and behavioral task performance collectively and simultaneously, allowing us to get insights into conscious and unconscious responses and their time-dependent efects on task performance. Based on DPT [37], we posit that user responses follow habituation or sensitization patterns and their time-dependent dependencies. Our findings reveal that users become physiologically habituated to repeated IT interruption, which leads to decreased arousal, and that they become psychologically sensitized, which leads to reinforcement of exhaustion. Task performance also recuperates over time, which suggests habituation towards repeated IT interruption. Our results also reveal that users with higher initial arousal also have a worse initial performance level, and users with lower initial arousal levels habituate faster than users with a high initial arousal level over the encounter with a repeated IT interruption. The findings demonstrate that users with a steeper upward trajectory of exhaustion have a more substantial deterioration of task performance.

The positive relationship between repeated IT interruptions, which are evaluated as hindering by users [51], and recuperating task performance might be perceived as counterintuitive. Even though IT interruptions are repeatedly encountered numerous times, they cost cognitive attention and interfere with current activities.<sup>3</sup> Regarding task performance, users get used to the IT interruption and can pay less attention to it, enabling them to allocate more resources to the task. Hence, performance recuperates with each repetition. Without a repeated IT interruption encounter, users might show even an overall higher task performance as their attention is not disrupted at all. In addition, our results demonstrate that especially the psychological response in terms of exhaustion increases over the encounter of the repeated IT interruption. Contrary to our hypotheses, our findings indicate that users with a higher decreasing trajectory of arousal do not experience a stronger recuperation in task performance than users with a less steep downward trajectory of arousal (H4b). One explanation might be the constrained time of the experiment: the speed of habituation arousal may have been too slow and hence had not yet afected task performance, but a longer-term experiment may reveal such an efect. Our results also fail to support H5a, which hypothesizes that users with higher initial exhaustion will experience worse initial task performance than users with lower initial exhaustion. This might be because the efect on task performance occurs only and specifically due to the exhaustion associated with the IT interruption and not with exhaustion from another source. Our results have theoretical, practical and methodological contributions, which we will discuss below.

## Theoretical Contributions

The present study contributes to theory by examining repeated IT interruptions, three types of user responses, their development over the repeated encounters of an IT interruption, and the time-dependent efects between these three types of user response.

Most research on IT interruption focuses on the structure and content of single IT interruptions and neglects the specific efects of repeated IT interruption. These investigations reveal that encountering IT interruptions leads to an increase in physiological [34, 79] and psychological [2, 80] user responses and a decrease in behavioral user responses, such as task performance [2, 80]. We complement these insights by demonstrating that the various user responses resulting from the encounter of repeated IT interruption follow diferent patterns over time. Thereby, this paper reveals that not only the structure and content of a single IT interruption are important [1, 2], but it is also relevant whether the IT interruption recurs. The paper advances this knowledge by developing theory that explains changes in user responses following repeated IT interruption over time and by testing the theory in an empirical study. We thereby contribute by showing that it is necessary to diferentiate between a single IT interruption encountered for the first time and repeated IT interruption encountered more than once in the same manner.

Second, the paper maps the simultaneous occurrence of three user responses and their time-dependent trajectories. Arousal decreases as users become habituated to a repeated IT interruption. We extend prior research by showing how users become habituated to a repeated IT interruption. In our experiment, repeated encounters with the same IT interruption enable arousal to be measured over six similar IT interruptions. While previous research [34, 79] demonstrates that users’ arousal increases in response to highdemanding IT interruptions, we complement this by providing insights into the intraindividual changes of arousal. We align with these findings by showing that arousal is increased by the first encounter of the IT interruption and extend them by empirically demonstrating that arousal follows a habituation pattern decreasing with each encounter. Despite the focus on disruptions, the results could also be beneficial for distraction research. For instance, Anderson et al. [4] showed reduced arousal over time (in this case, measured through eye-tracking) when encountering repeated security warnings, which can be classified as an IT interruption caused by distractions (intrusion informational interruptions, see [1]). We demonstrate that the IT interruptions caused by a disruption in terms of an intrusion system interruption (e.g., computer breakdown) lead to comparable user responses.

The paper demonstrates that users sensitize in terms of exhaustion. The examination enhances prior work that focused on exhaustion [21, 79, 80], by theoretically and empirically demonstrating the sensitized nature of exhaustion, shown by an increase in exhaustion due to the repeated encounter with an IT interruption. Previous literature demonstrates that a single encounter with an IT interruption leads to tiredness and fatigue [79, 80]. We extend this finding by showing that the perception of exhaustion increases significantly with each repetition of the IT interruption. Hence, the experience of a repeated IT interruption leads to greater vulnerability in terms of tiredness or depletion, such that this form of user response increases with each encounter.

Task performance recuperates as users habituate to a repeated IT interruption. Literature shows that a single encounter with an IT interruption leads either directly or indirectly to task performance deterioration [2, 60]. We add to this by revealing that the deteriorated task performance recuperates with each repeated IT interruption encountered. In other words, users become habituated to the repeated IT interruption, such that their task performance is less and less below the base level with each repeated encounter. This paper provides an evidence-based contribution to the discussion of how task performance develops over time. Research concentrating on IT interruptions and task performance should not only investigate an IT interruption’s unique occurrence, its frequencies, or structure [1, 2]. Instead, research should also consider the encounter of repeated IT interruption, since task performance changes over time with each encounter. Our findings also underline the importance of controlling for previously encountered IT interruptions of a similar type when comparing and explaining the efect of an IT interruption across users.

Third, task performance is influenced by the time-dependent evaluation of arousal and exhaustion. We extend extant literature by theoretically and empirically demonstrating that arousal and exhaustion’s time-dependent efect influences changes in task performance. For example, Tams et al. [79] show that physiological and psychological user responses impair task performance. Our results reveal that users with higher initial arousal will experience worse initial task performance. Regarding exhaustion, our results show that users with a higher increasing trajectory of exhaustion will experience deterioration in task performance. In other words, the present paper extends prior research by showing that the efect of physiological and psychological user response on task performance are time dependent. Hence, these dynamics should be considered when investigating user response in a repeated IT interruption context.

Fourth, existing research on single IT interruptions (i.e., IT events) has shown that these lead to the switch from an automatic to an adaptive IS use pattern [60]. Automatic IS use patterns are characterized by low physiological and psychological user responses, whereas adaptive IS use patterns are characterized by high physiological and psychological user responses [60]. We contribute to this literature by providing empirical confirmation that physiological arousal and psychological exhaustion change when encountering a repeated IT interruption. Our results question how users who switch to an adaptive IS use pattern due to a single IT interruption can switch back to an automatic pattern when psychological and physiological responses follow a diferent pattern over time.

Fifth, the discussion of these results sheds light on whether physiological and psychological measures are complements or alternatives. We contribute to existing knowledge by revealing that physiological and psychological user responses to a repeated IT interruption develop in the opposite direction over time. In particular, this study thereby extends the research concentrating on psychological and physiological measures, such as the work of Tams et al. [79], who demonstrate that physiological measures are complements to psychological ones, rather than alternatives. The importance of psychological and physiological measures as complements is amplified by the present research in that we theoretically and empirically show that these measures can develop diferently. We hence propose to treat psychological and physiological user responses as theoretically distinct constructs.

## Practical Contribution

Organizations should consider human health resources such as well-being and how their employees perform in their firm. IT interruptions are one cause of reduced well-being and task performance. Some insights about single IT interruptions are known. However, in practice, IT interruptions do not always occur just once, but rather often several times in a row. Hence, we help organizations to understand the importance of repeated IT interruptions because they are not simply the concatenation of several single IT interruptions. Users’ responses develop diferently when they encounter a repeated IT interruption. Organizations need to recognize that these negative consequences of single IT interruptions develop diferently when the same IT interruption is encountered repeatedly. While IT interruptions are negatively associated with task performance, the present paper helps organizations understand that their employees can get used to repeated IT interruption such that their task performance does not deteriorate with each IT interruption. Instead, task performance can recuperate without external support. Understanding task performance regarding repeated IT interruptions and the related timedependent antecedents is the first step for developing policies and support structures to react quickly and evaluate incident data diagnostically, especially concerning repeated IT interruptions.

The depletion of emotional resources was recognized as a practical organizational issue a long time ago. Exhaustion can lead to severe illnesses (e.g., burnout) and increase employees intention to quit their job. IT interruptions are one antecedent of exhaustion and lead to the fact that users are tense and fatigued from using IS, forcing them to stop using the IT. Our findings show that users get sensitized such that exhaustion is intensified with each encounter of the repeated IT interruption. These results help organizations to organize their support structure, for example, by developing quick processes to find a solution to IT interruptions. Organizations should learn to stop the repetition of an IT interruption (e.g., computer breakdown) by instituting reliable and fast support processes to prevent users’ exhaustion from intensifying and to prevent them from developing intentions to stop using the IT or even quit their job.

The health and well-being of employees is a significant organizational challenge. Organizations need to understand what physical demands their employees are exposed to. Distinguishing between single and repeated IT interruptions reveals that managing repeated IT interruptions is not trivial because the negative user responses develop diferently and demand diferent countermeasures. Organizations can learn from our findings that the physiological response of employees encountering a repeated IT interruption habituate, such that the response to each encounter with an IT interruption is reduced with each repetition without external support. Moreover, one strategy of managing single IT interruptions is to deal with them in batches and thus limit the switching costs between the tasks and the single IT interruptions [1]. Based on our findings, organizations can learn that such strategies that might be efective against single IT interruptions might not be efective in managing repeated IT interruptions. More rapid strategies are needed to prevent the sensitization of exhaustion. One countermeasure is to provide emotional support, rather than instrumental support, and consider gender [97], age, and self-eficacy because determining optimal support depends on these individual diferences [93].

Our finding can guide users themselves in learning how to cope with IT interruptions. Our results show that the initial level of arousal reduces the initial level of task performance, and exhaustion reduces task performance over time. To increase short-term task performance, users can focus on reducing their physiological user responses by regulating their body responses in the short term to increase task performance, but over time they might try to focus on reducing exhaustion, by for example seeking support or aiming to stop encountering the repeated interruption.

## Limitations and Future Research

The present investigation is limited in several ways. Following the lead of extant literature [28], in our experiment, the IT interruption was repeated six times to allow the subjects to habituate and sensitize to the repeated IT interruption. User responses might develop diferently if the IT interruption is repeated more often or with longer intervals between the interruptions. Encountering six repeated IT interruptions gives few insights into the long-term efects of habituation and sensitization and whether user responses will be stable as shown or develop diferently over days, weeks or months. If the intervals between the IT interruption are greater, we expect, based on CRT, that exhaustion will increase more slowly or even follow a habituation pattern because emotional resources can be replenished between the repetitions and the intensification of exhaustion is hindered. Hence, future research might investigate the efects of more repeated encounters with an IT interruption and the long-term efects of habituation and sensitization. In addition, our laboratory experiment created a controlled environment that enabled concentration on user responses to one specific IT interruption, excluding or controlling the influence of other factors and manipulating the situation to maximize our ability to attribute causality. However, recent research [89] notes that habituation, in particular, is related to the frequency of stimuli received, such that users might show diferent patterns of habituation within a short laboratory experiment (30 minutes on average) than they would show over a longer period, such as a day, several days, or several weeks, or without a seemingly endless number of repetitions.

The pattern of sensitization has been observed by repeated strong positive and negative stimuli. In both cases, an increase in responses over time is the result [35]. The present paper focuses on negative responses and stimulus in terms of exhaustion and a repeated IT interruption. Future research should also consider positive responses. Also, despite the fact that we measured the exhaustion regarding IS usage (see the Online Supplemental Appendix, Table A 8) and that technology and work exhaustion are distinct constructs [50], the overall user exhaustion might also be influenced by the nature of the work or the repeated tasks.

Regarding the habituation pattern of task performance, our results are limited by the fact that the recuperation of task performance might not result from habituation alone. Rather, recuperation might also be a function of associative learning how to accomplish the tasks more quickly. Encountering a repeated IT interruption follows a habituation pattern, but users not disrupted by a repeated IT interruption might demonstrate better performance because they can pay more attention to the task. In contrast, users disrupted by changing IT interruptions would show a decline in task performance over time because the changing IT interruption takes up more process memory, resulting in poorer task performance.

Literature on IT interruptions has identified diferent types of interruptions (e.g., distraction vs. disruption). For example, Addas and Pinsonneault [1] introduce a taxonomy including five diferent interruptions. As we focused on an IT interruption caused by disruption (e.g., system intrusion computer breakdown), it is unclear whether and how users would show diferent habituation and sensitization patterns when encountering repeated IT interruption of a diferent type. Future research might, therefore, concentrate on other types of IT interruptions. Nevertheless, the present results might also be beneficial for distraction research. Distracting IT interruptions in the form of security messages are perceived as positive because they might notify users about a virus or other threat [5, 89]. The psychological and behavior user responses are mostly neglected in the distraction context. As a result, future security research might investigate the psychological response pattern toward repeated static security messages as it might follow a sensitize pattern.

## Conclusion

As users repeatedly encounter IT interruptions, it is critical to understand how user responses change over time in response to these repeated encounters. This paper developed a longitudinal research model investigating the trajectories of the user responses of arousal, exhaustion and task performance. Users habituated in terms of decreases in arousal, sensitized in terms of increases in exhaustion, and habituated in terms of faster recuperation of task performance. Moreover, we demonstrate how arousal and exhaustion influence task performance, concluding that users show habituation and sensitization efects.

[4] distinguishes the construct of habituation from the construct of habit, defining IS habit as “the tendency to use an IT automatically as a result of learning in the past” [40, p. 1219] and habituation as a “response decrement that results from repeated stimulation” [65, p. 136].
