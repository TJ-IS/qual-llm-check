# Efects of Online Crowds on Self-Disclosure Behaviors in Online Reviews: A Multidimensional Examination

HanByeol Stella Choi <sup>a</sup>, Wonseok Oh <sup>a</sup>, Chanhee Kwak<sup>b</sup>, Junyeong Lee<sup>c</sup>, and Heeseok Lee<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>College of Business, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, Seoul, Korea; <sup>b</sup>Department of Data Science, Kangnam University, Yongin, Korea; <sup>c</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, Chungbuk National University, Cheongju, Chungbuk, Korea

## ABSTRACT

In online environments (i.e., product review sites in our case), consumers are increasingly interacting and socializing with many “strangers” (i.e., online crowds) as well as sharing personal and product information. Drawing from social norms theory, we examine how the multiple aspects of online crowds afect their self-disclosure behaviors as they provide online reviews and investigate the extent to which prior experience moderates this relationship. Our analysis of data from a leading apparel rental site in the United States uncovers that individuals are inclined to conform to the self-disclosure behaviors of a crowd and divulge more personal information as self-disclosure variance within the group increases. Conversely, individuals are more likely to conceal personal information as a review page becomes crowded. The findings reveal that a reviewer’s prior experience of writing a review on the website weakens conformity behavior and reduces the efects of crowdedness. The prior experience also positively interacts with self-disclosure variance in a crowd. Based on these results, we present theoretical implications to literature on social norms and privacy, prior experience, and online reviews. This study also has managerial implications for firms interested in content generation by online reviewers and in review systems where usergenerated content is essential.

## KEYWORDS

Online crowds; social norms; online self-disclosure; online reviews; fixed-efect twostage least squares models

## Introduction

Online reviews are important sources of consumer information [13, 52]. Previous studies revolving around these evaluations investigate how peer reviews influence purchase-related decisions through product information [70, 105]. Recently, online review pages have been considered communities where reviewers share information and interact socially with one another [26, 85]. As social beings, people easily influence one another, and the prevalent behaviors within a group—called social norms—considerably afect the behavioral decisions of individuals [18, 29]. Social norms are efective avenues through which to change review generation processes and behaviors [18, 25]. A case in point is herding propensity, which is a salient driver of online product ratings [66, 70].

As online reviews proliferate and become increasingly common, the content included in the reviews have diversified. Reviewers not only share their product experiences but also disclose private personal information, such as their body sizes or ages. This behavior is referred to as self-disclosure [59]. This self-revealing behavior may help increase product sales because online assessments that include a reviewer’s personal information are considered more trustworthy than those without such information [40]. Intimate details in reviews project credibility of the review [116], serving as an important ground for the adoption of online review messages [12, 83] and decision making on purchase. A reviewer’s disclosure of identity both supplements and replaces product information when a prospective buyer makes a purchase decision [40]. Popular websites, such as Amazon. com or TripAdvisor.com, value the personal information disclosed by reviewers and create detailed profile pages for this purpose. Individuals determine their privacy-related behaviors, such as self-disclosure based on contextual factors [2, 58, 76]. When personal information is shared in the process of communication, social context is vital in understanding self-disclosure [4].

Online review pages are platforms where a quantity of people with little or no social relationship—a so-called “online crowd”—gather and interact. Individuals can sense the online presence of others by observing traces of their activities, such as uploaded pictures or text-based content [6, 88, 114]. An online crowd can influence individuals even though they are absent in real time because the assessments posted on a review page remain observable.

Considering the essentiality of self-disclosure in the social context of online reviews, an important task is to investigate the antecedents of a reviewer’s self-disclosure behavior. Drawing from social norms theory, we examine the efects of online crowds on reviewers subsequent divulgence of information. Prior studies on social norms have clarified the efect of social norms such as the size of a group as well as levels and variances of group member’s behaviors on individual’s corresponding behavior [64, 71]. Studies on individual’s selfdisclosure found that an individuals’ self-disclosure behavior is often determined by social factors. Combined with these studies, social norms theory provide evidence that social norms aspects can be related to self-disclosure behavior. We therefore investigate the impact of multidimensional aspects of social norms on a reviewer’s information disclosure behavior. This study explores the efect of group size and the variance in self-disclosure of online crowd, which have not been investigated in prior studies regarding online reviewer’s information divulgence behavior. Additionally, theories on social influence maintain that individuals rely upon prior experience when processing information [75], with such experience altering susceptibility to social information [34, 112]. This phenomenon applies to the usage of the Internet, wherein an individual’s previous experience plays an essential role in modifying the use of information technologies and websites [100]. Accordingly, we employ prior experience as a moderator of the relationship between online crowding and self-disclosure.

We inquire into the efects of an online crowd on reviewers’ self-disclosure using field data from a leading apparel rental site in the United States. The website ofers each reviewer space for disclosing up to 10 pieces of information, along with the voluntary option to disclose information, such as nickname, size worn, and overall clothing fit. On the site, selfdisclosure level is measured by counting the number of information disclosed. In this study, the self-disclosure behaviors of an online crowd are determined based on the average and variance of self-disclosure level in previously posted reviews, and crowdedness is operationalized via the number of preexisting reviews on a review page. Our results show that each aspect of an online crowd relates diferently to the degree of self-disclosure in reviews. The average and variance in self-disclosure levels in previous reviews have a positive influence on such divulgence, whereas crowdedness has a negative impact. We also discover that prior experience moderates the efects of an online crowd. More experienced users are less likely to demonstrate herding behavior and are less afected by the crowdedness of a review page. With respect to the efects of variance on self-disclosure levels, prior experience has a significant, positive moderating role.

Focusing on the social impact of an online crowd on self-disclosure, this study extends the previous work on social norms and privacy by providing a holistic view of the relationship between social norms and a reviewer’s propensity to self-disclose through multiple dimensions of social norms. It also enriches the literature by employing prior experience as a moderator of the association between online crowding and self-disclosure. Furthermore, the study uncovers managerial insights into encouraging content generation or enhancing online review systems.

## Literature Review

Online reviews are essential for communication [26]. Previous studies have examined how prior reviews afect subsequent review-generating behaviors. As product reviews are collated on a specific product page, reviews within each page form a type of “group,” and group phenomena, such as bandwagon behavior, observational learning, or herding, are detected. Moe and Schweidel [73] found that review-writing behavior difers depending on how active a reviewer is. Less frequent reviewers tend to imitate prior reviewers, which demonstrates “bandwagon behavior,” while most active reviewers try to diferentiate themselves by writing negative opinions. Grounded in the observational learning and informational cascade theories, Lee et al. [66] illustrated herding behavior in review rating and Zhao et al. [117] showed herding behavior in software adoption. Observational learning suggests that following previous behaviors can be rational [86], while the informational cascades theory posits that in some circumstances, individuals make the same choice as others, independent of their own judgment [16]. Lee et al. [66] revealed that a subsequent reviewer follows the previous crowd, which demonstrates herding behavior. The social influence among reviewers is evidenced in the literature. Schlosser [87] found that reviewers adjust their reviews after being exposed to biased reviews, while Liu [68] and Duan et al. [36] revealed that previous reviews afect the number of subsequent reviews.

Prior studies have commonly shown that a subsequent reviewer’s rating and content generation depend on the behaviors of the reference group observed in online review pages. Online reviewers are influenced by preceding review behaviors, the so-called social norms [18, 25]. Specifically, the context of the review content such as review valence, review length, review rating, or review volume at an aggregated level, corresponds with social influence.

## Social Norms

Social norms are shared behaviors that are prevalent within a group [18]. Social norms provide evidence about socially accepted behaviors. In a given situation, individuals perceive the behavior of the majority as accurate [43]. In their seminal work, Deutsch and Gerard [34] indicated that social influence, particularly informational social influence, provides an important basis for decision making. Informational social influence refers to an individual’s tendency to conform to the opinions of others, accepting their information as evidence [34]. Under this influence, social norms become information referred to by an individual. This influence is apparent in the interaction among group members, even if they share little social relationship [34] and few expectations of a specific behavior exist. Informational social influence is based on the group phenomenon observed in the context of online reviews, such as the bandwagon efect, herding, or observational learning [10, 62]. When online users with little social relationship—as the case is with online reviews—interact, they leverage social norms as an informational anchor.

Examples of social norms are found in online dating [37] and social network [104]. Several studies have leveraged the efect of social norms in promoting online review generation. In an experiment involving a movie-reviewing website, Chen et al. [25] found that providing social behavioral information to reviewers afects the rate of newly generated reviews. Research by Burtch et al. [18] showed that social norms lead potential reviewers to increase the volume and length of their reviews. In summary, social norms in the online review context afect review-writing behaviors.

## Role of Social Norms in Self-Disclosure

Self-disclosure is a fundamental behavior when initiating a variety of relationships [32, 94], from interpersonal relationships with friends and partners to organizational relationships with employers and organizations. An individual may voluntarily disclose personal information to another person, group of people, or the public [110]. As technological advancements broaden communication, self-disclosure occurs in IT-enabled contexts such as social network [2, 113], online communities [82], online chat rooms [57], and online reviews.

Self-disclosure plays a critical role in determining the value of online reviews. The reliability of communication messages was found to be afected by their source and other relevant information [23]. A review with rich personal details is perceived as a reliable source of information [21]. Reviews are viewed as being more helpful or acceptable when information about one’s identity is presented with the review content [40, 42]. When reviewers are prior purchasers, self-disclosed information in a review may act as customized information that enhances the relevance of the review and may generate empathy for the reviewer, which increases credibility and promotes purchase intention [116]. Useful reviews improve social presence [63], afect purchase decisions [67, 115], and lead firms to make a profit. Review page owners need to encourage reviewers to disclose more personal information.

Realizing the growing importance of self-disclosure, scholars have investigated its determinants. Privacy-related behaviors, such as self-disclosure, are made within contextual factors [33]. In an online context, the risk of self-disclosure is dificult to evaluate. Individuals are uncertain about their preferences and often rely on environmental cues for guidance [91]. Because self-disclosure is a communicational behavior, social factors are important when seeking its determinants [11]. Self-disclosing subjects cast social norms as contextual cues for their behavioral decisions [24, 93], and the existence of others afects their psychological state [71]. To reduce uncertainty, individuals may employ social norms demonstrated by the self-disclosing behavior of others, although they are total strangers [52, 66] and endeavor to minimize risks by gathering as much information as possible [107].

Previous studies provide evidence that social norms afect disclosure behavior, and the association can be contingent upon individual factors. Acquisti et al. [4] showed that disclosure is afected by others’ readiness to divulge. Barasch and Berger [11] found that the audience of social network sites provides social normative pressure that results in diferences in the amount of shared information. The relationship is found to be moderated by reviewers’ self-focus, formed through personal experience. Forman et al. [40] revealed that previous reviews influence the type of disclosed identity-related information. The behavior of others was found to afect self-disclosure by alleviating privacy concerns [2, 96] since disclosure provides a perception that shared information becomes a public good [79]. This phenomenon indicates that the efect of social norms on self-disclosure varies according to privacy concerns, which depends on prior experiences [2]. In addition, theories on social norms suggest that an Internet user’s susceptibility to the behavior of others is bound by prior experience [34, 112]. For example, one’s prior experience of specific website moderates the impact of individual factors [100]. Combining the findings from the literature on self-disclosure and social norms, it can be inferred that the impact of an online crowd’s social norms on self-disclosure varies depending on a reviewer’s prior experience.

Previous studies imply social norm can be related to self-disclosure. However, their findings show limited impact of social norms, missing multidimensional factors of social norms such as group size, average and variance in the level of self-disclosure. In addition, related studies provide mixed implication and the contingent efects of social norms on selfdisclosure are limited [2, 11], and exploring the moderating factor is important. Hence, using field data, this study investigates how multiple aspects of an online crowd’s social norms simultaneously afect self-disclosure, and examines the moderating efect of prior experience on the divergent impact of each aspect.

## Hypotheses Development

With the proliferation of online reviews, review generation behaviors are increasingly afected by unknown strangers or the crowd [66]. We call this online crowd, which is defined as the people who gather in a specific online environment for some purposes. In our study, this is represented by the online review page of a particular product. An online crowd includes strangers who share their experiences of the same product, and this crowd serves as a reference group for subsequent review generation. Online reviews are appropriate for investigating the role of an online crowd, where social interactions are easily detected [66].

## Efects of an Online Crowd’s Self-Disclosure Level on Reviewers’ Self-Disclosure

The social norms and informational social influence theories together posit that individuals view others’ behaviors as being more reliable than their own [62]. The behavior of the majority is considered appropriate and is used as a reference, resulting in conformity [28, 29, 62]. Conformity to social norms is prominent when uncertainty exists [7, 90]. To alleviate potential risks in online review pages, where gathering of unknown others and the absence of clear standards for behaviors may display high uncertainty, reviewing behaviors depend on the prevalent behaviors of others. The higher the self-disclosure level of the surrounding group, the lower is the perceived risk of disclosing private information [4, 35], resulting in conformity in self-disclosure [28]. Forman et al. [40] studied identity-related disclosures and noted herding behavior by demonstrating that a reviewer’s disclosure follows prior reviewers’ information disclosed on online reviews. These findings imply that self-disclosure behavior depends on social norms such as the prevailing self-disclosure level of prior reviewers within the same product review page. Hence, we propose:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): The higher the self-disclosure level of an online crowd, the more likely reviewers will be to disclose their personal information in subsequent reviews.

With the self-disclosure level of others, behavioral variance may also afect disclosure. Social norms represent the shared behavior of individuals [18, 29], and behavioral variance indicates the strength of a social norm [38]. A high variance in behavior suggests that a group has weak social norms. Given that norms are predictors of attitude [101], individual attitude can be poorly defined under high variance. In such a situation, the salient behaviors of a few people, rather than the lack of commonly accepted behaviors, strongly influence behavioral decision-making [22]. Individuals are likely to accept informational social influence if the influencing factor shows environmental salience [17]. Reviewers focus on salient information sources—reviews that have more personal information—if the variance in the self-disclosure level of an online crowd is high [10]. Reviewers are likely to refer to earlier reviewers who disclosed more personal information, as their reviews are considered more credible [40]. Despite the potential risk in self-disclosure, a subsequent reviewer’s concern can be mitigated, leading their disclosure to be determined by those credible reviewers.

The variance in social norms may have the opposite efect from the viewpoint of perceived uncertainty. Social norms are used as an uncertainty reduction strategy for determining individual behavior [1], and behavioral norms enhance information sharing among group members [20]. A high variance in behaviors is related to perceived uncertainty toward behavioral consequences and protection of personal information [3]. Since self-disclosure in reviews indicates information sharing and intimacy [65, 74], its variance increases perceived uncertainty and decreases satisfaction with other reviewers, which results in individual choices to disclose less personal information. Since a high variance of review ratings signals a high mismatch between the focal product and customers’ needs [97], individuals exhibit conservative behavior by excluding the product from further consideration [55]. Since a high variance of previous self-disclosures increases these concerns, potential reviewers are likely to behave conservatively; therefore, they are less likely to disclose personal details. Considering these arguments, variance in online crowd’s selfdisclosure can have both positive and negative impact on a reviewer’s self-disclosure. We thus propose the following competing hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2A (H2A): Variance in the level of self-disclosure of an online crowd has a positive efect on a subsequent reviewer’s level of disclosure.

Hypothesis 2B (H2B): Variance in the level of self-disclosure of an online crowd has a negative efect on a subsequent reviewer’s level of disclosure.

## Role of Crowdedness on Reviewers’ Self-Disclosure

The mere presence of others afects individual decision-making [64]. For group communication, the number of people communicating is important [11]. The literature on crowds has focused on the number of people in the crowd, often called crowdedness. In analyzing the efect of an online crowd’s social norms on subsequent self-disclosure, crowdedness can be another critical determinant. Given that an online crowd refers to the group of people on a review page—represented by preexisting reviews—we define crowdedness as the number of preexisting reviews on a review page [89].

Prior reviews ofer subsequent reviewers a sense of the presence of others. Reviewers perceive the page as crowded when they find many reviews. Crowded situations may cause stress and psychological discomfort [54], which often lead to hostility [45]. Crowding is closely related to risk perception and sense of control. As the environment becomes crowded, individuals tend to be risk-averse [71] and lose their sense of control [31]. Correspondingly, they begin to sense higher risk [8, 47], which results in less selfdisclosure propensity [76, 82]. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): The more crowded a review page becomes, the less likely people will be to disclose their personal information in subsequent reviews.

## Moderating Role of Prior Experience

According to the social norms and informational social influence theories, prior experience moderates the efect of social influence. Social influence has a pronounced efect when prior experience is ambiguous [34, 112]. Experienced users are more likely to use their internal information sources than do inexperienced counterparts who tend to prefer external sources [75]. Prior experience can moderate the outcome where online crowds influence individuals and reduce uncertainty, since experienced reviewers know more about the complexity of a particular product and have a better understanding of their expectations. Previous research supports this mechanism. For example, Klein and Ford [61] found that online experiences negatively moderate the credibility that consumers assign to diferent information sources. Since past behavioral experience can shape beliefs and attitudes [56, 111], prior experience has been considered as a moderator in the context of IT and website usage [100]. The elaboration likelihood model (ELM) suggests that attitude changes may be attributed to two routes of influence—the central route and the peripheral route—that are distinguished by the amount of information processing or “elaboration” demanded from the individual subject [14, 80]. In particular, individual characteristics determine the route of information processing [81] and moderate the relationship of individuals’ behaviors [48, 99]. Prior experience plays a moderating role between the average rating and subsequent rating in online reviews [70]. Applying the concept of social influence and ELM to our context, an online crowd exerts informational influence, and we expect its efects to vary depending on prior experience.

The role of prior experience has also been found in the privacy domain. The perception of privacy risk varies according to prior experience [27]. Individuals with higher online knowledge are more likely to downplay personal risk [9] and perceive less privacy risk. A survey in the United States showed that experienced respondents with strong online skills have less fear about disclosing information because they believe their knowledge helps them avoid organizations that harvest personal information [103]. The risk perception of privacyrelated judgment changes as an individual accumulates experience, resulting in diferences in privacy behavior such as the level of self-disclosure. The more experienced an individual is regarding disclosure, the less an individual perceives uncertainty in his/her privacy behavior decision. This study employs prior experience as a reviewer’s characteristic that moderates the relationship between an online crowd and self-disclosure.

## Efect of Prior Experience on the Relationship between an Online Crowd’s Self-Disclosure Behavior and a Subsequent Reviewer’s Self-Disclosure

Studies have suggested that domain knowledge determines the degree of influence of others. Less experienced users of the rating system face higher uncertainty since they do not possess much domain knowledge. Given that individuals with prior experience have their own judgment [106], their tendency to follow the salient behavior of others will likely be diminished. Conversely, individuals with little prior experience have low confidence in their abilities to judge and thus seek advice from others who are perceived to be knowledgeable [17, 41]. From the perspective of ELM, individuals with more experience can scrutinize and critically deliberate on information processing through a central route [60], and lead to lower dependence on peripheral information or heuristics.

As articulated in earlier arguments regarding the role of prior experience, savvy Internet users are less likely to rely on online reviews, while novices are readily influenced by experienced users [34, 112, 118]. Users with previous online review experience face less uncertainty and are less likely to conform to prior users’ behaviors. Earlier studies of IT usage have confirmed that contextual influence is stronger on the behavioral intention of users without prior experience [49, 100]. In a similar vein, the efects of social norms on IT use may decrease as experience accumulates [102]. Applying ELM approach, since an online crowd is a source of peripheral information about the environment and guides one’s judgment about the potential risk of self-disclosure, the efects would be weakened under a central route of information processing. Reviewers who centrally deliberate are unlikely to be afected by prior reviews [70]. Taken together, experienced reviewers can reduce uncertainties because they can judge review credibility through domain knowledge and have comparative optimism about their own privacy risk; thus, they are less likely to be influenced by others when disclosing personal information. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4 (H4): As reviewers gain more experience, the positive efect of an online crowd’s selfdisclosure level on reviewer’s self-disclosure is weakened.

Hypothesis 5A (H5A): The positive impact of an online crowd’s self-disclosure variance on the level of a subsequent reviewer’s self-disclosure decreases among experienced reviewers.

Hypothesis 5B (H5B): The negative impact of an online crowd’s self-disclosure variance on the level of a subsequent reviewer’s self-disclosure decreases among experienced reviewers.

## Efect of Prior Experience on the Relationship between Crowdedness and a Subsequent Reviewer’s Self-Disclosure

Past experience is essential in behavioral control [100], which facilitates a sense of control [51]. Internet users have a higher sense of control as they gain cumulative experience in using a particular website [108, 109]. Conversely, individuals who have a lower sense of control tend to rely on external sources and become susceptible to social influence [39]. The sense of control and vulnerability to social norms are inversely related, implying that experienced individuals with higher sense of control are less likely to be afected by social norms. For one, the efect of social crowding depends on perceived controllability [44] and enhanced sense of control diminishes the negative outcome induced by social density [30]. Individuals become less sensitive to crowdedness when they gain more experience and thereby obtain a higher sense of control. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 6 (H6): The adverse efect of crowdedness on self-disclosure diminishes among experienced reviewers.

## Research Context

This study primarily examines how online crowds afect reviewers’ self-disclosure behavior. We use a website that enables potential reviewers to select their information disclosure level and easily find preexisting reviews and disclosed information. This website is owned and operated by the largest online apparel rental site in the United States. Established in November 2009, the company has more than 8 million customers and revenues worth \$100 million in 2020 [19]. The company provides rental services, primarily for designer dresses. More reviews are submitted and posted on designer dresses than daily apparel. Since designer dresses are relatively free from seasonal changes or individual preferences as compared with daily apparel, the review generation motives may be less bound to product characteristics. We therefore select review data on designer dresses only. Given that clothing products are experience goods whose use requires actual body sizes, individual consumers tend to rely on reviews that include previous users’ physical information (e.g., height and weight) when sharing rental experiences, such as size worn or dress fit.

## Data Description

We collected review data posted from August 2010 to September 2017. We gathered the data using Python to access and parse HTML and JSON pages on products and reviews. Our sample consists of 169,873 reviews of 3,445 designer dresses. Our dataset is comprised of the attributes of each review, including a reviewer’s nickname, physical information observable to the public, registration information (i.e., the date a user registered and the number of reviews she posted), and the review title, content, date, star-point rating, and photo. In addition, our dataset contains information on each product, such as the brand name, price, and average rating. Reviewers can skip posting any content included in the review box. ## Measures

## Dependent Variable

As shown in Figure 2, each review comprises three separate segments. The first includes the reviewer’s information; the second reveals the title, content, star rating, and upload date; and the third exhibits a reviewer’s photo. In the reviewer information segment, reviewers can disclose up to 10 pieces of information, including nickname, size worn, overall fit of a dress, the occasion for which a dress is rented, and the dress size that a reviewer usually wears, as well as his or her height, age, bust size, body type, and weight. A reviewer can choose to disclose any piece of information among these options. We define the information disclosure level, (SelfDisclose<sub>jr</sub>), as the number of pieces of information disclosed in review r for product j.

## Independent Variables

To determine reviewers’ conformity behavior, we consider the average self-disclosure level of prior reviews, as suggested by Ma et al. [70] and Lee et al. [66] when examining the herding behavior of reviewers in product ratings. We measured the average information disclosure level $( \mathrm { A v g D i s c l o s u r e _ { j r } } )$ based on the average amount of information disclosed on up to the 40 most recent reviews for product j at the time review r was posted. The variance of information disclosure level $( \mathrm { V a r D i s c l o s u r e _ { j r } } )$ is defined as the variance of the amount of information disclosed on up to these 40 most recent reviews of product j at the time review r was posted. We averaged the disclosure level of the prior 40 reviewers’ information on the basis of the bounded rationality theory, which suggests that individuals tend to make decisions on behavioral alternatives even though the outcome is not always globally rational [92]. Because of the trade-of between efort and accuracy, individuals settle for a less satisfactory decision in return for a decrease in the efort that converges to zero [46]. This behavioral pattern is more prominent as the number of alternatives increases [78]. In addition, due to the information cascade in today’s online environment, this phenomenon becomes more salient in the case of online pages, as customers rarely go beyond the first page when searching for information online [105], and there are 40 reviews per page on the website. Consequently, we restricted the number of previous reviews to 40 when measuring the average information disclosure level. Following Shen et al. [89], we measured crowdedness by the number of preexisting reviews for product j at the time review r was posted (Crowdedness<sub>jr</sub>). As the number of preexisting reviews is located at the top of the review page, all reviewers can see this figure when posting reviews. Owing to the high skewness of this variable, we employed the natural log of crowdedness $( \ln \left( { \mathrm { C r o w d e d n e s s } } _ { \mathrm { j r } } \right) )$ ) in the analysis model.

## Moderator

We measured prior experience $( \mathrm { P r i o r E x p } _ { \mathrm { j r } } )$ [70] as the natural log of the accumulated number of reviews posted by a reviewer at the time review r for product j was posted $( \ln \left( { \operatorname* { P r i o r E x p } _ { \mathrm { j r } } } \right) )$ ).

## Control Variables

We considered the factors related to the behavior of online product reviewers as control variables. We collected data that pertain to the characteristics of reviews and reviewers. Review characteristics include the recency $( { \mathrm { R e c e n c y } } _ { \mathrm { i r } } )$ of the latest review [70], measured as the days elapsed since the latest review at the time review r for product j was posted. To avoid confusion due to the inverted nature of the value itself, the final value is an inverted value obtained by subtracting this period from the maximum value and then taking the natural logarithm for analysis $( \ln ( \operatorname { R e c e n c y } _ { \mathrm { j r } } ) )$ . We measured the star rating (Rating ) [70] for review r for $\mathrm { \ p r o d u c t } j ,$ and the natural log of the length of review r for product j $( \mathrm { l n } ( \mathrm { L e n g t h _ { j r } } ) )$ [53, 70] was measured by the number of characters included in a review. Using a text analysis algorithm, we included the positivity $( { \mathrm { P o s i t i v e } } _ { \mathrm { j r } } )$ of review r for product j as a review-specific characteristic variable [53]. $\mathrm { P o s i t i v e _ { j r } }$ is a continuous variable that ranges from −1 to 1; if a variable is greater than 0, a review is positive; otherwise, it is negative. As some users of the website upload photos with their faces visible, we measured whether reviewers disclosed their faces in a review with a face-detection algorithm using OpenCV library and Python. Face is a dummy variable defined as 1 if the reviewer’s face is disclosed in review r for product j and 0 if otherwise. Our data included the date on which the reviewers joined the website. We used the natural log of the membership periods, measured as days elapse since the reviewer joined at the time review r for product j was posted $( \ln ( \mathrm { M e m P e r i o d _ { j r } } ) )$ as a reviewer characteristic.

One of our independent variables, ln Crowdedness<sub>jr</sub> , might be correlated with the error term for self-disclosure on a review. To address this potential endogeneity problem, we introduce an instrumental variable (IV)—the average star rating of product j at the time review r was posted $( \mathrm { A v g S c o r e _ { j r } } )$ . A high average star rating for a product grabs consumers’ attention [97], which results in a larger number of people staying on a product page. However, the average star rating presented on a product feature page can hardly have a direct impact on a reviewer’s self-disclosure decision. In this respect, the correlation matrix shows that the correlation between the average star rating and crowdedness (0.2654) exceeds that between the average star rating and self-disclosure measure (−0.0515). Thus, we believe that the suggested IV constitutes an exogenous source of variation for identifying the efect of crowdedness on the level of self-disclosure. We estimate our model using a panel 2SLS method with the suggested IV. We include the IV in the first stage for equations (1) and (2), respectively. In estimating the panel 2SLS model, we employ the Hausman [50] test to validate the use of a fixed-efects model or a random-efect model. ## Results

Table 3 presents the results of our fixed-efects 2SLS model in which we instrument crowdedness with the average star rating score for the product. Based on the results of the first stage estimation, our IV is shown to have a significant positive efect on the crowdedness or the number of preexisting reviews on a product page. Furthermore, the first stage F-statistics in the models are all significantly above the 1% level and surpass the Stock and Yogo [95] threshold value, suggesting that our IV is not a weak instrument of the endogenous regressor in the model.

The second stage results show the outcomes of our hypotheses. In Model (1), the 2SLS analysis result is shown for the efect of an online crowd on a reviewer’s level of selfdisclosure. Our results indicate that an online crowd has a significant efect on the amount of self-disclosed information in a subsequent review. Consistent with the hypotheses, the average and variance of a crowd’s self-disclosure level have a positive efect, whereas crowd size has a negative impact on self-disclosure.

In Models (2) and (3), we enter ln $\left( P r i o r E x p _ { j r } \right)$ and the interaction term with this variable and each of the variables for an online crowd. The results indicate that a reviewer’s cumulative experience of posting reviews acts as a moderator that weakens the positive efect of the crowd’s average self-disclosure level and the negative efect of crowdedness on the self-disclosure level of subsequent reviews. Additionally, prior experience is found to moderate positively the impact of an online crowd’s variance on self-disclosure.

## Robustness Check

We conduct several analyses to explore further the nature of the relationship between an online crowd and self-disclosure. First, the dependent variable in our main model, SelfDisclose<sub>jr</sub>, is measured by the amount of information, such as a reviewer’s nickname, size worn and overall fit of the rented dress, and the purpose of the rental, as well as a reviewer’s physical information, such as the usual apparel size worn, height, age, bust size, body type, and weight. However, the sensitivity may vary based on the type of information [98]. For example, “information on food preferences is inherently less sensitive than information on sexual preferences” [58 p. 860]. As such, the efects of social factors on the disclosure of diferent types of information can difer. Accordingly, we conduct estimations by limiting our dependent variable to the extent of the physical information disclosed in review r for product j (PhysicalDisclose<sub>jr</sub>).

Third, our data contain information on whether a user’s face is disclosed in a review. According to Qian and Scott [84], a photo, typically combined with other personal information, may be enough to completely identify a subject, whereas content without a photo or a photo containing a blurred face may give limited information. On the one hand, this visual anonymity allows an individual to express his/her true mind and authentic self, forgetting concerns about self-presentation [59]. Reviewers who do not upload photos or disclose their faces in the images may be less afected by social factors when disclosing personal information. On the other hand, whether a face is disclosed may indicate a reviewer’s baseline concern for privacy or sensitivity toward the social environment; reviewers who upload photos with their faces may be less concerned about an online crowd. To capture the efect of face disclosure, we split the sample according to whether a face is disclosed in a review and replicate the estimation on smaller samples.

## Discussion

This study examined the efects of an online crowd on online reviewers’ self-disclosure behaviors by drawing upon studies on social norms. Our empirical investigation reveals that online users are afected by diverse dimensions of an online crowd’s social norms regarding self-disclosure behavior. First, our results show that subsequent reviewers adapt their self-disclosure level to that of a specific online crowd as it is stated in H1and tend to divulge more information when the self-disclosure variance of that online crowd is high showing that H2A is supported. This finding confirms the existence of conformity or herding behavior [34, 62] and the role of social norm variance [38], as suggested by the social norms and informational social influence theories. Second, the results reveal that the subsequent reviewers’ self-disclosure level is relatively low when a review page is relatively more crowded, supporting H3. This finding demonstrates that crowdedness— one other aspect of the social norms of online crowd [64]—induces perceived risk or privacy concern [8, 47], resulting in lower tendency to self-disclose [76, 82]. Third, the results show a significant interaction between an online crowd and a reviewer’s prior experience. As reviewers’ prior experiences grow, their conformity behavior and the efects of crowdedness are weakened, supporting H4 and H6. These findings suggest that prior experience moderates an individual’s susceptibility toward social factors [34, 112].

Although the moderation efect of prior experience showed hypothesized result in terms of conformity behavior (H4) and the efects of crowdedness (H6), the analysis showed reversed result for hypothesis H5A. That is, a reviewer’s prior experience is found to strengthen the positive impact of variance of an online crowd on a subsequent reviewer’s self-disclosure. This diference in moderation efect may be attributed to the diference in the cognitive load required to recognize the variance in the self-disclosure level of a crowd compared with its other aspects. While the average self-disclosure or crowd size can be easily detected, greater cognitive efort is required to recognize the crowd’s self-disclosure level variance, unless the diferences in self-disclosure levels between preexisting reviews are extreme. The distinction in the amount of cognitive efort required to understand the cue might lead to diferences in the salience of each cue and behavioral intentions, which might result in varied efects of prior experience [5, 69]. Evidence for this result can be found in the study of online reviewers’ strategic behaviors in review generation [89]. Online reviewers tend to seek attention from the audience to generate reviews to receive recognition. For example, reviewers try to write reviews that can gain more helpfulness votes. As self-disclosed information enhances review credibility, reviewers with a stronger desire for attention and recognition may provide as much information as possible. Although this study assumed that every reviewer has the same baseline desire to grab attention, it can be inferred that reviewers with greater cumulative experience in writing reviews have a higher baseline desire for attention. Furthermore, according to the argument for H2A, as the variance in prior reviews increases, reviewers increasingly depend on their fundamental drive for online reviews, which leads to attention-grabbing. The aforementioned evidence suggests that prior experience strengthens the positive efect of self-disclosure variance on self-disclosure. The results of the hypotheses are summarized in the Online Supplemental Appendix G.

## Theoretical Implications

Our study ofers theoretical implications for the literature on social norms and privacy, prior experience, and online reviews. First, this study contributes to the literature on social norms and privacy by examining the efects of multiple aspects of social norms on selfdisclosure. Since social norms are critical determinants of privacy behavior, it is important to investigate how self-disclosure can be socially influenced [4, 11, 40]. Although social norms theory implies social norms have multidimensional impact on individual behavior [64, 71] prior studies on privacy behavior are limited in its investigation of the herding or conformity in self-disclosure. To overcome this limitation, we expand the concept of social norms to include multiple aspects. We define the reviewer group that afects online reviewing behavior as the online crowd and identify social norms with three dimensions —the average and variance of the crowd’s self-disclosure level and crowdedness. This study is the first to decipher how an online crowd’s self-disclosure level and size afect subsequent self-disclosure in online reviews in holistic manner by drawing upon the theory of social norms.

Second, we extend the relevance of prior experience in the study of the efect of an online crowd on self-disclosure levels. Our study validates the arguments derived from the social norms theory together with the literature on ELM that a person’s own experience can determine an individual’s susceptibility toward social factors [34, 80, 112]. Our analysis highlights the impact of social norms among inexperienced individuals. Specifically, experienced individuals are found to be more likely to draw on internal sources, whereas inexperienced individuals prefer external sources [14, 75]. By incorporating prior experience as a contingent factor, the result enhances the understanding of the nomological network from social norms to self-disclosure in online reviews. Moreover, with additional analysis on face disclosure, we found further evidence on how privacy behavior can be afected by one’s personal characteristics. The most important aspect of information disclosure is how single information or the combination of information is critical for identifying the individual [77]. Individual’s face is one of the information which has the highest degree of identifiability [72]. Taking account face disclosure as one of the efective ways of attaining identifiability, the behavior can reflect one’s baseline sensitivity in privacy behavior. By considering face disclosure behavior with empirical analysis, this study provides an additional interesting implication in that it revealed privacy behavior according to the degree of individuals’ baseline characteristics as well as the external antecedents. Combining these findings, our theoretical extension also lies in its scrutiny of how the personal characteristics of online users afect interdependence in the conduct associated with privacy. The extension leads to the insight that privacy-related behaviors, such as selfdisclosure, vary across diferent contexts [2].

Third, our study posits that self-disclosure is critical in improving online review systems. Prior studies on social influence in reviews revolve around the rating and review-generating behaviors of subsequent reviewers. The research focus has been on the review content related to product evaluation, such as review length or review valence [66, 70], or the aggregate aspects of review pages such as review volume [18, 36, 68]. The number of reviews is growing and the contents included in reviews are diversified. In this era of information cascade, online reviews become an important decision-making anchor. A reviewer’s personal information disclosure enhances the trustworthiness of online assessments [15, 40]. Our study serves as a stepping stone for further studies concerning online reviews by analyzing antecedents that afect personal information divulgence on a review.

## Managerial Implications

Our study also ofers valuable managerial implications for firms interested in content generation by online reviewers and in review systems where user-generated content is essential. First, our analyses provide practical insights for managers whose primary concern is to enable reviewers to disclose personal details, given that disclosure often enhances the helpfulness of the reviews [40]. Since apparel is an experience good whose design requires actual body sizes, the physical information of reviewers, along with their experiences, is instrumental in making purchase-related decisions. For example, a potential consumer can make a satisfactory purchase by selectively obtaining information from reviewers with similar body sizes. A commercial website, such as the one examined in our study, provides a review sorting function that helps viewers derive customized reviews through filter tabs that require them to input physical information. Our results suggest that managers should encourage reviewers to disclose information to the maximum extent possible to help potential consumers gather details that are specific to their needs. Furthermore, our results suggest that individuals follow others when sharing personal information. Designers of review systems may use previous reviews as social cues to induce self-disclosure by reviewers. Placing online reviews rich in reviewer information on the front page can motivate reviewers to reveal further their personal details.

Second, our finding on the significant moderating role of prior experience can help managers better understand that sensitivity toward social cues varies depending on the cumulative experience in a website. Web designers and developers may seek ways to treat customers diferently based on their cumulative experiences. Our additional analysis with the binary measure of prior experience reveals the importance of encouraging customers to have their first experience (e.g., review generation). Taken together, managers can formulate strategies to target customers by using diferent weights on prior experience and cumulative experience. Further insights are provided for regulators concerned with privacy protection. Since experience on a web page diferentiates susceptibility in privacy behavior, policymakers may consider the use of diferent types of messages based on the customers’ experience. Customized targeting is also a plausible alternative in political campaigning.

Lastly, our analysis of the role of face disclosure is of practical interest. The moderating role of face disclosure in the efect of online crowds on self-disclosure suggests that the revelation of a reviewer’s face to the public can alter the sensitivity toward the surrounding context. Managers should consider visual anonymity as an efective way to prompt information divulgence.

## Future Research Opportunities and Limitations

Our current study ofers avenues for future works. Drawing on prior research, we narrowly defined online crowds as the prior reviewers observed through preexisting reviews on a review page. Alternatively, the scope of an online crowd can be expanded to include anyone staying at a specific website, who can become the audience of preexisting reviews. The nature of our dataset only allowed us to observe an online crowd through preexisting reviews. It would be interesting to identify online crowds based on more diverse characteristics, aside from behavior and size.

The website in our study ofered a function to sort the reviews diferently according to the body size information that a customer inputs into the box located at the top of the review page. Although reviewers who come into the review page to write comments are unlikely to re-align the reviews as they do not benefit from doing so, the review page, if rearranged, may look diferent, and a subsequent reviewer can be afected by a diferent online crowd. While the review page in a default setting has the most significant efect on review generation, future researchers are encouraged to confirm how an online crowd afects reviewer’s self-disclosure even when each reviewer sees diferent online crowds. Our observational dataset is limited in exploring the possibility of a review-sorting function. The reexamination is beyond the scope of the current study, and we leave this challenge for future research.

## Conclusion

As personal information becomes critical in enhancing the review system, it is important to investigate the antecedents of reviewer’s self-disclosure. Drawing upon social norms theory, we explored the impact of multiple aspects of online crowd and the moderating role of reviewer’s prior experience. Our analysis supports the explanation that the subsequent reviewer’s self-disclosure varies depending on the average and variance in self-disclosure level of online crowd as well as crowdedness. Prior experience moderates such relationships. This study explicates the multidimensional mechanism of the efect of online crowd and adds empirical evidence to the literature on self-disclosure.
