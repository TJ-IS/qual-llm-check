# Assessing and Enhancing Adversarial Robustness of Predictive Analytics: An Empirically Tested Design Framework

Weifeng Li<sup>a</sup> and Yidong Chai<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens GA, USA; <sup>b</sup>School of Management, Hefei University of Technology, Key Laboratory of Process Optimization and Intelligence Decision Making, Ministry of Education, Hefei University of Technology, Hefei, China

## ABSTRACT

As predictive analytics increasingly applies supervised machine learning (SML) models to inform mission-critical decision-making, adversaries become incentivized to exploit the vulnerabilities of these SML models and mislead predictive analytics into erroneous decisions. Due to the limited understanding and awareness of such adversarial attacks, the predictive analytics knowledge and deployment need a principled technique for adversarial robustness assessment and enhancement. In this research, we leverage the technology threat avoidance theory as the kernel theory and propose a research framework for assessing and enhancing the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications. We instantiate the proposed framework by developing a robust text classification system, the ARText system. The proposed system is rigorously evaluated in comparison with benchmark methods on two tasks extensively enabled by SML: spam review detection and spam email detection, which then confirmed the utility and efectiveness of our ARText system. Results from numerous experiments revealed that our proposed framework could significantly enhance the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications.

## KEYWORDS

Predictive analytics; adversarial robustness; text mining; artificial intelligence security; supervised machine learning; design frameworks

## Introduction

The recent advancement in machine learning and the increasing availability of big data have encouraged organizations to apply predictive analytics to inform mission-critical decisionmaking [14, 25, 32, 48]. Such predictive analytics applications often leverage a supervised machine learning (SML) model to make empirical predictions based on the patterns captured from the data [48]. For example, review platforms leverage text classification to detect spam reviews [33]; email service providers leverage classification models to filter out spam emails [17]; mobile app platforms utilize text mining and image processing to detect fraudulent apps based on app descriptions and icons [57]; financial institutions predict default rate by leveraging borrower information [8]; human resources use SML to assist job candidate selection [60]; healthcare practitioner discover treatment barriers from patient communities [58]; and autonomous vehicles leverage sensory data to predict energy eficiency [50].

Despite its enormous potential, predictive analytics is not without its pitfalls. One emerging threat to predictive analytics has proven to be particularly problematic: adversarial attacks [23]. As SML-enabled predictive analytics become an integral part of organizational decision-making, adversaries are incentivized to exploit the vulnerabilities of SML models underlying predictive analytics applications and mislead these applications into making erroneous decisions favoring the adversaries. In particular, such adversarial attacks often involve meticulously perturbing the input to the target SML model for achieving the desired output. This could be concerning for predictive analytics applied in the domains where the input is provided by the individual who is immediately afected by the decision made by the SML model [8, 13, 60]. Examples of such application domains include spam detection [33], fraud detection [1], credit approval [8, 59], employee hiring [8, 59], and student admission [8, 59]. In particular, a spam review could be meticulously modified to bypass the spam review detection system and spam emails could be deliberately designed to deceive SML-based spam email filtering systems. Such adversarial attacks on SML models could undermine the decision-making enabled by predictive analytics, leading to potential reputation damages and financial losses.

The vulnerability of SML models is not new and has received increasing attention from the research communities [8, 13, 18, 23, 26]. Nonetheless, it warrants further investigation in the emerging applications of predictive analytics for three reasons. First, organizations have increasingly applied SML-enabled predictive analytics into their mission-critical business processes [11, 20]; however, predictive analytics in information system (IS) research has not fully acknowledged the potential security threat against the vulnerabilities of the underlying SML models. There is limited understanding and awareness of the issue in the predictive analytics literature to provide guidelines for organizations utilizing predictive analytics taking inputs from potentially unreliable sources. Second, the IS literature focuses the evaluation of predictive analytics primarily on the models’ predictive performance, such as accuracy [34, 48]. The systematic assessment of predictive analytics applications’ robustness to adversarial exploitation has been generally understudied. As organizations decision-making about predictive analytics is informed by its evaluation, there is a need for adversarial robustness assessment techniques to complement the existing practice of evaluating predictive analytics applications. Third, the limited awareness of adversarial attacks and the lack of robustness assessment have resulted in the deployment of predictive analytics applications that are potentially vulnerable to adversarial attacks. The exploitation of such applications could lead to financial losses, reputation damages, and health and safety risks, impeding the realization of predictive analytics’ enormous potential. As such, there is an urgent need for enhancing the adversarial robustness of these deployed predictive analytics applications to adversarial exploitation.

In this paper, we seek to address two critical research questions relating to the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications: (1) How to assess the robustness of predictive analytics applications to adversarial attacks? (2) How to improve the robustness of predictive analytics applications in an adversarial context? Drawing upon the technology threat avoidance theory, we propose a research framework for assessing and enhancing the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications. The research framework seeks to provide a principled approach for improving the adversarial robustness of existing predictive analytics applications. We further instantiate the proposed framework by developing a robust text classification system. Leveraging the technology threat avoidance theory as a kernel theory, our proposed framework comprises a novel assessment model for evaluating the adversarial robustness of a predictive analytics application, and design principles for enhancing the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications. We demonstrate the efectiveness of our framework in the context of text classification tasks. In particular, we develop the ARText system, which jointly incorporates ensemble learning and adversarial retraining to improve its robustness to adversarial attacks. To rigorously evaluate our proposed system, we conducted two sets of evaluations on the testbeds of spam review detection and spam email detection. The first set of evaluations examined the utility of our proposed adversarial robustness assessment. The second set of evaluations gauged the eficacy of our proposed system in enhancing adversarial robustness. Overall, the ARText system was able to outperform baseline models in detecting adversarial spam reviews, indicating the efectiveness of our proposed design framework.

## Research Background

## Predictive Analytics in IS

Predictive analytics leveraging SML models are becoming prevalent in various business contexts, including spam detection [3, 33], fraud detection [54, 57], credit approval [8, 59], student admission [8, 59], marketing [19, 22], and crowdsourcing [56]. Most predictive analytics applications draw upon the statistical learning theory to utilize or develop SML models. The statistical learning theory formalizes the predictive task as choosing a model $f : X \to Y ;$ , where X is the input space and Y is the output space, such that for a given input $x \in X , f$ predicts the corresponding output $y \in Y$ [53]. For example, detecting review manipulation could be formulated as identifying a model that predicts the spam review label given the review text and reviewer attributes [33]. The optimal model is chosen by applying the empirical risk minimization (ERM) principle on a training set containing l samples of input-output pairs. Specifically, the ERM principle finds the function that minimizes the loss $\mathcal { L } ( f ( x ) , y )$ between the output y to a given input x and the prediction f ðxÞ made by the function on the training set: $\operatorname* { m i n } _ { f } { \frac { 1 } { l } } \sum _ { i = i } ^ { l } { \mathcal { L } } ( f ( x _ { i } ) , y _ { i } )$ . The loss function could be the squared error $\left( y _ { i } - f ( x _ { i } ) \right) ^ { 2 }$ for regression tasks where $\gamma \mathrm { { s } }$ are continuous outputs, and the misclassification indicator function $1 ( y _ { i } = f ( x _ { i } ) )$ for classification tasks, where $\gamma \mathbf { \bar { s } }$ are discrete outputs.

The key assumption underlying the ERM principle is that all inputs x, including training inputs and unseen inputs from the actual application setting, are drawn independently from the same fixed distribution $P ( x )$ . Nonetheless, this assumption could be challenged in certain real-world application domains where the input that predictive analytics relies on to make a decision is provided by the individual who is immediately afected by the decision [13]. In such application domains, adversaries can meticulously manipulate the input data to trick SML models into favorable decisions and subsequently benefit from them [8, 60].

Examples of such application domains include including spam detection [33], credit approval [8, 59], employee hiring [8, 59], and student admission [8, 59]. Specifically, review spammers could meticulously write spam review content to bypass SML-based spam review detection systems. Similarly, spam emails could be deliberately designed to deceive SMLbased spam email filtering systems. Credit card applicants might provide fraudulent information to get approval from automated credit application processing systems. Job candidates might fake their background attributes in job applications to deceive human resources automation, and student candidates might alter their information to get admitted by automated student admission management systems. While these systems attempt to mitigate the adversarial exploitation by employing fraud detection systems, recent systems have extensively leveraged SML [1, 3, 33, 54, 57] and are, therefore, vulnerable to adversarial exploitation as well. Such threats against the SML model underlying predictive analytics necessitate the consideration of adversarial robustness when applying SML models.

However, the existing evaluation of predictive analytics primarily focuses on predictive performance, that is, the model’s ability to predict new observations accurately on testing datasets resembling the training datasets [48]. Such an evaluation procedure is insuficient for assessing the adversarial robustness of SML models in real-world application domains, where inputs can be perturbed to mislead SML models. For example, predictive analytics for classification tasks, such as detecting spam reviews and phishing websites, primarily use accuracy, precision, recall, F-measure (F1), and area under the receiver operating curve (ROC AUC) [3, 33]; predictive analytics for regression tasks, such as predicting sales, widely adopt mean absolute percentage error and root mean square of errors [22]; and predictive analytics for ranking tasks, such as recommender systems, often utilize top-k precision and rank correlation coeficient [19, 38]. These metrics cannot directly measure adversarial robustness. Hence, it is imperative to enrich the predictive analytics literature by developing a systematic adversarial robustness assessment model.

## Adversarial Attacks against SML Models

There are generally two attacking strategies against SML models: causative attacks and exploratory attacks [29]. Causative attacks, also known as poisoning attacks, manipulate the training data $\{ ( x _ { i } , y _ { i } ) | i \in [ 1 , \ldots , l ] \}$ to subvert the SML model. Exploratory attacks, also known as evasion attacks, mislead the trained SML model $f ( x )$ with meticulously designed inputs, known as adversarial samples. The adversarial sample is achieved by introducing a carefully designed perturbation $\delta$ to an original data point $x _ { i }$ in order to mislead a trained SML model to make erroneous decisions $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } , f ( x _ { i } + \delta ) \not = f ( x _ { i } ) )$ while maintaining its main properties (e.g., the negativity of review content). For example, a fake negative review on the competitor might be easily intercepted by the spam review detection system of an online review platform. To bypass the detection system, the adversary can carefully perturb this review (e.g., replacing words, introducing typos) to exploit the vulnerability of the underlying SML model. For most predictive analytics applications, it is dificult for adversaries to have access to the training process of the target SML model and therefore exploratory attacks are potentially more prevalent than causative attacks. Tactics of exploratory attacks are primarily diferentiated based on adversaries’ knowledge about the target SML model f ðxÞ in terms of its structure, parameters, gradients, and other specifications. In white-box exploratory attacks, adversaries have the full knowledge about the target SML model f ðxÞ and leverage the knowledge to generate adversarial samples through constrained optimization. The two prominent types of white-box exploratory attacks are maximal loss attacks and minimal perturbation attacks. The objective of maximal loss attacks is to maximize the error made by the target SML model subject to the maximum allowable perturbation ε from the original sample (e.g., $\operatorname* { m a x } _ { \delta } \lvert f ( x _ { i } + \delta ) - f ( x _ { i } ) \rvert , \mathrm { s . t . ~ } \lvert \delta \rvert < \varepsilon )$ . As adversarial samples are allowed to perturb further from their original samples, adversaries have increased flexibility to generate adversarial samples, raising the efectiveness of these adversarial samples in misleading SML models and diminishing the target SML model’s predictive performance. However, excessive perturbation could entail a higher faking cost [13], and therefore the maximum allowable perturbation is imposed to reflect this constraint. The objective of minimal perturbation attacks is to trick the target SML model into making a certain erroneous decision (e.g., $f ( x _ { i } + \delta ) = t$ where t is the desired label diferent from $f ( x _ { i } ) )$ while minimizing the perturbation δ from the original data point (e.g., min δ; s:t: $f ( x _ { i } + \delta ) = t )$ . Whereas maximal loss attacks can generate adversarial samples much faster than minimal perturbation attacks [24], the minimal perturbation attack outperforms by generating adversarial samples with a higher success rate and a lower detection rate.

Black-box exploratory attacks are more prevalent for predictive analytics applications in practice, where adversaries have limited or no knowledge about the specification of target SML models. As such, the black-box exploratory attack aims to first recover the missing knowledge about the target SML model through approximation and then leverages the white-box attack tactic with the approximated information. In particular, there are two common types of black-box exploratory attacks: transferability-based attacks and querybased attacks.

The transferability of adversarial attacks refers to the finding that certain adversarial samples designed to mislead one SML model can also cause another SML model to make erroneous decisions, despite their distinctive model specifications [46]. In particular, both intra-model transferability and cross-model transferability exist among the SML models. The former is the transferability among SML models trained with the same model structure (e.g., decision tree) but diferent parameter initialization and training set, whereas the latter is the transferability among SML models with diferent model structures (e.g., a decision tree and a neural network). Transferability-based attacks seek to approximate the target SML model with a substitute SML model, which is expected to share common vulnerabilities with the target SML model [39]. Subsequently, an adversary could generate adversarial samples against the substitute model and use the generated adversarial samples to attack the target SML model [40]. The intra-model transferability attack requires the adversary to know the specific SML structure of the target model, and such information could be acquired through reconnaissance. For example, many fraud detection systems are documented in academic literature and patent documents, and some are open-sourced on Github. Adversaries could investigate these resources, key persons involved, and their employment history to infer the specific SML structure of a certain organization’s system. In contrast, the cross-model transferability attack does not require any information about the target model [40]. While the cross-model transferability attack might not be as efective as the intra-model transferability attack, the former has wider applicability and could be particularly useful where the target predictive analytics application does not limit the number of inputs it takes.

Query-based attacks primarily seek to reverse engineer the target SML model behavior through sending queries and collecting the target model outputs. Subsequently, adversaries can train a substitute SML model, such as Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, and Deep Neural Networks, with data queried from the target SML model to imitate its model behavior [43]. Adversarial samples generated against this substitute model can be efective on the target SML model [6]. Existing query algorithms generally need a number of queries (e.g., proportional to the number of features) to be efective. In query-based attacks, the adversary needs to have the ability to repetitively query the target SML model with inputs and gather the corresponding model outputs. Such repetitive query access is quite common in several use-cases, such as spam review detection systems, spam email detection systems, and credit application processing systems. For example, adversaries could maintain multiple accounts on the review platform for submitting spam reviews and gather outputs based on whether the spam reviews are taken down by the target system. Moreover, in certain domains, historical data of model inputs and outputs could be collected from the target SML model, which could also train the substitute model for query-based attacks. For instance, Yelp publishes a dataset of spam and genuine reviews as determined by its renowned spam review filter for academic research purposes, which could be leveraged for training a substitute model [42].

## Defense against Adversarial Attacks

The IS literature has examined the defense of exploratory adversarial attacks from the perspective of the principal-agent model, where the principal leverages an SML model (e.g., a classifier) to make a decision (e.g., fraud, admission, credit approval) based on the data provided by the agent [7]. The adversarial agent has the incentive to strategically perturb the data provided to the principal in order to attain a favorable decision. In anticipation of the possible strategic perturbation, the principal can defend the SML model by incorporating the perturbation into the model optimization. Specifically, an enhanced support vector machine (SVM) capable of prohibiting adversaries constrained by a certain perturbation range has been developed by solving a Nash equilibrium between the principal and the adversarial agent through mixed integer programming [8]. Since numerically solving the mixed integer program can be computationally intensive, the genetic algorithm has been utilized to expedite the process by approximating the solution [10]. The genetic algorithm is further extended to address adversarial attacks where the inputs are composed of only binary variables [9]. Relatedly, the particular perturbation type of hiding data has been studied and the defensive measure of data imputation methods has been proposed for SVM [59] and support vector regression [60].

We seek to complement the existing IS literature on defense against adversarial attacks in several ways. First, while prior research extensively studies SVM and develops defensive measures with analytical solutions specific to this type of SML model, it is unclear whether defending other types of SML models (e.g., models with non-linear discriminant functions) can be solved with analytical solutions. Many predictive analytics applications leverage SML models other than SVM as their underlying SML models, and therefore we aim to develop a generalized approach for enhancing the SML models in these predictive analytics applications.

Second, past literature mostly studies the white-box exploratory attack which assumes the adversaries to have the full knowledge of the target SML model. We extend the literature by investigating the black-box exploratory attack where limited knowledge of the target SML model other than its outputs is available to adversaries. The black-box exploratory attack is common to numerous domains, such as fraud detection, spam detection, credit approval, employee hiring, and student admission. Adversaries targeting these domains often have little knowledge about the SML models but can repetitively access the model outputs given inputs.

Third, existing research based on the principal-agent model assumes all individuals interacting with predictive analytics applications are rational and have incentives to perturb the input data for favorable output. Our research allows for benign agents who truthfully report the input data. This is reasonable in several use-cases such as online review platforms, credit card applications, employee hiring, and student admission, where not all individuals intend to commit fraud.

In summary, this research seeks to address the black-box adversarial attack, where the predictive analytics application makes decisions about individuals based on the input provided by them, and adversaries aim to mislead the predictive analytics application into favorable decisions. In particular, the predictive analytics application makes decisions by minimizing the loss caused by incorrect decisions in anticipation of adversarial perturbation in inputs and the adversary has repetitive access to predictive analytics outputs but limited information about the underlying SML model specifications.

## Framework for Adversarial Robustness Assessment and Enhancement

Efective countermeasures against the black-box exploratory attacks to SML models entail the utilization of an IS security theory to provide design guidelines. Grounded in cybernetics theory and coping theory, technology threat avoidance theory (TTAT) serves as an appropriate kernel theory governing the design requirements of our proposed framework. In threat appraisal, the susceptibility of the system and the negative consequences of the IT threat is evaluated [44]. In threat coping, safeguarding measures are taken to avoid the IT threat. ## Adversarial Robustness Assessment

Drawing upon the existing practices, we propose to quantify the adversarial robustness of the SML model through empirical analysis. In particular, the empirical adversarial robustness measures a model’s robustness to a set of adversarial samples through the impact of adversarial samples on the model’s predictive performance. In particular, our proposed adversarial robustness assessment involves two steps: (1) adversarial sample generation and (2) adversarial robustness measurement. The former aims to emulate adversarial attacks by generating adversarial samples, whereas the latter seeks to measure the model’s adversarial robustness on testbeds containing emulated adversarial attacks. The first step generates adversarial samples with a domain-relevant and state-of-the-art adversarial sample generation algorithm. Most adversarial example generation algorithms are domain-specific: an adversarial sample generation algorithm designed for images might not be as efective in generating adversarial textual samples. Moreover, as weak adversarial samples that are easy to defend could lead to overestimating the adversarial robustness, strong adversarial samples generated by the state-of-the-art benchmark adversarial sample algorithm can reflect the model’s adversarial robustness in real-world applications more accurately.

A summary of the existing practice of adversarial robustness measurement is provided in Table 2. Adversarial robustness definition and quantification can be classified into two broad categories: performance-based adversarial robustness and perturbation-based adversarial robustness.

The performance-based adversarial robustness gauges the SML model’s predictive performance, under adversarial attacks of a given set of adversarial samples, in fulfilling a task (e.g., text classification, image classification). A relatively high predictive performance of the SML model on adversarial samples suggests that the SML model possesses high robustness to adversarial attacks. One common performance-based adversarial robustness measure is robustness accuracy, which measures the accuracy of the classification model on adversarial samples. Despite their usefulness, the existing measures of performance-based adversarial robustness are mostly based on the absolute predictive performance under adversarial attacks, which could raise validity concerns due to convoluting the measurement of adversarial robustness with that of predictive performance. Moreover, the robustness assessment testbed comprises only adversarial samples, which might overrepresent the adversarial attacks in real-world application domains.

While the performance-based adversarial robustness measures are conditioned on the perturbation range pre-specified by the maximum allowable perturbation, adversaries in real-world attacks might not be always bounded by the specified perturbation range in the robustness assessment. As the perturbation range expands, the SML model could become less robust to adversarial attacks. To that end, the perturbation-based adversarial robustness takes into consideration the cost of perturbing an original sample into a successful adversarial sample. The cost is quantified by the deviation between the successful adversarial sample and its corresponding original sample in $L _ { p }$ norms [12].<sup>1</sup> One common perturbation-based adversarial robustness measure is $L _ { 2 }$ norm, which gauges the Euclidean distance between the successful adversarial sample and its original sample. The overall adversarial robustness of the SML model is reported using certain descriptive statistics of the $L _ { p }$ norm similarity distances for each pair of samples, such as the minimum and the average. Nonetheless, the simple descriptive statistics might not fully capture the trade-of between the perturbation range of adversarial attacks and their impact on the SML model’s predictive performance, which could be critical to decision-making in the design and deployment of predictive analytics.

As such, we propose the meta-design of two adversarial robustness measures to complement existing predictive performance measures in evaluating organizational predictive analytics applications. Specifically, we propose that performance-based robustness measures should concentrate on evaluating SML models’ adversarial robustness by teasing out the predictive performance from the adversarial robustness using relative measures. Moreover, testbeds for robustness assessment should reflect realistic settings by including both adversarial and non-adversarial samples. We further propose that perturbation-based robustness measures should capture the trade-of between the perturbation range of adversarial attacks and their impact on predictive performance. Such trade-of enables a comprehensive understanding of the robustness of SML models in applications where adversaries are not bounded by a fixed perturbation range.

## Adversarial Robustness Enhancement

Existing research on defending black-box exploratory attacks mainly focuses on developing attack-specific defensive measures [30, 31, 51]. While efective, these attack-specific defensive measures might not be suitable for the real-world application domains, where the specific types of adversarial attacks are often unknown. To efectively improve the adversarial robustness of SML models, our meta-design proposes to mitigate black-box exploratory attacks with two design principles: ensemble learning and adversarial retraining. The former informs the architecture of a robust SML model, and the latter provides the guideline for training the SML model process for adversarial robustness.

Ensemble learning is the practice of harnessing the collective power of multiple SML models on the same task to enhance predictive performance, which has the potential to improve adversarial robustness. On the one hand, ensemble learning efectively mitigates the security risk of exploitation with diversification [15]. With the traditional practice of using one single SML model, the shared vulnerabilities between this SML model and the adversary’s substitute model lead to correlated failure, forming the basis of transferabilitybased attacks [46]. To that end, ensemble learning leverages the diversification strategy such that an adversarial sample needs to mislead the majority of the SML models in the ensemble for the attack to be successful. Generating an adversarial sample capable of simultaneously misleading multiple SML models is perceivably more dificult than generating an adversarial sample capable of deceiving one single SML model. On the other hand, ensemble learning increases the dificulty for adversaries to develop efective substitute models. Combining multiple SML models together, the ensemble learning model exponentially expands the function space in which adversaries need to search for their substitute models. In comparison with approximating one single SML model, developing substitute models for ensemble learning requires identifying the right combination of constituent models, approximating each constituent model, and using the correct type of ensembles (e.g., Bootstrap aggregating, boosting, stacking), none of which is trivial to adversaries.

Adversarial retraining involves training the target SML model with the original training set augmented with adversarial samples [36]. Adversarial retraining improves adversarial robustness because incorporating adversarial samples into the training set is mathematically equivalent to explicitly modeling adversarial risk in the SML model’s objective function. We illustrate this by examining the objective function after incorporating adversarial samples. Suppose the training set is $( x , y )$ , where x is the inputs and y is the corresponding outputs. Without loss of generality, we assume the target SML model $f _ { \beta } ( x _ { i } )$ minimizes the objective loss function in the form of $\begin{array} { r } { \underset { \beta } { \operatorname* { m i n } } \frac { 1 } { \left| x \right| } \sum _ { x _ { i } \in x , y _ { i } \in y } \mathcal { L } \left( f _ { \beta } ( x _ { i } ) , y _ { i } \right) } \end{array}$ , where $\beta$ is the model’s parameter vector and $\mathcal { L } ( \cdot )$ is the task-specific loss function. Suppose we have an adversarial dataset $( x ^ { \mathrm { A } } , y ^ { \mathrm { A } } )$ , where $x ^ { \mathrm { A } }$ are adversarial inputs and $y ^ { \mathrm { A } }$ is the correct outputs of the corresponding original data points. Drawing upon the empirical risk management principle, incorporating these adversarial samples into the loss function results in a loss function with two terms:

$$
\min _ {\beta} \frac {1}{| x | + | x ^ {\mathrm{A}} |} \left(\Sigma_ {x _ {i} \in x, y _ {i} \in y} \mathcal {L} \big (f _ {\beta} (x _ {i}), y _ {i} \big) + \Sigma_ {x _ {j} \in x ^ {\mathrm{A}}, y _ {j} \in y ^ {\mathrm{A}}} \mathcal {L} \big (f _ {\beta} (x _ {j}), y _ {j} \big)\right),
$$

where the first term in the parenthesis is the loss caused by the original training set, and the second term is the loss caused by adversarial samples, which captures the adversarial risk. While minimizing the first term enhances the SML model’s predictive performance, minimizing the second term improves the SML model’s adversarial robustness. Moreover, in this loss function, we find that the weights of the two terms are proportional to the ratio between the size of x and the size of $x ^ { \mathrm { A } }$ , which suggests that we can trade of predictive performance for adversarial robustness by adjusting the relative size of the adversarial sample set. Furthermore, adversarial retraining becomes more efective in enhancing adversarial robustness when coupled with iteration [36]. Multiple iterations of retraining in which new adversarial samples are generated and incorporated into the training set could efectively minimize the upper bound on optimal adversarial risk.

## System Design: The ARText System

Motivated by the extensive application of text classification in predictive analytics, we develop a generalized text classification system with adversarial robustness as an instantiation of our design framework (Figure 2). Specifically, organizations use a wide range of predictive analytics applications based on text classification, such as spam review detection [33], spam email filtering [17], sentiment analysis [61], and adverse event detection [2]. Developing a system capable of assessing and enhancing text classification could significantly benefit these textual predictive analytics applications. Our proposed ARText system generates adversarial samples using a state-of-the-art textual adversarial sample generator, quantifies adversarial robustness with novel measures, classifies texts using bootstrap aggregation of an ensemble of SML models, and further improves adversarial robustness through iterative adversarial retraining.

## Adversarial Robustness Assessment using Adversarial Textual Samples

## Adversarial Sample Generation

ARText’s adversarial sample generation simulates our black-box exploratory attack problem setting. Specifically, we first train a set of substitute models to approximate the target SML model and then leverage the benchmark textual adversarial sample generation algorithm to attack. Following the practice of the state-of-the-art attacks, our substitute model comprises of several traditional text classifiers, such as Naïve Bayes, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, deep neural network-based text classifiers (e.g., Convolutional Neural Networks), and recurrent neural network-based text classifiers (e.g., Gated Recurrent Networks, and Long Short-Term Memory networks), as well as a homogeneous ensemble learning model, Random Forest. We include most text classification models into our substitute model because adversaries can select various types of substitute models for generating adversarial samples. More importantly, perturbation being added to adversarial samples are model-specific and therefore we can expand the variety of adversarial samples to comprehensively quantify the adversarial robustness. We use the state-of-the-art benchmark textual adversarial sample generator, DeepWordBug, to craft adversarial textual samples [21]. Specifically, DeepWordBug first examines each word in the original text in terms of its impact on the prediction of substitute models and then alters the word with the largest impact by inserting human-readable typos. The adversarial sample generator has state-of-the-art performance in generating adversarial textual samples while maintaining their original semantics, which is a key objective of textual adversarial attacks.

## Adversarial Robustness Measurement

Drawing upon existing performance-based and perturbation-based adversarial robustness measures, ARText proposes novel adversarial robustness measures designed specifically for text classification tasks. In particular, two sets of adversarial robustness measures are proposed in ARText: performance ratio and area under the performance-perturbation curve.

The performance ratio measure primarily seeks to gauge the adversarial attacks’ impact on the SML model’s predictive performance within a given perturbation range. In particular, this measure compares the model’s predictive performance on a non-adversarial dataset against its predictive performance on a mixed dataset comprising both adversarial and non-adversarial samples. Specifically, the mixed dataset is derived from the nonadversarial dataset by perturbing a portion of the non-adversarial samples into adversarial samples with the perturbation range pre-specified by the maximum allowable perturbation $\varepsilon ,$ such that the change in predictive performance could be directly attributed to the adversarial perturbation. The proposed performance ratio measure advances the existing performance-based adversarial robustness measures by teasing out the predictive performance from the adversarial robustness through using the relative predictive performance that incorporates the baseline predictive performance on non-adversarial samples.

Predictive performance for text classification is commonly evaluated using five metrics: accuracy (A), precision (P), recall (R), F-measure (F1), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC). While existing performance-based robustness measures primarily leverage accuracy, we generalize and extend the idea of performance ratio to the holistic set of text classification metrics. Given the perturbation range ε, we quantify the performance ratio with the following formulation,

$$
R _ {A} (\varepsilon) = \frac {A _ {\mathrm{a}}}{A _ {\mathrm{n}}}, R _ {P} (\varepsilon) = \frac {P _ {\mathrm{a}}}{P _ {\mathrm{n}}}, R _ {R} (\varepsilon) = \frac {R _ {\mathrm{a}}}{R _ {\mathrm{n}}}, R _ {F} (\varepsilon) = \frac {F 1 _ {\mathrm{a}}}{F 1 _ {\mathrm{n}}}, \text { and } R _ {R O C} (\varepsilon) = \frac {R O C _ {\mathrm{a}}}{R O C _ {\mathrm{n}}},
$$

where $\mathrm { m e t r i c } _ { a } \ ( \mathrm { m e t r i c } \in \{ A , P , R , F 1 , R O C \} )$ is measured under the mixed dataset, ${ \mathrm { m e t r i c } } _ { n }$ is measured under the non-adversarial dataset, and $R _ { \mathrm { m e t r i c } } ( \varepsilon )$ is the ratio of classification metric under adversarial attacks within perturbation range ε relative to the non-adversarial context. These measures are non-negative scalar metrics, with a low performance ratio indicating weak adversarial robustness and a high performance ratio suggesting strong adversarial robustness.

Complementing the performance ratio measure is the area under the performanceperturbation curve measure that seeks to capture the trade-of between the perturbation range of adversarial attacks and their impact on predictive performance. In general, perturbation range positively correlates with adversarial attack success, and hence negatively correlates with the predictive performance of the SML model. This is because a wide perturbation range provides the adversarial sample generator with more flexibility to come up with stronger adversarial samples, leading to the diminished predictive performance of the target SML model.

Our proposed performance-perturbation curve aims to characterize the varying predictive performance at diferent levels of the perturbation range. Specifically, the performanceperturbation space is two-dimensional with predictive performance as the Y axis and the perturbation range as the X axis. In the context of text classification, the Y axis is the text classification metric (i.e., accuracy, precision, recall, F-measure, and ROC) and the X axis is the perturbation range, quantified as the percentage of words allowed for perturbation in a sentence, ranging between 0 (no perturbation allowed) to 1.0 (entirely new text). A higher performance-perturbation curve suggests the model is more robust to adversarial attacks across various levels of perturbation range.

The area under the performance-perturbation curve provides an aggregate measure of the adversarial robustness across all levels of the perturbation range. As all text classification metrics are scalar values between 0 and 1.0, the area under the performance-perturbation curve is a scalar metric ranging between 0 (extremely vulnerable) and 1.0 (perfect adversarial robustness). Our proposed area under the performance-perturbation curve measures includes the area under the accuracy-perturbation curve (A/P AUC), the area under the precision-perturbation curve (P/P AUC), the area under the recall-perturbation curve (R/P AUC), the area under the F-measure-perturbation curve (F/P AUC), and the area under the ROC-perturbation curve (ROC/P AUC).

## Adversarial Retraining

The ability of adversarial retraining to incorporate adversarial risk into the objective function of SML model optimization is a distinct advantage. The robustness of our proposed bagging SML model for text classification is further enhanced with adversarial retraining. We propose an iterative adversarial retraining algorithm to optimize our proposed bagging SML model with adversarial samples. The bagging SML model is first initiated with the original training set. In each iteration, we leverage our textual adversarial example generation algorithm to attack the bagging model. Adversarial samples capable of successfully misleading the bagging model are then added to the training set. The augmented training set is used for retraining the bagging model, which is then evaluated in terms of its text classification performance as well as adversarial robustness. ## Technical Novelties

To summarize, the technical novelties of our ARText system are three-fold. First, we propose two novel measures for adversarial robustness assessment. Compared with the extant performance-based robustness measures, the proposed performance ratio measure advances robustness measurement by teasing out the efect of predictive performance and concentrates on measuring adversarial robustness. Compared with the existing perturbation-based robustness measures, our performance-perturbation curve measure can comprehensively capture the trade-of between the perturbation range of adversarial attacks and their impact on predictive performance. Second, our system innovatively incorporates bootstrap aggregation into iterative adversarial retraining to further advance adversarial robustness enhancement. While prior research uses ensemble learning for generating adversarial samples [52], the ARText system innovatively leverages the diversification strategy for mitigating the risk of adversarial attack by adding bootstrap aggregation to adversarial retraining for robustness enhancement. Lastly, the ARText system constitutes a novel instantiation of our proposed framework. To the best of our knowledge, the ARText system is among the first systems for assessing and enhancing the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications on textual data.

## Experimental Evaluation

Consistent with Hevner et al. [27], we rigorously evaluated the efectiveness of the major components of our proposed ARText system through experimental evaluation. In particular, Evaluation 1 examined the utility of the adversarial robustness assessment component. Evaluation 2 evaluated the efectiveness of the adversarial robustness enhancement component. Considering the prevalent application of SML models in the spam detection domain, we evaluated our ARText system on the testbeds of spam reviews and spam emails. In our experiments, we evaluated the ARText system and the baseline SML models in the task of classifying whether a candidate review or email is spam or not. Our spam review testbed is built on a balanced benchmark spam review dataset encompassing 800 genuine reviews and 800 spam reviews for hotels in Chicago [45]. The genuine reviews are collected from well-known online review platforms, including TripAdvisor, Priceline, and Expedia, whereas the spam reviews are labeled on Amazon Mechanical Turk. The spam email testbed is an imbalanced benchmark dataset comprising 2,500 non-spam emails and 500 spam emails. The testbed was collected from Apache SpamAssassin’s public corpus.<sup>3</sup> Our baseline text classifiers included traditional SML models, such as Naïve Bayes (NB), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM), deep neural network, such as Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), and recurrent neural networks, such as Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) network, Bi-directional GRU (BiGRU), Logistic Regression (LR), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and Bi-directional LSTM (BiLSTM). Given the model characteristics, the traditional text classifiers used the bag-of-words representation as their input features and the deep neural network and recurrent neural networks used the word sequence as their input features. In our evaluation, we adopted the widely-used 5-fold cross-validation, where the testbed is randomly partitioned into five evenly-sized subsets and the models are iteratively trained on four subsets and tested on a diferent holdout subset. <sup>4</sup> The models’ overall performance is measured by the average of evaluation results across the five holdout subsets.

## Discussion

The experiment results demonstrate the efectiveness of ARText and its underlying metadesign. The first set of evaluations demonstrated the utility of the proposed performance ratio robustness measure and performance-perturbation curve robustness measure. We found that none of the baseline SML models were absolutely robust to adversarial attacks, confirming our research motivation. The second set of evaluations gauged the efectiveness of the two design principles of our ARText system and its underlying meta-design. Our experiment results suggest that both ensemble learning and adversarial retraining were able to significantly enhance the adversarial robustness of our ARText system. Our findings have important design implications for the growing body of IS literature on predictive analytics. The results also provide insights for the broader communities of practitioners developing predictive analytics applications. The major takeaways are three-fold.

First, adversarial robustness should be an integral part of evaluating predictive analytics. Predictive analytics applications are facing greater risks of exploitation as these applications are increasingly utilized for mission-critical decision-making. Our results in Experiment 1 have shown that adversarial samples with the perturbation range of 10 percent could distort standard SML models’ predictive performance such as precision and recall by at least 3 percent and 28 percent on our testbeds. This finding is generalizable to numerous application domains because standard SML models are generally plagued by their vulnerable assumption underlying the widely used empirical risk minimization principle. To account for threats exploiting vulnerabilities of SML models, the predictive analytics literature needs to be extended by incorporating the consideration of potential adversarial threats and the evaluation of adversarial robustness. For organizations, testing the predictive analytics for adversarial robustness is essential for ensuring the security of decisionmaking informed by predictive analytics. Specifically, model selection and optimization should be guided by predictive performance augmented with adversarial robustness in developing predictive analytics applications.

Second, both predictive performance and perturbation provide valuable perspectives for assessing adversarial robustness. As the predictive performance of SML models and the perturbation range of adversarial samples are negatively related, our two proposed robustness measures jointly leverage both for assessing adversarial robustness. The performance ratio robustness measure directly reveals the adversarial attacks’ impact on predictive performance at a given level of perturbation range, which is particularly useful in application domains having fixed perturbation ranges. The performance-perturbation curve robustness measure provides a comprehensive quantification of the trade-of between the model’s predictive performance and the adversarial sample’s perturbation range. This measure can be valuable in application domains where the perturbation range is unbounded and the overall adversarial robustness is preferred. In our study, these measures are instantiated in the context of text classification using classification metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F-measure, and area under ROC; however, these measures are generalizable to other predictive analytics tasks by incorporating predictive performance metrics from the specific application domain.

Third, ensemble learning and adversarial retraining are efective design principles in enhancing adversarial robustness. Ensemble learning enhances adversarial robustness because the diversification strategy is efective in mitigating security risks of adversarial exploitation. Successful adversarial samples have to mislead a majority of the constituent models of the ensemble learning approach. In Experiment 3, ensemble learning improved the accuracy robustness by over 7 percent and F-measure robustness by at least 10 percent on adversarial spam review samples with the 10 percent perturbation range. Adversarial retraining essentially reduces the vulnerable assumption underlying the empirical risk minimization principle by augmenting the training set to account for the distribution of adversarial samples. In Experiment 4, we observed increases up to 29 percent on performance ratio robustness and increases up to 27 percent on performance-perturbation curve robustness when adversarial retraining was paired with ensemble learning. Moreover, these design principles not only provide guidelines for developing new predictive analytics applications but can also be applicable to existing predictive analytics applications seeking to improve their adversarial robustness without significant changes to the underlying SML models.

## Conclusions

Our study makes several key contributions to research and practice. First, we contribute to the emerging literature on AI governance by examining the unique security concern of adversarial attacks facing human-AI hybrids. Specifically, we show that, while an AI system can be evaluated as efective in benign settings, the inherent vulnerability of its underlying algorithm may compromise the efectiveness of the AI system in adversarial attacks. Therefore, the security of AI constitutes a major consideration in developing human-AI hybrids for task substitution, augmentation, and assemblage. Second, we extend the predictive analytics literature by incorporating the adversarial robustness assessment into the evaluation. While the focus of predictive analytics research has been on developing novel algorithms to inform decision-making, we show that predictive analytics-informed decision-making could be threatened by adversarial attacks. As decision-making is increasingly dependent on predictive analytics, we expand the evaluation of the predictive analytics applications by incorporating adversarial robustness. Third, our study makes a design contribution by proposing a design framework based on the technology threat avoidance theory for assessing and enhancing the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications. We demonstrated the feasibility of the design framework through developing the ARText system for text classification tasks, which instantiated the design guidelines in its adversarial robustness measures and techniques for ensemble learning and adversarial retraining. A series of experiments were conducted to illustrate the utility and efectiveness of key components of ARText, demonstrating the eficacy of our design guidelines.

This study has important implications for practitioners. As predictive analytics that supports decision-making constitute an increasingly critical endeavor, the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics applications emerges as a paramount concern. Our research can shed light on the significance of this concern to organizations, and the guidelines presented in this study are to mitigate this concern. While predictive performance remains an essential benchmark for predictive analytics, the adversarial robustness emphasized by our proposed framework provides considerable complementary value to allow better decision-making at the risk of adversarial attacks. Moreover, in addition to traditional IT security strategies such as anti-virus, intrusion detection, and identity and access management [37], there is an increasing need for organizations to impose policies and procedures to secure important information about the underlying predictive model in designing, implementing, and operating predictive analytics applications.

This work has several limitations and future research will improve upon our design framework. First, while our proposed framework is efective in assessing and enhancing the adversarial robustness of predictive analytics, the ensemble learning and adversarial retraining design principles entail computational costs. Future research may explore extensions that could improve the computational eficiency of the framework. Second, our framework primarily focuses on enhancing the underlying SML model of predictive analytics. Feature engineering is also an important aspect of predictive analytics and could be potentially vulnerable to adversarial attacks. The attack and defense of diferent feature engineering techniques in real-world problem settings could be a promising future direction. Third, in alignment with prior literature, our research quantifies textual perturbation as the percentage of words perturbed. Under this assumption, inserting a typo had the same implications as replacing a word with its synonym. There could be contexts where this might not be the case and therefore the extent of perturbing words could be further examined, diferentiated, and quantified.
