# Leveraging Platform Boundary Resources: The Role of Distributed Sensemaking

Franck Soh <sup>a</sup> and Varun Grover<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information Systems and Supply Chain Management Department, Bryan School of Business and Economics, The University of North Carolina at Greensboro, Greensboro, NC, USA; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems Department, Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR, USA

## ABSTRACT

Although mobile platform ecosystems are highly competitive environments, they provide important entrepreneurial opportunities for app developer organizations. To facilitate app innovations, platform owners regularly release platform boundary resources (PBRs) by adding novel technologies at the platform level. App developer organizations, however, face high levels of technological uncertainty as they create app-level innovations based on PBRs. We examine the efectiveness of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs in a context of technological uncertainty arising from either PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs or app complexity. Using a dataset of 933 PBR-based app innovations, the findings significantly contribute to our understanding of the duality between competition and cooperation in a platform ecosystem. We demonstrate that distributed sensemaking among app developers, who potentially compete against each other, is crucial for enhancing app competitive performance, especially when technological uncertainty is high. Thus, we encourage greater cooperation among app developer organizations to leverage PBRs.

## KEYWORDS

Mobile platforms; boundary resources; innovation; sensemaking; technologica unrelatedness; app complexity; app innovations; app development

## Introduction

Mobile platforms provide important markets for app developer organizations. Organizations that publish apps on mobile platforms are considered app developer organizations (e.g., British Airways, CIBC). The number of mobile platform users is considerably high providing significant entrepreneurial opportunities to app developer organizations. In January 2019, the iOS platform reached 1.4 billion active devices [30]. Moreover, mobile platforms are characterized by highly competitive environments, making it a challenge to achieve high levels of app performance [28, 62]. Surviving in such environments makes it necessary for app developer organizations to continually evolve their apps by releasing new app services [15]. A growing strategy used by app developer organizations is to extend their app functionalities by creating app innovations that integrate platform boundary resources (PBRs)<sup>1</sup> (e.g., ARKIT, and SiriKit). These app innovations are PBR-based app innovations. They represent new app features (e.g., authentication features) that are created based on a given PBR and are released either as new apps or updates to existing apps [61]. PBRs play the dual role of resourcing and securing the platform by providing (1) the software tools (e.g., APIs, SDKs) to access new platform resources (e.g., new hardware features such as TouchID), and (2) the regulations to control interactions platform owners–app developers, respectively [19].

Creating PBR-based app innovations entails mitigating technological uncertainty (i.e., lack of declarative and procedural knowledge) arising from the integration between the app and the PBR. Uncertainty is defined as “the availability and reliability of the information that is relevant in a given situation.” [37]. App developer organizations face a high level of technological uncertainty undermining their ability to leverage PBRs. For example, soon after the release of ARKIT, several implementations of ARKIT-based features released publicly such as distance measurement and furniture visualization sufered from various technological limitations as app developer organizations did not take into account environmental parameters including lighting, user’s perspective, and user’s balance creating suboptimal apps that sufered in the market [5]. Hence, an important practical question is to understand how app developer organizations can leverage PBRs and improve app performance by mitigating technological uncertainty. PBRs ofer tremendous opportunities for app-level innovation, but if technology uncertainty results in poorly designed apps, then the highly competitive market could significantly erode the apps’ performance.

A platform ecosystem consists of the platform owners and the collection of organizations (i.e., complementors or app developer organizations) that create apps on the platform [26]. It plays a significant role in explaining app-level outcomes such as innovation [15] and performance [34]. Prior literature focuses on competition dynamics in the platform ecosystem and the implications for app-level outcomes. Foerderer et al. [15] find that the entry of complements created by the platform owner itself in the complementary market increases the number of reviews of competing complements. For instance, the entry of Google’s Photos app drove consumer attention on apps in the photography category. Moreover, Liang et al. [34] observe that featured apps benefit similar apps. Consumer awareness of featured apps spills over to similar apps. Finally, Li and Agarwal [32] highlight that the integration between Facebook and Instagram positively influenced the consumer demand of big competing third-party apps while negatively influencing the consumer demand of small competing third-party apps.

While competition dynamics play a major role in understanding organizational outcomes (see [7]), there is limited attention on cooperation dynamics in the platform ecosystem. Cooperation is equally prevalent to, if not more prevalent than, competition in markets [69]. Prior research supports the duality between competition and cooperation indicating that both are interdependent and complementary in such a way that cooperation attributes might impact competitive outcomes [21], and competitive attributes might impact cooperation outcomes [20]. Cooperation is an important aspect of the platform ecosystem [23]. Prior literature highlights cooperation between the platform owners and the complementors. The platform owners share the platform resources with complementors through APIs, facilitating the creation of third-party innovations [27]. The cooperation among complementors is less explored, limiting our understanding of the impact of cooperation dynamics on app-level outcomes.

Following prior studies that highlight the duality between competition and cooperation [38], we emphasize cooperation dynamics in the platform ecosystem. We examine distributed sensemaking about PBRs occurring as app developers interact with platform owners and app developers from diferent organizations to understand how to integrate PBRs with their apps. Distributed sensemaking about PBRs is not merely to understand how the changes fit into existing ideas of current apps, but it can also serve the purpose of providing new ideas for new types of functionalities within an existing app or indeed for a brand-new app—such that sensemaking is a major source of inspiration for further innovation. We distinguish distributed sensemaking<sup>2</sup>—happening at the ecosystem level—from situated sensemaking—occurring at the organizational level. We define the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs as the degree to which app developers create and maintain meaning about PBRs among themselves through interactions with the platform owners and app developers from diferent organizations. While sensemaking is key to resolving uncertain situations [72], there is a dearth of research about the efectiveness of distributed sensemaking at enhancing app-level outcomes. It is unclear whether the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs can influence app-level outcomes irrespective of whether the focal app developer organization participates in the distributed sensemaking process or not. Against this backdrop, we seek to examine the extent to which app developer organizations mitigate PBRs’ technological uncertainty to improve app performance in a hypercompetitive environment by addressing the following research question: what is the efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs on app performance?

Sensemaking allows individuals and organizations to address uncertain situations [72]. To answer the research question, we follow prior research that highlights the existence of diferent sources of uncertainty [67]. We propose a research framework that examines the efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs on app performance across diferent sources of technological uncertainty. We identify two sources of uncertainty related to the developer task environment: one at the app level—app complexity<sup>3</sup>—and the other at the platform level—PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs. Indeed, technological uncertainty related to the integration between the PBR and the app can arise from the complexity of the app or the level of PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs.

We suggest that the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs is crucial to leverage PBRs and realize high app performance. Whether a focal app developer organization participates or not directly in the distributed sensemaking process, we argue that it will benefit as long as other app developer organizations participate in the distributed sensemaking process. The level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs puts app developer organizations—irrespective of whether they participate in the distributed sensemaking process or not—at a better place to resolve technological uncertainty because the option of using shared external understanding exists, and they can forgo it if it is not beneficial for alleviating technological uncertainty. And having such flexibility is important because the distributed sensemaking process has unique characteristics regarding solving technological uncertainty when compared to the situated sensemaking process. Drawing on organizational information processing theory [17], we propose that the relationship between the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs and app performance varies across diferent app and platform contexts. This is because the varying app and platform contexts influence technological uncertainty, creating diferent information processing needs that should require the flexibility ofered by the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs to difering extents. We posit that the efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs on app performance rises based on challenges encountered due to high PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs and app complexity.

We tested the research model by collecting data about PBR-based app innovations released from September 2014 to July 2018. We focus on the iOS platform, a highly dynamic platform, looking at the following PBRs: TouchID, Apple Pay, “Hey Siri,” ARKIT, CoreML, and FaceID. Our findings corroborate our hypotheses, thereby providing guidance on how app developer organizations can leverage PBRs to achieve high app performance through the creation of PBR-based app innovations. We organize the rest of the paper into four sections. First, we present the theoretical background of the study in the second section. Following this, we provide the research model and develop the hypotheses. We describe the research design and report the results of the analyses in the next section. Finally, we discuss the contributions, implications, and limitations of the study.

## Theoretical Background

According to organizational information processing theory (OIPT)—also known as the information processing view of organizations—an organization, to achieve greater performance, must either increase its capacity to process information or reduce the need for information processing in such a way that information processing capabilities meet information processing needs [17]. Uncertainty is a problem that organizations must overcome to increase performance. Galbraith [16, p.5] defines uncertainty as the “diference between the amount of information required to perform a task and the amount of information already possessed by the organization.” Thus, uncertainty increases an organization’s information processing needs (i.e., the amount of information the organization needs to gather and process to execute a task). When an organization faces uncertainty, matching information processing capabilities (i.e., capacity to handle more information and make decisions to improve the execution of a task) with information processing needs enhances its performance [67].

In this study, we focus on technological uncertainty, that is, the uncertainty that app developer organizations face during the integration of PBRs into their apps. Technological uncertainty stems from the app (e.g., app complexity) and the PBR (e.g., PBR’s technological unrelatedness). We use app complexity and PBR’s technological unrelatedness to capture increased information processing needs (e.g., questions about how to solve some coding problems) during the integration of PBRs into apps. Information processing capabilities help organizations cope with technological uncertainty and increased information needs during the integration of PBRs into apps. We use distributed sensemaking as a source of information processing capability that helps organizations access shared understanding about the integration of PBRs into apps. Distributed sensemaking operates as a source of information processing capability because it supports interactions between individuals addressing uncertainty about the integration of PBRs into apps. Moreover, it increases an organization’s capacity to handle more information. For example, it indicates solutions to problems avoiding that organizations rely on trial and error to find a solution.

We propose the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs in an online community is efective at increasing the flexibility of app developer organizations to address such technological uncertainty. In diferent app and platform contexts, technological uncertainty changes as the app developer organizations’ information processing needs either increase or decrease, hence moderating the efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs. We describe sensemaking, technological relatedness, and software complexity to lay out the theoretical foundation of the study. This section provides important insights into how app developer organizations mitigate technological uncertainty regarding PBRs (Online Supplemental Appendix T provides additional content on the theoretical background).

## Distributed Sensemaking in Mobile Platform Ecosystems

In mobile platform ecosystems, sensemaking happens at two levels: (1) internally at the organizational level (i.e., situated sensemaking), and (2) externally at the ecosystem level (i.e., distributed sensemaking). A platform ecosystem includes the platform owners and the collection of organizations that create apps on the platform [54]. Situated sensemaking refers to app developers of the same organization interacting to address uncertainty to maintain common beliefs towards PBRs and integration issues. Distributed sensemaking characterizes app developers of diferent organizations interacting among themselves as well as with the platform owners to create and maintain common beliefs regarding PBR integration. Although app developers create competing apps, their collective interest in resolving uncertainty regarding PBRs translates into greater distributed sensemaking through online forums. Technological uncertainty manifests as app developer organizations collectively seek procedural<sup>4</sup> and declarative<sup>5</sup> knowledge (See Online Supplemental Appendix A). This collective knowledge-seeking enables app developer organizations to address technological uncertainty by increasing the level of distributed sensemaking or shared understanding of PBRs.

Distributed sensemaking occurs through dynamic communicational interactions among heterogeneous app developer organizations of diverse backgrounds. The objective of the dynamic communicational interactions is to collectively seek solutions to solve common problems, hence increasing shared understanding of PBRs (See Online Supplemental Appendix A). These dynamic communicational interactions characterize the “milling” and “keynoting” processes [66]. Distributed sensemaking arises as app developer organizations’ dynamic communicational interactions evolve from chaotic milling interactions into organized keynoting interactions.

Knowledge seeking about PBRs resembles milling as app developer organizations turn to others looking for cues. Milling involves many diferent app developer organizations of diverse backgrounds who interact in a chaotic manner by writing posts to resolve problems. Keynoting replaces milling enabling the creation of a shared understanding of PBRs. Keynoting occurs as solutions emerge overriding the divergent and chaotic opinions of app developer organizations about how to resolve problems about integrating PBRs. In the context of the creation of PBR-based app innovations, milling and keynoting can be detected through the reporting of problems and identification of solutions to problems about integrating PBRs. Hence, we focus on problem-solution pairs to assess the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs. In the Online Supplemental Appendix B.1, we map the seven properties of distributed sensemaking to our context.

The digital innovation literature indicates that socio-cognitive sensemaking—the same as distributed sensemaking—is a critical element of digital innovation processes and outcomes [43]. This occurs as a result of the fluid innovation boundaries, dynamic pairing of problems and solutions, and heterogeneous innovation actors [43]. As PBRs span multiple app categories, any single app developer organization may not be able to understand the scope of PBRs. Moreover, distinct app developer organizations may assign diferent cognitive frames to PBRs. As a result, an app developer organization’s cognition needs to be expanded beyond traditional product features to integrate PBRs. Communicational interactions among app developer organizations with diferent frames increase app developer organizations’ awareness of new cognitive frames. Adopting new cognitive frames enables app developer organizations to pursue new ways to integrate PBRs, creating new PBR-based app innovation opportunities.

Two behaviors contribute to distributed sensemaking. First, app developer organizations seek knowledge in the community of heterogeneous app developer organizations by writing about the problems they encounter. Second, app developer organizations share knowledge in the community of heterogeneous app developer organizations by writing about solutions in response to the described problems. Knowledge seeking and knowledge sharing reduce technological uncertainty about integrating PBRs and creating PBR-based app innovations. This explains why the release of new PBRs generates high trafic of new posting. Digital technologies (e.g., online forums and social media) accelerate distributed sensemaking by facilitating knowledge seeking and sharing [46]. In this study, we examine whether the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs mitigates technological uncertainty about the integration app–PBR, hence increasing app performance.

## Technological Relatedness

Technological relatedness has been used in various contexts including business diversification [58], product portfolio diversification [73], and IT relatedness [65]. In these diferent contexts, technological relatedness represents the degree of similar technological knowledge across firms’ businesses, or products. Through acquisitions and divestitures of businesses, firms can modify the level of technological relatedness within their business portfolio [1]. Researchers have extensively examined the impact of technological relatedness on organizational performance. Previous studies indicate that the level of technological relatedness across business units has a positive efect on corporate performance [64] and joint venture performance [73]. Technological relatedness is important for organizational performance as it facilitates the creation and exploitation of cross-unit synergies [64]. Moreover, it enhances cross-unit knowledge sharing as it increases the similarity of technological knowledge across units [73]. Finally, prior literature indicates that technological relatedness facilitates coordination across units [65] leading to the centralization of business activities such as R&D [4] and IT governance [24] to leverage economies of scope. In this study, we suggest that PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs—a platform-related characteristic —is important to understand the impact of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs on app performance.

Following previous research [58], we define a PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs as the extent to which a given PBR demands technological knowledge distinct from that of previous PBRs. On the one hand, the level of PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs increases technological uncertainty related to the integration between PBR and the app since app developer organizations cannot reuse knowledge about prior PBRs, limiting their information processing capabilities. On the other hand, the level of PBR’s technological relatedness with prior PBRs reduces technological uncertainty related to the integration between PBR and the app as app developer organizations who have experience with prior PBRs can rely on that experience, and those who do not have experience can rely on the level of distributed sensemaking about prior PBRs. Examining PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs enables us to unravel the role of the platform context on the efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs.

## Software Complexity

Software complexity is an important element of software design [11]. A software is a system composed of distinct subsystems that interact. Complexity arises as the number of subsystems increases as well as the interactions among the subsystems [70]. Complexity is defined as “the amount of relevant information that is available in a given situation” [37, p.172]. Moreover, Singh [60, p.340] defines a complex technology as “an applied system whose components have multiple interactions and constitute a non-decomposable whole.” Complexity emphasizes the number of parts of a system and their interconnections making the whole system dificult to understand [70]. The number of parts of the system is highly correlated with the number of interconnections. A system with a high count of parts has a high number of interconnections [70]. As a result, prior research often defines complexity as the number of distinct parts in a given system [75].

Complexity has the advantage of rendering a product dificult to imitate. However, it also makes the design and implementation of the software challenging [70] and increases the uncertainty in the time and efort required to create and maintain the software [14]. Prior research indicates that high product complexity might even reduce product quality [10]. Furthermore, previous studies demonstrate that technological complexity increases the risk of technological failure as organizations face high costs and competency limitations [60]. The creation of a complex product requires a large amount of information to build each part of the product and the connections to other parts of the product. The amount of information required can often exceed the organization’s processing capability as organizations have bounded rationality [59]. Hence, complexity can hinder an organization’s ability to make a rational decision. As software complexity increases, app developer organizations information processing capabilities might reach their limits creating uncertainty during the integration between PBR and the app. Following the concept of bounded rationality, there are limits to the app developer organizations’ information processing capabilities, and the app developer organizations’ ability to extend the app is reduced as app complexity increases.

We consider the role of the level of distributed sensemaking<sup>6</sup> about PBRs. Consistent with OIPT, we argue that the efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs—a source of information processing capability—is contingent on two sources of technological uncertainty: the level of PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs and app complexity. Our hypotheses build on the concept of duality between competition and cooperation. Studies increasingly support the existence of duality between competition and cooperation indicating that both are interdependent and complementary [38]. Markets are characterized by competition and cooperation, and considering the duality between both is crucial to understanding market dynamics [38]. Competition and cooperation simultaneously exist as relationships between cooperators may also be open to competition [22]. Achieving competition outcomes often requires exploring cooperation-related variables [21], and achieving cooperation outcomes often requires examining competitive attributes [20]. A growing number of studies highlight such interactions between competition and cooperation by adopting coopetition and business network perspectives (see [38]). For example, scholars suggest that cooperation may enhance a firm’s ability to compete in markets [63]. Also, in unstable situations, actors might prefer cooperation to reduce uncertainty (e.g. [68]) without ignoring competition [49]. Chen [6] categorizes studies on the competition-cooperation relationship into three groups including co-opetition, competition-oriented cooperation, and cooperation-oriented competition studies. Our research belongs to the last group as it examines the impact of cooperation through distributed sensemaking on competitive outcomes (i.e., ranking).<sup>7</sup>

## Efect of the Level of Distributed Sensemaking about PBRs

PBRs are important resources that app developer organizations can build upon to create app innovations. However, leveraging PBRs entails significant risks as app developer organizations have yet to be familiar with the technology. Following the release of PBRs, app developer organizations explore ways to apply PBRs to the app context to make the app services competitive in the market. The exploration stage is characterized by high technological uncertainty as app developer organizations must make sense of the working of PBRs. Technological uncertainty represents a lack of declarative and procedural knowledge related to the adoption of PBRs.<sup>8</sup> Moreover, app developer organizations must experiment with diferent use cases wherein PBRs can provide value to customers. The success of the exploration stage is dependent upon the degree to which app developer organizations understand PBRs. An adequate level of understanding of PBRs enables app developer organizations to develop app services with high quality and select use cases with high utility for the users. For example, HomeCourt is an example of such apps developed by engineers with a great understanding of the CoreML [29].

An important argument of OIPT is that greater information processing capability generates greater organizational performance [16]. The level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs—a source of information processing capability—is crucial as app developer organizations seek to leverage PBRs by developing new app services with high quality and utility. It increases the flexibility of app developer organizations, irrespective of whether they participate in the process of distributed sensemaking or not, to address uncertainties related to PBRs as these organizations can choose to rely solely on the level of situated sensemaking about PBRs or leverage the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs. Notably, app developers with a superior level of situated sensemaking about PBRs when compared to the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs might choose to rely on the level of situated sensemaking about PBRs while others with an inferior level of situated sensemaking about PBRs might opt for using the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs. Generally, external knowledge is considered valuable when it is unique, that is, distinct from internal knowledge [40]. App developers with an inferior level of situated sensemaking about PBRs are likely to rely on distributed sensemaking to access unique external knowledge that might extend their internal knowledge. With low levels of distributed sensemaking about PBRs, app developers are limited to the level of situated sensemaking about PBRs. This could cause them to miss the collective wisdom of the ecosystem that could alleviate technological uncertainty about PBRs.

As app developer organizations in the ecosystems have distinct interpretive schemes, the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs facilitates the construction of meaning and interpretation about PBRs. Prior literature illustrates the similar benefits of distributed sensemaking in product development [9]. Hence, the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs enables app developer organizations to face technological uncertainty by enhancing app developer organizations’ understanding of the technological capabilities of PBRs. For example, on online forums, app developer organizations can seek knowledge from the ecosystem regarding the working of PBRs by asking questions. Moreover, distributed sensemaking increases the amount of information that organizations can access, thus reducing uncertainty and facilitating rational decision-making [8]. The level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs might enable app developer organizations to detect feasible and optimal actions to leverage PBRs. Also, it facilitates the discovery of meaningful applications of PBRs, enabling the creation of high-quality and utility app services. It enables app developer organizations to respond to novel problems during the development of app services. Prior work associates sensemaking with product innovation [57]. Thus, we hypothesize that.<sup>.</sup>

Hypothesis 1 (H1): The level of distributed sensemaking about a PBR has a positive efect on the performance of apps integrating that PBR.

## Efect of the PBR’s Technological Unrelatedness with Prior PBRs

Any given PBR introduces a level of technological uncertainty that app developer organizations must resolve to create app services with high quality and utility. It is critical app developer organizations acquire technological knowledge about the general working of PBRs and how PBRs can be used to create new app services. The existence of prior PBRs that are related to a given PBR facilitates sensemaking about the PBR as app developer organizations can benefit from their experience if they have used the prior PBRs or other app developer organizations’ experience through the level of distributed sensemaking about the prior PBRs: shared external understanding. However, a PBR that is technologically unrelated to previous PBRs necessitates more learning - acquisition of new knowledge. As app developer organizations explore the PBR and experiment with diferent use cases, they cannot build upon prior experience as the PBR is not similar to any previous PBRs. The level of technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs does not facilitate the creation of synergies among PBRs as it increases the need for app developer organizations to generate new meanings and interpretations. Prior research identifies performance benefits of Information Technology (IT) relatedness [64] and product relatedness [73] to be mainly driven by synergies across business units [64] and knowledge sharing between firms [73]. As a result, app developer organizations are more likely to exploit the level of distributed sensemaking about the focal PBRs, which is a source of information processing capability, to understand PBRs that are technologically unrelated to previous PBRs. Moreover, app developer organizations are more likely to rely on the level of situated sensemaking and not on the level of distributed sensemaking about the focal PBRs when PBRs are technologically related to previous PBRs due to similarities across PBRs in terms of understanding. Hence, consistent with OIPT (i.e., information processing capability is a remedy for uncertainty), the flexibility ofered by the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs to address technological uncertainty matters more when PBRs are not technologically related to prior PBRs. We hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): The efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about a PBR on the performance of apps integrating that PBR is greater when PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs is high.

## Efect of App Complexity

The creation of app services with high quality and utility is particularly challenging when an app is complex. It is easier to understand how parts of an app afect each other when the app is less complex. However, as software complexity rises, developers are increasingly challenged to capture all interdependencies in the software logic [11]. When working with complex apps, app developer organizations face more dificulty trying to understand and resolve app issues that result from interactions among app parts. Large amounts of information are necessary to address app complexity. In situations where software complexity is high, it is strenuous cognitively for developers to understand (1) how software parts are assembled, (2) how the software parts are interrelated, and (3) how such interactions afect the software behavior and performance []. Adding a PBR requires the creation of additional app parts and interactions or the update of existing app parts and interactions.

Complex systems are dificult to manage (See [55]). Maintaining a high degree of reliability in the app behavior is particularly challenging for app developer organizations with complex apps. When adding a PBR in a complex app, app developer organizations are likely to face significantly greater challenges due to competency limitations, thus increasing the risk of app failures and reducing app quality. For example, complex apps are more likely to crash [53]. This follows prior research associating technology complexity with organizational failures (see [60]). The level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs—a source of information processing capability—enhances app developer organizations’ ability to manage complex apps and mitigate technological uncertainty as they can access additional competencies from app developer organizations of diferent organizations. Hence, consistent with OIPT (i.e., information processing capability is a remedy for uncertainty), the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs—by providing more flexibility to address uncertainty related to integrating PBRs—matters more when apps have high complexity. We hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): The efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about a PBR on the performance of apps integrating that PBR is greater when app complexity is high.

## Methods

We observe PBR-based app innovations released on the iOS mobile platform within the U.S. market. We select the iOS mobile platform over other mobile platforms (e.g., Android, and Windows Phone) because, in a given year, the difusion rate of the latest generation of the iOS mobile operating system is high. For example, a year after the release of iOS 11 -the latest generation of iOS in 2017-, the adoption rate passed 85 percent of compatible devices [48]. On the other hand, the adoption rate of Oreo 8.0 (the latest generation of Android in 2017), was less than 15 percent of compatible devices a year after the release date [25]. Since PBR-based app innovations mostly require devices to operate the latest generation of the mobile operating system, it is important to choose a mobile platform whose latest generation difuses quickly. Even though PBR-based app innovations can operate on older generations of some mobile platforms (e.g., Android), it is not appropriate to select these mobile platforms because they are highly fragmented. The iOS mobile platform by far is the least fragmented mobile platform [41].

We observe PBR-based app innovations supporting at least one of the following PBRs: TouchID, Apple Pay, “Hey Siri,” ARKIT, CoreML, and FaceID. The six PBRs were released at diferent times: FaceID, CoreML, and ARKIT in 2017 with iOS 11; “Hey Siri” in 2016 with iOS 10; ApplePay and TouchID in 2014 with iOS 8. We select these six PBRs because they are major technologies that have attracted considerable attention from both iOS developers and users (see [61]). For example, the number of downloads of ARKIT-only apps passed 13 million six months after the release of iOS 11 [44]. Moreover, app developer organizations are more likely to mention the six PBRs in their PBR-based app innovations description and release notes. Thus, we can identify PBR-based app innovations by reading the description and release notes. We apply a keyword search mechanism to identify PBR-based app innovations. We use the following keywords adjusting for the letter case: “touch id,” “touched,” “applepay,” “apple pay,” “siri,” “ARKIT,” “ar kit,” “coreml,” “core ml,” “faceid,” and “face id.” We search through all the release notes starting at the release date of the PBR.

## Data

We collected data from several sources including Apple developer documentation,<sup>9</sup> Apple developer

Forum,<sup>10</sup>, appfigures,<sup>11</sup> and App Annie.<sup>12</sup> The Apple developer documentation provides guidance (e.g., API reference, articles, and sample code) about PBRs. The Apple developer forum facilitates discussion on PBRs among app developer organizations. Appfigures and App Annie are leading companies in app data and analytics. Moreover, they are reliable data sources that have been used in prior literature (e.g., [28]). App developer organizations and analysts use extensively Appfigures and App Annie to analyze the iOS app market. The Appfigures and App Annie databases contain data on iOS apps retrieved from Apple iTunes since 2009.

We use Appfigures’s keyword search engine to identify all iOS apps that support at least one of the following PBRs: TouchID, Apple Pay, “Hey Siri,” ARKIT, CoreML, and FaceID. The search engine goes through apps’ descriptions and release notes to locate the given keywords. Additionally, we collect data about app ranking using the appfigures application programming interface (API). Furthermore, we use the Apple developer forum to gather data about iOS app developer organizations’ discussions on PBRs. The Apple developer documentation provides information about the level of technological unrelatedness of PBRs with prior PBRs. Finally, we collect iOS apps’ descriptions and release notes using App Annie. We use App Annie data to identify the release date of PBR-based app innovations.

Apple provides three major ranking lists including the top 1,000 free apps, the top 1,000 paid apps, and the top 1,000 grossing apps.<sup>13</sup> Considering that there are millions of apps available on the iOS app market, the ranking of an iOS app on any of these three lists provides a clear indication of app performance. App ranking is commonly used to understand app performance [28]. We access iOS apps ranking daily to capture the performance efects of PBR-based app innovations. We later aggregate daily app ranking over 28 days maximum after the release of PBR-based app innovations. We do not go beyond 28 days because app developer organizations in top lists mostly release their app innovations at intervals of one month. In this case, it is important to use periods shorter than 28 days.

We collect data about the Apple developer forum from the release date of the PBRs to app developer organizations till one year later (i.e., from June 2014 to May 2015 for ApplePay and TouchID, from June 2016 to May 2017 for “Hey Siri,” and from June 2017 to May 2018 for CoreML, ARKIT, and FaceID) because the Apple developer forum does not diferentiate between the original (i.e., the 1<sup>st</sup> version) PBR and the subsequent updates (i.e., later versions). For example, as Apple updates ARKIT (ARKIT in 2017, ARKIT 2 in 2018, ARKIT 3 in 2019, and ARKIT 4 in 2020), the Apple developer forum does diferentiate between all the versions. Hence, it becomes dificult to identify the posts about ARKIT after ARKIT 2 or another later version is released.

Moreover, we focus on PBR-based app innovations released not more than one year after the date when the PBRs are released to app developer organizations to ensure that the PBRbased app innovations are not integrating later versions of the PBRs. The earliest PBR-based app innovations are released the following September after the release of the PBRs to app developer organizations. Hence, PBR-based app innovations supporting ApplePay and TouchID are identified from September 2014 to May 2015, those supporting “Hey Siri” are identified from September 2016 to May 2017, and those supporting CoreML, ARKIT, and FaceID are identified from September 2017 to May 2018.

The final dataset comprises 933 PBR-based app innovations for which we can have complete data on key variables of interest (e.g., ranking, distributed sensemaking, PBR’s technological unrelatedness, and app complexity).<sup>14</sup> We do not include new apps as we cannot measure prior-release ranking. The number of observations with TouchID, Apple Pay, “Hey Siri,” ARKIT, CoreML, and FaceID is 257, 31, 29, 62, 10, and 544, respectively.

## Measures

## Dependent Variable

We use a pre-post design to measure the performance of iOS apps (See Online Supplemental Appendix C). We compute the average app ranking before and after the release date of the PBR-based app innovation. We calculate the average post-release ranking over 28 days after the release date of the PBR-based app innovation. Moreover, we assess the average pre-release ranking throughout 7 days before the release date of the PBR-based app innovation.<sup>15</sup> We measure app performance by calculating the diference between the average post-release app ranking and the average pre-release app ranking. Furthermore, we use diferent periods as robustness checks. The results remain consistent across these periods (See Robustness Checks Section). Since we are using a pre-post design, our sample of PBR-based app innovations does not include new apps as these apps do not have app ranking before the release date.

## Independent Variables

Sensemaking has been extensively used in the management literature [57, 72]. However, there are few objective measures of sensemaking in prior literature. We propose an innovative approach to objectively measure the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs. Prior research suggests text-based analyses (e.g., word count analysis) of conversations among individuals can reveal patterns of social interactions [50]. Text-based analyses can ofer insights independently of the semantic context or the literal meaning [51]. For example, patterns of words usage can be used to identify cognition [46]. Following Pennebaker’s work, prior research suggests a word-based analysis can provide a highlevel picture of distributed sensemaking in uncertain situations [47].

In the Online Supplemental Appendix P, we discuss why activities on the Apple developer forum can be qualified as distributed sensemaking. The level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs represents the shared understanding about PBRs among app developer organizations and occurs through the pairing of problem-solution. New problems—defined as problems that are not similar to prior resolved problems—indicate a lack of shared understanding. The level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs flattens when there are (1) no additional problems posted, (2) additional problems but with no solution given, or (3) solutions to additional problems that are not new problems. We use a text similarity measure based on words usage across diferent texts to measure similarity among problems reported through posts. More specifically, we use the text-similarity score among posts to assess the evolution of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs over the period following the release of the PBRs. Our text similarity score measures how text similarity among posts evolves as new posts come out. As a result, we can measure the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs over time starting at the release date of the PBRs (See Online Supplemental Appendix B). Our measure is particularly robust as it can capture subcomponents of distributed sensemaking called milling and keynoting (see [66]).<sup>16</sup> Distributed sensemaking evolves from a chaotic milling phase into a stable keynoting phase. In our study, milling is reflected early after the release of PBRs through new unresolved problems (i.e., problems with low similarity and no solution). As distributed sensemaking evolves into the keynoting phase, solutions to problems emerge, and the newness of problems decreases.

We use the posts written by app developer organizations on the Apple developer forums (See Online Supplemental Appendix A). App developer organizations engaging in distributed sensemaking first search the forums looking for a post similar to their concern. Second, if they cannot find a post that might address their concern, then they will write a new post. Also, it can happen that app developer organizations write posts that are similar to prior answered posts (i.e., posts with solutions). Hence, there are two key indicators of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs. The first indicator is the number of resolved problems (i.e., helpful<sup>17</sup> posts) relative to each PBR at a specific point in time (Eq. 1). The second indicator is the newness of resolved problems (i.e., the distance between a resolved problem and prior resolved problems) relative to each PBR at a specific point in time (Eq. 2). For each PBR-based app innovation, we measure the level of distributed sensemaking about the PBR at a specific time corresponding to the release date of the PBR-based app innovation. For the first indicator, we count the number of resolved problems relative to the PBR before the release date of the PBR-based app innovation. And regarding the second indicator, we calculate the average newness of resolved problems relative to the PBR before the release date of the PBR-based app innovation. Specifically, first, we weight each resolved problem appearing before the release date of the PBR-based app innovation using a newness index. The weight corresponds to 1 minus the similarity index representing the highest cosine similarity score<sup>18</sup> between a given resolved problem and all previously resolved problems. Second, for aggregation purposes, we sum all the resolved problems with their respective weights. Third, we divide the weighted sum by the total number of resolved problems relative to the PBR before the release date of the PBR-based app innovation to calculate the second indicator of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs (See Online Supplemental Appendix Q for more details).

Furthermore, we use a dummy variable to measure the level of PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs. The dummy variable takes a value of 1 if the PBR has a high level of technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs, and a value of 0 otherwise. We determine the value of the dummy following a two-step approach. First, based on Apple developer documentation [3], we create a list of frameworks (See Online Supplemental

Appendix D). Second, we determine the frameworks that contain each of the 6 PBRs studied in this paper. For example, we found that FaceID and TouchID belong to the Local Authentication framework. In total, we identify 5 frameworks. Third, for each PBR, we identify whether the framework was created at the same time as the PBR or not. We code 1 if that is the case or 0 otherwise. When the PBR and framework are created at the same time, it means that the PBR is the first in the framework, thus there is not a technologically related prior PBR. However, when the PBR is created after the framework, meaning that the PBR joins an existing framework, it signifies that there is(are) already existing PBR(s) in the framework, hence there is a technologically related prior PBR. Because the PBRs that belong to the same framework have a similar implementation, we consider them to be technologically related. We use the dummy code to represent the level of PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs. Hence, FaceID and Apple Pay have a low level of technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs because they joined existing frameworks (i.e., the Local Authentication and the PassKit frameworks respectively) and are technologically related to prior PBRs (i.e., TouchID and Apple Passbook respectively). Moreover, ARKIT, CoreML, “Hey Siri,” and TouchID have a high level of technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs since they were created at the same time as their respective frameworks. In our sample, the numbers of PBR-based app innovations integrating PBRs with low and high levels of technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs are 575 and 358, respectively.

Finally, we use a composite measure to determine app complexity. Since we do not have access to the code of the iOS apps, we conduct an exploratory factor analysis including various metrics that could explain app complexity. We find that the size of the code file, the number of SDKs used by the app, the number of permissions requested by the app load on the same factor. As a composite factor, they collectively represent app complexity (the number of parts of the app and their interactions). The results of the exploratory factor analysis indicating the factors, the eigenvalues, and the factor loadings are illustrated in Online Supplemental Appendix E. We estimate the composite factor which we consider to be a measure of app complexity. We assess app complexity as of July 2018.<sup>19</sup>

## Control Variables

We control for variables that may influence app performance. Since PBRs are heterogeneous, the type of PBR can explain some variance in the app performance. The extent to which a PBR-based app innovation attracts users depends on the users’ interest in the PBR. For example, if users are more interested in “FaceID” compared to “ARKIT,” we expect FaceID-based app innovations to have a greater efect on app performance than ARKITbased app innovations. Hence, we control for each type of PBRs including TouchID, Apple Pay, “Hey Siri,” ARKIT, CoreML, and FaceID. Also, we use several app-level control variables. We measure the number of categories, languages, and countries per app. These variables afect the discoverability of the app, hence influencing app performance. It is easier for iOS users to discover apps that span diferent categories, support multiple languages, and are accessible in diferent countries. Moreover, we include the app’s entry timing as prior research argues that entry timing is critical for performance [18]. Furthermore, we measure the number of and the average app ratings controlling for the efect of app popularity, app usability, and app quality on app performance as apps with high popularity, usability and quality are more likely to attract iOS users. We consider the efect of app seniority on app performance by calculating the number of days between the first release date of the app and the release date of the PBR-based app innovation. iOS users are more likely to use senior apps because they are more familiar with senior apps than recent apps. Also, app seniority has been used to characterize app development superiority, marketing capabilities, and brand strength [28]. Additionally, we control for the efect of app price on app performance as low app prices attract more iOS users. We measure the price, the number of categories, languages, and countries per app as of July 2018.<sup>20</sup> Finally, we use dummy variables to control for the types of ranking (i.e., handheld versus tablet, and top free versus top paid versus top grossing), year-specific efects, app category-specific efects. We report the descriptive statistics and the correlation matrix in Table 3. Moreover, we present the results of the analyses in Table 4. An increase of one standard deviation in the number of resolved problems is associated with a gain in the app ranking corresponding to 150 positions. Additionally, one standard deviation growth in the newness of resolved problems is associated with a gain in the app ranking corresponding to 85 positions. Hence, the first hypothesis is supported, showing that irrespective of the participation of the focal app developer organization in the process of distributed sensemaking, the level of distributed sensemaking about a PBR has a positive impact on the performance of the app integrating that PBR.

## Robustness Checks

We conduct several robustness checks to ensure that our main findings are robust to issues such as omitted variables, measurement errors, and endogeneity. We demonstrate that our findings are robust to alternative measures of app performance (see Online Supplemental Appendix H), and resolved problems newness<sup>23</sup> (see Online Supplemental Appendix S). Moreover, we use Heckman’s two-stage approach to control for endogeneity (see Online Supplemental Appendix I). Additionally, we run analyses wherein we reduce the sample to app innovations based on either TouchID or FaceID (See Online Supplemental Appendix J). To reduce identification issues, we conduct diference-in-diference analyses (See Online Supplemental Appendix K). Furthermore, we use app downloads as an alternative measure of app performance (see Online Supplemental Appendix L). To rule out issues about price fluctuations, we reduce the sample to free apps (See Online Supplemental Appendix M). Also, we control for the diferentiation of the PBR-based app innovations (see Online Supplemental Appendix N), and the number of questions without solutions and replies (see Online Supplemental Appendix R). Finally, we follow the suggestion of Anderson and Hsiao [2] and estimate a dynamic model using an instrumental variable estimator (see Online Supplemental Appendix O).

## Discussion

## Research Contributions

Our study provides important research contributions. First, we contribute to the literature on intra-platform competition which examines factors that determine app performance (e.g., [75]). The service ecosystem plays an important role in service delivery, but there is a lack of understanding of the specificities of such a role. While prior literature on platforms focuses on institutional logics in service ecosystems [52], there is a minimal amount of emphasis on the interactions among service actors. The interactive aspect of the service ecosystem is particularly important as it supports information seeking, ofering, and sharing [47]. We unravel the role of the platform ecosystem in a mobile platform context by emphasizing distributed sensemaking occurring through interactions among the app developer organizations and platform owners (i.e., service actors). We highlight cooperative dynamics in the service ecosystem. While service actors (e.g., app developer organizations) are naturally competitors, we unveil how they collaborate through distributed sensemaking to mitigate technological uncertainty when leveraging PBRs.

Our findings indicate that such a type of collaboration is critical to explain app performance and understand how app developer organizations leverage PBRs. We support the existence of duality between competition and cooperation confirming that both are crucial to understanding market dynamics. A rich means of interaction is not necessary for distributed sensemaking. Despite the limitations of lean text-based interactions [12], the existing shared context among app developers makes it possible to rely on these lean interactions to facilitate distributed sensemaking (see [45]). Hence, we contribute to the literature on intra-platform competition by shedding light on cooperation at the ecosystem level on app performance. By emphasizing sensemaking on iOS developer forums, we unveil the role of the community of developers on app performance through their contributions to sensemaking. Theoretically and empirically, we highlight the influence of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs on the performance of apps integrating PBRs through PBR-based app innovations. Our study provides a significant contribution to the literature on app performance as app developer organizations face growing competition in mobile platform ecosystems, struggling to survive [13].

Second, we contribute to the literature that investigates the role of PBRs on app performance [27, 35]. This line of research indicates PBRs have both positive [27], and negative [35] efects on app performance. Prior research suggests that platform transitions from one generation to a newer generation negatively influence app performance due to platform APIs creating interdependencies between the apps and the core platform [35]. Changes in platform APIs caused by platform transitions deteriorate app developers’ ability to sustain their performance [28]. Similarly, the app’s average rating diminishes as a result of API change proneness and API fault proneness [35]. Moreover, prior research finds a positive impact of PBRs on complementary innovation. Platforms APIs impact app novelty by influencing imitation and innovation [74]. Also, platform toolkits increase app developers’ intention to innovate and the quantity of innovation [27]. We extend prior literature about the role of PBRs on app performance by proposing a theoretical framework about the efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs. By doing so, we provide an interesting perspective about how app developer organizations leverage PBRs (i.e., toolkits) and increase app performance. Our research unveils that whether platform innovations exert a positive or negative efect on app performance might depend on the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs.

Third, we contribute to the literature on sensemaking which examines the efect of sensemaking on organizational performance [57]. Sensemaking is important as shared meaning about uncertain situations afects individuals’ interpretations of actions and events [72]. Previous studies indicate sensemaking is a source of competitive advantage highlighting it is an internal resource (i.e., organizational sensemaking) that is critical to cope with complex and turbulent environments [57]. We study sensemaking as an external resource (i.e., distributed sensemaking) occurring in an online community of app developer organizations who work together toward a shared understanding of the integration between PBRs and apps (See Online Supplemental Appendix P). Our paper extends prior studies that describe distributed sensemaking in online communities [36, 42]. We demonstrate that distributed sensemaking (i.e., cooperation) matters in an environment characterized by high competition (i.e., entities potentially preferring to rely on situated sensemaking). Also, we propose a way to quantitatively evaluate distributed sensemaking in online communities.

Finally, since we anchor our research model on OIPT, we contribute to that theory by showing that distributed sensemaking can be an efective information processing capability leading to greater app performance when information processing needs increase due to technology uncertainty about the integration of PBRs. Furthermore, we unveil the role of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs considering diferent sources of technological uncertainty. Our contribution is to demonstrate theoretically and empirically that distributed sensemaking is important besides the efect of organizational sensemaking shown in previous studies [57]. Unraveling the role of distributed sensemaking provides an adequate picture of the impact of sensemaking as it complements previous studies that focus on the efect of organizational sensemaking [57].

## Practical Implications

Mobile platform ecosystems are hypercompetitive environments requiring app developer organizations to innovate to remain relevant to app users [56]. While app developer organizations can innovate using only internal resources (i.e., proprietary toolkits), they are increasingly using PBRs to create new app services. Mitigating the risks surrounding the implementation of PBRs is critical to ensure app performance. Our study provides important practical implications about how app developer organizations can leverage PBRs to realize higher app performance.

First, our findings inform the allocation of resources suggesting an approach to overcome the limitations of internal resources (i.e., proprietary toolkits) to cope with technological uncertainty about the integration app - PBR. App developer organizations on mobile platform ecosystems are mostly small organizations. They are limited in the resources they can assign to the development of apps. As a result, they are more and more dependent on external resources (e.g., third-party toolkits). We suggest that app developer organizations must utilize knowledge shared on online forums about PBRs to complement their internal knowledge. App developer organizations tend to release their app upgrades early, soon after the release of PBRs, with the intent of benefiting from first-mover advantages. As this strategy might seem profitable, it requires app developer organizations to rely mostly on organizational sensemaking about PBRs. As a result, there is a risk that app developer organizations create app innovations with low quality because they do not have a clear understanding of PBRs. Our findings indicate that app developer organizations do not benefit from taking that risk. Hence, we recommend app developer organizations make decisions about entry timing based on the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs. App developer organizations should release app innovations when they are not facing high technological uncertainty resulting from high app complexity or high PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs, or the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs is acceptable. We encourage app developer organizations to include online communities of app developer organizations in their innovation strategy by monitoring exchanges about specific PBRs. This is particularly important for small teams of developers as they are more likely to have limited resources.

Second, our findings inform app developer organizations about optimal product strategies to leverage PBRs. Mobile platform owners release yearly various types of PBRs such as machine learning (e.g., CoreML by Apple, ML Kit by Google), AR (e.g., ARKIT by Apple, ARCore by Google), and voice assistant (e.g., SiriKit by Apple, voice interaction API by Google). Moreover, these PBRs have diferent levels of technological unrelatedness with previous PBRs. Our research suggests that the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs is less efective when PBRs have low technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs. Hence, app developer organizations that plan to release app upgrades early should choose PBRs with low technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs (e.g., FaceID). They should delay their app upgrades release plans in case they select PBRs with high technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs (e.g., TouchID). Furthermore, our findings inform app design choices to leverage PBRs. App developer organizations follow diferent app design approaches. Some app developer organizations prefer creating single-purpose apps (e.g., WhatsApp) while others opt for building multi-purpose apps (e.g., WeChat). App complexity increases as app developer organizations create apps supporting more purposes and features. Our research indicates that the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs is less efective when app complexity is low. Thus, we encourage app developer organizations with complex apps (e.g., multi-purpose apps, apps with a high number of features) to rely on the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs, even if they might not participate directly in the process of distributed sensemaking – and release app upgrades late when the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs is at an adequate level. App developer organizations whose strategy is to release app upgrades early must reduce app complexity by diminishing the number of purposes and features supported by the app.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several empirical limitations that can inform future research. First, we limited our sample to apps that explicitly mention the PBRs they support in their release notes. We reviewed the release notes of app upgrades, and we identified several PBRs including “Hey Siri,” CoreML, ARKIT, TouchID, FaceID, and Apple Pay. Then, we collected apps that support the six PBRs. We are unable to observe additional PBRs that are less likely to be mentioned in the app upgrade release notes. Hence, future research might extract all PBRs supported by the app directly via the code if the researchers have access to the app code.

Another approach could be to survey app developer organizations even though it is not certain they are willing to disclose the PBRs they support. Despite our reduced sample size of PBRs, our findings are robust as we control for the type of PBRs.

Second, we measure the short-term impact of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs by observing app performance 1 week before and 4 weeks after the app innovation release date. Since app ranking is very volatile [28], we aggregate the daily app ranking per week(s) up to one week before and one month after the app innovation release date. We choose to limit this study to the short-term impact of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs because there are additional factors over time that create noise in the assessment of the long-term impact. For example, some app developer organizations release app upgrade once per month, thus increasing the identification problem. Future research could tackle the challenge of assessing the long-term impact of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs by conducting experiments wherein researchers can control the environment.

Third, even though our measures of app performance, app complexity, and technological unrelatedness have some theoretical [10, 28], and practical [3] basis, they could benefit from a more fine-grained measurement. We ofer some suggestions for future research. We propose that future measures of app performance may capture the costs of app innovations, the revenues, and profits generated by app innovations. Even though app ranking reflects the performance of an app relative to other apps, it excludes unpopular or low-performing apps and does not capture costs incurred by apps. Future studies may also explore outcomes related to the labor market [33].

Fourth, our study is not assessing integration issues between PBRs and non-iOS APIs within apps. Our data collection focuses on PBRs and iOS APIs. We consider the significance of non-iOS APIs by including the number of SDKs installed in the calculation of app complexity. Future studies might attempt to directly account for integration issues between PBRs and non-iOS APIs by collecting additional data on diferent online developer forums. Fifth, future research might extend our findings by measuring the extent to which each app developer organization consumes distributed sensemaking. This might require surveying app developer organizations about the level of use of distributed sensemaking (e.g., Apple developer forum). The survey might collect data about the extent to which app developer organizations implement the recommended solutions—found in the Apple developer forum—in their apps. Our study will benefit from such findings as we focus on the flexibility—to use or not use distributed sensemaking—that app developer organizations have when the level of distributed sensemaking is high.

Finally, we suggest researchers who have access to the app code should measure app complexity instead of relying on the composite measure of app complexity. For example, McCabe’s cyclomatic complexity factor measures the understandability and intricacy of software projects [39] and has been used in studies on open-source projects to assess the code’s structural complexity [11]. Additionally, measuring the level of coupling and modularity of the app code will help evaluate app complexity. Regarding PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs, future research can develop a diferent measure using a continuous scale. Our measure of technological unrelatedness is a categorical variable based on the platform owner’s classification. Future measures can assess the level of reusability of the code from previous PBRs. They can also evaluate the level of similarity with previous PBRs code.

## Conclusion

In the highly competitive complementor market, how app developer organizations can efectively innovate by leveraging PBRs can make the diference between success and failure. We bring to researchers’ attention the risks app developer organizations face when they choose to support PBRs. We propose that the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs, regardless of whether the focal app developer organization participates in the distributed sensemaking process or not, is efective at reducing technological uncertainty regarding the app–PBR integration, hence increasing the performance of apps integrating the PBRs. By focusing on cooperation dynamics in the platform ecosystem, our research extends prior literature which mainly considers sensemaking to be an internal resource. We demonstrate that the efect of the level of distributed sensemaking about PBRs is contingent on two sources of technological uncertainty including the level of PBR’s technological unrelatedness with prior PBRs and app complexity. Our study provides both theoretical and practical implications for how complementors can efectively leverage PBRs.
