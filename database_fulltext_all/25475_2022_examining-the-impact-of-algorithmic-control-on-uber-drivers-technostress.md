# Examining the Impact of Algorithmic Control on Uber Drivers’ Technostress

W. Alec Cram <sup>a</sup>, Martin Wiener<sup>b</sup>, Monideepa Tarafdar<sup>c</sup>, and Alexander Benlian<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Accounting & Finance, University of Waterloo, Waterloo, ON, Canada; <sup>b</sup>Technische Universität Dresden, Dresden, Germany; <sup>c</sup>Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts Amherst, Amherst MA; <sup>d</sup>Technische Universität Darmstadt, Darmstadt, Germany

## ABSTRACT

This study examines how the use of algorithmic control within gig economy platforms relates to the well-being and behavior of workers. Specifically, we explore how two diferent forms of algorithmic control —gatekeeping and guiding—correspond with (positive) challenge technostressors and (negative) threat technostressors experienced by Uber drivers. We also examine the moderating impact of algorithmic control transparency on these relationships, as well as the outcomes of technostressors in terms of continuance intentions and workaround use. Based on a survey of 621 U.S.-based Uber drivers, we find that gatekeeping and guiding algorithmic control positively relate to both challenge and threat technostressors. The study bridges the literature on control and technostress by conceptualizing algorithmic control as a condition that puts workers under stress. This stress is found to contribute to important behavioral consequences pertaining to both continuance intentions and workaround use. Findings from our work suggest that gig economy organizations can use algorithmic control to enhance challenge technostressors for their workers, thereby contributing to the cultivation of a more committed workforce. Furthermore, we find evidence disputing the assumption that algorithmic control transparency can mitigate the negative efects of threat technostressors.

## KEYWORDS

Algorithmic control; Uber drivers; technostress; challenge technostressors; threat technostressors; algorithmic control transparency; gig economy

## Introduction

Digital sensors and algorithmic decision-making are altering the way that organizations set work rules, monitor worker activities, provide feedback to workers, and enforce compliance [23, 64]. This approach, termed algorithmic control, enables organizations to guide and influence day-to-day worker behavior using technology rather than human managers [71]. However, by doing so, organizations are faced with uncertainty, not only in terms of how efectively worker behavior is influenced by the digital algorithms, but also in terms of the corresponding impact on worker well-being (e.g., stress, overload, enjoyment, challenge) [30].

These organizational challenges are epitomized within the context of gig economy platforms, such as the ridesharing service Uber, where worker behavior is controlled through interconnected applications that utilize high-intensity digital algorithms. Gig economy platforms represent a unique case for the application of algorithmic control, since workers are often contractors instead of formal employees. Such organizations rely exclusively on technology to facilitate interactions between providers and consumers of services, and to track and steer worker actions [25, 89, 111]. For example, Uber uses technology to screen driver eligibility to work on the platform, direct drivers to pick up passengers, facilitate periodic driver background checks, provide guidance to drivers on how to improve performance, and discipline drivers who are not meeting minimum standards.

Algorithmic control difers from traditional forms of control in that the control originates from algorithms, the delivery of the control to the worker is provided by a technology interface (e.g., smartphone app) rather than by a human manager, and the control can be applied on a continual, real-time basis. These distinctive attributes underlying the use of algorithmic control provide a novel context to explore worker well-being and behavior. As organizations increasingly rely on technology, rather than human actors, to initiate, deliver, and apply control, it is critical to determine if the changes serve to help or harm workers. This view of algorithms “doing” the controlling is one of several elements (others include matching workers with customers and work tasks) that make up the broader concept of algorithmic management, which is referred to as “the large-scale collection and use of data on a platform to develop and improve learning algorithms that carry out coordination and control functions traditionally performed by managers” [71, p. 2001].

As the use of algorithmic control in organizations has grown, so have accounts of the varied ways they are perceived by individual workers. On the one hand, algorithmic control can have beneficial efects by providing timely, customized feedback on how workers are performing on the job and what actions they could take to improve [27]. On the other hand, algorithmic control has also been associated with negative impacts on worker well-being, such as increasing levels of anxiety associated with constant surveillance and the risk of being automatically fired for underperformance [50, 89]. Although recent research [3, 63, 70, 98] has increasingly considered the potential “dark side” of relying on technology to communicate and implement rules and workflows within organizations, there has been relatively little focus on the potentially positive impacts that algorithmic technologies may have. In order to reconcile these diferent viewpoints, this research is motivated by the inherent tension caused by organizations that employ automated technology systems to eficiently monitor and control worker behavior, while remaining mindful of the uncertain impact that such technology systems can have on worker well-being.

To investigate this issue, we draw on the technostress literature, which has conceptualized both positive technostressors (e.g., challenges that motivate workers) and negative stressors (e.g., threats to workers) [97]. In this regard, we consider two diferent forms of algorithmic control as sources of (positive) challenge technostressors and (negative) threat technostressors: gatekeeping algorithmic control, which refers to the screening and monitoring of workers permitted to start or continue working for a company; and guiding algorithmic control, which refers to overseeing and guiding day-to-day worker behavior, including performance feedback 111. We further consider the role of algorithmic control transparency, in terms of how the relationship between algorithmic control and technostressors could change when workers have a clear (or obscured) view of the rationale behind an algorithm’s use. Finally, we examine the propensity for Uber drivers to continue working for a platform and to pursue system workarounds when they experience algorithmic control-driven technostress. These behavioral consequences are particularly important since platform owners are increasingly concerned about rapid worker attrition and intentional manipulation of gig applications by workers [68, 70]. As such, this research aims to understand the impact of algorithmic control use within gig economy platforms on workers. Specifically, we pose two research questions: 1) How does algorithmic control afect Uber driver behavior via technostressors? 2) How does algorithmic control transparency shape the influence of algorithmic control on technostressors?

This research makes a key theoretical contribution by integrating the concept of algorithmic control with both positive and negative forms of technostress. Past research on technostress has examined a range of specific jobs, including librarians [104], nurses [12], and sales professionals [99], as well as particular countries [103, 106]. These and related studies suggest that the phenomenon of technostress is closely tied to the nature of work and new forms of work warrant examination of novel sources of technostressors [97]. Our novel focus is on the conceptualization of algorithmic control as an antecedent of technostressors, which we investigate in the context of Uber drivers. Our research theoretically integrates the control and technostress literature to conceptualize algorithmic control as a condition that Uber drivers face and that is associated with challenge and threat technostressors. We further examine the moderating role of algorithmic control transparency as a mechanism for workers to appraise the demands from the algorithmic control that they are faced with. Overall, this study extends the ongoing discussion on algorithmic control [e.g., 50, 82, 91] by showing how its use within online gig-economy platforms can impact both worker well-being (in terms of their experience of threat and challenge technostressors) and behavior (in terms of their continuance intention and workaround use). It further answers emerging calls for research examining how algorithmic transparency relates to worker well-being [71, 92]. From a practical perspective, our study provides valuable insights for organizations responsible for designing and implementing algorithms by highlighting the consequences that control design choices have on their workforce.

## Conceptual Foundations

Our study draws on past research from two foundational areas: control and technostress. We begin by considering the existing conceptualizations of control, in terms of its history within the study of information systems (IS), as well as its unique application in an algorithmic and gig economy platform context. We then provide an overview of the process of technostress, including the possible sources, appraisal, and outcomes. Similar to related studies [e.g., 16, 71], our study uses Uber, the global ride-sharing company, as the contextualized example of a prominent gig economy platform that relies extensively on algorithmic control as part of its day-to-day operations [47, 89].

## Information Systems Control and Algorithmic Control

Control is the attempt by managers to influence the behavior of workers to act in a manner that creates value and is consistent with organizational objectives [49, 51, 75, 111]. Within IS, control has been frequently studied within the context of systems development projects, including the choices made by managers in terms of “what” controls to configure, “how” to enact those controls, and “why” controls are put into place [22, 111, 112, 113]. In regard to “what” controls are used, the control mode framework has been of particular interest to researchers, due to its classification of control mechanisms into groupings of either formal controls (i.e., input, behavior, output) or informal controls (i.e., clan, self) [17, 52]. Since this traditional conceptualization of control assumes both a human manager and human subordinate, organizations that utilize algorithms as a proxy for the manager are utilizing a distinct form of control, which we conceptualize as algorithmic control. Specifically, algorithmic control refers to an organization’s attempt to influence worker behavior by using digital algorithms to enact the control (e.g., to identify a worker behavior that needs to change) and then delivering the control to the worker by a technology interface (e.g., smartphone app). Algorithmic control is also distinct from traditional forms of control in that it can be facilitated on a continual, real-time basis. Further, we distinguish the concept of algorithmic control from traditional internal controls that are embedded in software programs (e.g., ERP systems) on the basis that algorithmic control typically relies on a range of ubiquitous and embedded technologies (e.g., mobile apps, smartphones, sensors) interconnected through underlying algorithms and thus ofers a more dynamic and sophisticated level of worker monitoring [63, 89].

Within the context of gig economy platforms, the algorithmic control research conducted to date has primarily focused on two forms 111. The first, gatekeeping algorithmic control, is used by platforms to restrict worker access. This includes permission to join the platform as a worker (e.g., verifying that a prospective ride-sharing driver owns an approved vehicle), as well as continued access to the platform (e.g., ensuring the driver maintains a clean criminal record). The second form, guiding algorithmic control, is used to oversee day-to-day worker behavior and encourage performance targets to be met. Refer to Table 1 for details.

In this context, questions are being raised in terms of how algorithmic control, which drives the work of gig economy platforms, can impact worker well-being. Since algorithmic control removes almost all human-to-human contact by “automating” the role of a traditional manager [23], Uber drivers are efectively directed, evaluated, and disciplined by the algorithms [50]. For some workers, this approach can be perceived as positively impacting their well-being in terms of facilitating learning through real-time feedback on performance and suggestions on how to improve [27]. Also, algorithmic control can serve to motivate and encourage some workers by reminding them of performance targets, such as a level of desired earnings [23]. Still, algorithmic control can also negatively afect worker well-being. For example, algorithms commonly employ sensors, biometrics, natural language processors, and text analytics to monitor worker behavior, which can lead to fears of discipline and privacy invasions [50, 89]. Potential challenges emerge from such algorithmsubordinate interactions, such as work overload (e.g., algorithms demanding more than a worker can handle) and complexity (e.g., workers finding it dificult to understand what the algorithms are demanding from them) [71, 89].

An important factor that influences the association with algorithmic control and worker well-being is the level of transparency (or lack thereof) that workers are provided in regard to the algorithmic control. For example, the justification for instructions communicated to Uber drivers is often viewed as ambiguous by workers, which can lead to frustration [71]. Yet the impact of algorithmic control transparency on worker well-being and behavior remains unclear [50, 92]. In order to further examine how workers are afected by the increasing use of algorithmic control, we focus on its relationship to technostress, which we discuss next.

## Technostress

Technostress has emerged as an important representation of the well-being related efects of IS use on individuals in the work context, which we consider in light of the increasing use of algorithmic control. Broadly, technostress refers to stress that is experienced by workers due to an inability to cope with technology demands [9, 86, 100]. The prevalence of technostress is often attributed to the increasing dependence on and complexity of technology, alongside the ongoing changes the technology introduces to organizational life [86, 101]. The transaction-based model of stress frames technostress as a multi-step process, consisting of environmental conditions, a primary appraisal by an individual of the conditions, the experiences of technostressors by the individual, the coping response (including a secondary appraisal of the response), and the resulting strain [97] (refer to Table 2 for details).

A variety of environmental and situational factors have been identified as demand conditions that can give rise to technostress. For example, Ayyagari et al. [4] consider the role that technology characteristics have in influencing stressors, including aspects of usability (e.g., complexity), pace of change, and intrusiveness (e.g., anonymity). Organizational conditions can also play a role, such as the degree of centralization and innovation of the firm [106].

These demand conditions are then appraised as being stressful in a positive or negative way [12, 97], such that an individual experiences technostressors, which represent technology-related conditions that create stress. For example, negative technostressors are threatening, in terms of techno-overload, techno-uncertainty, and techno-complexity [86, 101]. However, there is increasing consideration given to the possibility of positive technostressors [12, 14, 24]. For example, Tarafdar et al. [97] bifurcate the phenomenon of technostress into a positively oriented form (i.e., techno-eustress that is driven by challenge technostressors) and a negatively oriented form (i.e., techno-distress that is driven by threat technostressors). In the former, individuals appraise technology as providing a positive challenge and the potential for beneficial outcomes. Benlian [6] considers how technologydriven challenge stressors at work can positively afect employee relationships with partners at home.

Upon perceiving technostressors, individuals engage in a secondary appraisal where they evaluate the response options that they have available. This appraisal can subsequently influence the individual’s coping response, which can take the form of afect or action [28, 97]. For example, an individual facing a threat technostressor associated with a dificult software application may have access to a training program and this prompts them to enroll in a class that can help manage the stress.

The final stage is concerned with behavioral and psychological outcomes. Much past work has been oriented around negative behavioral outcomes such as employee turnover [86] and decreased productivity [100], as well as negative psychological outcomes that include job dissatisfaction and burnout [4, 20, 86, 100]. Although investigations of positive behavioral and psychological outcomes have been conceptualized [86], empirical evidence remains scarce and represents a promising opportunity for investigation [6, 12, 97, 99, 103].

## Research Model

Our research model (see Figure 1) builds on and integrates the control and technostress concepts previously outlined to extend theory and practice in several unique directions. First, we conceptualize algorithmic control as a new environmental condition that can lead to the individual’s experience of technostressors (see Table 2). Specifically, we hypothesize relationships between algorithmic control and both challenge technostressors and threat technostressors. Second, we examine the concept of algorithmic control transparency as an element of the primary appraisal process, in terms of the role that it plays in afecting a worker’s view of algorithmic control as a positive or negative technostressor. Finally, we consider the behavioral impacts of technostressors in terms of both compliant and noncompliant worker consequences, which we operationalize as continuance intention and workaround use, respectively. Both of these constructs are important measures of gig economy platform health and sustainability, since such platforms can only thrive with committed and compliant workers [25, 47, 68, 69]. For each of the hypotheses, we frame our study within the context of the ride-sharing service Uber, due to the firm’s extensive algorithmic control use aimed at influencing driver behavior [23, 70, 89]. We note that our model does not explicitly consider the coping response of individuals or a secondary appraisal for coping responses. This focused examination of a subset of elements within the transaction-based model of stress is a common approach due to the complexity of simultaneously assessing all elements of the process. Furthermore, secondary appraisal and coping are complex processes [e.g., 56], based on which there are studies focused solely on coping with technostress [78].

## Algorithmic Control and Challenge Technostressors

Technology can present opportunities that challenge and motivate workers to improve their skills and efectiveness [97]. For example, technology can act as an accelerator and provide developmental job experiences that help workers accomplish their work tasks and achieve goals faster [6].

Algorithmic control connects to challenge technostressors as it enables workers a continual, real-time view of their performance, which can provide a unique opportunity for motivation toward improvement. As well, since the control originates from algorithms and is delivered via a technology interface, rather than a human manager, there could be opportunities for increased eficiency and responsiveness. Past research has found a positive relationship between technology connectedness and flexibility [57], as well as between flexibility in a worker’s use of technology and technology use, satisfaction, and reduced work-life conflicts [33].

We anticipate that for Uber drivers, gatekeeping algorithmic control such as automated verifications of car registration and insurance, as well as verifications for clean driving records, will positively influence challenge technostressors. This is because drivers will view the gatekeeping requirements as a worthwhile motivational challenge. Since past research suggests that meeting the initial and ongoing Uber requirements is viewed as being relatively dificult [62, 90], we expect that individuals who are successful in fulfilling the gatekeeping controls to become and remain active Uber drivers will view the experience positively in terms of their enhanced ability to serve customers and garner improved ratings. For example, the strict criteria imposed by Uber around the necessity of maintaining a minimum driver rating could serve as a positive motivation to help drivers remain diligent in performing their tasks and encourage them to enhance their skills. Similarly, gatekeeping algorithmic controls may motivate drivers to undertake their work in a manner that adheres to Uber’s requirement to maintain a clean driving record by avoiding behavior such as speeding or driving under the influence of alcohol.

Similarly, we anticipate that guiding algorithmic control will provide positive challenges to workers in terms of skill development and performance opportunities. For example, the Uber app embeds a series of “nudges” to drivers to help them earn more, by encouraging continued driving and providing suggestions on how to get higher ratings [71]. Also, the use of driver ratings (out of five stars) and other performance metrics (e.g., acceptance rate), along with the earning of badges (e.g., for “excellent service” or “great conversation”), can be perceived by drivers as helpful to push them to achieve more by quantifying, tracking, and reporting their performance regularly. Based on these arguments, we propose:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): (a) Gatekeeping algorithmic control and (b) guiding algorithmic control are positively related to a worker’s challenge technostressors.

## Algorithmic Control and Threat Technostressors

Control facilitated through algorithms also corresponds with threat technostressors. Past research has found that where a work environment is tightly controlled by technology, workers experience increased rigidity in their activities [21]. Part of this rigidity is associated with the requirement to be constantly connected and always reachable, which can lead to perceptions of not being able to influence one’s own actions [101]. For workers, this can result in beliefs that technology is intruding on their privacy [4]. In the context of Uber, gatekeeping algorithmic control objectives such as the need to meet vehicle requirements (e.g., age, condition) and fulfill legal status (e.g., no criminal convictions) are automatically monitored via algorithms. However, in contrast to non-algorithmic settings that commonly verify compliance at a single point in time (e.g., an individual has no criminal record at the time of hiring), in an algorithmic context, gatekeeping controls are continually monitored in real-time [54]. This “constant threat” of adherence to company standards that is accompanied by high-stakes consequences, including termination, can be perceived by drivers as a hostile invasion of privacy. Likewise, with guiding algorithmic control, drivers have ongoing pressure to fulfill minimum ride-request acceptance rates and behavioral expectations (e.g., avoiding phone use while driving), which contribute to techno-overload and techno-invasion [69, 76]. Distinct from many traditional jobs that allow at least some worker behavior to be unobservable by supervisors, in the Uber context, virtually all activities are closely tracked and monitored by the driver app [89]. This constant scrutiny can pressure drivers to adapt their work habits to the pace and style dictated by the platform.

The level of worker dependence on technology is another factor associated with technostress [93]. In particular, where a technology quickly becomes obsolete or changes rapidly, it can lead to worker stress [4, 101, 109]. In the case of Uber, drivers rely solely on the app as the source of guidance for their day-to-day activities, “with minimal flexibility to diverge from the instructions given by platform algorithms” [71, p. 2009], including where to go and what route to take. Guiding algorithmic controls are routinely enacted to dynamically provide reminders and suggestions (e.g., music choices, gentle braking) to drivers on how to perform better. Such continuous feedback in an already data-intensive environment (e.g., new ride requests, navigation instructions) can lead to an overload of information and drivers becoming overwhelmed.

Adding to this, platform organization characteristics link algorithmic control to threat technostressors. For example, technostress tends to be highest for workers in companies that are highly centralized and innovative [106]. These attributes characterize gig economy platforms, such as Uber, which tend to be centrally managed and rely heavily on advanced technology tools such as digital algorithms. Likewise, where workers are prevented by technology from having workplace relationships with coworkers (including supervisors, who no longer are responsible for directly facilitating control of worker behavior), stress is positively amplified [95]. Since Uber drivers operate independently from one another, their work exemplifies a tension between self-identity and a broader community of coworkers, leading drivers to struggle to communicate and interact with one another and establish meaningful bonds [71, 89]. Therefore, we suggest:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): (a) Gatekeeping algorithmic control and (b) guiding algorithmic control are positively related to a worker’s threat technostressors.

We recognize the potential contradiction that could be perceived between H1 and H2 in terms of gatekeeping and guiding algorithmic control being positively associated with both challenge and threat technostressors. In considering this issue, we note that challenge and threat technostressors are conceptualized and measured as part of two independent phenomena, techno-eustress and techno-distress, respectively, rather than on opposite ends of a single continuum [97]. Classical stress research suggests that challenge and threat appraisals of the same environmental condition or demand may not be mutually exclusive—the same demand may be appraised as a threat and a challenge [56]. Accordingly, positive and negative appraisals and subsequent responses can occur simultaneously and distinctively, as a result of the same demand. For any given demand, an individual can have both positive and negative appraisal. Nelson and Simmons [72] contend that most work demands elicit a mixed bag of both positive and negative stressors in individuals. Mounting evidence in research on emotions and hormonal response to arousing conditions supports this idea by showing that our brains may indeed be wired to simultaneously experience positive and negative psychological states separately [29, 110]. Therefore, it is plausible that a worker could experience both challenge and threat technostressors as a result of the same algorithmic control. For example, an Uber driver may recognize that the “nudges” they receive through the app (e.g., suggestions on how to get higher ratings) are genuinely helpful in challenging them to perform better, while also finding that the quantity of nudges contributes to a growing sense of overload. Such a “love-hate” relationship can explain a situation where a driver may experience both challenge and threat technostressors. In such a situation, the role of appraisal is critical, which we turn to next.

## Algorithmic Control Transparency

The transparency of algorithms is a well-known concern for both internal and external stakeholders, as the issue is associated with both insight and governance [3, 92]. Our interest is not on the transparency of the technical inner-workings of the algorithmic design, but rather on the algorithmic controls that guide worker behavior. Indeed, “compared to previous regimes of control, algorithmic control is often more opaque in terms of how it directs, evaluates, and disciplines workers” [50, p. 387]. In this context, algorithmic control transparency refers to the disclosure of information about algorithmic control to workers [3, 31, 32, 91]. Such information could include, for example, reasons why specific algorithm-based work instructions are provided as they are, or how a worker is performing relative to others. Whereas workers in a traditional organization can typically follow a formal procedure to inquire about questions surrounding performance or pay, there is a minimal amount of opportunity to do so at gig economy organizations. Relatedly, Schafheitle et al. [91] find that organizations difer widely in the degree of transparency provided to workers regarding how or why they are controlled. In addition, the rapid evolution of algorithmic technologies also results in new data sources or methods for control that are not revealed to workers, thus reducing transparency.

Where gatekeeping algorithmic control is increasingly transparent, we expect that the improved understanding of the algorithms that Uber drivers obtain will strengthen the anticipated positive relationship of gatekeeping algorithmic control with challenge technostressors. Likewise, increased transparency related to guiding algorithmic control will strengthen the latter’s anticipated positive relationship to challenge technostressors. From an Uber driver perspective, part of this algorithmic control transparency originates from an understanding of relevant criteria and thresholds and how these fit into Uber’s overall operations, as well as from insights pertaining to the data that is being supplied to the algorithms [31]. For example, if a driver understands that the data used for periodic background checks is reliable and timely, they will be increasingly motivated to keep their skills updated and maintain a good performance rating. Similarly, with guiding algorithmic control, if drivers believe that the behavioral nudges and performance feedback they receive are based on extensive analysis of past rides and can actually help them improve their skills, they will be more likely to accept them as beneficial. Further, algorithmic control transparency can be viewed by drivers as a factor that enhances their engagement and enthusiasm. Hong et al. [47] find that information transparency features can encourage Uber drivers to join the platform, but can also allow drivers to make better decisions on accepting or declining ride requests; that is, the more drivers understand about how the algorithms allocate ride requests and evaluate performance, the more they learn about when and where it is most advantageous to work. Based on these arguments, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Algorithmic control transparency strengthens the positive relationship between (a) gatekeeping algorithmic control and a worker’s challenge technostressors, and between (b) guiding algorithmic control and a worker’s challenge technostressors.

Separately, where algorithmic control is increasingly transparent, workers will get a better sense of the basic motivation and rationale for the control, thus weakening the anticipated reinforcing efect on threat technostressors (i.e., they will experience less negative technostress). Past research has recognized the frustration experienced by Uber drivers who do not understand the logic of the algorithms that control their day-to-day activities [47, 69]. Where the underlying logic for gatekeeping algorithmic control is made clear to drivers (e.g., the rating level that will cause a driver to be deactivated from the platform), it is likely to reduce the level of threat technostressors since workers will feel more secure and certain in their job. Here, Kellogg et al. [50] suggest that control-related reward systems and payment calculations are often intentionally kept opaque in order to discourage manipulation by workers. These challenges are particularly pertinent at gig economy organizations where there is minimal recourse for workers who seek to get clarity on what is going on “behind the scenes” [89]. However, by making algorithmic control more transparent, the knowledge gained by workers regarding how they are evaluated and paid can reduce their perceived complexity of providing ride-sharing services. In particular, the level of algorithmic accuracy and potential for error is a key driver of algorithmic transparency [31]; where drivers trust that the algorithmic control is operating as intended, they may view it to be obstructing the completion of tasks to a lesser extent. Therefore, we suggest:

Hypothesis 4 (H4): Algorithmic control transparency weakens the positive relationship between (a) gatekeeping algorithmic control and a worker’s threat technostressors, as well as between (b) guiding algorithmic control and a worker’s threat technostressors.

## Technostressors and Continuance Intention

Since worker commitment to a gig economy platform is a key metric of platform health and sustainability, we investigate the role that stress can play in a worker’s desire to continue working for the platform. Past research finds positive links between positive work challenges, worker engagement, and job satisfaction [24, 86], while also finding negative links between challenge stressors, and job searches and intentions to quit [8, 14, 79]. With respect to technostress, threat technostressors are positively associated with turnover intentions and low job satisfaction in the general work context [60, 86] and with a negative psychological response for nurses [12]. Such conditions are highly likely to lead to greater intentions to leave the organization.

From a gig economy perspective, we expect that where an Uber driver is positively challenged by and engaged with the app, such as they would in earning performance badges or pursuing a higher driver rating, they will be more inclined to continue working for the platform. In contrast, where drivers feel insecure and threatened because they fear being deactivated from the platform or overloaded because of the constant pressure to drive, they will be more likely to stop working for the platform. On this basis, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5 (H5): (a) Challenge technostressors are positively related to a worker’s continuance intention, while (b) threat technostressors are negatively related to a worker’s continuance intention.

## Technostressors and Workaround Use

Workarounds represent “conscious adaptations of work activities that are not expected or specified to be changed in this manner” [55, p. 335]. Past research finds that where the interests of an organization are aligned with the worker’s personal interests, workarounds are less likely, but they can occur where there is a misalignment [2, 74, 81]. Furthermore, Woltjer [114] finds that workers who face high demands (in terms of handling information and performing tasks) are more likely to consider workarounds—in execution of their work tasks and in their decisions to comply with company directives. In general, a lack of satisfaction is associated with a higher incidence of workarounds.

In the particular case of technology use, we expect challenge technostressors to negatively correspond with workaround use, and threat technostressors to positively correspond with workaround use. Past research has found a negative relationship between threat technostressors, and user/job satisfaction [86, 100], as well as a negative relationship between user satisfaction and workarounds [55]. These findings taken together suggest that workers faced with threat technostressors are less satisfied both in their jobs and with the technology, and are thus increasingly likely to engage in workarounds to avoid technology-related obstructions to their work. Those experiencing challenge technostressors would be satisfied with their enhanced skills or improved ratings, and are thus less likely to pursue workarounds.

In the context of the gig economy, Uber drivers could respond to technostressors by engaging in workarounds such as turning the app of and then back on, in order to get more advantageous (e.g., longer, higher paying) ride requests and avoid the inconvenience of rides that take them further from home [70, 89]. They can also engage in other workarounds that trick the system into thinking they have abandoned and then rejoined the platform—resulting in better incentives—such as by alternating work weeks between diferent ride-sharing companies (e.g., one week with Uber, then the next week with Lyft) [69]. Similarly, Uber drivers experiencing threat technostressors associated with the complex and changing features of the app may choose a workaround that utilizes a separate smartphone with a more familiar interface (e.g., navigation directions via Waze or Google Maps) [10], rather than using the Uber navigation app. Based on these arguments, we propose:

Hypothesis 6 (H6): (a) Challenge technostressors are negatively related to a worker’s use of workarounds, while (b) threat technostressors are positively related to a worker’s use of workarounds.

## Methodology

## Survey Instrument & Data Collection

For the purpose of data collection, we developed a survey instrument and distributed it to U.S.- based Uber drivers using Qualtrics’ online survey tool. Participants were asked about their interactions and experiences with the Uber driver app. Measurement items for all constructs (except for challenge technostressors—see the following section) were derived from existing scales, which had been shown to have good psychometric properties in earlier studies. Due to a lack of existing measurement instruments for algorithmic control, we adapted established scales for input control [25] and behavior/output control [15, 108] to measure gatekeeping algorithmic control and guiding algorithmic control, respectively. We chose to draw on both behavior and output control elements for guiding algorithmic control in order to encompass aspects of managerial monitoring as well as establishing and monitoring performance targets [52, 112]. In our study, the challenge technostressor construct is oriented around the positive (work-related) challenges experienced by Uber drivers in relation to using the driver app. Due to a lack of existing instruments, we developed a new measurement scale by drawing on established guidance, such as Hinkin [45] and MacKenzie et al. [59]. In doing so, we undertook many of the recommended steps, including developing a conceptual definition of the construct (refer to Table 2), generating items to represent the construct (derived from Tarafdar et al. [97]), assessing the content validity of the items (via author team discussions, as well as reviews of Uber press articles and driver forums), and assessing the measurement model (refer to Online Supplemental Appendices 1-3). For threat technostressors, we relied on a four-dimensional second-order construct derived from Tarafdar et al. [101], using a reflective-reflective specification [61, 101]. In this specification, each lower-order construct (techno-complexity, technoinvasion, techno-overload, and techno-uncertainty) is assumed to reflect the higher-order construct (i.e., threat technostressors) [41]. For algorithmic control transparency, we adapted items from Remus et al. [87]. Finally, to assess survey participants’ continuance intention and workaround use, we adopted items from Goldbach et al. [39] and Laumer et al. [55], respectively. All first-order constructs were measured reflectively with multiple items and rated on 7-point Likert scales, using “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree” anchors. An overview of all construct items is provided in Online Supplemental Appendix 1. To account for alternative explanations, we added a total of six control variables to our research model, covering both personal information (age, gender, and education) and work-related information (income from Uber, driver rating, and overall job satisfaction).

The survey instrument was pretested with four experienced information systems (IS) scholars and four senior PhD students, none of whom were members of the author team. The pretest resulted in minor adaptations in the survey flow and wording. To recruit participants, we used the support of a market research firm, Qualtrics Research Services, which provides access to a wide range of vetted participants who are recruited based on studyspecific characteristics [47]. The use of online panels is advantageous due to the anonymity that it grants participants, as well as the broad spectrum of individuals that can be contacted [58, 83]. The actual data collection took place during April and May 2019. All respondents were required to be active Uber drivers in the United States. Multiple attention filters were included in the online questionnaire and responses submitted in less than the minimum expected time were automatically terminated. In total, 630 Uber drivers completed the questionnaire. Upon completion of the data collection process, we analyzed our data sample to identify potential straight-liners and ensure the logical consistency of the collected data (e.g., by checking working hours against the total number of ride-hailing trips reported). On this basis, we excluded nine data records, resulting in a final sample size of 621 participants.

The majority of the survey participants worked as a ride-hailing driver to make a supplementary income (407 drivers), earned up to \$2,000 per month (426), had up to two years of ride-hailing experience (526), and held a college degree (380). We believe that our data sample is a good representation of the Uber driver population as a whole, as Hall and Krueger [42] find that the large majority of Uber drivers are under the age of 50 (85 percent in our data sample) and work only part-time (87 percent in our sample). Moreover, recent studies on Uber [e.g., 19, 47, 70] indicate that a notable proportion of drivers work for Lyft in parallel, referred to as multi-homing (37 percent in our sample), and that about one third of all Uber drivers are female (32 percent in our sample).

## Measurement Model Assessment

To transform our research model into a structural equation model, we used covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) using IBM SPSS Amos 26.0. We first conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of the measurement model constructs. Each construct exceeded the recommended 0.70 [73] for composite reliability (CR) and the recommended 0.50 [34] for average variance extracted (AVE), with the exception of gatekeeping algorithmic control (CR: 0.644; AVE: 0.479), which still exceeds Fornell and Larcker’s [34] more flexible guideline of $\mathrm { C R } > 0 . 6 0$ even if $\mathrm { A V E } < 0 . 5 0$ . As such, these values indicate good reliability and convergent validity. Discriminant validity was assessed using the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratio, which has been advocated as a robust technique for CB-SEM [35, 40, 105], as well as the $\mathrm { C I _ { C F A } }$ (sys) and $\chi ^ { 2 }$ (sys) techniques proposed by Rönkkö and Cho [88]. Our HTMT results found that all values except the one for gatekeeping algorithmic control-workaround use were within the strict 0.85 threshold, and that all values were within the more liberal 0.90 threshold [44]. Using the $\mathrm { C I _ { C F A } }$ technique, the upper limit of all factor correlation confidence intervals was beneath the 0.90 cut-of, with the exception of gatekeeping algorithmic control-workaround use (0.954). This result falls within Rönkkö and Cho’s ‘moderate problem’ classification, though it is still below the more liberal 1.00 cut-of threshold [88]. Furthermore, the $\chi ^ { 2 } ( \mathrm { s y s } )$ results indicated that three construct pairs (gatekeeping algorithmic control-threat technostressors, gatekeeping algorithmic control- workaround use, and guiding algorithmic control-transparency) met the lower ‘marginal problem’ classification, but that no construct pairs met either the ‘severe’ or ‘moderate’ classification criteria. Taken together, we acknowledge that although the gatekeeping algorithmic control and workaround use constructs are highly correlated, our discriminant validity assessments indicate that the issue is not severe. Conceptually, the two constructs are notably diferent in terms of an individual’s perception of the design of controls within an algorithm (gatekeeping algorithmic control) and the extent that an individual intends to avoid following algorithmic instructions or guidelines (workaround use). Although past studies have utilized similar constructs (e.g., see Croitor and Benlian [25] for gatekeeping controls and Laumer et al. [55] for workaround use), we are not aware of any single study that includes both constructs together (except for a companion study [111] that is based on the same dataset) and are thus unable to determine if the high correlation is a systemic problem with the constructs or scales. ## Common Method Bias

Since our data were self-reported from a single source, common method bias (CMB) is a potential risk. To reduce this risk, we used numerous procedural remedies during the design of our survey instrument, such as keeping questions simple, focused, and concise; avoiding double-barreled questions and conceptual dependence between dependent and independent variables; using randomized items and reiterating respondent anonymity along with the exclusive research purpose of our study [1, 80]. Adding to this, we conducted several statistical, post-hoc analyses to check for CMB. First, extremely high construct correlations are a possible indicator of CMB [80]. The highest correlation between constructs is 0.89 (see Online Supplemental Appendix 2), which is slightly below the suggested threshold of 0.90 [5, 77]. Second, we performed a full collinearity test, which is considered an efective test for the identification of CMB [53]. Although this test was intended for partial least squares methods, the principles also apply to covariance-based methods [38]. We calculated the VIF values for all constructs and control variables included in our research model. All values are clearly below the most conservative threshold of 3.3 [36], with workaround use showing the highest VIF value (1.875). Third, we examined the efects of social desirability (defined as “the tendency of some people to respond in a socially acceptable manner”), which is one common source of CMB in studies that use self-reported data [80, p. 887]. To measure social desirability, we used Strahan and Gerbasi’s [96] 10-item version of the original measurement instrument developed by Crowne and Marlowe [26]. All items were rated on a true/false scale and scores were summed up to create an overall score [1]. Using the measured specific bias test [38], we added social desirability to our research model and linked it to all main constructs [1, 13]. We then conducted a chi-square diference test to compare the unconstrained model to a model where shared variances are constrained to be zero (i.e., no bias) and found that social desirability was potentially afecting the model. As a result, we retained the social desirability factor as a covariate of the other factors and included social desirability as a control variable in our causal model. Results revealed that the path estimates changed only slightly in magnitude and remained unchanged in significance. By including social desirability in the structural model, we therefore account for its potential efects [38].

We also examined the efects of six control variables (age, gender, education, monthly Uber income, Uber driver rating, and overall job satisfaction) on the four endogenous constructs (challenge technostressors, threat technostressors, continuance intention, workaround use). As noted in Online Supplemental Appendix 5, age is negatively related to both challenge technostressors and workaround use, suggesting that older workers are less likely to experience challenge technostressors or engage in workarounds. Gender has no significant efect on any of the four constructs. Education level (Uber driver rating) is positively (negatively) related to workaround use. This means that those workers who are more educated (have a higher rating) are more (less) likely to make use of workarounds. The income earned from Uber is positively related to continuance intention, which suggests that workers who earn more are increasingly inclined to continue driving for the firm. Finally, job satisfaction was positively related to each of the endogenous constructs, except threat technostressors, where it was negatively related. This indicates that drivers who are happy in their jobs will experience more challenge and fewer threat technostressors, while being increasingly inclined to both continue working for Uber and engage in workarounds. ## Contributions to Research and Practice

Our results show direct links between algorithmic control and both challenge and threat technostressors, the moderating role of algorithmic control transparency, and the outcomes of continuance intention and workaround use. As our overarching research contribution, we integrate the control and technostress literatures to reveal new theoretical and empirical insights in terms of how algorithmic control embody demands faced by Uber drivers and are associated with the technostress experienced by them. Algorithmic control is thus a novel source of technostress, manifested specifically in the context of gig work.

Interestingly, we find that gatekeeping algorithmic control and guiding algorithmic control are positively associated with both challenge technostressors and threat technostressors. This suggests that Uber drivers do not broadly view algorithmic control as uniformly “good” or “bad,” but instead form a type of love-hate relationship that allows a simultaneous recognition of both their benefits and drawbacks. This view is consistent with recent findings suggesting that some technologically advanced control systems can both enhance managerial authority, while also providing benefits to workers [82] and are thus viewed both positively and negatively by the latter. A further explanation for these links is that gatekeeping algorithmic control is partly viewed by drivers as a perpetual target that is a challenge to continually satisfy, but is also viewed as a “hygiene factor,” which, although useful for maintaining driver eligibility, can obstruct a driver’s focus on other objectives, such as maximizing earnings. In contrast, guiding algorithmic control may be perceived by drivers as more of a normative factor that pushes them to improve their work performance in some instances, while also hindering their activities in other cases. These distinctions may be of interest to organizations implementing algorithmic control and to algorithm designers, who could seek to ofset the threat technostressors associated with gatekeeping algorithmic control by integrating more motivational elements and ongoing challenges. For example, rather than orienting gatekeeping algorithmic control primarily during a driver’s onboarding process, gig companies could award digital badges or bonus payouts for ongoing adherence to the initial criteria, in order to facilitate a longer-term motivation (e.g., safe-driving award for no accidents or tickets during the year).

Our findings on the role of algorithmic control transparency lend a minimal amount of weight to the often-touted notion that workers who understand the rationale behind an algorithm will necessarily experience a reduced level of threat technostressors emanating from gatekeeping algorithmic control, as the corresponding hypotheses (H4a and H4b) were unsupported. Although we also hypothesized (in H3a) that algorithmic control transparency would strengthen the positive relationship between gatekeeping algorithmic control and a worker’s challenge technostressors (i.e., transparency will lead to more positive stressors stemming from gatekeeping algorithmic control), our results indicate that transparency actually weakens the positive relationship we found in H1a (i.e., transparency leads to less positive stress stemming from gatekeeping algorithmic control). This suggests that although gatekeeping algorithmic controls may serve as a worthy motivational pursuit, as workers gain transparency into the algorithmic controls themselves, the challenge of fulfilling them becomes less inspiring. These results bring into question the assertion [36, 107] that transparency is a “silver bullet” that could make algorithmic control more palatable for workers. Although past research acknowledges that algorithmic control transparency is important to some users, others can simply be indiferent to it [31] or too time-pressed due to the rapid pace of gig economy work, which could partly explain our lack of significant results for H3b and H4a and H4b. Broadly, Ananny and Crawford echo this view by arguing that “transparency is an inadequate way to understand—much less govern—algorithms” [3, p. 974]. Another possible explanation for the lack of definitive conclusions in this regard is that the potential benefits of algorithmic control transparency are diminished in less skilled, less knowledge-intensive, and more time-pressured jobs. For example, the additional information presented to Uber drivers via transparency, when they are mentally focused on their driving, is likely to add to their already high cognitive overload and increase threat stressors. In contrast, within an industry such as financial services, investment bankers may feel heightened positive stress when they understand the rationale of an algorithm that tracks their performance relative to peers. It is noteworthy that some commentators have argued that it is in the best interest of gig economy firms to avoid “full” algorithmic (control) transparency, as it can introduce intentional manipulation and gaming by the worker, and threaten competitive advantage of the organization [31]. As a consequence, organizations may ofer trivial transparency concessions to workers but stop short of providing truly meaningful insights into their algorithms [3]. In cases where algorithmic control transparency is enhanced, but workers view the resulting insights with scepticism (e.g., when transparency has only been provided in selected areas, while other areas remain concealed), it may actually serve to increase threat technostressors and decrease challenge technostressors. As such, future research may benefit from delving deeper into how the diferent elements of transparency (e.g., data, model, inference, interface) impact worker perceptions of algorithmic control [32].

Despite the popular sentiment that algorithmic control is generally “bad” for workers, we find evidence to the contrary. Indeed, both gatekeeping and guiding algorithmic control can present positive challenges and motivation for workers. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine positive challenges from algorithmic control. In this regard, we find algorithmic control as a novel source, and algorithmic control transparency as a novel appraisal factor, for challenge technostressors. The context of gig economy work provides further novelty, conceptually extending emerging literature in the ofice-home spillover domain [6]. This line of inquiry answers the call by Tarafdar et al. [97] to take into account techno-eustress in conjunction with techno-distress and to consider the contextual nature of work. Without considering challenge technostressors, we would not have been able to paint this more nuanced picture.

Our results further reveal the importance of technostress in relation to Uber driver behavior, which has the potential to impact how gig economy companies interact with their workforce. As gig economy platforms continue to struggle with high worker attrition [68], it is becoming increasingly important that they find new ways to maintain worker motivation and loyalty. Our study suggests that technostress is a key factor associated with this objective, due to its relationship with stronger continuance intentions and weaker workaround use. By enhancing challenge technostressors and minimizing threat technostressors, gig economy organizations can contribute to the cultivation of a more committed workforce.

## Limitations and Future Research

We recognize several limitations of this study, some of which present promising opportunities for future research. First, the collected data is self-reported and cross-sectional in nature, which introduces concerns such as common method bias. Although we undertook several procedural and post-hoc remedies, a risk still exists that this could bias our results. Second, the focus of our study was on one gig economy platform, Uber. As a consequence, but it remains unclear if our findings generalize to other gig platforms, such as with Airbnb; or in cases where platform rivalries are less intense, such as with Handy [18]. Future studies could seek to uncover if workers experience, and respond to, technostress diferently depending on the platform they use. Third, in this study, we focused on the role of algorithmic controls as generators of technostressors (i.e., challenge and threat), one particular factor that influenced the primary appraisal of the technostressors (i.e., algorithmic control transparency), as well as on outcomes (i.e., continuance intention, workaround use). In doing so, our study focuses on the front-end of the transaction model of stress as described above, and provides early and credible evidence of a link between algorithmic control and technostress experienced by Uber drivers. That is not to say that the other elements of the transaction model of technostress are not important. Indeed, a natural extension for future research is to consider drivers’ and other gig workers’ secondary appraisal and their coping responses [97], and investigate how they evaluate and act following their recognition of challenge and/or threat technostressors. Such insights could serve as a valuable extension of our study by clarifying the relationship between an individual’s perception of technostressors and the eventual emergence of positive and/or negative outcomes. Moreover, insights from recent work on mixed emotions and user ambivalence vis-à-vis IS use [e.g., 85, 94] can help shed light on simultaneous positive and negative orientations from algorithmic controls. Finally, we acknowledge that gatekeeping algorithmic control was measured with only two items, and that the measurement scale for challenge technostressors was self-developed. As such, further development of the measurement instruments for these two constructs (e.g., scale cross-validation with additional samples) could add more nuanced insights. Relatedly, future research could also delve into the specific algorithmic characteristics and features (e.g., customer-based ratings and location tracking) that are associated with technostressors, including elements of algorithmic control transparency and privacy. By better understanding the micro-level human responses to algorithmic control, the design of respective algorithms could increasingly be customized to maximize the benefits and limit the shortcomings.

## Conclusion

This study examined how the use of algorithmic control in gig economy platforms brings about challenge and threat technostressors for workers. Furthermore, it examined the moderating impact of algorithmic control transparency as an appraisal factor, as well as the impact of technostressors on workers’ continuance intention and workaround use. We find that gatekeeping algorithmic control and guiding algorithmic control positively relate to both challenge and threat technostressors, which consequently relate to continuance intention and workaround use. Our study contributes to the control and technostress literature by viewing algorithmic control as a work-related demand that puts gigeconomy workers under stress, which can have diverging downstream efects on their behavior. Such efects are particularly important to gig economy organizations, which continue to struggle with worker motivation and loyalty. Our findings suggest that gigeconomy organizations can use algorithmic control to enhance challenge technostressors by integrating design elements in the app that help workers to improve their skills. Furthermore, we uncover evidence disputing the assumption that algorithmic control transparency can reduce the level of threat technostressors experienced by Uber drivers. We encourage future research to delve further into the transparency elements that impact worker perceptions of algorithmic control in gig economy organizations and beyond.
