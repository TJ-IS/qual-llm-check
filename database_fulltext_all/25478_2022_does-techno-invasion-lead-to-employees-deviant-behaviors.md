# Does Techno-invasion Lead to Employees’ Deviant Behaviors?

Yang Chen<sup>a</sup>, Xin Wang<sup>a</sup>, Jose Benitez<sup>b</sup>, Xin (Robert) Luo<sup>c</sup>, and Dechao Li<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Business Administration, Southwestern University of Finance and Economics, Chengdu, China; <sup>b</sup>EDHEC Business School, France; <sup>c</sup>Anderson School of Management, University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, NM, USA; <sup>d</sup>Sichuan TIANFU Bank Co. Ltd., Chengdu, China

## ABSTRACT

Techno-invasion refers to constant connectivity of being “always exposed” that blurs desired boundaries between work and personal life and represents an emerging phenomenon of scholarly investigation. As work-related use of information technology (IT) during non-work time leads to techno-invasion, a minimal amount is known about whether techno-invasion may engender employees’ deviant behaviors, a behavior that violates organizational norms and may be intended to harm the organization, its members, or both. Drawn upon the selfregulation theory, this study examines this relationship by focusing on psychological processes. Based on time-lagged survey data collected from customer service teams from two financial service companies in China, our results show that (1) techno-invasion is associated with selfregulation impairment (i.e., the reduction of one’s self-regulatory resources needed to control undesirable impulses), which in turn provokes employee’s deviant behavior; (2) perceived team bottom-line mentality (i.e., mindset to prioritize the team bottom-line outcomes) reinforces the relationship between techno-invasion and self-regulation impairment; and (3) IT mindfulness (i.e., employees’ focus in being creative using IT, and learning and discovering new and more eficient ways of using IT) negatively moderates the relationship between techno-invasion and self-regulation impairment, thus serving as a remedy to alleviate or prevent self-regulation impairment and employee’s deviant behavior. We explain theoretically and test empirically the impact of techno-invasion on employees’ deviant behavior, emphasizing the intermediate psychological processes and the boundary conditions that afect this relationship.

## KEYWORDS

Techno-invasion; deviant behavior; self-regulation impairment; IT mindfulness; team mentality; work-life balance; online deviance; work boundaries; organizational norms

## Introduction

Attributable to the recent explosion in workplace information technology (IT) use such as cloud-based ofice tools and telecommuting applications in the era of Web 4.0, there has been a large body of references to a phenomenon termed techno-invasion. Demonstrated to be one of the dominant predictors of stressors in the digitalized workplace, techno-invasion refers to constant connectivity of being “always exposed” that blurs desired boundaries between work and personal life, maintaining that individuals are available to work requests continuously [73,92]. According to a survey conducted by CPA Canada, 46 percent of professional accountants use their mobile devices for work purposes outside of regular hours. Additionally, 38 percent check their inbox right up until they go to bed and 40 percent feel they have to respond before they show up for work the next day [78]. These behaviors show evident symptoms of techno-invasion.

Recently, IT has been proved to lead to a range of adverse outcomes attributed to technoinvasion, such as decreased organizational and continuance commitment [73], invasion of privacy [78], work exhaustion [62], and work-life conflict [56]. Given the significance of IT in both organizational processes and outcomes, recent studies have established a negative impact of technology-induced stressors on workplace deviance [83, 101]. The growing ubiquity, connectivity, portability, and boundary-spanning nature of IT resources exacerbate the potential costs and risks of deviant behavior [98]. Defined as “the voluntary behavior that violates significant organizational norms and in so doing threatens the wellbeing of an organizations, its members, or both” [76, p. 556], deviant behavior is a widespread and destructive phenomenon throughout organizations that ultimately results in an unacceptable violation believed to threaten organization’s profitability and sustainability, and society’s well-being [53]. Deviant behavior includes wasting time on the job, taking property without permission, or purposely performing work badly. Overlooking or underestimating deviant behaviors in organizations may result in substantial fines, damage to corporate’s reputation, cause organizational security breaches, and, ultimately, financial loss of business [47, 58]. Theorizing, testing, and understanding the mechanisms that lead to deviant behaviors and helping organizations to prevent or remediate deviant behaviors is crucial. Our study covers this theoretical gap through the lens of techno-invasion.

Does techno-invasion lead to employees’ deviant behaviors? Recent research claims that the impact of environmental stressors on sequent outcomes should be examined by recognizing psychological processes as intermediaries and situational factors as moderators [63, 66, 95]. Echoing this viewpoint, this study draws on self-regulation theory as a theoretical foundation to investigate the mediating role of self-regulation impairment in the relationship between techno-invasion and deviant behaviors. The crux of self-regulation theory rests on the viewpoint that all acts of self-regulation are empowered by a limited, metaphorical strength that can diminish after predominant self-regulatory exertion, thereby impairing subsequent self-regulation [6]. In the context of techno-invasion, our central proposition suggests that techno-invasion may impair employees’ self-regulatory strength, which may ultimately lead to deviant behaviors in the workplace.

We also rely on a self-regulation framework to propose that team bottom-line mentality and IT mindfulness may serve as two boundary conditions that may alter the impairment efect of techno-invasion. Team bottom-line mentality is defined as a one-dimensional frame of mind that revolves around securing bottom-line outcomes to the neglect of competing priorities [35]. The impairment efect of techno-invasion in the workplace may vary depending on the degree to which individuals perceive their personal priorities may contradict what their teams expect. While some employees may be guided by the team’s pressure, other employees may not. Teams that adopt a one-dimensional frame of mind that revolves around realizing bottom-line outcomes are inclined to neglect competing individual priorities. In this sense, we argue and expect that team bottom-line mentality (i.e., perceived pressure from the team) may reinforce the efect of techno-invasion on selfregulation impairment. We also propose that the impairment efect of techno-invasion may also be conditioned by how individuals use, focus, and extract value from IT. Individuals with a predominant mental mindset driven by individual awareness of context that involves eficiency, openness to, and value-adding applications of IT (i.e., IT mindfulness [95]) may remediate the positive efect of techno-invasion on self-regulation impairment. In this sense, IT mindfulness may exert as a mechanism to remediate deviant behaviors provoked by techno-invasion.

This paper makes three contributions to information systems (IS) research. First, we explain theoretically and show empirically that techno-invasion afects employees’ deviant behavior. We focus on the role of psychological processes in the efect of techno-invasion on deviant behavior by extending prior IS research on the negative consequences of technoinvasion and technostress in the workplace. Second, this paper uses self-regulation theory to examine the mechanisms and psychological processes through which techno-invasion influences deviant behavior. Specifically, we propose and explore self-regulation impairment (i.e., a reduction in employee’s self-control) as a mechanism explaining the underlying relationship between techno-invasion and deviant behavior. Finally, our research contributes to IS research by examining potential boundary conditions at both team level (i.e., team bottom-line mentality) and individual level (i.e., IT mindfulness) through which techno-invasion afects self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior.

## Research background

## Prior Studies of Techno-Invasion

Techno-invasion relates with the employee’s continual availability and instant response due to the ubiquitous IT use in both private (i.e., personal) and organizational contexts [90]. Long recognized as a crucial factor for understanding end-user attitudes toward workrelated IT use, IS scholars are increasingly highlighting the need to examine the consequences of techno-invasion. Exploring how individuals engage in the healthy IT use provides significant implications for both instrumental goals (e.g., productivity) and wellbeing goals in IS [81]. Recently, there has been a range of studies applying various research perspectives to investigate the non-beneficial or adverse consequences emanating from a direct or indirect relationship with techno-invasion. Drawing from the prior study of Tarafdar et al. [91], we sort these consequences into three main categories: job-related consequences, IT-related consequences, and well-being-related consequences. Online Supplemental Appendix A presents a more detailed discussion of our literature review and studies revealing the negative consequences of techno-invasion.

First, techno-invasion has been demonstrated to influence job parameters such as perceived information fatigue and prolonged multitasking aided by IT use, often leading to demotivated, exhausted, and frustrated individuals resulting in job-related adverse consequences. Drawing from a transaction-based model, Ragu-Nathan et al. [73] and Lee et al. [55], for instance, examined the influence of techno-invasion on job satisfaction, organization’s commitment, and intention to stay. Grounded on these findings, further studies have extended their findings through a discussion of other counterproductive outcomes of techno-invasion such as decreased overall performance [90]. Second, technoinvasion has been proven to result in negative cognitions in individuals, such as information interruptions and privacy depriving, afecting their satisfaction with the IT they use and their performance in IT-mediated tasks. Consequences relating to IT use include lack of technology-enabled innovation and productivity, low end-user satisfaction [69], resigned or unwilling compliance with IT use requirements such as respond to ofice e-mail in real-time [4], and discontinuous usage intentions and behaviors [62]. Third, constant IT connectivity to the workplace can prevent individuals from mentally detaching from work, resulting in decreased well-being. Well-being-related consequences include work exhaustion, burnout, and strain [33, 84], invasion of privacy [2], lower happiness [18], and work-life conflicts [56].

Within this stream of research, some studies [62, 69] have acknowledged the importance of examining the role of psychological processes in influencing the perceived stress caused by techno-invasion and its workplace impacts. However, there is a perceptible gap in the theoretical acknowledgment of the indirect impact of techno-invasion on workplace outcomes. It is, therefore, crucial to adopt a more holistic perspective and focus on the underlying mechanisms through which techno-invasion afects deviant behaviors. In the present study, we further draw on self-regulation theory to address this gap in extant knowledge in IS research on techno-invasion efects on employees’ deviant behavior.

## Self-Regulation Theory

Self-regulation is conceptualized as an individuals’ process of utilizing self-regulatory resources to constrain responses that may arise as a result of psychological processes, habit, learning, or the press of the situation to control undesirable impulses and alter consequent inappropriate behavioral responses in a goal-oriented manner [7, 100]. Efective self-regulation requires a crucial psychological resource named self-regulatory strength, defined as the internal resources available to inhibit, override, or alter responses [100]. All acts of self-regulation are empowered by a limited, metaphorical strength that can diminish after predominant self-regulatory exertion [6]. To the degree that individuals exert self-regulatory strength in a context or task, they will have lower self-regulatory strength available for subsequent tasks.

Prior empirical studies have found that stressed individuals with high degrees of selfregulatory strength exhibited superior performance in cognitive tasks [12]. However, selfregulatory strength ability is limited. Techno-invasion generates stress blurring the lines between work and personal life and brings new cognitive challenges [6, 23], and it may consume employee’s self-regulatory strength (i.e., self-regulation impairment) [7, 100]. In the context of techno-invasion, individuals who feel stressed and resentful must exert the self-regulatory strength to constrain their deviant impulses to react negatively to the intrusiveness and respond in situationally and socially appropriate ways, avoiding responding with deviant behaviors. To the extent employees sufer from a self-regulation impairment provoked by techno-invasion, they may have a lower strength required to self-regulate their base instincts and respond with deviant behavior. This study uses the self-regulation theory to investigate the impact of techno-invasion on self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior. We argue that techno-invasion may impair self-control, which in turn may provoke deviant behavior. Given the significance of self-regulatory strength for selfcontrol, scholars are gaining insights into the boundary conditions under which selfregulation impairment may occur. We argue and integrate team bottom-line mentality and IT mindfulness with self-regulation theory as two potential moderating conditions that may afect the impact of techno-invasion on self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior.

## IT Mindfulness

The crux of IT mindfulness rests on its definition as a dynamic IT-specific trait whereby users focus on the present pay attention to detail, exhibit a willingness to consider other uses, and express genuine interest in investigating IT features and failures [95]. Butler and Gray [19] suggested that organizations can attain reliable outcomes of information systems in dynamic contexts by adopting a mindfulness perspective. Several IS studies followed grounded either on Langer’s [52] work in mindfulness that described the attributes of a mindful individual or Weick et al.’s [102], who extended the concept of mindfulness from individual level to organizational level. However, the majority of existing IS research on mindfulness has focused primarily on the organizational level [20, 99], suggesting that mindfulness is positively related to organizational IT innovation, IS reliability, and highquality managerial decision-making, whereas research on the individual level remains limited [95]. Online Supplemental Appendix B illustrates the literature review on mindfulness in IS studies at both organizational- and individual-levels.

Given its research relevance and practical significance, IS scholars have extensively adopted mindfulness as a compelling lens and extended this concept into the arena of IT use context, examining the influences of IT mindfulness on IT adoption decisions. However, there is a surprising paucity of research developing a domain-specific instrument for the evaluation of individual mindfulness in IT use. Recently, Thatcher et al. [95] address this gap by systematically developing the concept of IT mindfulness and present a domainspecific individual-level assessment of IT mindfulness, conceptualizing it as “a dynamic ITspecific trait, evident when working with IT, whereby the user focuses on the present, pays attention to detail, exhibits a willingness to consider other uses, and expresses genuine interest in investigating IT features and failures” [95, p. 832]. IT mindfulness provides more predictive power within the IS context than general trait mindfulness [95].

Grounded on Langer’s [52] definition, Thatcher et al. [95] identified IT mindfulness as a second-order construct composed of four dimensions: alertness to distinction, awareness of multiple perspectives, openness to novelty, and orientation in the present. Alertness to distinction refers to the extent to which an IT mindful individual understands the IT abilities of applications and the context in which they will prove more reliable. Hence, individuals can generate innovative ways of using IT while they perceive silent discrepancies between their usage and the actual potential of IT [95]. Awareness of multiple perspectives refers to an IT mindful individual who can identify and realize multiple uses of a specific IT application as well as develop innovative solutions to problems that may arise in the working settings [75, 95]. Openness to novelty refers to the willingness of individuals to explore more potential and novel applications of the deployed system as they are continuously curious and flexible to experiment with it. Orientation in the present refers to an IT mindful individual who is involved as well as focused on the present moment and current context and able to adapt IT at several diverse contexts [75].

Despite IT mindfulness may have a crucial relevance for the efects of techno-invasion on individuals’ behavior, prior IS research has not paid enough attention to this role, which generates a theoretical and empirical gap this study tries to fulfill. We argue that technoinvasion may influence positively employees’ deviant behavior because techno-invasion reduces individuals’ self-regulation. Additionally, our central proposition also relates to IT mindfulness in a way that IT mindfulness may serve as a potential remedy for the technoinvasion efects on deviant behavior. IT mindfulness, by improving self-regulation, selfawareness, and self-eficacy in using, leveraging, and experimenting with IT can optimize the eficiency of psychological, cognitive, and behavioral reactions to techno-invasion, and help employees to use it as a potential remedy to the techno-invasion efect on selfregulation impairment. The proposed research model includes and tests these unexplored propositions.

## Proposed Research Model and Hypotheses Development

We use self-regulation theory as an overarching framework to guide our investigation into the relationship between techno-invasion, perceived team bottom-line mentality, IT mindfulness, self-regulation impairment, and deviant behaviors. Figure 1 presents the proposed research model. We first explain the impact of techno-invasion on self-regulation impairment, which in turn afects employees’ deviant behaviors. We then hypothesize that perceived team bottom-line mentality and IT mindfulness may moderate the relationship between techno-invasion and self-regulation impairment.

## Impact of Techno-Invasion on Self-Regulation Impairment

IT has unprecedentedly invaded every nook and cranny of our lives, ulteriorly knocking down the walls between public and private spheres. Recently, IT has become an indispensable component of businesses and has emerged as a strategic resource for organizations to address the challenges of a volatile business environment [36, 79]. For example, the notion of IT infrastructure underscores the importance of mobilizing and deploying IT-enabled knowledge ambidexterity to improve business flexibility and innovation performance [8, 9]. Nonetheless, the tougher requirements for individuals to promptly get connected at any time, as well as to constantly adapt to new applications, IT know-hows, and workflows have also led to various undesirable outcomes. Considering the three-aforementioned main categories of negative consequences of techno-invasion, we aim to explain how the psychological processes coping with techno-invasion deplete self-regulatory resources and impair one’s ability to constrain subsequent inappropriate responses in three main ways: job-related depletion efects, IT use related depletion efects, and well-being related depletion efects.

Figure 1. The proposed research model.

First, techno-invasion depletes one’s self-regulatory resources because of the constant demands placed by continual connectivity, resulting in ambiguity on which task or work process to perform. Specifically, with the IT-triggered changes in the “technical system,” that is, changes in the tasks and processes, individuals tend to reorganize their familiar work habits and cope with increased possibilities for remote supervision, multitasking, and ubiquitous connectivity. Constant connectivity via various mobile communication tools (e.g., laptops and smartphones) has made it a new routine for individuals to simultaneously cope with diverse streams of information from both internal and external sources [73]. Moreover, exposing oneself to constant floods of invasive push notifications can cause a nervous breakdown, concentration problems, and disturb daily routines [80]. Given the fact that workplace norms require individuals to behave in a certain manner such as adherence to rules and procedures, they need to constantly engage in self-regulation, diminishing their reserve of self-regulatory strength.

Second, techno-invasion depletes one’s self-regulatory resources through struggles to deal with constantly evolving IT and the changing cognitive and social requirements related to their use. Arguably, enhancements in connectivity heighten the speed of workflow and amplify expectations for IT-enabled productivity [24]. Consequently, those “tech fluent” individuals perceiving high levels of techno-invasion experience dificulties of volitionally controlling their attention, thus tend to depend in substantial part on their self-regulatory strength to constrain the deviant urges. Third, techno-invasion depletes one’s selfregulatory resources by fewer possibilities of being mentally disconnected from work. The need to instantly respond to work demands is significantly stimulated by techno-invasion and ultimately takes “time away” from work [2]. Evidence suggests that techno-invasion leads to burnout by enabling individuals to be accessible to the ofice anytime and anywhere via smart appliances [84]. The deluge of work emails, text messages, and phone calls lead to a 24-hour interconnected, hyperactive, sleep-deprived, work-fueled world for individuals, resulting in a detrimental spillover of work-related IT to the private sphere [97]. For instance, BlackBerry®’s common pseudonym of “CrackBerrys” in popular literature and even initial academic research identifies individuals’ over-identification with IT, which thereby contributes to diminished well-being in individuals [2]. Being “always exposed” leads individuals to believe that their out-of-the-ofice life is intertwined with IT, which creates feelings of losing control over their private time and space, leading to a longstanding battle against self-regulatory strength depletion resulted from techno-invasion. Thereby, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): Techno-invasion is positively related to self-regulation impairment.

## Self-Regulation Impairment and Deviant Behavior

Deviant behavior refers to the voluntary behavior that violates significant organizational norms and in so doing threatens the well-being of an organization, its members, or both [76]. Employees’ deviant behavior includes wasting time on the job, taking property from IT and non-IT resources without permission, purposely performing the work badly, or taking excessive online or ofline breaks. Deviant behavior can be examined with a focus on IT, non-IT, or overall covering IT and non-IT relatedness. This study focuses on deviant behavior related to IT and non-IT deviances. Deviant behavior has been demonstrated to be the consequence of various organizationally-valued issues, such as job dissatisfaction [11], workplace stressors [83, 101], and poor organizational support [65]. Hence, organizations have enhanced their awareness of recognizing the potential antecedents of deviant behavior to aid the development of coping approaches to reduce, avoid, or alleviate such behaviors. By what mechanism might individuals be motivated to engage in deviant behaviors? More recently, it has been suggested that, as opposed to motivation, deviant behavior might be brought about through one’s inability to self-regulate their undesirable urges [63]. Self-regulation theory [7] claims that individuals have impulsive needs concerning certain stimuli. Individuals may apply self-regulation to contain these impulses by using their executive function, whereby manage their basest instincts [100] that can be destructive to the self and others. Prior research has established a strong advocate of these viewpoints that impaired self-regulation is related to various dysfunctional and counterproductive behaviors, such as cheating [23], deception [103], abuse [63], and excessive use of Internet [39]. We expect that a critical threshold might be reached when an individual no longer has required resources to maintain self-regulation (provoked by techno-invasion), thus enhancing the likelihood of exhibiting deviant behaviors. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Self-regulation impairment is positively related to deviant behavior.

## Moderating Role of Perceived Team Bottom-Line Mentality

Perceived team bottom-line mentality is a team-level construct referring to the onedimensional mindset that conveys the environment calls for the pursuit of bottom-line (i.e., the team) outcomes above all else [3]. Central to this conceptualization is the plausible belief that a high bottom-line mentality team’s pursuit of success often comes at the expense of the individual interests of its members. However, organizations may have both employees that possess similar and diferent interests regarding their team bottom-line. Despite bottom-line mindset is supposed to promote the overall competitiveness and vitality of the organization [35], we hypothesize that, when co-occurred with techno-invasion, these desirable performance-related circumstances can have detrimental efects on selfregulation. We draw on self-regulation theory to explain theoretically how team bottomline mentality positively moderates the relationship between techno-invasion and selfregulation impairment.

From the self-regulatory perspective [7], the depletion efects of techno-invasion can be aggravated by circumstances that pose inconsistent information, whereby recipients experience stress that they fixate on and are motivated to lessen. This fixation and efort to address the stress drain self-regulatory resources needed to appropriately respond to those undesirable conditions in the workplace. Hence, with the IT-enabled persistent connectivity, there is an inherent tension between the types of behaviors that benefit individuals, such as being not connected during non-work time, and those demanded by a highly pressing bottomline mentality team, in which individuals can be reachable outside work settings to align with the interests of the team. Thus, a debilitating efect on self-regulatory resources is more likely to occur because of the perceived pressure received from the team.

Furthermore, prior research has demonstrated that attention toward a goal related to current conditions leads to impaired self-regulation [63]. In line with self-regulation theory, goals can be identified as internal desired end-states arranged hierarchically, so that superordinate goals are established based on individuals’ central needs representing their most important personal objectives [28]. More significantly, superordinate goals and desired endstates exert top-down control of actions such that behavior is originated from comparisons between current states and superordinate end-states [6]. Thus, in the stressful context of techno-invasion, one’s behaviors deviating from normative expectations lead to a less than desirable current state for the team afterward. Further, the contradictions between current and desired states can be ulteriorly exacerbated when the team has explicit, tough, and superordinate-desired states that are highly discrepant from the current state. In this regard, a high bottom-line mentality team carrying in its core a substantially exclusive emphasis on bottom-line achievements (e.g., profitability, business activities, and the bottom-line) tends to lengthen the regular workday of its members by IT-enabled connectivity to ensure they enhance rather than undermine bottom-line accomplishment. Consequently, the use of limited self-regulatory strength presents a resource allocation dilemma, in that individuals must at times make psychologically challenging trade-ofs between expending eforts to benefit the team (e.g., vigorously pursuing bottom-line success) or meeting individual needs (e.g., spending more time in personal pursuits and activities).

As proposed by Baumeister [5], motivational conflict presents the essential context for understanding self-regulation, insofar the clashing demands of nature and culture can be viewed as the most principal motivational conflict regarding the current state (natural impulses) and superordinate-desired state (cultural or team demands). Specifically, the motivation to prevent work-related IT from exorbitantly invading the private sphere is human nature, meanwhile, the fact that IT use can remarkably promote work performance and strengthen supervision to achieve bottom-line success is a cultural value in a high bottom-line mentality team. Hence, when techno-invasion is paired with a high team bottom-line mentality, there remains a chronic nexus of motivational conflict between natural impulses and cultural (i.e., organizational, team) demands. Put another way, in the context of techno-invasion, individuals’ cognitive responses can contradict their high bottom-line mentality team’s expectations that every member will do whatever it takes to pursue bottom-line success, including being “always-on” or “always exposed.” Thus, they tend to substantially rely on their self-regulatory resources to reconcile the internal conflicts and are more likely to experience self-regulation impairment when the pressure and mentality from the team are high.

In sum, techno-invasion presents an example of conditions that are beneficial for a high bottom-line mentality team while being physically and mentally exhausting for individuals. In comparison to a low bottom-line mentality team, members in a high bottom-line mentality team tend to invest self-regulatory resources to cope with stress and frustration resulting from techno-invasion. Specifically, a high bottom-line mentality amplifies discrepancies between the team’s current and desired states, magnifying self-regulation impairment and constraining abilities to overrule detrimental impulses stimulated by technoinvasion. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): The positive relationship between techno-invasion and self-regulation impairment is positively moderated by team bottom-line mentality, such as the relationship is stronger when bottom-line mentality is higher rather than lower.

## Moderating Role of IT Mindfulness: IT Mindfulness as a Potential Remedy to Techno-Invasion Consequences

Mindfulness refers to an individual’s continuous scrutiny and refinement of expectations based on new experiences, appreciation of subtleties, and identification of novel aspects of context that can improve foresight and functioning [52]. Prior research on mindfulness has focused on how organizations and individuals can display more of these mindful behaviors and thereby reduce the logical opposite: mindlessness, which refers to a state of reduced attention accompanied by rigid, inflexible use of existing categories and procedures, by the non-nuanced appreciation of context, and by acting on “automatic pilot,” resulting when fewer conscious cognitive processes are activated less often [102]. Techno-invasion will impair one’s self-regulation ability and alter the eficiency of optimal behavioral responses, which, accordingly, may lead to mindless behaviors. Conversely, while individuals sense an escalating state of involvement and focus at the moment (i.e., being mindful), they are more sensitive to environmental changes and corresponding opportunities for action. Hence, individual mindfulness may enhance the ability to sense and recognize unwitting psychological responses to eliciting situations or events, thereby substituting such uncontrolled reactions with controlled adaptive responses and reinforcing self-awareness, and empowering self-regulation ability [104]. Moreover, individuals may also develop an adaptive detached observational stance regarding stressful conditions and related psychological reactions by applying mindfulness practice. Thus, among various stress coping strategies, mindfulness practice has been progressively recognized as one of the new potential approaches for supporting stress management and for restraining the outcomes of impaired self-regulation [50].

Mindfulness can be applied and leveraged in the context of IT use and leverage. Pickert [68] argues that mindfulness in IT use (IT mindfulness) can help individuals navigate IT thus making IT works for us, not the other way around. IT mindfulness is a dynamic ITspecific trait whereby users focus on the present, pay attention to detail, exhibit a willingness to consider other uses, and express genuine interest in investigating IT features and failures [95], and experimenting with IT resources. IT mindfulness includes employees’ focus on being creative using IT, and learning and discovering new and more eficient ways of using IT. We argue and expect that IT mindfulness, by improving self-regulation, self-awareness and self-eficacy can optimize the eficiency of psychological, cognitive, and behavioral reactions to techno-invasion, and help employees to use it as a potential remedy to the techno-invasion efect on self-regulation impairment. IT mindful individuals can use, leverage, and experiment with IT with greater awareness, focus, and self-eficacy thus increasing self-regulation resources [13] and acting as a potential remedy to the selfregulation impairment generated by techno-invasion and techno-stress. For instance, IT mindfulness can enable individuals to be more psychologically flexible in the moment to mentally filter information about stressors [10]. IT mindfulness can also improve behavioral control and self-regulation [48], and help employees to eficiently avoid routine-based or automatic action generated by techno-invasion [75]. Hence, mindfully utilizing IT can contribute to heightened self-awareness and enhanced attention to current IT use and IT experimentation, leading to reinforced self-regulation in the context of techno-invasion, which suggests that IT mindfulness can act as a remedy to reduce self-regulation impairment. Thereby, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4 (H4): The positive relationship between techno-invasion and self-regulation impairment is negatively moderated by IT mindfulness, such as the relationship is stronger when IT mindfulness is lower than higher.

## Research Methodology

## Data Collection

Given the concerns to reduce potential common method bias [70] and to better infer the causal relationships in our model, we included only organizations for which data were available for multi-wave collection. The original population consisted of a convenience sample of two financial service companies in Southwestern China with which a Chinese faculty member on the research team had a personal connection. Chinese culture stresses in-group versus outgroup status diferentials and thus places a heavy emphasis on interpersonal relationships [42]. Chinese business highlights a high-context communication style in which “most of the information is either in the physical context or internalized in the person” [38, p. 79]. Thus, to improve the response rate, we considered it necessary to use personal contacts [88]. Before data collection, based on the short interview with several randomly selected supervisors and employees from these two companies, we were told that their companies do not demand their employees to be reachable to fulfill their tasks every day. Thus, we consider constant availability is not expected by these two companies, thus employees’ perceptions of techno-invasion and its consequences are not afected.

We invited 280 team members and their 90 supervisors working in 90 customer service teams from these two companies to participate in our study. Team members were responsible for answering customers’ inquiries, promoting financial products and services, and dealing with complaints. Their salary and commissions were based on team-level performance so that team members require strong communication and collaboration. One feature of customer service that makes it particularly well suited for testing our hypotheses is that service quality highly relies on the extent to which IT (e.g., customer relationship management applications) enables marketing and operations functions of the teams [49]. An increasing number of financial service teams provide IT-based service options to their customers [49]. To elaborate, IT practices could help enhance customer service by increasing convenience, collecting service performance information for management use, and ofering extra services [16]. Consequently, while IT serves a substantially supportive function in financial service firms, especially in customer service teams, it is inevitable for the team members and their supervisors to highly depend on IT to meet the working demands, which may potentially lead to techno-invasion. In sum, our data collected from customer service teams allow us to better assess the relationship between techno-invasion and its workplace consequences. As participating companies desired to constrain the time involvement for their workforce and we were only able to survey a subset of team members – two to five respondents were randomly selected from each team. The human resources (HR) department of each company invited team members and team leaders to voluntarily participate in the survey and assured to keep confidential their responses. Team members and leaders separately completed their respective paper-and-pencil surveys and returned their surveys directly to the researchers. Two waves of data collection, which lasted one month, were performed. Table 1 presents the data collection procedure. We collected data at a two-week interval which is consistent with the surveyed teams’ operations. After discussion with company employees (i.e., HR professionals, team leaders, and subordinates), we were informed that the surveyed teams held a biweekly meeting to discuss workrelated issues. Thus, we consider the two-week intervals between measurement points appropriate for testing the hypotheses.

At $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ , we distributed surveys to 280 team members to identify and provide ratings of their experienced techno-invasion, IT mindfulness, perceived team bottom-line mentality, and demographic data. Their 90 immediate supervisors were asked to provide ratings of their teams’ demographic data (i.e., team size and age). According to past studies (e.g., [21, 89]), we consider supervisors as the appropriate respondents to provide their teams’ demographic data (i.e., team size and age) because they need to contact each employee for his/her task fulfillment and are responsible for the evaluations of their employees’ performance. Among the employees, 256 responded to our survey (91.43 percent response rate). All 90 supervisors responded to our survey (100 percent response rate). At $\mathrm { T } _ { 2 }$ (two weeks later), we asked the employee participants who responded at $\mathrm { T } _ { 1 }$ to rate their self-regulation impairment and deviant behaviors. We received responses from 234 employees (91.41 percent response rate) afiliated with 85 teams.

## Participants

Our final sample included 234 employees and 85 team leaders. For employees, about 52.6 percent of them were less than 30 years of age, about 89.7 percent of them had an undergraduate degree, about 31.2 percent of them were men, about 63.2 percent of them are married or living with a partner, and about 43.2 percent of them have children under 18 years old. On average, employees had 3.53 years of working experience (SD = 3.82). The average team size was about 27.8 and the average team age is 2.41. There were no statistically significant diferences between respondents and non-respondents concerning age, gender, educational level, job tenure, marital status, and parental status.

## Measurement Instrument

We adopted measures established and verified in previous studies to ensure the validity of the survey (see Online Supplemental Appendix D). Surveys were provided in Chinese, the native language of the participants. We followed the translation-back translation procedures outlined by Brislin [17] to develop the Chinese version of the surveys. A five-point Likert scale was used in the measures related to the key constructs of the research model. The three-item scale for techno-invasion was adapted from Ragu-Nathan et al. [73]. The measurement items for IT mindfulness were adapted from Thatcher et al. [95]. The measurement items for perceived team bottom-line mentality were adapted from Bonner et al. [15]. Self-regulation impairment was measured with the impact of events scale [44], adapted to specific events [64], and here to the employees’ IT usage at work. A seven-item scale was adapted from Mawritz et al. [63]. Respondents indicated how often they experienced these thoughts in their past few workdays. Employees’ deviant behaviors were accessed with a six-item antisocial behaviors measure [77, 94]. Respondents indicated the frequency with which they engaged in the behaviors listed.

We controlled for employee age, gender, educational level, tenure, marital status, and parental status because it has been found to relate to employee behavior [40] and influence individuals’ psychological afections [59]. Employee age was included because previous research has found that younger tend to engage in more deviant behaviors than their older counterparts [27]. Gender was included because men and women may difer in emotional control [1], and men tend to engage in more deviant behaviors [34, 65]. Tenure was included because employees with no job tenure were more likely to engage in deviant behaviors [43]. Education level was controlled as individuals with high levels of education tend to higher levels of moral judgment which may, in turn, influence their workplace deviance [72]. The marital and parental status were controlled as past research has demonstrated that employees’ relationships (for example, conflicts) with their spouses and children may afect their psychological status at work which may, in turn, influence their deviant responses [31]. We included additional controls to take both the heterogeneity of the sample and the practices particular to self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior into account. Specifically, we controlled for team size and age because as suggested in prior deviance research [82], and may influence group processes and employee performance (e.g., [29]).

## Variance Decomposition and the Assessment of Non-Independence

To test whether the group membership (i.e., nesting of members in teams) accounts for a significant amount of variance in the individual-level dependent variable, we calculated its inter-member reliability. To test inter-member reliability, we calculated ICC (1), the proportion of variance in the rating caused by team membership [14]. We also tested whether the average scores were significantly diferent across teams by calculating the F-test using a one-way analysis of variance. The results for deviant behavior were as follows: ICC (1) = .13, F-value $= 1 . 4 3 , p < . 0 5$ . The ICC (1) value exceeded the value of .12 suggested by James [46]. Thus, there is evidence of group-level influences on the individual-level deviant behavior outcome. Given the substantial amount of between-group variance in the outcome variable, multilevel modeling was an appropriate analytical strategy for testing this study’s hypotheses [74].

## Post-hoc Analysis

We conducted mediation and mediated moderation analyses to establish the robustness of the mediation by self-regulation impairment of the hypothesized interaction efects (i.e., techno-invasion and interactions between techno-invasion and perceived team bottom-line mentality and IT mindfulness) on deviant behavior. To extend the of test the indirect efect of techno-invasion on deviant behavior via self-regulation impairment, we further applied the Monte Carlo bootstrapping approach and bootstrapped 20,000 estimations of this indirect efect. The confidence interval of the indirect efect based on the bootstrapping estimation was significant (indirect efect = .03, 95 percent confidence interval $[ \mathrm { C I } ] = [ . 0 0 2 $ .069], excluding 0), yielding the support for this indirect efect. To verify H3, we further tested the impact of techno-invasion on deviant behavior via self-regulation impairment was moderated by perceived team bottom-line mentality. We applied the Monte Carlo bootstrapping approach and bootstrapped 20,000 estimations of this mediated-moderating efect. According to the results in Table 5, the indirect efects of techno-invasion on deviant behavior through self-regulation impairment were positive if perceived team bottom-line mentality was high (indirect efect = .06, 95 percent CI [.02, .13]). The indirect efects were not significant when the perceived team bottom-line mentality was low (indirect efect = .00, 95 percent CI [-.04, .04]). The diference between the conditional indirect efects was significant (diference = .06, 95 percent CI [.01, .13]), yielding support for H3. Similarly, to recheck H4, we further tested whether the impact of techno-invasion on deviant behavior via self-regulation impairment was moderated by IT mindfulness. According to the results in Table 5, the indirect efects of techno-invasion on deviant behavior through selfregulation impairment were positive if IT mindfulness was low (indirect efect = .07, 95 percent CI [.02, .13]). The indirect efects were not significant when the IT mindfulness was high (indirect efect = -.00, 95% CI [-.04, .04]). ## Discussion

## Discussion of Results

In a digitalized workplace, techno-invasion threats employees by blurring the lines between work and personal life, forcing individuals to be available to work requests continuously. As one of the negative consequences, techno-invasion can generate is employee’s deviant behavior, this study examines this cutting-edge and unexplored relationship in the context of IS research, self-regulation theory, and IT mindfulness. We emphasize the theorization and empirical examination of the psychological processes and the boundary conditions under which techno-invasion can afect deviant behavior. We use self-regulation theory to investigate the role of self-regulation impairment (i.e., a reduction in individuals’ selfcontrol to respond to basic instincts and impulses) as a potential mechanism in the relationship between techno-invasion and deviant behavior. This study also proposes that IT mindfulness and team bottom-line mentality can serve as two boundary conditions that can alter the impairment efect of techno-invasion on self-regulation. The empirical evidence from a two-wave time-lagged survey lent support to the proposed research model. We find that techno-invasion impairs employees’ self-regulatory strength, which ultimately leads to deviant behavior in the workplace. Techno-invasion consumes self-regulatory resources and impairs one’s ability to constrain subsequent inappropriate responses, thus generating self-regulation impairment. When employees have fewer resources to keep selfregulation (caused by techno-invasion), they increase deviant behavior. We also discover that team bottom-line mentality reinforces the efect of techno-invasion on self-regulation impairment and that IT mindfulness acts as a remedy for the negative consequences of techno-invasion by reducing the efect of techno-invasion on self-regulation impairment. When employees work on teams with a substantially exclusive emphasis on bottom-line achievements (e.g., profitability, business activities, and the bottom-line) (i.e., team bottomline mentality), the efect of techno-invasion on self-regulation impairment is reinforced. Diferently, IT mindfulness helps employees to reduce the negative efect (i.e., impairment) of techno-invasion on self-regulation. IT mindfulness includes employees’ focus on being creative using IT, and learning and discovering new and more eficient ways of using IT. We find that IT mindfulness, by improving self-regulation, self-awareness and self-eficacy optimizes the eficiency of psychological, cognitive, and behavioral reactions to technoinvasion, and help employees to use it as one remedy to the techno-invasion efect on selfregulation impairment. Our analysis controls for a significant number of control variables on self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior (e.g., employee age, employee gender, employee tenure, marital status, parental status). It is important to control for all these variables on self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior to ensure the results obtained are consistent. Regarding these control variables’ efects, we find that employee age and employee gender to be negative and significantly related to self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior. In this study’s context, female gender and younger employees exhibited higher self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior. These results are consistent with prior research in the case of employee age but diferent in the case of employee gender [e.g., 34, 65]. The team size efect on self-regulation impairment was positive and significant, which suggests team pressure derived from a greater size reduces employees’ self-control.

## Incremental Contributions to IS Research

Through the intersection of technostress, and organizational and individual behavior, and the examination of the way to remedy or alleviate the negative consequences of technoinvasion, this paper makes three key incremental contributions to IS research. First, we explain theoretically and show empirically that techno-invasion afects employees’ deviant behavior. We focus on the role of psychological processes in the efect of techno-invasion on deviant behavior by extending prior IS research on the negative consequences of technoinvasion and technostress in the workplace. Second, this paper uses self-regulation theory to examine the mechanisms and psychological processes through which techno-invasion influences deviant behavior. Specifically, we propose and explore self-regulation impairment (i.e., a reduction in employee’s self-control) as a mechanism explaining the underlying relationship between techno-invasion and deviant behavior. Contrasting prior research that has investigated counterproductive behaviors emanating from a direct relationship with techno-invasion [33, 69], our research explains deviant behavior as resulting from the depletion of self-regulatory resources (i.e., self-regulation impairment) due to the exhausting nature of techno-invasion, such that individuals engage in deviant behavior because their abilities to constrain the deviant desires are impaired. Our research points to the possibility that even those who have relatively strong self-control and self-discipline engage in deviant behavior when provoked by techno-invasion. Despite the important influence of self-regulation impairment on deviant behavior, a theoretical explanation and empirical evidence for its mediator role are scarce. This is the second contribution of this paper to IS research. We hope that, by demonstrating the mediating role of self-regulation impairment, this study encourages additional interest in how techno-invasion results in workplace outcomes through psychological processes.

Finally, our research contributes to IS research by examining potential boundary conditions at both team level (i.e., team bottom-line mentality) and individual level (i.e., IT mindfulness) through which techno-invasion afects self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior. This study explains theoretically and tests empirically the moderating role of team bottom-line mentality and IT mindfulness in the positive relationship between techno-invasion and self-regulation impairment. On the one hand, we extend prior research on team bottom-line mentality by demonstrating its dysfunctional nature in instances of techno-invasion. Our findings indicate that, when co-occurred with techno-invasion, the current states of a high bottom-line mentality team fall short of their desired states, making its members more likely to experience self-regulation impairment. On the other hand, we develop the emerging IS research on IT mindfulness (e.g., [19, 95]) by showing that IT mindfulness serves as a remedy to reduce the impairment efect of techno-invasion on self-regulation, and indirectly on employees’ deviant behavior.

## Lessons Learned for IT and Business Executives and Employees

Our results also provide insightful lessons for IT and business executives and employees. First, our findings suggest that IT and business executives should be cautious because techno-invasion leads to employees’ deviant behavior. Techno-invasion (e.g., the expectation being accessible to work-related messages anytime and anywhere via smart IT) provokes employees’ actions such as wasting time on the job, taking property from IT and non-IT resources without permission, purposely performing the work badly, or taking excessive online or ofline breaks (i.e., deviant behavior), which will be harmful to organizations and employees. Mitigating these negative consequences clearly contributes to the vision of an IT-enabled bright society [54]. Our findings should inspire IT and business executives to design IT and HR policies that prevent and help employees manage these negative consequences [51, 60]. Deviant behavior can result in employees’ dismissals, thus suggesting employees ask organizations and employees to search for potential remedies. Second, our research indicates that techno-invasion contributes to individuals’ deviant behavior through its impairment efect on their self-regulatory resources. Individuals have impulsive needs (e.g., deviant behavior) concerning certain stimuli (i.e., technoinvasion). As such, IT and business executives should limit or prevent techno-invasion in non-work time because it could result in individuals’ self-regulation impairment and deviant behavior subsequently.

Third, despite the importance of bottom-line success for organizations, our research suggests that high team bottom-line mentality serves as a condition that reinforces the impairment efect of techno-invasion, making deviant behavior more likely to occur. As such, organizations need to understand that, when a high bottom-line mentality team is paired with techno-invasion, its members will experience impaired self-regulation, which facilitates deviant behavior. Hence, there is a need for IT and business executives to foster a focus on employees’ physical and mental health-related to IT use in addition to securing bottom-line outcomes. This awareness will facilitate organizations to create a healthy workplace where the team and organizational business benefits, and work-life balance co-exist.

Finally, our findings indicate that IT mindfulness counteracts the impairment efect of techno-invasion has on self-regulatory resources. Prior research indicates that IT mindfulness can be trained and learned and that IT mindfulness interventions have positive health and organizational consequences [45, 95]. We find that IT mindfulness-based workplace interventions can help alleviate the depletion efect of techno-invasion on selfregulatory resources. Employees’ focus on being creative using IT, and learning and discovering new and more eficient ways of using IT improve self-regulation, self-awareness, and self-eficacy, thus acting as a remedy to alleviate the efect of techno-invasion on selfregulation impairment, as implemented by Google. We encourage IT and business executives to invest resources to assimilate and implement IT mindfulness projects and initiatives to prevent self-regulation impairment and employees’ deviant behaviors.

## Limitations and Future IS Research Directions

Despite these contributions, our research is limited in several ways. First, we collected data by the common method using a self-rating questionnaire, which is subject to biases that could have afected the results due to respondents’ own knowledge and personal insights. Nonetheless, since our data was collected from more than one source, potential limitations related to self-rating scales are somewhat lightened. Further objective assessment is required to validate our results, such as by measuring objective indicators of techno-invasion like phycological changes in stress hormones. Second, because the present study examines negative workplace behavior (i.e., deviant behavior), our participants may not have answered questions truthfully attributed to social desirability. Prior studies have suggested that a longitudinal analysis of such behavior can constrain the impact of social desirability [11, 22]. Hence, we believe our two-wave study alleviates the influences of social desirability. Finally, future IS research should use qualitative research methods (e.g., in-depth case studies) to shed additional light on how employees feel and react when confronted with techno-invasion.

## Conclusion

Given the ubiquity of IT resources in organizational and personal life, it is important that the impact of being “always exposed” (i.e., techno-invasion) is acknowledged, prevented, and managed. This study examines the relationship between techno-invasion and employees deviant behavior in the context of IS research, self-regulation theory, and IT mindfulness. Using a dataset from a two-wave time-lagged survey, we find that that techno-invasion impairs employees’ self-regulatory strength (i.e., self-regulation impairment), which ultimately leads to deviant behavior in the workplace. We also discover that team bottom-line mentality (i.e., a mentality with a high emphasis on team performance and achievements) reinforces the efect of techno-invasion on self-regulation impairment and that IT mindfulness acts as a remedy of the negative consequences of techno-invasion by reducing the efect of techno-invasion on selfregulation impairment. We find that IT mindfulness, by improving self-regulation, selfawareness and self-eficacy optimizes the eficiency of psychological, cognitive, and behavioral reactions to techno-invasion, and help employees to use it as one remedy to the technoinvasion efect on self-regulation impairment. This research theorizes and examines empirically the psychological processes and the boundary conditions (e.g., IT mindfulness) under which techno-invasion afects deviant behavior. As IT mindfulness has become to serve as a potential remedy or prevention mechanism of the negative consequences of techno-invasion, we hope this paper can inspire IS scholars to develop this inspiring emerging research topic on the role of IT mindfulness in building healthier and more work-life balance jobs and workplaces.
