# Why Do Data Analysts Take IT-Mediated Shortcuts? An Ego-Depletion Perspective

Maryam Ghasemaghaei <sup>a</sup> and Ofir Turel <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>DeGroote School of Business, McMaster University, Hamilton, Ontario, Canada; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems Management, University of Melbourne, Victoria, Australia

## ABSTRACT

We aim to understand why employees take information technology (IT)-mediated shortcuts, that is, skipping one or several steps for completing tasks quicker by bending the rules. This is a specific and often detrimental form of noncompliant behavior. Adopting an egodepletion perspective, we posit that IT complexity drives IT-mediated shortcuts by increasing employees’ ego-depletion. Extending this view, we use a modified Delphi study and build on self-regulatory and goal setting theories to point to key boundary conditions for these efects. First, in a preliminary study we found that taking IT-mediated shortcuts in our context is, on average, detrimental to employee performance. This highlighted the need to focus on IT-mediated shortcuts. Next, we tested our assertions with three experiments focusing on the use of dashboards with 584 data analysts. The results show that (1) dashboard complexity increases ego depletion, (2) ego depletion fully mediates the impact of dashboard complexity on taking ITmediated shortcuts, (3) moral integrity moderates the influence of ego depletion on taking IT-mediated shortcuts, and (4) outcome compared to learning goals enhance the impact of ego depletion on ITmediated shortcuts. In all studies, objectively measured IT-mediated shortcut-taking was negatively associated with objectively measured task performance. Ultimately, the integrated perspective explains whether, how, and under what conditions IT complexity drives ITmediated shortcuts.

KEYWORDS

IT complexity; IT-mediated shortcuts; work noncompliance; workarounds; moral integrity; goal setting; ego depletion; IT-mediated noncompliance; data analysts

## Introduction

Work is often characterized by stringent deadlines and competing demands [74]. Subsequently, employees may take shortcuts to finish job tasks on time [14]. Taking shortcuts entails skipping one or several steps for completing tasks quicker by bending the rules [48]. For example, instead of analyzing both structured and unstructured data, a data analyst may only focus on the structured data in order to finish job tasks in a timely manner. As such, taking shortcuts can be viewed as a specific instance of noncompliant behavior, which reflects deviation from prescribed work procedures and/or norms. Applied to information technology (IT), taking IT-mediated shortcuts is a noncompliant ITmediated behavior that reflects the partial performance of implied or explicated job instructions.

While in some situations taking shortcuts is harmless, given that job instructions are typically designed based on experience and with outcome optimization and risk reduction objectives in mind, deviating from implied or explicated job instructions is typically unhealthy and should, on average, result in suboptimal decisions [35] and reduced safety [88]. Thus, while taking shortcuts enables individuals to cope with complex job tasks and complete them faster [93], it makes them vulnerable to partial information biases and hasty decisions [15]. Here, we do not make assumptions whether IT mediated shortcuts are good or bad for performance because such efects relate to the firm and employee emphases and preferences (e.g., accuracy vs. eficiency). We instead test this association. Specifically, as a preliminary step, we conducted a pilot study using 69 data analysts. We found that those who took shortcuts had lower objective performance (i.e., less optimal decisions) compared to others. Of course, this may not generalize to all types of situations and decisions. Nevertheless, it demonstrates that IT-mediated shortcuts can, at least in some contexts, adversely afect decision quality.

Unfortunately, taking IT-mediated shortcuts is common, especially in contexts in which IT is complex and supports complex decisions, such as data analytics. This is because completing complex job tasks, often under time pressure, may motivate individuals to respond quickly instead of deliberate cognition [27]. We noted this while working with both students learning analytics and users of analytics solutions in organizations. All often took shortcuts to overcome their mental constraints and deal with competing demands. This is consistent with recent literature on employees taking IT-mediated shortcuts while doing their job tasks [58] and industry data showing that around 23 percent of employees feel pressed to deviate from prescribed instructions [56]. Therefore, it is important to understand the mechanisms that lead to taking IT-mediating shortcuts. Here, we strive to provide a starting point for research that addresses the questions of (1) whether, (2) when, and (3) how complex technologies (the use of which is cognitively demanding [e.g., data analytics solutions]) can drive taking IT-mediated shortcuts. Addressing these questions can serve as a departure point in the journey to help organizations not only better understand the risks they take when implementing cognitively demanding IT but also for developing interventions aimed at reducing the adverse efects of complex solutions. Given that the literature is relatively silent about such issues and the unique complexity of modern IT (e.g., the ability of data analytics tools to analyze and display information on both structured and unstructured data), there is a pertinent need to understand the processes that motivate shortcut taking, and later designing and implementing efective policies and interventions for curbing undesirable shortcut taking.

While there is a growing body of research on diferent noncompliant IT-mediated behaviors, specifically in the context of information security [25], this literature is fairly context-specific (mostly regarding security policy violations) and assumes rationality of decision makers. It may not generalize to other noncompliant behaviors, such as taking ITmediated shortcuts, and it does not typically account for reflexive and less-rational behaviors [101, 102]. Thus, instead of relying on these highly informative works, the importance of which should not be discounted, we leverage theories from domains in which people are in similar situations to one’s in which they are compelled to take IT-mediated shortcuts without thinking. In such situations people want to achieve goals but do not always have the resources to deliberate and act optimally or as prescribed. These situations are captured by the core ideas of ego depletion (i.e., when the self does not have all the resources it typically has for reflecting on and controlling future behaviors [12]). This theory is well suited for explaining noncompliant behaviors from a holistic perspective [115]. This also represents an opportunity for information systems (IS) scholars, because this important theory has been well established in reference disciplines [47], but has rarely been directly discussed in IS research. As such, future research can leverage our ideas to study other noncompliant ITmediated behaviors. This perspective suits our context because cognitively-demanding technologies have the potential to deplete people’s resources [103]. They can leave users with insuficient means to reflect on and control tempting but suboptimal behaviors [33], such as taking IT-mediated shortcuts.

Further contextualizing this perspective, we theorize on and test boundary conditions or factors that can afect ego depletion processes. Because there are no prescribed boundary conditions, we conducted a modified Delphi study with 18 data analysts (see Online Supplementary Appendix A). It identified two key factors (moral integrity, an individual diference; and a goal setting type [outcome vs. learning], an organizational context variable) that can serve as boundary conditions for the aforementioned ego-depletion mechanisms. We then theoretically integrate them into the model and conducted a series of three online experiments with data analysts, in which we examine the efect of manipulated IT complexity (here, dashboard complexity<sup>2</sup>) on ego depletion (Studies 1-3) and actual (objectively measured) engagement in shortcuts while using IT (Studies 2-3). We also examine how (1) moral integrity (Study 2) and (2) goal-setting type (Study 3) influence the efects of IT complexity on taking IT-mediated shortcuts, as mediated via ego depletion. In all studies, we also show that IT-mediated shortcuts, at least in the examined task, can be detrimental to task performance. Ultimately, we shed light on whether, how, why, and when IT complexity increases taking IT-mediated shortcuts. We contribute to the literature by providing a general theory regarding taking IT-mediated shortcuts, contextualizing it by finding and testing two key boundary conditions, and focusing on a relatively under-studied, yet common and important, IT phenomena. Thus, we pave the way for future research on diferent noncompliant behaviors and for more deeply understanding the root causes, mechanisms, and consequences of IT-mediated shortcuts.

## Theoretical Background

## Taking Shortcuts as a Noncompliant Behavior

Taking shortcuts is a specific instance of the broader concept of workarounds, which is part of the even broader concept of noncompliance. It is diferent from using a subset of IT features.<sup>3</sup> Workarounds refer to changes to aspects of a work system in order to bypass, overcome, or reduce the impact of obstacles that are perceived as preventing individuals from achieving desired goals [2]. Noncompliance concerns gaps between informal (e.g., expectations) and formal (e.g., guidelines/ regulations) norms and human behaviors [113]. Employees can comply (or not) with IT-related expectations and policies [57, 83]. Noncompliance is often driven by putting more weight on the goal of completing tasks in a timely manner vs quality [111].

We posit that the workarounds and noncompliance concepts are broader than taking ITmediated shortcuts because they can describe other noncompliant behaviors. For example, a workaround and noncompliant behavior may involve delegating tasks from one employee to another (e.g., a physician may ask a nurse to enter clinical data into the medical records system) but still completing all the steps in the task [53]. Thus, to be consistent with our phenomenon of interest, we focus on IT-mediated shortcuts and not the broad concepts of noncompliance or workarounds. Nevertheless, the literature on IT workarounds and noncompliance can be informative. Specifically, engagement in this broad family of behaviors, presumably also specifically with IT-mediated shortcuts, can lead to skipping important work procedures. Additioanlly, as we found in the preliminary study, and later in the three studies, it can, at least in some contexts, reduce individuals’ job performance [90] and consequent organizational performance [58].

Table 1 summarizes key studies on workarounds and noncompliant behavior in the IT context. These works have been conducted mostly in the information security context, using theories of morality/ethics, criminology, sociology, and psychology. Without discounting the importance of such nuanced models that are contextualized to security noncompliance, we believe that the literature has overlooked a broader explanation for noncompliant behavior, namely ego-depletion. This has been shown to be a key driver of noncompliant behaviors across contexts [47]. In addition, the extant literature was contextualized to information security, with factors such as security training and fear appeals, and behaviors such as password sharing and illegal downloads [25]. This has been helpful, but it may be less relevant to other noncompliant behaviors, such as taking shortcuts. The role of the IT artifact complexity is also not clearly expressed in most past models, despite its potential to deplete people’s resources and drive noncompliant behaviors [54]. Nevertheless, there is still a lack of understanding of whether, when, and how IT complexity can motivate taking IT-mediated shortcuts. In addition, we view this behavior as a unique type of noncompliance, which can difer from other forms of noncompliance (e.g., security policy violation). Thus, we build on the established literature on ego-depletion to take a more fundamental perspective on noncompliant IT-mediated behaviors, and then contextualize it to the case of IT-mediated shortcuts, a behavior that has become a major problem for organizations.

## IT Complexity

IT complexity refers to the extent to which a system is dificult to understand and use [87]. It depends on the amount, variety, visual richness, and rate of information that the IT provides [112]. When IT is complex, finishing a task is more dificult because there will be multiple paths of achieving it, or diferent paths may conflict with each other [19, 108]. Complexity dimensions can include coordinative, component, and dynamic complexities [112]. Coordinative complexity describes interdependencies among information cues; component complexity describes the dissimilarity and density of information cues; and dynamic complexity captures the uncertainty and ambiguity of information.

Importantly, compared to the use of simple IT, complex IT needs more cognitive resources [108]. This is in line with cognitive complexity theory [99]. It suggests that the complexity of a task is determined by the number of new information elements to be learned. While information elements could be processed in isolation, they can only be understood when all are processed simultaneously [85]. For example, when data analysts use a dashboard to make decisions, they need to obtain and elaborate on various types of data to obtain the full picture [1, 36]. With the increased variety, velocity, and volume of information in the era of big data [37, 38, 40], it is reasonable to explain the increased complexity of analytics systems based on the abovementioned dimensions. Some even argue that complexity is the most important characteristic of advanced technologies [67]. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that IT complexity may drive IT users, at least under certain circumstances, to take shortcuts. The reasoning is that the characteristics of the technology (e.g., its complexity) may act to increase the motivation for deviance or to act opportunistically because the use of such technologies can be time consuming, cognitively demanding, and compete with other employee goals, such as completing tasks quickly or on time [61]. For example, with the increase in data variety, data analysts may find it dificult to process diferent types of data in almost real-time, and thus they may choose to generate recommendations based only on partial data.

In this study, we argue that when employees conduct complex IT-mediated job tasks, they subsequently lack the cognitive resources that guard against shortcuts. This view is derived from two mechanisms advocated by theories of self-regulation. First, when cognitive resources are lacking, people experience diminished self-control abilities and consequently may find it more appealing to take shortcuts [6]. Second, this lack of cognitive resources may also induce individuals to use defensive behaviors focusing on conserving resources (e.g., focus, energy) by preferring expedient behaviors, such as taking shortcuts. This explanation is based on the conservation of resources theory [50], which argues that humans’ resources are imperative to completing activities and tasks and, thus, individuals are encouraged to retain, obtain, and protect their resources. Indeed, complex cognitive tasks lead to more pressure to conserve resources, and hence to a more myopic focus on the present (rather than the future), and consequently drives impulsive actions [54].

## Ego Depletion and the Moral Self-Regulatory Perspective

Ego depletion theory suggests that humans have limited resources for self-control [10, 69]. Self-control is an individual’s capacity to refrain, override, or inhibit behavior based on impulses and desires [98]. The negative efect of ego depletion on self-control is well established [29, 34]. This phenomenon is known as self-regulatory resource depletion [23], and when it happens, it leads to self-control failures, which have been linked to noncompliant behaviors [47]. This happens because self-control requires mental energy, which is limited [10]. Baumeister et al. [13] compared self-control to a muscle, which needs energy and strength to exert force for a specific time. Similar to muscles that tire from exertion after repeated use, self-control performance may also deteriorate [47]. The reduction of resources following exertion of self-control is called “ego depletion” [10]. When people’s egos are depleted, they are more likely to engage in deviant behavior [5] because they lack the resources to inhibit wrongdoing but tempting behaviors that will help them conserve resources or reach their goals faster [41]. Importantly, complex tasks can drive ego depletion [106] because they consume mental energy and diminish self-control resources [73]. Applied to our context, we posit that when individuals engage in highly demanding IT-mediated activities, they consume their available cognitive resources, which can lead to ego depletion.

We note that concepts such as frustration, fatigue, and mental overload can be similar to ego depletion. They focus on humans’ limited capacity of working memory for problem solving [16]. However, unlike the ego depletion perspective, they do not directly focus on self-control failures, and they are rather narrow in their focus on attention. As such, while they can certainly be relevant, we found the ego depletion perspective to be more closely tied and relevant to our phenomenon of interest, namely taking IT-mediated shortcuts as it represents a self-control failure.

Ego depletion efects can depend on situational and personal factors. Personal factors capture the degree of integrity and honesty of a person, and situational factors refer to the characteristics of the decision setting [89]. Nevertheless, focusing on the whole range of situational and personal factors would be beyond the scope of this study or any other single study. Thus, in an attempt to find a reasonable starting point, we noted that key studies [45, 89, 109] suggest that in the organizational/situational realm, factors such as a code of ethics, ethical climate, and performance pressures may drive noncompliant behaviors. In the personal factors realm, moral character/moral integrity is a key determinant [63, 117]. We used these factors as inputs for a modified Delphi study (see Online Supplementary Appendix A). The results indicated that a good starting point would be to focus on moral integrity (an individual factor) and performance pressures (an organizational-contextual factor) as boundary conditions for the ego depletion efects.

## Boundary Condition 1: Moral Integrity

Consistent with our modified Delphi study findings, one crucial regulator could be the extent to which employees emphasize morality; that is, their moral integrity [95]. Moral integrity is a schema or a cognitive representation of moral traits, goals, values, and behavioral scripts [65]. For people with high moral integrity, moral ideals and values (e.g., being a good person) are keys to their self-concept [95]. Moral integrity afects individuals’ behavior and cognition as people have a strong inclination to maintain selfconsistency [18]. As such, individuals with high levels of moral integrity are less likely to skip one or several steps for completing the task quicker by bending the rules [95]. We expect that people with high moral integrity will expend extra efort to self-regulate their behavior [60], but this behavior will be automatic for them [92]. Subsequently, individuals high in moral integrity will have automatic responses to wrongdoing behaviors [3], require fewer resources to inhibit shortcuts, and may be less afected by self-control depletion [60]. Thus, moral integrity may bufer against the impact of ego depletion.

## Boundary Condition 2: Goal Setting/ Type

The Delphi study also pointed to performance pressures stemming from the goal type set by the organization as an important boundary condition for individual behaviors (e.g., taking shortcuts). This is consistent with the literature on goal setting. For example, Schweitzer et al. [91] argue that dificult, specific, and unmet performance goals motivate individuals’ wrongdoing behavior. As such, goal-based compensation systems may enhance taking shortcuts in the workplace. One explanation for these arguments is that employees’ motivational calculus may change based on a goal’s perceived obtainability. Performance goals may enhance individuals’ motivation to take shortcuts by generating high cognitive loads that concentrate on mental resources and attaining goals instead of moral values. Thus, employees with high performance goals may experience low levels of moral awareness when they conduct their job performance. Goal-setting theory [72] also provides an explanation by suggesting that high performance goals prompt employees to allocate extra energy to align their behavior toward a particular target, which can explain why users take IT-mediated shortcuts to achieve goals, such as completing work faster. Indeed, research argues that there are usually unintended ethical concerns linked with high performance goals [109]. That is the very mechanism through which high performance goals enhance employees’ performance can also enhance their wrongdoing/ deviant behavior.

Goal type is the main aspect of the goal-setting theory that controls employees’ motivational tendencies for accomplishing a goal [8, 30]. The theory points to two main goal types: learning goals and outcome goals. While in learning goals the emphasis is on personal growth and self-improvement, and thus on the process of goal achievement, in outcomes goals the emphasis is on performance achievement [79]. DeShon and Gillespie [28] argue that the patterns of individuals’ cognition derive from goal type, which consequently has a considerable efect on how the goal is pursued. Employees who have outcome goals are under more pressure to complete the task [31], and failing to do so quickly may indicate a failure within themselves [110]. In contrast, employees who have learning goals are inspired by the desire to learn [62]. Importantly, goals of the workplace can be changed from outcome goals to learning goals when the goal’s framing is altered so that the core of the motivation is changed from obtaining specific outcomes to personal development and growth [7, 8].

The diferent motivational processes linked with outcome versus learning goals have important consequences for employees’ deviation from prescribed behaviors. For example, dishonest behavior is elevated when it can change a failure into a gain [94] and unmonitored individuals cheat more when in the domain of losses [44]. Using these arguments with goal-setting theory in our context, data analysts with outcome goals may lean towards taking shortcuts to avoid the negative outcomes of not achieving their goal and turn the loss into a gain. Conversely, data analysts with learning goals may not feel similar amounts of loss aversion because when the goal is to develop skills, taking shortcuts may not help in attaining the goal. As such, one’s goal type can influence the efort invested into goal attainment and ego depletion processes.

## Hypotheses Development

Integrating the aforementioned literature streams, we developed a moderated-mediation model that explains taking IT-mediated shortcuts (Figure 1).

Complex IT consumes users’ cognitive resources [76], because of the need to synthesize and integrate informational cues [42] and to juggle tasks [55]. Like many other complex tasks, using complex technology reduces the amount of working memory and available resources for inhibition [78]. It may also induce the unpleasant and taxing state of ambivalence [82]. The more cognitive efort a task demands, the higher is the ego depletion a person would feel [68]. Dashboards, as an instance of potentially complex technologies, require data analysts to use various charts that include diferent types of data (e.g., both structured and unstructured), and this would require cognitive efort. When dashboards are complex, analysts would require more efort for insight generation. This extra efort should reduce one’s cognitive capacity and lead to stronger depletion than otherwise [9]. Therefore, drawing from the limited pool of resources users have to complete complex IT-mediated tasks [17], using complex IT depletes the pool of resources and limits a person’s consequent reflection and self-control abilities [13]. Thus, we hypothesize:

## Hypothesis 1 : Dashboard complexity will increase ego depletion. (Studies 1, 2, 3).

According to the self-regulation perspective, cognitive eforts draw from a finite pool of resources, and such eforts leave the person fewer self-regulatory resources that remain accessible for subsequent acts of self-control. This leads to an increased focus on the conservation of resources [78]. Thus, self-control is often reduced when a person works on a dificult task; this can drive behaviors that deviate from instructions [41], such as taking IT-mediated shortcuts. Similarly, efort-accuracy theory [81] argues that individuals often focus on efort reduction instead of the accuracy of their decisions because of limitations in cognitive abilities [39]. Therefore, when individuals use complex IT which requires using cognitive resources, they may prefer to take shortcuts to finish their tasks quickly with the minimum efort. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2a : Dashboard complexity will increase taking IT-mediated shortcuts. (Studies 2 3).

H1 suggests that dashboard complexity can increase ego depletion. As explained earlier and shown in many studies, ego depletion can drive noncompliant behavior [11]. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that the efect of dashboard complexity on taking IT-mediated shortcuts is at least partially mediated through ego depletion. That is, we posit that complex IT can deplete data analysts’ ego or self-regulatory resources, which in turn can make it dificult for them to comply with prescribed processes. In other words, one mechanism that can explain the association between IT complexity and taking IT mediated shortcuts may be the depleted egos of individuals who exert cognitive eforts for dealing with complex IT. Thus:

Hypothesis 2b : The efect of dashboard complexity on taking IT-mediated shortcuts is at least partially mediated through ego depletion. (Studies 2 3).

Individuals high in moral integrity have a readily and easily accessible moral schema compared to individuals who have low moral integrity [18]. As such, moral integrity is an important resource pool from which people can draw when facing ethical dilemmas [60]. Consequently, moral integrity is linked to reduced levels of noncompliance [95]. This happens because individuals with high moral integrity are more inclined to expend extra efort, even when their egos are depleted, to self-regulate noncompliant behavior. People high in moral integrity have internalized and automated ethical and compliant reactions to situations, such as being tempted to take shortcuts. As such, not caving to tempting shortcuts becomes a more automatic reflex for them and it uses fewer cognitive resources [60]. Therefore, when individuals are depleted, high moral integrity may bufer against the efects of ego depletion on taking shortcuts. Simply put, individuals who strongly internalize moral values do not require many self-control resources to comply with instructions, as their moral judgment is on “autopilot.” Therefore, we expect them to be less likely to take shortcuts, even when their self-control resources are depleted, compared to those with low moral integrity:

Hypothesis H3 : Higher moral integrity will weaken (reduce) the efect of ego depletion on taking IT-mediated shortcuts. (Study 2).

Task performance pressures can drive deviation from prescribed behaviors [89]. This is because goal type is an important factor that alters the impact of individuals’ perceptions on making decisions. Based on goal-setting theory, outcome goals may exacerbate the impact of IT complexity on ego depletion as a user becomes considerably more concerned about not achieving the goal. In contrast, learning goals may decrease the impact of IT complexity on ego depletion because concerns about failure are reduced, given individuals’ focus on the process of obtaining the goal. In addition, there is intrinsic motivation linked with learning goals that does not come from the externally imposed goal of obtaining a specific outcome; instead, one’s focus is on growth and improvement [109].

IT complexity coupled with an outcome goal may intensify both the concerns about obtaining specific outcomes and the encouragement to do whatever is needed to obtain the goal [109]. In contrast, when IT complexity is coupled with a learning goal, the dificulty of using complex IT may be perceived as instrumental for the learning. Thus, under the outcome goal condition, the impact of IT complexity on ego depletion may be exacerbated because individuals will try to use their cognitive resources to make sure they obtain a specific outcome. However, when individuals have a learning goal, the impact of IT complexity on ego depletion may be reduced because individuals are less motivated to stretch their resources to reach an optimal outcome and may be satisfied with a suboptimal outcome [71, 109]. Ultimately, goal type can regulate the potency of IT complexity to produce ego depletion; this potency is expected to be higher in outcome compared to learning goal settings:

Hypothesis 4a : Higher levels of outcome goals will strengthen (increase) the efect of dashboard complexity on ego depletion compared to learning goals. (Study 3).

Outcome goals may push people to do better, but they can also motivate noncompliance [91]. This is since individuals’ motivational calculus may change based on a goal’s perceived obtainability. Specifically, outcome goals may enhance individuals’ motivation to take shortcuts by generating high cognitive loads that focus one’s mental resources on obtaining the goal instead of moral standards. Thus, outcome goals can exacerbate the efect of ego depletion on taking shortcuts. They do so by further constraining people’s ability to reflect on the long term outcomes of their acts [47]. Specifically, when individuals’ egos are depleted and they have outcome goals, they would have a stronger motivation than otherwise to take shortcuts, as this action will serve both their outcome goals (completing the task no matter what) and the efort to conserve resources. In contrast, when individuals cognitive resources are reduced and their organizations have set learning goals, they do not feel the pressure to achieve a particular outcome and would be therefore less motivated than otherwise to engage in shortcuts [110]. Therefore:

Hypothesis 4b : Higher levels of outcome goals will strengthen (increase) the efect of ego depletion on taking IT-mediated shortcuts compared to learning goals. (Study 3).

## General Method

This study began with conducting a preliminary study to examine the impact of taking ITmediated shortcuts in our context on employees’ performance, using 69 data analysts. Next, we conducted a modified Delphi study (Online Supplementary Appendix A) that helped us select boundary conditions for extending the ego depletion model. After developing the model, including integration with the Delphi study findings, we conducted three studies with diferent samples. Each study provides supplementary and complementary insights, such that deeper theoretical insights regarding the phenomenon of interest were generated. Specifically, Study 1 aimed at establishing that dashboard complexity can increase ego depletion in users (H1). Studies 2 and 3 aimed at testing our assertions that dashboard complexity drives taking IT-mediated shortcuts and that this efect is at least partially mediated through ego depletion (H2a & b). Study 2 also aimed at investigating whether individuals’ moral integrity can reduce the impact of ego depletion on taking IT-mediated shortcuts (H3). Lastly, Study 3’s objective was also to examine whether goal type can attenuate the hypothesized antecedents (H4a) and efects (H4b) of ego depletion. All studies were performed in the data analytics context, specifically with dashboards. This context has been selected because even with the development of advanced technologies, the increase in the volume, velocity, and variety of data has made it dificult for data analysts to generate insights from big data [38]. Indeed, dashboards can provide charts that require heavy cognitive processing for insight generation [107]. In all studies, we also tested post-hoc whether objectively measured shortcut taking negatively influenced the objectively measured task performance.

To manipulate dashboard complexity, we designed two versions of a dashboard: simple and complex. Complexity was achieved by increasing the variety (diferent types of data) and velocity (speed of changes in data/ dynamism) of the dashboard, as these are two key attributes of big data that can influence the complexity of analytical applications [38].<sup>4</sup> The dashboards were fine-tuned through a pilot study involving 50 participants. Both dashboard versions had the same number of charts. While the simple dashboard focused only on static and structured data (low in variety and velocity), the complex dashboard involved both structured and unstructured data, and the data were dynamic and were updated every minute (high in variety and velocity).

Participants were charged with using the dashboard to decide about the best product, country, and channel for maximizing profit. The information provided was about a fictitious company in the apparel industry. This focus was taken because all participants were expected to be familiar with apparel. The task required them to reflect on the charts provided by the dashboard. The charts were presented in a small and static format on the dashboard. Participants were explicitly told that to enhance their performance in making accurate decisions, they must examine all charts by clicking and expanding them, one by one, which would allow them to drill down as needed. They were informed that without going through all the charts, they would not be able to make an accurate decision. Unbeknown to them, the number of skipped charts served as a direct behavioral measure of IT-mediated shortcuts in Studies 2 and 3. See specific instructions and dashboard versions in Online Supplementary Appendix B.

Prior to starting the first study, we tested whether taking IT-mediated shortcuts influences employees’ performance. With the help of a market research firm, we recruited 69 data analysts and randomly assigned them to use dashboards. They were asked to make a decision about the best (1) product, (2) country, and (3) channel for maximizing profit. Since there were correct answers for each decision dimension, we used their responses to rate the quality of their decisions in order to measure participants’ task performance. The findings showed that those who took more IT-mediated shortcuts had significantly lower performance (t = 4.43; P < 0.05) compared to others.

## Discussion: Study 1

First, Study 1 shows that the manipulation of dashboard complexity works, as it produces a significantly higher sense of dificulty in the high dashboard complexity condition than in the low dashboard complexity condition; through this, it depletes users. Thus, we can use this paradigm in subsequent studies for depleting users. Second, Study 1 supports the core ideas we put forth. It shows that dashboard complexity can increase ego depletion (H1) and that this happens because complex dashboards make inference tasks dificult and cognitively consuming. This is in line with the “muscle” perspective on limited self-control resources [78]. Thus, it is possible that complex dashboards have the potential to increase taking IT-mediated shortcuts, as depleted individuals are more likely to present noncompliant behaviors [41]. We also showed that at least in inference from dashboard chart type tasks, taking shortcuts leads, on average, to suboptimal decisions.

## Study 2

Study 2 first aims to replicate Study 1’s support for H1. It next aims to extend this view by showing that the produced ego depletion contributes to taking IT-mediated shortcuts (H2a & b). Lastly, it further extends this perspective by accounting for the role of moral integrity in attenuating ego depletion deficit efects on taking IT-mediated shortcuts (H3).

## Participants: Study 2

We followed the same data collection procedures as in Study 1. In addition to the two sampling frame criteria, we also asked the data collection firm to avoid repeats. To verify this, we asked participants whether they have already participated in the “analytics aptitude”

study. This has produced a sample of 204 data analysts after we discarded 36 participants who did not complete the experiment, provided monotonous answers, or failed the quality assurance question. No exclusion based on sample frame criteria was made. Sample characteristics are given in the “Study 2” column of Table 2.

## Procedures and Measures: Study 2

We used the same between-subject design as in Study 1. The random assignment exposed 116 people to the simple dashboard and 89 to the complex dashboard. The slightly uneven distribution is due to quitting the study more in the complex dashboard condition. We recorded ego depletion and control variables like in Study 1. We also captured the number of skipped charts, which served as a measure of our dependent variable (IT-mediated shortcuts). Lastly, we captured moral integrity as a work environment trait using six items adapted from Reed et al. [84].

## Results: Study 2

Manipulation checks indicated that perceived complexity was significantly higher (p = 0.005) in the complex (M = 4.67, SD = 1.23) compared to the simple (M = 4.14, SD = 1.39) dashboard condition. The ego depletion (α = 0.98, M = 2.52, SD = 1.24) and moral integrity (α = 0.87, M = 5.91, SD = 0.81) scales were reliable. We, hence, used averages as index scores. Dashboard complexity produced expected diferences in ego depletion, with a higher (p = 0.001) ego depletion in the complex (M = 2.85, SD = 1.25) than in the simple (M = 2.26, SD = 1.18) dashboard condition. The number of skipped charts (IT-mediated shortcuts) ranged from 0 to 6, with an average of 2.88 (SD = 2.43).

Extending this model, we examined H3 by estimating Model #14 in the PROCESS 3.5 macro [49] in SPSS 27. ## Discussion: Study 2

First, Study 2 shows that the manipulation of dashboard complexity works beyond just producing ego depletion. It also drives taking IT-mediated shortcuts. Study 3 reafirmed the ability of increased dashboard complexity to induce ego depletion and, through this, promote taking IT-mediated shortcuts. ## Post-hoc Analysis

Studies 2 and 3 support the hypothesized association between ego depletion and taking ITmediated shortcuts. It is possible that the linear relationship simplifies a more nuanced association between these variables. We hence venture to test this possibility. To do so, we combined datasets 2 and 3 to create a suficiently large sample size. We started by plotting a locally weighted smoothing line (LOESS) in SPSS 27 depicting the unspecified relationship between ego depletion and taking IT-mediated shortcuts. The fitted line had two clear “knots” (points in which the regression coeficients change: ego depletion = 2.44 and 3.76). We next regressed each data subset, as separated by these knots, separately. Results supported the observed nuanced associations. See Table 3. They show that the increase of ego depletion from none to 2.44 modestly increases taking shortcuts. They further show that medium ego depletion level (2.45-3.76) does not contribute to additional taking of shortcuts; it remains at the same level as when ego depletion = 2.44. However, when ego depletion is above 3.77 there is a very strong association between ego depletion and taking IT-mediated shortcuts; the former explains 15.9 percent of the variance in the latter. This analysis suggests that the observed association is pronounced in higher levels of ego depletion, but that it also exists, though to a smaller extent in low levels of ego depletion.

## General Discussion

Taking IT-mediated shortcuts is a growing concern because it can lead to suboptimal decisions [15]. To better understand this issue, this paper investigated key antecedents and outcomes of taking IT-mediated shortcuts. To this end, it integrated IT design aspects (here IT complexity), self-regulation, and goal setting theories, as well as insights from data analysts. Integrating these perspectives helped to theorize on whether, through which mechanisms, and when IT complexity can drive taking IT-mediated shortcuts. Our postulations were then tested in three studies with data analysts, in which we manipulated dashboard complexity (simple vs. complex). In one study, we also manipulated goal type (outcome vs. learning goal). A key strength of our paper, beyond its important theoretical and practical insights, is the use of objective measures of IT-mediated shortcuts and task performance and integrating objective and subjective data in multiple studies.

Study 1 showed that dashboard complexity increases ego depletion. Study 2 extended this view and revealed that IT complexity can drive IT-mediated shortcuts through ego depletion and that the potency of ego depletion efects depends on one’s moral character. Ego depletion did not drive IT-mediated shortcuts in people high in moral integrity. This could be due to the fact that individuals with high moral integrity have to use fewer personal resources in moral dilemma situations and because acting ethically is an automatic response for them that does not require additional resources [60]. Lastly, Study 3 confirmed the efect of IT complexity on taking IT-mediated shortcuts through ego depletion. It further showed that while goal type moderated the impact of ego depletion on taking IT-mediated shortcuts, it did not significantly moderate the impact of IT complexity on ego depletion. This could be due to the fact that using complex technologies requires high cognitive processing, which decreases the amount of working memory and available resources for inhibition [78], regardless of goal type. All studies showed that when quality manifests in decision accuracy, taking IT-mediates shortcuts is detrimental to task performance. Overall, the findings provide novel and interesting insights regarding whether, how and when IT complexity can result in taking IT-mediated shortcuts.

## Theoretical Contribution

Using Corley and Gioia’s [24] idea that good theories have both utility and originality, we posit that this study makes several theoretical contributions. First, we increase the understanding of why employees take IT-mediated shortcuts, which is an important phenomenon. Answering this question has both scientific and practical utility because current research on workarounds [32, 64, 97, 105, 116] or noncompliant security behaviors [4, 25, 51], while informative, may be less relevant to explain IT-mediated shortcuts (a specific self-centered type of noncompliant behavior), is limited with regard to assumed employee rationality, and largely focuses on self-reported intentions. Consequently, there is a need to consider less rational, more autonomous motivations to engage in IT-mediated shortcuts and see if such autonomous or reflexive motivations can drive actual IT-mediated shortcuts (beyond intentions). Addressing this need, we noted (based on ego depletion theory) that it is possible that the very qualities that make IT useful—its ability to process and present complex data—can also be a double-edged sword and drive IT-mediated shortcuts by depleting people’s egos. Thus, in this study, we identify and challenge existing assumptions by integrating IT design (complexity) literature, ego depletion theory, the self-regulation perspective, and goal setting theory to develop a model of IT-mediated shortcuts that emphasizes the reflexive nature of such behaviors.

Whereas ego depletion has been an important topic across disciplines [47], it has rarely been directly discussed in IS research, despite its potential to explain reflexive reactions to IT. Here, we identified ego depletion as an underlying influential mechanism that drives ITmediated shortcuts and pointed to moral integrity and goal type (outcome vs. learning) as regulators of the ego-depletion processes. This is a novel perspective (as per Corley and Gioia’s [24] conditions). It highlights the importance of ego-depletion mechanisms in IT user behaviors. It can therefore inform many streams of IS research because ego depletion is not unique to the examined context (dashboards) and it may result in a broader set of noncompliant IT-mediated behaviors (beyond IT-mediated shortcuts). For example, our findings can inform research on cybersecurity policy violation, cyberbullying, and online reviews because all of these behaviors may be produced reflexively in response to depleted egos. Moreover, ego depletion can be, as we show here, produced by the IT artifact.

In addition, our findings raise important questions about the efect of using complex technologies to improve firm outcomes. Regardless of the actual performance of technologies, if their use is complex, users will take shortcuts to overcome their mental constraints. Thus, while such technologies can benefit organizations, they can also have “dark sides” by enhancing opportunistic user behaviors aimed at the conservation of resources. A program of research can (1) identify and test the black box (beyond ego-depletion) between the use of advanced technologies and noncompliant IT-mediated behaviors, and then (2) test interventions aimed at reducing the adverse efects of such technologies. This addresses the practical aspects of Corley and Gioia’s [24] conditions and points to avenues for challenging common wisdom that complexity is good.

While there is much emphasis on the positive impact of data analytics tools on firms [77], there is still a lack of understanding about whether, how, and when such technologies could harm firms. Here, by focusing on ego-depletion mechanism, we show that complex IT can reflexively motivate cutting coroners. To extend our findings, future studies can (1) explore whether there are other variables that influence the relationships shown in the model, (2) develop a complete taxonomy of mechanisms that mediate the efect of IT complexity on taking IT-mediated shortcuts, and (3) investigate the impact of other new technology characteristics (beyond IT complexity) on user’s noncompliant behaviors. In this study, we laid the groundwork for such important extensions and showed that self-regulation and ego depletion theories can serve as a solid theoretical foundation for further studying employees’ IT-mediated behaviors and perhaps more broadly noncompliant IT-mediated behaviors. This extends and contextualizes the application of ego depletion and selfregulation theories from employee behavior [6] to the case of IT-mediated employee behaviors. This is an important extension because IT has become complex and, consequently, it can motivate various forms of shortcuts, which managers try to prevent [53]. Our findings provide important insights not only into the question of why it happens but also regarding the circumstances that aford it, or when it happens.

The findings of this study also have theoretical implications for research focusing on the impact of situational and personal factors on the use of IT in firms. We extend previous research by identifying boundary conditions for the association between IT complexity and IT-mediated shortcuts. We amalgamate qualitative findings with conceptual suggestions that situational and personal factors are key drivers of employee shortcut behaviors while doing their job tasks [89] to suggest that the moral integrity of employees and the goal setting of the firm can alter the ego depletion generated by IT-complexity and the response to ego depletion, in the form of taking IT-mediated shortcuts. These are important theoretical extensions of the IT-design and ego-depletion literature because they highlight two mechanisms that can regulate the efects of IT complexity on taking IT-mediated shortcuts. These mechanisms can apply to important theoretical streams in IS research (e.g., moral integrity can relate to ego-depletion processes in the contexts of cyberbullying and cybersecurity policy violations), and hence, our findings pave the way for further usage of these mechanisms for better understating IS users’ behaviors.

The findings also extend the goal setting theory to the context of IT. This theory serves as a valuable theoretical lens to understand the performance pressures in an organization, which could influence employees’ judgment; dificult goals have been shown to motivate noncompliant behaviors [91]. Our integrated theoretical framework provides new insight into the employee behavior outcomes of goal setting in the context of IT. Our results indicate that IT users who perceive depletion take more shortcuts when they have outcome goals, that is, when quick goal attainment is important, compared to learning goals. Thus, the findings suggest that when firms set outcome goals for IT users, they may generate an environment in which employees may be tempted to cut corners to reach the goals. This paves the way for further integration of goal setting into IT user behavior models.

Lastly, our study contributes to the existing literature on employees’ noncompliant behaviors. Whereas ethics scholars have long questioned whether such behavior is mainly a result of situational influences (“bad barrel” approach), character flaws (“bad apple” approach), or both (see the issue-contingent model in [59] or person-situation interactionist model in [100]), IS scholars have given insuficient emphasis to the behavioral ethics of IT users or how IT users deal with ethical dilemmas. Such dilemmas may be common and represent a struggle between the need to use complex IT and the desire to complete tasks fast. Our results support the importance of individuals’ morality (personal characteristics) and organizational goal settings (situational characteristics) for triggering reflexive noncompliant behavior (here IT-mediated shortcuts) in response to ego-depletion. This suggests that future research on noncompliant IS user behaviors should consider both individual diferences and situational factors for explaining how and when employees use IT in a noncompliant fashion.

Overall, following Corley and Gioia [24], we hope that our work provides transformative thinking regarding the relational black box between the usage of complex technologies and engaging in noncompliant IT-mediated behaviors. We also hope that it ignites research that more directly tackles ego depletion induced by IT and its individual, societal and organizational consequences.

## Practical Contributions

This study provides several practical insights. Specifically, our findings point to ways through which firms can reduce employees’ noncompliant IT-mediated behaviors. First, the findings suggest that firms need to pay attention to the complexity of their IT and ITmediated tasks because regardless of the potential benefits of such artifacts, there can be adverse outcomes, such as bending the rules by cutting corners, that can compromise the firm performance and employee safety. This means that firms should provide training for employees to make sure they will be able to use the new technologies (e.g., dashboards). It also means that firms should focus on removing the temptation of or the ability to take ITmediated shortcuts. For example, by forcing employees to go through all steps before being able to submit a recommendation or simply advising employees that their actions are monitored, companies can reduce IT-mediated shortcuts. Nevertheless, these steps may backfire, and more research is needed for striking the right balance between full employee autonomy and full control over employees’ IT-mediated conduct.

Second, the findings that moral integrity moderates the impact of ego depletion on taking IT-mediated shortcuts also provide important prescriptions. As some employees who have high moral integrity can better resist noncompliant behavior even when they are depleted, they could be used as role models or chosen to handle important tasks, particularly in situations that deplete self-control. Firms may also consider moral integrity in their recruitment and selection procedures, especially for tasks that require meticulous procedures and cannot allow for shortcuts. Moreover, in line with behavioral ethics scholars [110], we show that firms’ situational factors (goal type in our case) could generate pressures that motivate employees to take shortcuts while doing their job tasks. Thus, when employees are conducting complex tasks (e.g., using complex IT), if firms consider outcome goals, employees may take IT-mediated shortcuts when they perceive ego depletion. Our findings suggest that firms should consider some flexibility in goal types. They should strike a delicate balance between focusing on goals, which can help performance, while also focusing on learning, at least in some instances, in order to reduce IT-mediated shortcuts.

Third, our findings also suggest that firms should focus on the use of IT tools that support users’ cognitive processing. For example, if firms are processing and analyzing data with high velocity and variety, they should consider that using a complex dashboard, which encompasses both structured and unstructured data with constantly changing velocity, will drive ego depletion, which motivates employees to take IT-mediated shortcuts. Future studies could use guidelines from design science to improve existing technologies in order to reduce the ego-depletion impacts of IT. Lastly, although eficiency is important in the workplace, firms can remind employees that eficiency should not come at the expense of quality, safety, and ethicality. Managers and firms should be aware that using outcome goals may lead to unanticipated consequences such as employees doing whatever it takes (e.g., taking shortcuts) to finish their job tasks on time. Therefore, firms can encourage employees to focus on the quality and accuracy of the decisions they make while considering the eficiency of their tasks.

## Limitations

While this study makes clear contributions to theory and practice, there are some limitations that illuminate directions for future research. First, we focused on the impact of IT complexity on taking IT-mediated shortcuts. Future studies could consider the efects of other characteristics of IT (e.g., IT information quality) on employee behavior. For example, information complexity may be a downstream by-product of IT complexity that should be studied in future research. Second, we considered the complexity of IT in the context of data analytics using data analysts as our participants. Future studies could explore the impact of IT complexity in other contexts (e.g., website complexity) and investigate whether the findings of this study are generalizable to other contexts and other categories of employees in firms. Third, this study examined the roles of moral integrity and goal setting. Future studies could investigate additional boundary conditions. Fourth, another potentially fruitful direction for future studies involves investigating efective ways of strengthening and developing individuals’ self-control when they confront ethical dilemmas while using IT. Such studies can examine how we can train and educate employees to complete their job tasks both eficiently and efectively. Fifth, ego depletion is not specific to IT-mediated tasks and can lead to other negative outcomes. Future studies could consider such outcomes (e.g., stress, employee citizenship behavior). Finally, as this study was an experimental study done in a context that is diferent from participants’ organizations, we have not examined the impact of participants organizational norms and policies in their workplace. Future studies could conduct experiments within organizational contexts, in which case, organizational factors such as policy enforcement eforts, threats and rewards should be accounted for.

## Conclusion

This paper investigates whether, why, and when data analysts take IT-mediated shortcuts. It does so by integrating IT design literature, ego depletion, self-regulation, and goal setting theories and applying the integrated perspective to the context of IT-mediated shortcuts. We call for future studies to investigate the generalizability of our findings in other contexts related to the use of advanced IT in firms. Given growing concerns regarding shortcuts and the recent increase in the use of IT to make firm decisions, we call for future studies on how IT use produces ego depletion and when this can motivate taking IT-mediated shortcuts or other noncompliant behaviors. We further call for applying and considering the ideas we put forth in this study to develop interventions for reducing IT-mediated shortcuts.
