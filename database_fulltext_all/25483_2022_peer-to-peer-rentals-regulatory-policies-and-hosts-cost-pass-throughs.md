# Peer-To-Peer Rentals, Regulatory Policies, And Hosts’ Cost Pass-Throughs

Michelle Müller, Jürgen Neumann, and Dennis Kundisch

Paderborn University, Warburger Str. 100, 33098 Paderborn, Germany

## ABSTRACT

Peer-to-peer (P2P) rental markets have been shown to adversely impact the traditional hospitality industry and housing afordability, fueling the public demand for regulation. While localities around the globe have implemented policies to address these issues, little is known about how rental suppliers respond to those regulations. This study aims to empirically analyze the impact of such policy regulations on the prices charged by diferent types of rental suppliers. We employed a quasi-experimental research design based on an extensive dataset including more than 50,000 Airbnb listings to uncover the impact of a policy implemented in New Orleans, which introduced annual bring-to-market (BTM) costs through a mandatory licensing system while simultaneously banning listings from one city-center neighborhood. We find that, while non-commercial hosts completely pass their additional costs onto their consumers, irrespectively of demand and supply shifts, commercial hosts’ responses are more nuanced. Those with legalized listings located in the city center only partially pass on their costs to guests, while even decreasing their prices in the rest of the city. Our study contributes to the understanding of pricing in P2P rental markets and its efects. Further, it informs localities and supports policy analytics. With P2P renting remaining attractive in city parts where BTM costs can easily be passed through to consumers, this suggests that these regulatory policies fall short of reducing pressure on housing afordability in the city-center.

## KEYWORDS

Bring-to-market costs; computational social science; quasi-experimental research; propensity-score matching; regulatory policies; policy analytics; online pricing; sharing economy; P2P rental markets; peer-to-peer

## Introduction

Peer-to-peer (P2P) platforms have changed the way we live, work, and consume [44]. These platforms have emerged due to the widespread access to the Internet that significantly changed the availability of product and price information [23, 24]. As a result, it has become easy for consumers to find suppliers and engage in convenient, trustworthy transactions entailing low risk. At the same time, barriers for individuals to participate as suppliers on these platforms have been substantially reduced [17]. One important segment for P2P platforms is the market for short-term accommodations (i.e., P2P rental markets). P2P rental markets (e.g., Airbnb) pave the way for improved usage eficiency of accommodations by temporarily delivering unused housing inventory from private owners to renters [19]. Meanwhile, the rapid growth of such platforms has famously disrupted traditional industries. Scholars have already uncovered the economic consequences resulting from the emergence of such marketplaces, especially for the hotel industry [60] as well as the residential house prices and long-term rental prices [6] . These studies find that P2P shortterm rentals are frequently used as a substitute for hotel rooms, resulting in reduced hotel revenues. Additionally, as a growing number of landlords acquires condominiums from the long-term rental market solely to rent it out on a short-term rental platform, P2P rental markets are to blame for decreasing the long-term rental housing supply, which increases housing prices and long-term rents [33].

Both scholarly [28] and anecdotal evidence [58] points towards a range of heterogeneous types of hosts, from individuals renting out private homes to commercial suppliers with professional renting experience. However, only the former represents the set of hosts that was originally intended to provide the supply side of the most popular of such platforms, Airbnb [30]. The intention behind individuals sharing their private spaces was to enable paying guests to “live like a local” [27]. In contrast, professional suppliers, such as hostels or vacation home providers, are seen to rent out standardized accommodations as a second mainstay to generate additional income [22]. Anecdotal evidence also points to the increasing number of hosts with hundreds of listings [58]. Such commercial suppliers have heated up the public debate on commercialization of P2P rental platforms. A homeowner living in a neighborhood with lots of Airbnb rentals commented: “Something has to be done for us owners who live here and pay our taxes. We want to feel safe and we want to enjoy our homes, not [get] stressed out and bothered by these Airbnb homes . . . ” [24, p. 1]. Besides ensuring the serenity and safety of neighborhoods, municipalities are concerned about Airbnb’s impact on housing afordability [13].

Naturally, these trends have attracted the attention of municipal governments, many of which have turned to adopting regulatory policies with measures aimed at regulating the economic activity of P2P rentals in local markets [43]. Measures that have been introduced by several cities, such as Barcelona, Miami, Berkeley, and New Orleans, include restricting the neighborhoods in which P2P rentals can operate and, at the same time, introducing a licensing system with additional license costs in the rest of the city [43, 51].

However, little is known about how P2P rental suppliers (e.g., Airbnb hosts) respond to such regulatory policies. They can either reduce their activity on P2P rental markets, leave the market, decide to operate illegally on the rental platform, or comply with the regulations. If they decide to stay in the rental market, it remains unclear how the new market setting afects their price-setting behavior. For example, do they increase short-term rental prices in response to restrictions, such as a partial ban of short-term rentals in several neighborhoods, and the introduction of a licensing system in the remaining parts? Do commercial suppliers—as key targets for the introduction of regulatory measures—respond diferently to these policies compared to private suppliers? And, if so, would this cool down the debate about commercialization?

Even though the literature has informed us about the impact of P2P rental markets on some traditional industries, there is little empirical research to date on how P2P rental suppliers have responded to regulatory policies. This lack of knowledge presents a handicap, especially for legislators. Apart from the income generated from levies, the legislators’ main aim is to mitigate the negative externalities, for example, to avoid increases in housing and long-term rental prices [13]. Prior theoretical work [19] suggests that if bring-to-market (BTM) costs (e.g., cleaning, licenses, taxes) are borne by P2P rental suppliers, acquiring a property merely for the sake of P2P renting becomes relatively unappealing. This, in turn, can keep the increase in housing and long-term rental prices at bay, which is a key aim for legislators. However, if BTM costs can be passed through to guests either partially or completely by increasing short-term rental prices while still enjoying a high demand, not only would peer renting remain attractive, it also would undermine the measures introduced by policymakers aimed at reducing the pressure on the housing market.

To shed light on the potentially diferent short-term rental pricing responses by P2P rental suppliers, we examine a regulatory policy in New Orleans that was announced in December 2016 and implemented in April 2017 [42]. This policy banned P2P rental suppliers from the French Quarter neighborhood, a popular tourist destination located in the citycenter. However, all listings in the remaining neighborhoods were legalized by establishing BTM costs in the form of annual licenses. The New Orleans City Council aimed to address commercialization issues by ofering diferent types of licenses. Commercial hosts, for example, were asked to pay \$500 for their annual license for each listing, whereas a license for hosts being physically present during guest stays only cost \$200 per year. This policy fundamentally reduced P2P rental supply in the French Quarter while simultaneously shifting the demand to legalized neighborhoods [4]. As the supply in the rest of the city could increase, due to the legalization, or decrease, due to the BTM costs, it has remained unclear how diferent types of hosts are setting their short-term rental prices in response to this new market situation.

Hence, the aim of our study is to analyze the impact of these policy regulations on the short-term rental prices (hereafter referred to as prices) charged by diferent types of P2P rental suppliers. Thus, we formulated the following research question: How did commercial and non-commercial P2P rental suppliers set their prices in response to a policy shift which afected supply, demand, and BTM costs? This study explores this research question using a quasi-experimental (QE) research design applied to a large dataset for all Airbnb listings in New Orleans (treated city) and New York, San Francisco, and Portland (control cities) along with propensity-score matching (PSM) to support causal inferences [29].

We leverage a panel dataset that integrates geolocational information about the listings and accommodation-, host-, and review-level information, including the type of license purchased for a listing. In doing so, we ofer a novel approach to derive the causal efect of policy shifts that introduce multiple measures at a time on a P2P rental market for diferent types of rental suppliers and contribute to the growing field of computational social science (CSS) [10, 32].

We found that hosts on average responded to the policy shift by increasing prices between 1% and 2.9% in the first six months after the policy was announced. Moreover, noncommercial hosts completely passed on their additional costs to their guests, neglecting potential policy-related changes in demand and supply. In contrast to the behavior of noncommercial hosts, commercial hosts located in the vicinity of the French Quarter (where such rentals were banned) partially passed their additional BTM costs onto their guests, while decreasing their prices in the rest of the city. Hence, our results provide evidence that the main driver for the price-setting behavior of commercial hosts were changes in demand and supply. We conducted robustness checks to ensure the validity of our QE research design.

## Related Literature

This research contributes to three literature streams: research on (1) the societal impact of P2P rental platforms; (2) the heterogeneous price-setting behavior of rental suppliers; and (3) policy regulations for short-term rentals.

The literature stream that deals with the societal impact of P2P rental platforms has already uncovered that such rental platforms create both positive and negative externalities for local residents and adjacent industries. Rental platforms provide various advantages for travelers, such as an unconventional and authentic traveling experience [54], opportunities for social exchanges with local hosts [9], and the relatively low short-term rental prices in comparison to hotel rooms [26]. Rental suppliers also benefit from P2P rental platforms in a number of ways, including increased occupancy, additional economic income, and by meeting new people [9, 28]. Given these advantages for both sides of the market, it is unsurprising that rental platforms like Airbnb benefit from two-sided network efects, which explain their rapid growth in the last decade [44].

However, given the increasing popularity of P2P rental platforms, local residents complain about rental platforms leading to increased touristification of their neighborhood, causing more noise, trafic, and competition for parking spaces [52]. Likewise, the hotel industry also sufers from the disruptive nature of P2P rental platforms. For example, research has shown that an increase in the number of Airbnb listings of 1% is associated with a decrease in hotel revenues in that area of 0.05% [60]. This negative externality is especially detrimental to budget hotels, as renting out a cheap hotel room is substituted by P2P short-term rentals [36]. Moreover, as an increasing share of property owners remove their houses from the long-term rental market and ofer their property on the P2P shortterm rental market instead, the supply on the long-term rental market is decreased by P2P rental platforms [33].

This supply shortage of the long-term rental market results in a rise in housing and longterm rental prices [6, 35]. Research has shown that an increase in the number of Airbnb listings of 1% is associated with a 0.026% increase in house prices and a 0.018% increase in long-term rents [6]. However, by bringing visitors to neighborhoods that are not typical tourist destinations, P2P rental platforms also create positive spillover efects on complementary local services, like the restaurant industry [4]. We contribute to this stream of literature by analyzing how regulatory policies that aim to mitigate the negative externalities associated with peer renting afect rental suppliers’ price-setting behavior. If regulatory policies introduce additional BTM costs and these costs can easily be passed on to guests partially or completely by increasing prices, P2P renting would still remain attractive and negative externalities associated with rental platforms will persist even after the market has been regulated.

Further, we contribute to research on general price-setting behavior on P2P rental markets. Given that a substantial number of rental suppliers expect financial returns from their participation [28], they also face a profit maximization problem as they can typically decide freely on how to set their prices on rental platforms. There is evidence that hosts on Airbnb, for example, exhibit dificulties when it comes to adjusting their prices in response to changes in demand [22, 37]. This is especially true for hosts with less renting experience (i.e., those managing only one listing, also referred to as non-commercial hosts), that often show pricing ineficiencies, for example, by failing to charge a higher price in peak seasons (e.g., during Christmas holidays) [37].

Commercial hosts, in contrast, or those with renting experience, tend to adjust their prices in response to weekly or seasonal demand. This means that their daily revenue and their occupancy rates often exceed those of non-commercial hosts [22, 37]. Still, there is evidence of strategic price-setting on P2P rental platforms, even from non-commercial hosts. Several studies have shown that hosts try to monetize the quality signals presented on their listings’ pages. For instance, an improvement of the listing’s reputation in the form of online reviews and ratings can cause hosts to increase their prices [25, 41]. Similarly, some hosts ask for a price premium after having received virtual recognition, such as an Airbnb’s Superhost badge [41, 53].

To the best of our knowledge, no research has investigated how P2P rental suppliers adjust prices in response to significant geographical changes in the market situation caused by regulatory policies. Therefore, our study contributes to the field of price-setting on P2P rental markets by (1) providing insights on pricing responses to a policy which influences demand, supply, and BTM costs, and (2) studying heterogeneous price responses of hosts with diferent levels of renting experience.

Lastly, with regard to existing research on policy regulations, four approaches towards regulating P2P short-term rentals are recognized: quantitative restrictions – restricting the number of short-term rentals per year; qualitative restrictions – requiring safety objects like a smoke detector; locational restrictions – confining short-term rentals to specific locations; and density restrictions – limiting the number of short-term rentals in certain areas [43].

While there are studies that conceptually and empirically derived policy recommendations for certain cities [46], only a few investigated the direct efect of already-implemented restrictions on the P2P rental market. Alyakoob and Rahman [4] analyzed a policy shift in New Orleans that legalized short-term rentals and introduced licensing costs as qualitative restrictions. Simultaneously, the city imposed a locational restriction banning short-term rentals in the French Quarter, a tourist hotspot. They found that supply based on the number of listings on Airbnb in this area decreased after the policy shift had been implemented, while demand for short-term rentals had increased in adjacent districts.

In light of policy shifts in multiple U.S. cities, Chen et al. [12] analyzed changes in supply on Airbnb. A qualitative regulation implemented by some cities that required hosts to be present in the city when renting out their property was not found to statistically afect supply. In contrast, license costs levied on suppliers have negatively afected supply in the short-term but increased it in the long term. Further, qualitatively regulating the market by requiring hosts to adhere to health and safety standards, such as installing fire alarms, efectively reduced the number of listings in non-afluent neighborhoods. Moreover, limiting hosts to renting out only one property with a quantitative restriction has been associated with reduced rents in the long-term rental market and lower home values in the for-sale housing market [11]. The strictness of policy shifts in general is correlated with both the growth rate of the demand- and supply-sides in the short-term rental market as well as the growth rate of housing prices and rents in the long-term rental market [21].

While these studies focused on supply and demand, there is evidence that rental suppliers adjust their prices when tax-raising policy has been introduced [8, 16]. Airbnb hosts responded to an increase in taxes for short-term rentals by decreasing their prices only slightly, meaning that the additional tax costs were mainly borne by the consumers. As outlined above, many policy interventions entail changes to demand and supply which in turn can afect price-setting behavior. However, pricing responses towards these kinds of policies have not yet been investigated. We aim to address this gap by studying pricing behavior in response to policies that not only increase BTM costs for short-term rental suppliers but also afect supply and demand in the market via locational regulation of shortterm rentals in certain neighborhoods. Furthermore, only one study has so far considered the heterogeneous supply side stemming from diferent types of hosts. It shows that commercial hosts did not set their prices diferently compared to non-commercial hosts when taxes were introduced to a peer rental market [16]. Our study ofers a more holistic perspective on the diferent rental suppliers on P2P rental markets by integrating data about the intended renting frequency and presence during guest stays for host listings.

## Theoretical Background and Hypotheses

Our empirical analysis builds on theoretical work by Filippas et al. [19] who shed light on the economic efects of introducing bring-to-market (BTM) costs to a sharing market. Their microeconomic model extends seminal work by Becker [7]. The former authors modeled the introduction of a P2P rental market to explain the distribution of ownership of the goods to be shared among the mass of consumers. These goods were assumed to have an intensive margin of usage, which drives the ownership decision of consumers. Consumers were perfectly informed and difered in their intensive margin of usage, and, hence, in the utility they derived from the good. Accordingly, participation in the sharing economy was driven by utilitarian value, that has been empirically found to be a primary driver for sharing economy participation on both sides of the market [e.g., 49, 57].<sup>2</sup>

Filippas et al. [19] further distinguished between short-run and long-run equilibrium analysis. While in the former the original purchase decisions for the good to “share” are fixed, in the latter they are not. Although the results of interest for our study are qualitatively similar in both analyses, we focus on the short-run equilibrium analysis in the following as we study the price setting behavior of listings that already existed prior to the policy shift. In their baseline analysis it is assumed that owners can rent out their unused capacity at zero cost. Once positive (linear) BTM costs (e.g., labor, asset depreciation, and transaction costs) are introduced, their theoretical model suggests that such introduction is associated with a decrease in supply. Renting out a good suddenly entails additional costs for suppliers, and it becomes less profitable in the short-run to stay in the market: “As a result, owners use the good more, and make less available on the P2P rental market” [19, pp. 4163-4164]. A reduction in supply would imply an increased rental price in market equilibrium and, therefore, the BTM costs can be partially passed through to the consumer. If the supply side of the sharing market is further reduced (e.g., due to regulations that ban several suppliers), the remaining hosts might be more incentivized to raise their prices. However, the degree of the cost pass-through depends on the elasticity of demand- and supply side. Formally, the cost pass-through rate ρ is $\rho = 1 / \big ( 1 + | \varepsilon _ { d e m a n d } | / \varepsilon _ { s u p p l y } \big )$

For example, if demand elasticity in a sharing market is suficiently high compared to supply elasticity, then the supply side cannot pass through the additional BTM costs, as demand would be drastically reduced with a price increase.<sup>3</sup> By contrast, if supply elasticity is suficiently high compared to demand elasticity, then costs could be completely passed through to consumers. If so, a substantial share of suppliers would leave the market after BTM costs have been introduced, resulting in surplus demand. The supply-side reduction would then balance out this demand surplus by increasing rental prices, enabling a complete cost pass-through to consumers. However, in a real-world market setting, neither the demand nor supply side will react in a completely inelastic way with respect to price changes, so costs can always be passed through up to a certain point. Hence, if

BTM costs are exogenously introduced to a peer rental market while simultaneously banning several suppliers, theory suggests suppliers may be able to partially pass those additional costs onto their consumers.

However, there is empirical evidence pertaining to heterogeneity on the supply side in sharing markets [22, 37]. On the one hand, commercial hosts with renting experience base their pricing behavior on seasonal demand patterns and fluctuations in supply. Thus, commercial hosts act as economically rational market participants and tend to address profit maximization by setting prices according to changes in demand and supply – including the ones that are induced by additional BTM costs and a banning of several suppliers. Hence, a partial cost pass-through can be expected for commercial hosts. On the other hand, there is empirical evidence that non-commercial hosts with little renting experience will exhibit pricing ineficiencies by failing to charge more in peak-demand seasons [37].

So, when BTM costs are introduced in a P2P rental market, empirical research suggests that diferent host types will respond diferently in their price-setting. Non-commercial suppliers may not take demand and supply changes into account. For example, they may overlook a decrease in supply due to the introduction of BTM costs and the ban of some suppliers, and a subsequent shift in demand from the banned neighborhood toward nearby areas. Instead, they may consider only their individual increase in BTM costs they want to recover. So, we should observe non-commercial hosts completely passing on additional costs to customers by increasing their rental prices.

In sum, the literature suggests that the supply side responds heterogeneously to the introduction of BTM costs. Our two hypotheses for commercial and non-commercial hosts address this:

● Hypothesis 1 (Commercial Hosts Hypothesis): When BTM costs are introduced to a P2P rental market, commercial hosts adapt their short-term rental prices only according to changes in supply and demand, including the ones induced by the introduction of additional BTM costs.

● Hypothesis 2 (Non-commercial Hosts Hypothesis): When BTM costs are introduced to a P2P rental market, non-commercial hosts increase their short-term rental prices beyond price changes induced by changes in supply and demand, including the ones induced by the introduction of additional BTM costs, at least to cover the additional costs.

## Research Environment

The approach we used to answer our research question and to test our hypotheses is a quasiexperimental (QE) research design. We analyze a policy shift in New Orleans, Louisiana, where regulators exogenously introduced additional BTM costs to the short-term rental market as annual licenses for suppliers while simultaneously banning rental supply in one city-center neighborhood. In December 2016, the city’s government voted to legalize and regulate short-term rentals. Previously, although these were indeed deemed illegal, Airbnb was nonetheless active in the city [4]. Prior to the vote, there were intensive discussions in the New Orleans City Planning commission. The main concerns against short-term rentals raised during this debate were housing afordability and landlords evicting long-term tenants so that they could ofer their houses for more profitable short-term rentals [1]. An additional concern, which was less emphasized during the City Planning commission’s debate was neighborhood safety. These concerns were later reiterated by the mayor of New Orleans [13].

With efect from April 2017, the regulatory policy proposed by the city council essentially consisted of two parts [42]. ## Empirical analysis Variables and Methods

## Data and Variable Groups

We collected monthly panel data from Insideairbnb.com for all Airbnb listings available in New Orleans (our treatment city) and Portland, New York, and San Francisco (our control cities)<sup>5</sup> between July 2016 and October 2017 [15]. This dataset has been used in various empirical studies on Airbnb [e.g., 4, 21] and contains the entirety of information usually observable by a visitor at Airbnb.com. For each listing, we obtained data on the accommodation, host, reviews, and booking rules.

Accommodation-level variables. The accommodation attributes include the listing price (PRICE), the number of guests a listing can accommodate (NUM GUESTS), the number of baths (NUM BATHS), the number of bedrooms (NUM BEDROOMS), the number of beds (NUM BEDS), the minimum number of nights (MINIMUM NIGHTS), the number of amenities ofered by a listing (e.g., hair dryers, Wi-Fi, etc.) (NUM AMENITIES), dummies for selected amenities (HAS POOL, HAS KITCHEN, HAS FREE PARKING; HAS GYM) and for the room type (SHARED ROOM; PRIVATE ROOM; WHOLE ROOM). We calculated a listing’s geodistance to the city-center as the haversine distance between the listing’s geolocation on Airbnb and the city’s town hall (CENTER DISTANCE) [53].

Host-level variables. For hosts, we obtained data on how many months a host was registered on Airbnb (MEMBERSHIP DUR), dummies indicating whether they acquired a Superhost badge at the time of observation (IS SUPERHOST), and whether their account was verified with an oficial ID (HAS VERIFIED ID). We also introduced binary variables which indicate whether a host replies to a minimum of 90% of guest messages (RESPONSE RATE), whether they respond to messages in an hour (RESPONSE FAST), the number of listings this host ofers on the website (NUM LISTINGS), and a dummy for whether the host uploaded a profile picture (HAS PROFILE PIC).

Review-level variables. We used online ratings of a listing, for overall rating (REV RATING) and six-dimensional ratings for accuracy (REV ACCURACY), cleanliness (REV CLEANLINESS), check-in (REV CHECKIN), communication (REV COMMUNICATE), location ðREV LOCATION), and value for money (REV VALUE).

Booking rules variables. These pertain to indicators for the possibility to instantly book the listing (INSTANT BOOKABLE), the strictness of cancellation (CANCELLATION, ranging from 0: flexible to 2: strict), dummies for the requirement to pay a cleaning fee (HAS CLEANING FEE), and whether the listing requires a deposit (DEPOSIT REQUIRED).

We enriched our panel dataset with publicly-available data from the New Orleans government Department of Safety and Permits, indicating which hosts purchased a license for their listing during our observation period [14]. The resulting dataset allowed us to not only distinguish legal from illegal listings, but also to observe which listings are linked to a commercial STR, a temporary STR, or an accessory STR. For our analysis, we define commercial hosts as those who obtained a commercial STR, as this type of license restricts only a few activities on Airbnb and thus opens up space for commercial renting. Hosts with a temporary or accessory STR represent our subsample of non-commercial hosts. Property management restricts renting frequency and requires hosts to be on site.

To gain a better understanding of the market situation that hosts face when a regulatory policy is implemented, we computed variables to proxy supply and demand for each listing in each month:

● Supply. To proxy the supply of listings available on Airbnb, we counted the number of other Airbnb listings within a 1-mile radius of the focal listing in each month. This measure was captured in the variable SUPPLY LOCAL and enabled us to control for the competitive landscape around a focal listing in a fine-grained manner. We also computed a variable that indicates how many listings in our dataset are located in the center of each city by counting the number of listings within a maximum distance of 1 mile to the city-center as represented by its town hall (SUPPLY CITYCENTER). We also computed the total number of listings for each city and each month, representing a more general proxy for supply (SUPPLY MARKET).

● Demand. To proxy for the demand a particular listing enjoys, we used the number of new reviews a listing obtains in a month multiplied by the minimum number of nights that guests have to stay when booking the listing [6, 60]. As Airbnb only allows reviews of guests who spent at least one night at a listing, this measure is a lower-bound metric for the demand of a listing (DEMAND LOWER). Considering that not all guests leave a review, we rely on Fradkin et al. [20] who found from proprietary Airbnb data that 68% of all bookings result in a review. Thus, we multipled DEMAND LOWER by 100/ 68 to get an adjusted measure for demand (DEMAND ADJUSTED). As an upper bound, we examined the listing’s calendar, by counting the number of days a listing was unavailable over a period of a month, either because the listing was fully booked or because the host was not ofering any listing on a given day (DEMAND UPPER). Finally, we computed the mean lower, adjusted, and upper demand of the local competitors within a 1-mile radius for each listing in each month (DEMAND LOW COMP;DEMAND ADJ COMP;DEMAND UP COMP).

To examine the role of the 90-day renting limit for temporary hosts, we additionally computed a variable that indicates whether a host reached the 90-day limit in a given month by summing up the mean of DEMAND LOWER and DEMAND UPPER since the hosts received their license. Moreover, we created dummy variables indicating the percentage of the monthly occupancy rate during the 90-days the listing was rented out, ranging from 0%- 10%, 10%-20%, . . ., to 90%-100%.

Further, we want to rule out that our estimations were influenced by hosts self-selecting into our treatment. Thus, we only included listings in our dataset that were already set up at the time of the policy announcement month (December 2016). Moreover, we excluded illegal listings located in the French Quarter from our sample. All our supply variables were nonetheless computed with all the listings available in a given month. Of the remaining 5,017 listings located in New Orleans, we found 1,711 listings (34.1%) associated with one of three types in the STR licenses dataset. Of those, 182 (10.6%) could be linked to a commercial STR, 860 (50.3%) to a temporary STR, and 669 (39.1%) to an accessory STR. We restricted our treatment group to those listings that purchased a valid license during our observation period to rule out that illegally posted listings are biasing our results. In total, our panel data set comprises 71,489 listings operated by 55,831 hosts. Table 2 reports an excerpt of the summary statistics of our panel dataset.

## Main Variables

We used the listing price in US\$ for one night (PRICE) as the dependent variable.<sup>6</sup> There are two main independent variables: TREAT LICENSE and TREAT BAN. The regulatory policy we analyze in New Orleans consisted of two components: the French Quarter ban and the requirement to obtain a license in the rest of the city. We assumed that all listings in New Orleans should have been afected by the licensing system, as each host was required to purchase a license once the policy was implemented. Thus, the first treatment variable, TREAT LICENSE, equals 1 if the listing was in New Orleans and required to be licensed, and 0 if it was located elsewhere.

![](/api/attachments/EVV2JN28/fulltext/images/4d0ae1a7f231ff8b399720303d96bba6b4269d8b7d03e47a1d4051740d4de1ab.jpg)

By contrast, we assumed that the second component of this policy, the French Quarter ban, influenced listings nearby this neighborhood, as demand shifted from the banned area to adjacent legalized listings [4]. That is why our second treatment variable, TREAT BAN, was set to 1 if the listing was located close to the banned French Quarter, with a maximum distance of 1 mile, and 0 otherwise. Figure 2 depicts the geodistribution of our treatment groups.

All listings in the banned French Quarter (the black bubbles) were excluded from our dataset. The light-grey bubbles are listings for which TREAT LICENSE were set to 1 and TREAT BAN to 0. The dark-grey bubbles show listings with TREAT BAN and TREAT LICENSE set to 1. In that sense, all listings near the French Quarter (the darkgrey bubbles) were afected by both the French Quarter ban (TREAT BAN) and the newlyestablished licensing system (TREAT LICENSE).

The construction of our treatment groups allowed us to diferentiate between these two components of the regulatory policy and their corresponding relationship with hosts’ pricing behaviors. However, because all listings that were afected by the French Quarter ban were also afected by the licensing system, we only observed the efect of TREAT BAN relative to the efect of TREAT LICENSE.

## Empirical Model

For our QE research design, we estimated a diference-in-diference model (DiD) with monthly time leads and lags [5], as depicted in Equation 1:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln \left(P R I C E _ {i t}\right) = \beta_ {0} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {T} \alpha_ {j} \cdot M O N T H _ {i t j} * T R E A T \_ L I C E N S E _ {i} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {T} \delta_ {j} \cdot M O N T H _ {i t j} \\ \quad * T R E A T \_ B A N _ {i} + \beta_ {1} \cdot T R E A T \_ B A N _ {i} + \beta_ {2} \cdot T R E A T \_ L I C E N S E _ {i} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {T} \theta_ {j} \\ \quad \cdot M O N T H _ {i t j} + \beta_ {3} \gamma_ {i t} + \delta_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Here, ln $\left( P R I C E _ { i t } \right)$ represents the natural logarithm of the price for one night at listing i in month t. Then, we incorporated month dummy variables into our specification, where the dummy $M O N T H _ { i t j }$ represents a single month j set to 1 if t equals j. The key variables of interest are the interactions $M O N T H _ { i t j } \times T R E A T \_ L I C E N S E _ { i }$ and $M O N T H _ { i t j } \times$ TREAT $. B A N _ { i }$ which represent the DiD estimators and capture the average treatment efect on the treated (ATT) listings. Here, we left out December 2016 (the policy announcement month) and its respective interaction terms from our regression, such that they serve as a reference point for the interpretation of subsequent and prior prices [11]. We also added the treatment variables $T R E A T \_ L I C E N S E _ { i }$ and $T R E A T \_ B A N _ { i }$ separately in our specification, which were omitted due to the listing fixed efects $\delta _ { i }$ that we incorporated to control for time-constant heterogeneity across listings (e.g., spatial proximity to tourist attractions). Finally, $\gamma _ { i t }$ is a vector of time-varying control variables (accommodation-level, host-level, review-level, booking-level information) and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is a random error term.

To investigate the mechanisms behind hosts’ price-setting behavior, we controlled for the demand proxies for each listing as well as the supply proxies from the preceding month (t   1) for each listing i in a second specification. When we controlled for all these variables and thus kept them constant, we could conclude how many hosts change their prices irrespective of policy-driven changes in DEMAND and SUPPLY. Therefore, comparing the two specifications allowed us to observe how much of the hypothesized partial cost passthrough can be explained by fluctuations in DEMAND and SUPPLY following the introduction of the policy. Reverse causality between our demand measures and price setting behavior should not be a concern because our demand measures precede price in time [34].

## Treatment Mechanism and Identification

We employed a QE research design<sup>7</sup> where listings in New Orleans were subject to a treatment (the policy consisting of the French Quarter ban and the license system establishment) and listings in our remaining cities served as the control group. Technically, $M O N T H _ { i t j } \times T R E A T$ LICENSE<sub>i</sub> identifies the ATT (the efect of the policy shift on the prices of all the listings in New Orleans. In line with that, the interaction $M O N T H _ { i t j } \times T R E A T \_ B A N _ { i }$ represents the ATT of the French Quarter ban relative to the efect of the license system on listing prices in New Orleans). We exploited the availability of price information along with accommodation-, host- and review-level information<sup>8</sup> to identify price responses by diferent types of hosts. As such, our approach reflects contemporary CSS methods [32, 45] involving big data and causal inference [10]. Establishing causality in empirical results is important to support policy analytics [39]. A QE design can be efectively used to address endogeneity issues, too [56, 57].

The identification of the causal efect of the regulatory policy hinges upon the conditional independence assumption of the treatment and the common trends assumption. Given the exogenous nature of the policy, we have reason to believe that the conditional independence assumption was not violated. We addressed potential endogeneity concerns pertaining to self-selection and sample composition in our robustness checks. The common trends assumption will be assessed when we present the empirical results as it can be directly analyzed with our specification. We also examined whether any automatic Airbnb pricing tools were launched during the time of our treatment. Those tools help Airbnb hosts in setting prices, such as by taking property characteristics as well as seasonal demand into account, resulting in daily listing price suggestions [22]. If a pricing tool was launched during our observation period, then price changes could also be attributed to suggestions made by the automatic pricing tool, which could bias our results. Reassuringly, we found there was no such incidence.

To further strengthen our QE research design and to increase causal inference for our estimates, we wanted to rule out any systematic diferences between the listings in our treatment (listings in New Orleans) and control group (listings in New York, Portland, and

San Francisco). For example, if listings between the cities difered systematically in terms of their attributes, they might have diferent price-setting potentials that could confound our results. Thus, we identified listings in the control group cities that were statistical twins of the New Orleans listings using propensity-score matching (PSM) [48, 49]. The rationale is that the listings thus identified do not statistically difer from each other based on observable covariates, but only in respect to treatment status. We applied a kernel matching algorithm with an Epanechnikov kernel and an automatically-calculated bandwidth of 0.000187 to arrive at our PSM sample of statistical twins. We used the control variables (except for CENTER DISTANCE and MINIMUM NIGHTS for reasons of computational complexity as well as NUM LISTINGSÞ as matching variables. We matched the variables from the policy announcement month (December 2016).

Assessing the relative bias before and after matching each covariate, we see that our PSM substantially reduced the bias between the treated and control listings. In fact, after matching, all our control variables exhibited insignificant mean diferences $( p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . We conclude that our PSM approach balanced the sample’s treatment and control listings and that we established treatment and control groups that were comparable prior to the implementation of the policy, a central assumption to establish causal inference [29]. The matched sample had 50,347 listings operated by 41,116 hosts. (See the Online Appendix for related findings.)

## Policy Efect on Listings with a Commercial STR

In the following, we obtain a more nuanced picture of the policy’s efect on listing prices by estimating the regression separately for listings assigned to a specific license type. Column 3 in Table 3 depicts the policy efect on listings with a commercial STR (i.e., commercial hosts). Here, we even observed decreased prices in New Orleans outside the city-center after the policy has been implemented. Starting in July 2017, listing prices dropped by 4.7%, and the coeficients remain negative and significant until October 2017. However, listings of commercial hosts located with spatial proximity to the French Quarter experienced increased prices. In August 2017, commercial listings located in neighborhoods next to the French Quarter raised their listing prices by about 5.3% (-0.048 + 0.101 = 0.053, the sum of coeficients for the interaction terms of TREAT LICENSE and TREAT BAN).

When controlling for DEMAND and SUPPLY (Column 4 in Table 3), the interaction terms between the months and TREAT LICENSE became insignificant and smaller in magnitude. Thus, our results suggest that the decreasing prices from commercial listings located outside the city-center can be primarily attributed to policy-related changes in demand and supply. However, the coeficients of the interaction term between TREAT BAN and the months of August 2017 to October 2017 remained significant when controlling for DEMAND and SUPPLY.

We calculated the additional amount of income from each commercially-licensed listing after the policy’s announcement. In the 10-month period from January to October 2017, the average revenue per listing due to policy-related price changes was approximately \$210 for listings located in New Orleans’ city-center, and -\$133 for other listings in New Orleans. We conclude that a partial cost pass-through was observable for hosts with a commercial STR in the vicinity of the French Quarter but not for commercial hosts located in the rest of the city. Moreover, we provide evidence that the pricing behavior of commercial hosts loutside the city-center was driven by policy-related changes in demand and supply.

In a further analysis, we re-estimated this model separately for commercial hosts managing 6 or more listings (the median number of listings from hosts with a commercial STR) and commercial hosts with less than 6 listings. (See the Online Appendix.) The results for commercial hosts that managed less than 6 listings reveal that the coeficients of the interaction term between TREAT LICENSE and the months after the policy announcement (i.e., February to March 2017) became positive and significant. However, when controlling for demand and supply, these coeficients became insignificant. Moreover, the coeficients of the interaction term of TREAT BAN and the months revealed insignificant results. This suggests that commercial hosts managing up to 6 listings in all parts of the city partially passed on the additional BTM costs to their guests by increasing their listing prices. This increase can be explained by changes in demand and supply following the policy.

The interaction coeficients between TREAT LICENSE and the months after the policy announcement (May to October 2017) for commercial suppliers managing 6+ listings were negative and significant with values to -16.8%. The coeficients of TREAT BAN and the months after the policy announcement were positive and significant, with coeficients to 30.7% though. So, we observed that the coeficients of TREAT BAN and TREAT LICENSE for hosts managing 6 or more listings were like the analysis for all commercial suppliers but with coeficients that were higher. When controlling for demand and supply, they became insignificant and smaller. Commercial suppliers that managed half a dozen-plus listings were the main results drivers for commercial STR hosts. Commercial listings partially passed on licensing costs to guests and considered policy-related demand and supply changes when adjusting prices. Hypothesis 1 (Commercial Host Hypothesis) also was supported.

## Policy Efect on Listings with a Temporary or Accessory STR

As Columns 5 and 7 in Table 3 depict, hosts managing properties with either a temporaryor an accessory STR (the non-commercial hosts) also change their prices in response to the policy. Temporary licensed listings significantly increased their prices over a period of 6 months (February to July 2017), compared to 4 months (February to May 2017) for hosts with an accessory STR. For temporary and accessory licensed listings, prices peaked in March 2017, with a 2.7% increase for temporary licensed listings and 3.1% for accessory STR listings. In October 2017, listings with an accessory STR slightly decreased prices by 1.4%, compared to those set in December 2016. Unlike commercially-licensed listings, properties with a temporary or accessory STR near the French Quarter did not ask for higher prices compared to listings outside this area. When controlling for DEMAND and SUPPLY (Columns 6 and 8, Table 3), temporary- and accessory-licensed listings still exhibited increased prices. The coeficients are like those in Columns 5 and 7. For temporary-licensed listings, increased prices of \~3% lasted for longer – an additional 3 months (from August to October 2017). This suggests non-commercial hosts increased their prices mostly irrespective of supply and demand changes.

Our estimates show that temporary-licensed listings increased their prices after controlling for demand and supply. Recall that those licenses were only valid for a maximum of 90 days per license year. To examine the role of the 90-day renting limit for temporary hosts, we included a dummy variable that indicates whether a host had already reached the 90-day limit in a given month, with the dummies for the percent monthly occupancy rate during the 90 days the listing was rented out (ranging from 0%-10% to 90%-100%) in further analysis. When controlling for the dummies, the interaction term coeficients of TREAT LICENSE and August to October 2017 became smaller and insignificant. A closer look at our added dummy variables suggested the following: the coeficients for the dummies indicating that the listing’s demand has already reached over 60% of its 90-day limit since its license acquisition month were nearly all positive and significant. Thus, we conclude that the price increase gaps we observe in Column 6 for the 3 months at the end of our observation period can be partially explained by hosts whose listings had already reached demand capacity above 60% of their 90-day limit for having been licensed.

On average, higher price levels for non-commercial listings resulted in additional revenue of \$257 for a temporary-licensed listing, and \$146 for an accessory-licensed listing between January and October 2017. As a temporary STR cost between \$50 (with homestead exemption) and \$150 (without homestead exemption), our results indicate that hosts with a temporary licensed listing not only passed on all the additional license costs to their guests, but even generated additional income as a result of the policy. Accessory STRs cost \$200, so such suppliers had 73% of their license costs reimbursed in the first six months after policy implementation. Since accessory-licenses are valid for a year, it is likely that hosts with an accessory-licensed listing had their additional BTM costs fully covered within the license year. As non-commercial rental suppliers increased their prices irrespective of policy-related changes in demand and supply, we found support for Hypothesis 2 (Noncommercial Hosts Hypothesis).

## Robustness Checks

To lend robustness to our estimates, we: (1) conducted sensitivity tests to address potential selection bias, (2) re-included illegal rental suppliers into our sample to rule out that these hosts are biasing our estimates, (3) addressed concerns regarding our strategy to disentangle the two major policy measures, (4) controlled for events in our control group cities, and (5) conducted further robustness checks. These included using an unmatched sample, controlling for hosts responsible for price-setting behavior of multiple listings, and changing the operationalizations of the focal constructs.

Selection bias. We complemented the PSM approach that uses observable covariates to balance the sample for our main analysis by applying the Rosenbaum bounds approach [47]. This strategy allowed us to test the sensitivity of our results by quantifying the amount of bias Γ represented by an unobserved covariate that could cause selection in the treatment. The underlying concern is that hosts set up new listings because they predicted that, due to the policy, they could ask for higher prices. While our sampling strategy is designed to address this concern by only including listings that were already registered when the policy was announced, we nevertheless wanted to rule out any distortions created by hosts who could have entered their Airbnb listings shortly before the announcement, based on the public discussion prior to the passage of the policy. The Rosenbaum bounds approach enabled us to check how strong this bias would have to be to require us to revise our findings.

Applying this approach showed that our base results in Column 1 of Table 3 remained significant up to Γ values of 2.5 for interaction terms for February 2017 and TREAT LICENSE. (See Figure 5.)

Afterwards, the maximum value of Γ decreased to 1.3 for the interaction term for May 2017 and TREAT LICENSE, and equaled 1 for the interaction term for June 2017 and TREAT LICENSE. Even though the literature lacks consensus over what constitutes substantial insensitivity, values between 1.2 and 1.5 are generally deemed reasonably insensitive [50]. This means that our results between TREAT LICENSE and the months from February 2017 to May 2017 were not likely to be biased by unobserved covariates that could cause selection into the treatment.

Illegal listing bias. One might be concerned that our results are biased because we excluded listings from our dataset that had not purchased a valid license during our observation period. We would like to highlight that our supply variables were computed before we excluded these illegal listings from our sample. Thus, the illegal suppliers were indirectly considered in our models by controlling for the competitive landscape for each legal listing in each month. However, to alleviate further concerns, we re-ran our regression and (1) incorporated the banned French Quarter listings into our analysis and (2) included all suppliers that had not purchased a valid license in our observation period. In both specifications, we found qualitatively similar results. We conclude that it is unlikely that the existence of illegal listings biased our estimates. (See the Online Appendix for the results.)

Treated area definition bias. As the regulatory policy we analyze consists of two parts, the French Quarter ban on the one hand and the introduction of mandatory licenses on the other, we constructed two independent variables, TREAT LICENSE and TREAT BAN, to disentangle the efects for these regulatory measures. However, one may be concerned that our results may be biased because listings that are geographically wider-distributed from the French Quarter were also afected by the ban. To address these concerns, we employed 1.25, 1.5, and a 1.75-mile radius instead of a 1-mile radius from the French Quarter to define the treatment variable TREAT BAN For hosts with an accessory- and temporary-STR license, the estimates remained qualitatively unchanged. However, for commercial hosts, one can see that the price increase in the city-center started in May 2017 when we used 1.5 and 1.75- mile thresholds for TREAT BAN. Moreover, the price decreases of the interaction terms between TREAT LICENSE and the months after May 2017 persisted even after controlling for demand and supply changes. Yet the partial pass-through of the additional license costs was still observable for commercial hosts located near the French Quarter. (See the Online Appendix for that analysis.)

We further estimated the interaction terms for our treatment variable TREAT LICENSE and the months with a variable, indicating the distance to the French Quarter (in miles) while removing TREAT BAN from our model. Reassuringly, we found positive and significant coeficients for the two-way interactions between TREAT LICENSE and the month dummies across all sub-samples. This suggests that non-commercial hosts and commercial hosts with a relatively small distance to the French Quarter increased their prices. Also, we found significant and negative coeficients for the three-way interactions, suggesting that commercial hosts with a distance of \~2-3 miles to the city-center decreased their prices.

Control group events. Even though our matching procedure has substantially reduced biases in observable variables between our treatment and control group listings and we found support for the common trends assumption, it is possible that policy regulations implemented prior to our observation period in the control group cities (New York, Portland, and San Francisco) could have biased our results. Accordingly, we considered the time of introduction and the nature of regulations in the control cities: New York introduced regulations for its short-term rental market since 2011, including the requirement that the permanent tenant needed to be present during guest stays in their apartment [51]. San Francisco, too, imposed regulations on its short-term rental market since 2014, requiring hosts to be a permanent resident and only allowing bookings for a maximum of 90 days if the host is not present during guest stays. In Portland, where the short-term rental market has been regulated since 2014, hosts need to obtain a license and can only list properties if they reside in them for at least nine months per year [40].

All three policies were implemented at least three years before the regulatory policy in New Orleans and thus, it is unlikely that these regulations are biasing our estimates. However, in all three cities, local authorities struggle with the enforcement of these regulations, leading to relatively late attempts at enforcing these policies [1, 2, 3, 25]. Starting from November 2016 (San Francisco and New York) and January 2017 (Portland), Airbnb was thus forced to remove some of the listings that were “shared by hosts with multiple listings that could impact long term housing availability” [2, p. 1; 3, p. 1]. To rule out that this policy enforcement measure that was implemented during our observation period biases our estimates, we conducted the other robustness checks: First, we included the share of hosts operating multiple listings in each city and each month as a control variable. Second, we created a dummy variable indicating the starting point of the policy enforcement measure and added it to our set of controls. Third, we excluded Portland and New York from our control group as these cities had the highest drop in the share of hosts operating multiple listings, and in the number of listings during our observation period, respectively. These robustness checks, had qualitatively unchanged results.

Further robustness checks. One may be concerned that our results were driven by employing a matched sample instead of the full sample for our main analysis. So, we replicated our results using the full sample. Reassuringly, we found qualitatively unchanged results. Further, as hosts can have multiple listings, our efects may be overestimated by hosts that are responsible for the price responses for several listings in our dataset. To rule out this potential bias, after re-estimating our baseline models with robust standard errors clustered at the host level, we found qualitatively unchanged results for the interaction terms between TREAT LICENSE and all months. However, the interaction terms between TREAT BAN and the months of August and October 2017 became insignificant for listings with a commercial STR. This result strengthens our finding that the price hike in the city-center can primarily be attributed to a few commercial hosts that ofered multiple listings.

Lastly, to alleviate concerns about possible measurement error for city-center supply, we conducted robustness checks for the SUPPLY CITYCENTER measure. We re-ran our regression utilizing a 0.75-mile and a 1.25-mile radius thresholds instead of a 1-mile radius for SUPPLY CITYCENTER. One may also be concerned that our results were driven by our dependent variable, which does not represent the transaction price. So, we adjusted it by adding the cleaning fee to the price set by the host, and and then re-estimated our model. In all three robustness checks, we found qualitatively unchanged results. (See the Online Appendix for results related to the past three paragraphs.)

## Contributions, Limitations, and Conclusion

We believe this article to be the first to empirically evaluate the price responses of such a regulatory policy for diferent host types, which entailed the ban of Airbnb in a certain neighborhood in the center of a city, and the introduction of mandatory licenses in the remaining neighborhoods. The main insight from this study is that hosts have overall increased their prices in response to the announcement and implementation of the policy, and on average earned approximately \$245 of additional revenue in the first seven months after the policy came into efect. Further, we observe that non-commercial hosts completely passed their additional license costs to their guests, while commercial suppliers, especially those with multiple city-center listings partially passed on their license costs to their guests.

## Theoretical Contributions

From a theoretical perspective, our results reveal that when BTM costs are introduced to a sharing market, a partial cost pass-through should be observed [19]. Our results showed something diferent though: the expected partial cost pass-through was only observed for commercial hosts located in an area which experienced a substantial decrease in supply. Further, our results suggest that in real-world market settings, where neither the supply-side nor the demand-side respond completely inelastically, the microeconomic model proposed [19] may not be directly applicable to non-commercial hosts.

Although our findings partially contradict the theoretical predictions, they match the previous literature on the heterogeneous price-setting behavior of suppliers in P2P rental markets [22, 37]. From these studies we have learned that commercial hosts behave mostly as predicted by economic principles, by adjusting their prices more frequently than noncommercial hosts in response to fluctuations in demand and supply. Non-commercial hosts are more likely to be subject to behavioral constraints, as they mostly act as inexperienced microentrepreneurs who lack economic knowledge about appropriate price setting behavior [37]. Our results thus enrich the understanding about how diferent types of rental suppliers respond to a policy shift in P2P rental markets. They also contrast with the literature, which emphasizes other drivers for participation like social value [31]. If noncommercial hosts focused on social value over economic benefits, one could expect them to not fully pass through their license costs.

We extend the literature on the heterogeneous supply side in sharing markets by demonstrating that commercial and non-commercial rental suppliers and their respective intentions about renting frequency and presence during guest stays in P2P rental markets should be assessed in more detail with the aid of publicly-available governmental data after the introduction of regulatory policies. Taking these aspects together, our empirical results inform theoretical models for sharing markets in two major ways. First, theory needs to account for heterogeneous types of suppliers with diferentiated economic behavior, and second, the geographical aspects need explicit and thorough consideration, as pricing behavior is fundamentally afected when supply and demand shift from one area to another.

Finally, our study contributes to the growing field of CSS and the use of QE designs to estimate the efects of regulatory policies in P2P rental markets [10]. On top of that, we proposed a novel approach of using geolocational information, on the fixed geographical locations of suppliers, to decompose the efects of regulatory policies that introduce several measures at one point in time.

## Practical Contributions

Our results inform policymakers about the causal efects of regulatory measures that simultaneously ban short-term rentals from one area and legalize it in others, requiring P2P rental suppliers to obtain an annual license. Such a two-part regulatory policy was already implemented in several cities, like Barcelona, Miami, Berkeley, and New Orleans. As P2P renting remains attractive for hosts when BTM costs can be passed through to guests partially or completely by increasing short-term rental prices, our results imply that, even though the French Quarter ban substantially reduced P2P rental activity in this neighborhood, the policies’ efectiveness was limited in the rest of the city. For hosts, acquiring a property solely to rent it out on P2P rental platforms remains promising.

So, the efectiveness of the policy highly depends on how policymakers spend the earnings through the licensing system. A regulatory measure without enforcement may not result in an outcome that policymakers and local residents are aiming at. That is why cooperation between local authorities and P2P rental platforms, for information sharing to track the rules, is crucial for efective regulation of P2P rental platforms. We understand Airbnb’s recent move to launch such an information-sharing platform for government and tourism to foster compliance as anecdotal confirmation of our findings [59].

Further, our results demonstrate that most of the commercial hosts, especially those with multiple listings per host, were represented in neighborhoods adjacent to the banned French Quarter and that they partially passed through their additional BTM costs to their guests. This implies that the city-center remains appealing to commercial suppliers despite the introduction of the policy. Thus, it seems plausible that the problems associated with P2P rental markets may merely shift from one area to another. Indeed, anecdotal evidence from New Orleans suggests that problems have arisen in the Garden District, a neighborhood located directly next to the French Quarter, where citizens have reported seeing lots of neighbors leaving the area due to the proliferation of short-term rentals [5]. In response to such claims, the New Orleans City Council voted to impose new restrictions on short-term rentals in 2019, including a prohibition of Airbnb activity in the Garden District.

To avoid another shift of Airbnb supply toward adjacent neighborhoods, our results suggest that policymakers should consider allowing only temporary- and accessory-, but not commercial-licenses for owner-occupants – at least in areas close to the city-center. Otherwise, local authorities could also think about implementing a regulatory measure that limits the number of listings for each host or to impose a 90-day rental limit on the listings of commercial suppliers. Thus, the more individual regulatory policies on P2P rental markets are designed, the more precisely they will reflect the rental frequencies and owner presences during guest stays, and the regulation will be more fruitful.

## Limitations, Future Research, and Concluding Thoughts

As with any research, this study comes with limitations, which, at the same time, ofer great opportunities for future research. First, we only analyzed a single and the most popular P2P rental platform, Airbnb. It may be possible that regulators especially focus on such big players when enforcing the regulations while simultaneously underestimating other P2P rental platforms on which rental suppliers also can operate. Even though New Orleans’ municipalities have efectively reduced the share of illegal suppliers on Airbnb, some hosts may have switched to other P2P rental platforms and remain undetected illegal suppliers on the market. Hosts with a temporary STR may also ofer their listing on multiple rental platforms to increase their 90-day rental limit. Our study has not yet taken these spillover-efects into consideration, which provides an interesting avenue for future research.

Second, we investigated the price efects for regulations in New Orleans, which arguably limits the generalizability of our results. In other cities in the U.S. or around the world, P2P rental suppliers may respond diferently to such a policy. Rental suppliers may have diferent cultural backgrounds in other regions that afect their risk aversion to operate an illegal listing. Future research can extend our analysis by undertaking replication studies in other cities where regulators introduced similar policy measures.

Third, some of our variables only represent approximations and may not present a precise picture of the market situation faced by P2P rental suppliers. For example, we only used proxies for our demand variable, which may not reflect the actual demand of a listing in all cases. Further, as some hosts reported their license codes inaccurately on the Airbnb website, some licenses could not be mapped to the listings. Hence, there may be listings assigned to a certain license type that we could not account for. Also, we did not include alternative accommodation oferings such as hotel rooms in our study that could also have influenced both peer rental suppliers as well as the decisions of visitors to New Orleans. Further research should extend our study in these directions.

Fourth, even though we carefully defined two diferent treated areas in New Orleans, we could not precisely separate the diferent efects of the two parts of the policy. To disentangle the policy measures, future research can analyze regulations in cities which implemented only one of these measures.

Fifth, our study mainly focused on analyzing whether demand and supply shifts played a role in hosts’ price-setting behavior after introduction of a regulatory policy, as suggested by theory. But we could neither easily measure demand-supply elasticity with our research design nor observe the underlying motivation for each host’s price-setting behavior. Future research should investigate further drivers of sharing economy participation as a theoretical underpinning when analyzing hosts’ responses towards a policy shift, including social value, hedonic value, sustainability value, and trust [31].

Finally, as we only observed hosts’ price reactions in the first ten months after the policies were announced, scholars, policymakers and property owners should be especially interested in further empirical evidence concerning the diverse long-term efects of policies regulating short-term rentals in areas popular with tourists, and the efectiveness of regulatory measures in keeping at bay any excessive price increases in the most afected areas. These long-term efects might, for instance, be driven by non-commercial hosts learning over time to adapt their pricing strategies to better account for changes in supply and demand in the P2P rental market.

This study has shown how P2P rental suppliers respond to regulatory interventions implemented by municipal governments aiming to minimize the negative externalities of P2P rental markets to local communities. We provide empirical evidence that P2P renting remains attractive when a regulatory policy is implemented that entailed the ban of shortterm rentals in a certain neighborhood in the center of a city, and the introduction of mandatory licenses in the remaining neighborhoods, as rental suppliers pass on the additional costs to their guests. We believe that our research significantly contributes to research on the efectiveness of regulatory measures on P2P rental markets and hope that our work will inspire future research to further investigate how diferent types of rental suppliers respond to other policy interventions on P2P rental markets.

1. In fact, price and product information is readily available in sharing markets through P2P platforms both for consumers of short-term accommodations as well as suppliers on these platforms. This is important as there is a high degree of heterogeneity among the large numbers of buyers and sellers. Only through a market design that allows for advanced search functionalities and appropriate price discovery mechanisms, such markets can successfully emerge [17]. Note that in such information-intensive industries not only consumers are better informed through these platforms but also the competition. This allows for pricing strategies that consider changes in supply and demand and even facilitate tacit collusion [23].

2. The literature has also analyzed social value, hedonic value, sustainability value, and trust as alternative or complementing drivers of sharing economy participation [31].

3. For the case of indefinitely elastic demand, either every consumer would already own the good or no consumer would rent the good. Further, if the unused capacity before P2P renting was an option exceeds the unfulfilled demand for the good, a glut result. Owners then compete on rental prices, and the market clears at the bring-to-market costs (which equal the marginal costs of production).

4. There is empirical evidence that neither the demand side nor the supply side will react in a completely inelastic way with respect to price changes. Farronato and Fradkin [18] found that the average price elasticity of P2P rental supply on Airbnb across ten U.S. cities is 3.4 (ranging between 3.1 and 4). The average price elasticity of demand for accommodations (including Airbnb listings and hotel rooms), is estimated at -4.3 and exhibits substantial heterogeneity across cities and accommodation options (ranging between -2.1 and -5.5 for Airbnb listings).

5. These cities were also subject to regulations prior to our observation period. However, when the regulations in New Orleans were implemented, there was no major adaption of the already existing policies. We assess the suitability of our control group listings in our robustness checks.

6. The listing price is diferent from the final transaction price customers have to pay. For instance, there is a service fee that covers Airbnb’s expenses. This service fee is typically proportional to the sum of the listing price and cleaning fees. The listing price is the main price component and is fully controlled by the host. It is ideal to study hosts’ price setting behavior.

7. Naturally, a gold standard randomized experiment is typically not an option in policymaking for local governments.

8. This information is also available to consumers through the Airbnb platform. This increased consumer informedness has changed consumer behavior in recent years [38]. It is important to control for this detailed product information in our empirical approach as consumers focus more on product details as opposed to price in the highly diferentiated market segment of P2P short-term rentals.
