# Is College Education Less Necessary with AI? Evidence from Firm-Level Labor Structure Changes

Mei Xue<sup>a</sup>, Xing Cao<sup>b</sup>, Xu Feng<sup>c</sup>, Bin Gu<sup>d</sup>, and Yongjie Zhang<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Carroll School of Management, Boston College, Chestnut Hill, USA; <sup>b</sup>School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China; <sup>c</sup>College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, Tianjin, China; <sup>d</sup>Questrom School of Business, Boston University, Boston, USA

## ABSTRACT

As a general-purpose technology, artificial intelligence (AI) is expected to transform almost all industries and aspects of our society. Thus, it is important to understand the potential changes within the firms related to how AI applications change their labor force. Using a panel dataset with over 1,300 publicly-traded companies in China from 2007 to 2018, we examine the relationship between AI applications and firm labor structure with workers with or without formal college education. The study indicates that AI applications were positively associated with the overall employment as well as the employment of nonacademically- trained workers with no college degrees at the firm level. These associations were more significant in the service sector than in the manufacturing sector. Further causal analysis shows increasing AI applications have a positive efect on a firm’s employment of nonacademically-trained workers and its overall employment but a negative efect on academically-trained workers. We attribute the findings to the technology deskilling efect of AI. The findings suggest that, in response to the potential labor force transformation with increasing AI applications, information-systems research needs to focus on structural changes of labor forces and the implications for preparing human employees to work with AI side by side.

## KEYWORDS

Artificial intelligence; augmentation efect; automation efect; information technology; job polarization; labor structure; skill-biased technological change; technology-enabled deskilling

## Introduction

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has propelled technology and business innovations in many industries, including healthcare [22, 32], higher education [62], and financial services [29]. As a result, firms undergo significant changes in their processes, operations, and organizational structures [37].

## Research Background

One such area that has attracted a significant amount of attention is the labor structure of a firm. In a report published by the Brookings Institution in January 2019, the authors predicted that in the AI era, automation will afect tasks in virtually all occupational groups and in the U.S., 25% of the current 36 million jobs are highly susceptible to automation with a 70%-plus chance [42]. Similarly, Fortune reports that AI could replace 40% of all jobs within 15 years [45]. Other studies acknowledge the potential job losses but note the prospect of AI to create new jobs. For example, the World Economic Forum estimates in its “Future of Jobs Report 2020” that 85 million jobs will be displaced by AI by 2025, while 97 million new jobs will be created at the same time. It notes that these new jobs will require significant reskilling and upskilling of workers [58]. The prospects of large-scale job displacements of workers by AI have significant economic and political implications. Business and political leaders (e.g., Bill Gates, Bill De Blasio, Andrew Yang [61] have proposed solutions from a robot tax to universal income to address the issue [47].

To prepare the society for these potential profound changes, it is important to understand how exactly the applications of AI may lead to changes of the labor force. AI can have conflicting influences on a firm’s labor demand through two conflicting efects: automation and augmentation [44]. On the one hand, AI can augment a worker’s skills and hence improve the worker’s productivity and thus increase labor demand. On the other hand, it can replace human workers for various jobs even for those requiring advanced and complex cognitive, analytical, or decision-making skills. Moreover, these two efects are not simply in conflict with each other. They are complementary to each other at the same time. Automation can lead to more augmentation and vice versa. Hence, like previous generations’ general-purpose technologies (GPT) such as information technology (IT) and robotics, AI can have complex efects on employment. The discussions on this topic by academics, practitioners, and policy-makers have also shown a wide spectrum of opinions from pessimistic (e.g., AI will outpace most human workers and eventually take over the control of the society) to optimistic (e.g., AI will allow most of humanity to enjoy an abundance of wealth and leisure by unlocking unprecedented productivity and creativity) [54]. Nevertheless, with competing theories, there has been relatively scarce empirical evidence, especially at the firm level, to help better understand the issues involved.

The current research is set to add such an understanding by focusing on a specific topic. With the applications of AI, has traditional formal academic higher education (four-year college and above) become less necessary for jobs afected by AI? In an article that appeared in the Wall Street Journal on May 16, 2021, Levi Strauss & Co., the fashion retailer, was reported for organizing a machine learning (ML) bootcamp to retrain its workers to master skills for tasks including advanced market research, new product development, supply chain management, and finding optimal locations of new brick-and-mortar stores . One unique aspect about this machine learning bootcamp is that many of the attending workers never went to a college and had only worked in blue-collar positions before. After the bootcamp, about a quarter of the workers moved to new positions as data scientists while the others returned to their previous positions but with AI-enabled skills (e.g., mining through hundreds of images scraped from the Internet to predict the fashion trend). The executive at Levi Strauss who organized the ML bootcamp to retrain its workers called the program an efort to democratize the field or to level the new playground driven by data analytics for those workers who are disadvantaged for the lack of a formal academic higher education through equipping them with AI augmented skills and capabilities.

The example illustrates AI’s ability to revolutionize the retail industry. One revolution AI has brought is to break the corporate glass ceiling for those without formal academic higher education, which traditionally divided the workforce with enormous implications for pay, benefits, career trajectory, and lifestyle for those on the diferent sides. A college diploma, which often resembles a ticket to white-collar jobs, seems no longer a necessity to become a manager, analyst, designer, or data scientist at firms like Levi Strauss. At Choice Market, a supermarket chain on the west coast of U.S., past supermarket cashiers who have never gone to college were retrained to become e-commerce analysts in charge of data entry that involves using a computer to create detailed store layout to monitor inventories and shelves and run the platform to integrate the data with the third-party delivery systems (e.g., Uber Eats and GrubHub) [33].

A global survey on AI by McKinsey & Co. [15] conducted from March to April 2019 with 2,360 participating executives, representing a wide range of regions, industries, company sizes, functional specialties, and tenures found that at least 83% of the companies using AI had retrained or uptrained their employees to master skills to work better alongside AI during the year before the survey and expected such retaining or uptraining of employees in the year after. As many as 38% of firms in the survey expected more than a quarter of the workforce to be retrained or uptrained eventually. It is reasonable to speculate that some of those retrained or uptrained workers won’t have college educations that used to be required for their future new jobs. These jobs are now within their reach because of AI applications.

## Research Questions

Are the firms hiring more nonacademically-trained employees with increasing AI applications? This is a core question we set out to answer in our study. We examine whether AI applications are systematically linked to or even cause the changes of the employments for workers with or without traditional four-year college higher education training (academically-trained vs. nonacademically-trained) in the firms. The findings provide empirical evidence about how AI applications might have shifted skill requirements for the labor force at the firm level.

The other question of interest is whether AI applications are linked to or cause any systematic change of a firm’s overall employment. The answer to this question sheds lights into the overall results of the two conflicting and complementary efects of AI on labor, the automation efect, and the augmentation efect. Understanding the overall impact AI could have on a firm’s total headcount, provides guidance for business and societal planning, and prepares organizations for potential far reaching changes brought by AI. If as many have feared, the application of AI will lead to the diminishing role of human capital in societal production and function, then governments need to plan accordingly with shrinking demand for human labor. In such a case, the government may want to reduce worktime, lower retirement age to decrease the labor supply, plan for unemployment, retirement coverage, and benefits accordingly, and meanwhile promote lifestyles that would keep people upbeat rather than depressed for going out of the workforce too early.

However, if the opposite is happening – that the prevalence of AI applications is linked to an overall job boom, then the government focus might be on retraining the existing workforce to fill the new positions. This is what Levi Strauss has been doing, by preparing young, novice workers for the newly-created jobs that require very diferent skills and knowledge. Thus, to understand how the employment outlook may be, valuable information needs to be generated for managerial decision and policy-making.

The diversity of processes within firms of diferent industries might moderate the potential efect of AI on firm labor structures and their associations. Therefore, another question to explore is whether business or industry heterogeneity plays a role related to AI applications and firm labor structure.

## Analyses and Findings

We conducted a firm-level analysis to investigate the relationships between a firm’s AI applications and its labor structure characterized by worker education level, both in terms of associational and causal relationships. Our analysis used a unique firm-level dataset of 1,387 publicly traded companies in China from 2007 to 2018. During this period, many firms in China embraced AI in diferent aspects of their R&D, operations, customer service, marketing, and management. However, direct measures of AI applications in the firms were not available. Thus, we extracted AI related terms from their annual reports to use the ratio of these AI related terms among all terms in the firms’ annual reports as the proxies of their AI applications. This approach is like previous studies on the impact of AI and other technology advancement on firms [37]. Our assumption is that such ratios of AI related terms in the annual reports indicate the relative amount of managerial attention and resources dedicated to AI applications by the firms, which influence their human resource decisions and consequently their labor structures. With this assumption, this proxy measure has limitations and the potential of underestimation bias for AI’s influence on labor structure. We use employee head counts classified by their highest level of education to describe the labor structure of a firm [19].

We ran a fixed-efects panel-data models first without instrumental variables (IVs) to examine the associations and then with IVs to establish causal relationships. We sought to understand how the relationships between AI applications and firm labor structures at these two diferent levels are both necessary. The analysis for association provided a high-level overview of the relationships that are useful to guide further research and practice. The causality analysis revealed whether and how AI applications drive firm labor structure changes, which are crucial for understanding the underlying mechanisms and advancing theory development. In addition to the cross-industry analyses, we also conducted an industry breakdown analysis for manufacturing and service to gain insights on the variations due to industry and business heterogeneity.

We found that AI applications are associated with an overall expansion of a firm’s labor force as well as a significant increase of the employment of nonacademically-trained workers. Further causal analysis confirmed that AI applications are indeed a factor driving the rising demand of nonacademically-trained workers and declining demand for academically-trained workers. We argue these changes of firm labor structures with increasing AI applications can be best explained by technology-enabled deskilling theory. The industry breakdown results generated interesting findings about the diferences between service and manufacturing sectors with industries such as IT, Retail & Wholesale, and Financial Services standing out.

Our study provides empirical evidence regarding how AI, which is deemed to have profound impact on our society at large, could afect the labor structure of firms. To the best of our knowledge, research that empirically analyzes the efects of AI on labor force has been scarce [5, 63]. As noted in Frank et al. [23] and Dixon et al. [19], to gain a better understanding of the impact of technology on the labor force, it is of significant value to use more fine-grained data at the firm level, especially when many of the existing studies had been conducted at the industry or regional levels which obscure the diferent countervailing efects. As one of the first to examine the relationships between AI applications and firm labor structure empirically at the firm level, our analysis helps to develop a better understanding of the micro-level process through which firms change their labor structures in response to AI applications.

## Literature and theoretical background

Following Acemoglu and Restrepo [4], we define AI as machine-based agents, which are machines, software, or algorithms that act intelligently by recognizing and responding to their environment. This definition highlights an important characteristic of AI – its ability to recognize and respond to its environment. This ability allows AI to provide customized or individualized responses, while earlier generations of information technologies are known for automation of mainly routinized responses and processes. This distinction of AI has great implications for its impact on labor demands.

## Three Theories about the Impact of Technology Applications on Labor Demand

Our research is related to two streams of research work in the literature. The first focuses on building the theories to explain how the applications of advanced technology influence labor demand. For example, our study on how AI applications could be related to and impact firm labor structure changes. The second studies how the applications of innovative technologies influence various aspects of organizations, including labor structure – and productivity, organization structure, innovations, and financial characteristics. These firm characteristics may influence firms’ AI applications, labor structure changes, or both.

For the first stream of literature, several theories and model frameworks had been proposed to explain the impact of technologies on labor demand over time, including the skill-biased technological change (SBTC) theory, the task-based framework with the U-shaped job polarization (USJP) efects theory, and the technology deskilling efects theory.

SBTC theory. This was developed based on the finding of a strong correlation between the adoption of automation technologies and the increased use of better educated (e.g., college-educated) labor since the late 1970s [10, 34]. According to this theory, labor forces can be divided into two groups based on their skills. An important hypothesis is that technology change is skill-biased [28]. In particular, the complementary efect of technology advancement is stronger for jobs requiring higher skills. Thus, the gain in productivity of high-skilled workers due to technology advancement will be larger than that of low-skilled workers. As a result, technology advancement drives employers to hire more high-skilled workers and fewer low-skilled workers [12]. This period had a demand surge for highskilled IT workers and a supply shortage of such workers due to the burst of newly-created IT-related jobs [28].

The task-based framework. However, later studies found evidence of a USJP efect in the employment trends from the 1980s to 1990s in the U.S. [2, 9], the U.K. [30], and Europe [31]. USJP refers to the finding of growth in employment in both high-skilled and betterpaid professionals and low-skilled and less well-paid occupations (e.g., cleaning, and personal care) and meanwhile declining employment in the middle of the distribution (e.g., manufacturing, and routine ofice jobs).

The skill-biased theory does not explain this non-monotone growth of employment by skill level. Thus, some researchers extended the skill-biased theory and proposed a task-based framework [3]. This stream of research suggests that ITs are not suitable to take on non-routine cognitive tasks that require advanced skills, such as problemsolving skills, adaptability, and creativity. The workers that are the most suitable to take on these jobs are typically those with higher education. Meanwhile, IT and other automation technologies are also not suitable to take on non-routine manual tasks, such as cleaning and personal care, which were usually handled by workers with low education. As for routine cognitive and manual tasks that can be easily programmed, automation technologies such as IT and robotics can substitute middle-skill human workers with significant improvement of accuracy, eficiency, and economies of scale. These lead to the USJP efect that IT and robotics have on labor demands. Moreover, the positive impact of IT on firm productivity led to an explosive growth of businesses, which in turn lead to more demand for labors for non-routine cognitive tasks and non-routine manual tasks and further enhances the USJP efect.

Technology-enabled deskilling. With the observation of overall job growth along with significant technology advances in the previous decades, some researchers started to question whether job polarization, that had been found to widely exist from the 1980s to 1990s in the developed countries, would persist [7, 8]. For the period 2000-2007, Autor [6] and Acemoglu and Autor [2] have shown that the share of low-skilled jobs increased rapidly while the shares of high-skilled or middle-skilled jobs did not. This contradicts the predictions of both the SBTC theory and the task-based framework with the USJP efects theory but supports the technology-enabled deskilling efect theory [11, 24]. Deskilling, also known as downskilling [41] or the degradation of work [1], means reducing the skill level needed for a job.

The first large-scale technology-enabled deskilling appeared during the first industrial revolution. Reviewing the history of industrialization makes it clear that technology advances unleashed a strong demand for low-skilled workers at that time. In the textile industry, low-skilled sewing machine operators took the jobs of the high-skilled loom workers [38]. In the shipping industry, because of the adoption of the steam engines, middle-skilled professional sailors were replaced by the low-skilled engine operators [17]. In the manufacturing industry, with the prevalence of assembly lines, low-skilled assembly line workers replaced the craftsmen who possess more comprehensive set of skills [55].

The first step for the deskilling efect to happen is that a complex job that used to be done by high skilled workers is divided into smaller and hence simpler pieces. The pieces are essentially divided into two groups with one group handled by technology instead of a highskilled worker and the other group which still requires some level of low-skill human interactions or intervention handled by a low-skilled worker. Automation technologies have been able to lower the required skill and education thresholds of workforce for these jobs [46]. Using technology deskilling efect theory, Downey [20] noted that low-wage workers have become part of the production process due to partial automaton, where adoption of automation technology replaces high-skilled workers with low-skilled workers but cannot dismiss human labor from the process:

“As [an] example, statistical clerks compile data and tabulate statistics for use in statistical studies . . . [and] tabulate statistics using adding machine and calculator. Certainly, Microsoft Excel has simplified these tasks so that a much wider range of workers can perform them. . . . One explanation is that fully-automated robots are not yet available to do these jobs, but technological simplification allows less-skilled workers to do them, which I call technology-enabled deskilling” [20, p. 2].

Kronblad [35] also has argued that increasing applications of digital technologies reduces the level of knowledge intensity in legal service delivery and thus lowers the skill requirements, which results in higher capital intensity and lower professionalization of the workforce in these professional service firms.

In another recent work in this area, Ong and Png [43] documented an interesting finding: that the technological deskilling efect would increase the supply of low-skilled labor. Our work adds to this stream of literature by contributing empirical evidence of the technology deskilling efect of AI at the firm level with a large-scale multi-year panel-data study.

## Research on How Technology Applications Impact Organizations

The second relevant stream of literature focuses on the impact of the applications of advanced technologies including AI on firms’ labor structures, productivity growth [12, 13], returns on IT investments [51, 52], intangible capital growth and market valuation [14, 53], innovations [59, 60], and organizational structure [19]. These are the aspects or characteristics of firms that could potentially influence AI applications, firm labor structure, and the relationship between.

For example, Bresnahan et al. [12] studied the efect on productivity and labor demand of the innovations of information technology, complementary workplace reorganization, and new products and services using the SBTC theory. They found empirical evidence of complementarities among all three types of innovations studied in their paper and a stronger shift to more skilled workers with these innovations and organizational change. Tambe [51] investigated the impact of labor market on big data technology investment returns using a firm-level panel dataset and identified two factors that contribute to higher returns: a firm’s ownership of significant data assets and proximity to labor markets which supply workers with complementary technical skills. The findings underscore the importance of geography, corporate investment, and skill acquisition channels for productivity growth with IT innovations. We hence include the local labor supply of high-skilled workers in our framework.

Brynjolfsson et al. [14] developed a model to show complementary investments required for GPT such as AI are typically intangible and often poorly measured. As a result, the output and total factor productivity (TFP) attributed to new GPT are often underestimated initially and then overestimated later. They called this as the productivity J-curve. Dixon et al. [19] investigated the efects of robots as GPT on employment and other aspects of business practice using a region-level data. They found that the investment in robots is associated with higher employee turnover and higher firm employment. They also found that more investments in robotics is linked to less managerial and more non-managerial posts. This provides insights into how robots as GPT are changing the labor structure within a firm. Our findings are consistent with the earlier work: the use of GPT seems to have led to overall increases in labor demand for firms.

## Hypotheses

Drawn from the literature and theoretical background for the research questions discussed earlier, we formulated hypotheses regarding the relationships between AI applications and the employments of nonacademically-trained workers and academically-trained workers, the overall employment at a firm, as well as the role of business heterogeneity in such relationships.

## AI Deskilling Efects: Employment of Academically and Nonacademically-Trained Workers

When AI performs the complex tasks of a job that require sophisticated information processing and analysis as a substitute for a highly-skilled worker, if there are remaining tasks that require human intervention or interaction, they are typically handled by a worker without such sophisticated analytic skills. Often such skills only come after a formal fouryear college academic training or even graduate school level professional education. Thus, when deskilling of AI happens, it essentially substitutes the high-skilled workers who are academically-trained with lower-skilled workers who are not academically-trained. This consequently creates an increase in the employment of nonacademically-trained workers for the firm and a decrease in the academically-trained workers. Since direct measures of high or low skills are often unavailable, we use workers’ education levels to categorize workers in our hypothesis testing.

We define an academically-trained worker as a worker with a formal four-year college education and otherwise as a nonacademically-trained worker. This categorization allows us to directly examine the role of education in the workers’ future with advancement of technology. We thus propose the following hypotheses with the technology deskilling efects of AI:

● Hypothesis 1a (AI and the Employment of Nonacademically-Trained Workers): AI applications are positively related to the employment of nonacademically-trained workers in firms.

● Hypothesis 1b (AI and the Employment of Academically-Trained Workers): AI applications are negatively related to the employment of academically-trained workers in firms.

## AI and Overall Employment

Using a paradox framework, Raisch and Krakowski [44] argued that AI is replacing humans for certain jobs – the automation efect, but it also is creating new job opportunities for humans – the augmentation efect. Meanwhile, the automation and augmentation efects can also be complementary to each other with one leading to the other. The co-existence of the two efects means that the overall impact of AI on total employment is complex. Using technology deskilling theory for H1, when an automation technology like AI replaces human workers for the complex tasks of a job, often there is still a remaining part that requires human interaction or intervention. This is done by another worker without the sophisticated skills possessed by the human worker replaced by AI. In such a case, even with the automation efect taking place, AI doesn’t eliminate the job position for a worker but has fundamentally changed the contents and format of the job and its qualification requirements.

Hence, on such occasions, the number of jobs for humans remains the same, although the nature of the jobs and consequently the human labor structure in the firms change drastically. Due to the productivity gains and economies of scale from the implementation of AI technology, there can also be occasions when the number of nonacademically-trained workers needed may not be exactly equal to the number of academically-trained workers being replaced. Our point is that even with the automation efect considered, the resulting job losses may not be as bad since in real life it is almost impossible to completely erase human intervention or interaction in most jobs.

On the other hand, through the augmentation and spillover efects, like previous GPT, with productivity gains, innovative applications, and new business models, AI applications can give birth to a whole new set of job positions [52]. Considering more automation is likely to be partial than full automation and the potential of new job creations with AI applications, we hypothesize the resulting job increase should exceed job losses from AI applications in a firm:

● Hypothesis 2 (AI and Overall Employment): AI applications are positively related to the overall employment in a firm.

## AI and Business Heterogeneity: Service vs. Manufacturing

Most job tasks cannot be fully automated because there is almost always a part of these jobs that still requires some level of human intervention or interaction. Instead, automation simplifies the jobs and allows less skilled workers to do them, which is the deskilling efect of the automation technology used. We argue such a mechanism prevails more in the service sector than in the manufacturing sectors following AI applications due to their diferent characteristics of the two sectors. While tasks in manufacturing are usually standard, repetitive, and mostly human touch free, a service delivery process often consists of tasks that require significant level of customization and a human worker’s involvement. This suggests a lower automation level can be achieved in service than in manufacturing with AI applications. With a lower automation level, more jobs are preserved while more jobs for less skilled workers are created. Meanwhile, compared to a manufacturing process, a service delivery process often has higher knowledge intensity [27]. This characteristic of the service process has made it challenging to automate with previous automation technologies, although they have been successful in automating manufacturing processes.

On the other hand, so much automation has already taken place in manufacturing via previous waves of automation that there is little more to automate via AI (or considerably less). This makes the overall base for automation smaller and reduce the efectiveness of AI’s deskilling efect in manufacturing than in service. In short, we expect the technology deskilling efect of AI is likely to be more significant in service than in manufacturing. As a result, we are more likely to observe the positive relationship between AI applications and the overall employment (H2) and the employment of nonacademically-trained workers (H1a) and the negative relationship between AI applications and the employment of academically-trained workers (H1b) in service versus manufacturing. Since AI is capable of cognitive and analytic assessment, it is reasonable to assume AI has a bigger role to play in automating service processes and consequently result in more deskilling in service sector because of the high knowledge intensity of service processes.

● Hypothesis 3a (AI and Business Heterogeneity: Service vs. Manufacturing - AI and Overall Employment): The positive relationship between AI applications and the overall employment in a firm is more significant in service than in manufacturing.

● Hypothesis 3b (AI and Business Heterogeneity: Service vs. Manufacturing – AI and the Employment of Nonacademically-Trained Workers): The positive relationship between AI applications and the employment of nonacademically-trained workers in a firm is more significant in service than in manufacturing.

● Hypothesis 3c (AI and Business Heterogeneity: Service vs. Manufacturing – AI and the Employment of Academically-Trained Workers): The negative relationship between AI applications and the employment of academically-trained workers in a firm is more significant in service than in manufacturing.

We will conduct hypothesis testing using fixed-efects panel-data analysis without and with IVs. The first analysis examines the associations between AI applications and the labor structure changes. The second analysis focuses on investigating the potential causal relationships between them.

## Data and analysis

## Data and Measures

Our empirical analysis uses a panel dataset of 1,387 publicly-traded firms in China from 2007 to 2018, a total of 12 years. Our original sample has all 3,597 companies publicly traded in the Chinese A-Share stock market with 25,665 observations for the study period. We removed abnormal observations if it meets one of the following exclusion criteria: (1) firms whose stock trading was halted for more than half of all trading days each year; (2) firms with abnormal variations in stock returns (e.g., more than five times of the variables’ standard deviation); and (3) firms with missing data. A significant number of firms don’t report their IT infrastructure data every year. A firm is deleted from the sample for the year this data is missing. This left us with an unbalanced panel, so the sample we used for the main study has 1,387 companies and 4,563 observations.<sup>1</sup> The dataset was assembled from several data sources.

Table 1 provides definitions for all variables. We describe the variables used in our empirical analysis:

● Labor structure (LaborStructure). To examine the deskilling efect of AI for workers consistent with Dixon et al. [19], we classified company workers into two categories based on whether they received a formal four-year college education or not as the academically-trained or the nonacademically-trained group. We used the log count value of the two types of employees to capture the labor structure of a firm in our model estimations.<sup>2</sup> The data for the educational levels of employees of each firm were obtained from the RESSET database (www.resset.cn/).

● AI application (AIApp). For a firm’s deployment of AI applications, we applied a proxy measure from each firm’s annual reports. Since AI application is still at its early stage, there are no publicly available sources for firm-level data on firms’ AI activities. Several recent studies [16, 37] extracted measurements of a firm’s AI or digital technology adoptions from its public disclosure texts such as annual reports. We adopted the same approach. We used a dictionary to count AI relevant terms in a firm’s annual reports and used the ratio of AI terms to all terms in the annual report as a proxy of the amount of the firm’s AI applications.<sup>3</sup> A total of 45 such words and terms were identified from the model. (See Appendix A for their Chinese-English equivalents and Appendix B for our dataset construction and analytics.) We calculated the AI app deployment ratio by Equation 1:

$$
\mathrm{AIApp} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} = \frac {\# \text { AI   terms   in   annual   report } _ {\mathrm{i} , \mathrm{t}}}{\# \text { total   words   in   annual   report } _ {\mathrm{i} , \mathrm{t}}} \times 1, 0 0 0 - \text { IndYrAvg } _ {\mathrm{AI}, \mathrm{t}}\tag{1}
$$

IndY $\mathrm { \dot { r } A v g _ { A I , t } }$ is the mean of the ratio (the count of AI-related terms or words divided by the count of all terms in the annual reports) of all firms in the sector that firm i belongs to in year t. Besides the variables above, we also used the following as control variables in our models:

● IT application (ITApp). As AI applications are enabled by IT, including a control for IT applications of the firm allowed us to better identify the impact of AI applications and distinguish it from the impact of IT. To develop the IT application measure, we again used the TALWEM algorithm. In this case, we started with the seed word “information system” for the TALWEM model. We found 40 words having a correlation with the word “information system” above 0.75. We used these dictionaries to search the firms’ annual reports to calculate the proxy for IT applications with Equation 2:

$$
\mathrm{ITApp} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} = \frac {\# \text { IT   terms   in   annual   report } _ {\mathrm{i} , \mathrm{t}}}{\# \text { total   words   in   annual   report } _ {\mathrm{i} , \mathrm{t}}} \times 1, 0 0 0 - \text { IndYrAvg } _ {\mathrm{IT}, \mathrm{t}}\tag{2}
$$

IndY $\mathrm { r A v g } _ { I T , \mathrm { t } }$ is the mean of the ratio (the count of IT related words divided by the count of all terms in the annual reports) of all the firms of the industry sector that firm i belonged to in year t.

● IT infrastructure (ITInfr). The application of AI also relies on a firm’s IT infrastructure. We used a company’s annual technology expenditure and investment as a proxy for its IT infrastructure. The measure includes investment and expenses in electronic devices, computers, and their accessories. This measure is consistent with prior IS literature that uses IT investment or expenditure to measure IT inputs [40, 51]. The dataset came from RESSET.

● Labor supply (BAGrads). To control for heterogeneity in local labor supplies [51], we include the number of local high-skilled workers measured by the annual number of undergraduate graduates (four-year college graduates with bachelor’s degrees) in the province as a control variable. The number of annual undergraduate graduates comes from the National Bureau of Statistics of China.

● Firm size (Assets) and age (Age). Flórez et al. [25] found that there is a systematic relationship among firm size and age and the employment growth rate. To control for the influence, we include firm size, measured by the firm’s total assets, reported in firms’ annual report, and firm age, measured by the time since its inception, as control variables. The data came from the WIND database (www.wind.com.cn/).

● Financial leverage (FinLev) and capital expenditure (Capex). Sharpe [49] reported a relationship between a firm’s financial leverage and its labor force cyclicality. Chen et al. [16] also found that a firm’s capital expenditure and digital activities are related. So, we included the firm’s financial leverage, measured by the ratio of total assets to owner’s equity, and firm’s capital expenditure, measured by the cash paid for purchase and construction of fixed assets, intangible assets, and other long-term assets in a year, as control variables. The data are from the WIND database.

● Annual stock return (Returns). To control for increasing employment due to stock market consumption wealth efects [18], we included the annual stock return as a control variable. The data were obtained from the WIND database.

● Provincial GDP value: To control for the efect of regional economic growth, we included the gross domestic product (GDP) value of the province where the firm is located as a control variable.

## Propensity Score Matching (PSM) and Summary Statistics

To ensure that we are comparing similar firms with and without AI applications, we match the firms in our sample using propensity score matching (PSM) with firms that didn’t mention AI in their annual reports. As shown in the previous literature, the scale of the firm [25] and its business characteristics [8] are influential on its labor structure. Meanwhile, the scale of the firm [65] and its business characteristics [15] also have significant impacts on firm AI adoption and deployment. Thus, using a firm’s scale measured by its total assets and the industry sector it belongs to as the matching criteria and whether the firm mentions AI applications in its annual reports as the dependent variable, we matched the firms in the sample using PSM. We matched the 1,353 firms in our sample with 4,195 observations. ## Empirical Model

To estimate the relationship between AI application and a firm’s overall employment, we ran a panel-data regression model with time and firm fixed efects as in Equation 3.

$$
\ln (\text { AllEmp }) _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \text { AIApp } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} + \beta_ {2} \text { ITApp } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} + \sum_ {\mathrm{j}} \gamma_ {\mathrm{j}} \text { Control } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} ^ {j} + \text { Firm } _ {\mathrm{i}} + \text { Year } _ {\mathrm{t}} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}}\tag{3}
$$

$\ln ( { \mathrm { A l l E m p } } ) _ { \mathrm { i , t } }$ is the natural logarithm of total headcount of firm i in year t. AIApp and $I T A p p$ are proxies for AI applications and IT applications (the ratio of AI term count and IT term count in a firm’s annual reports).

To estimate the relationship between AI applications and firm labor structure, we ran a panel-data model with time and firm fixed efects as in Equation 4.

$$
\text { LaborStructure } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \text { AIApp } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} + \beta_ {2} \text { ITApp } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} + \sum_ {j} \gamma_ {j} \text { Control } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} ^ {j} + \text { Firm } _ {\mathrm{i}} + \text { Year } _ {\mathrm{t}} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}}\tag{4}
$$

LaborStructu $\mathrm { r e } _ { \mathrm { i , t } }$ is measured by the headcounts of nonacademically-trained and academically-trained workers of firm i in year t with one regression model for each.

To compare the relationships between AI applications on firm labor structure in the manufacturing sector with those in the service sector, we identified each firm’s industry sector with a dummy variable. Then we added interaction terms for the sector dummy variable and AI applications to allow AI to have diferential impacts on firms in diferent industry sectors by using a fixed-efects panel-data model:

$$
\text { LaborStructure } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} \text { AIApp } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} \times \text { IndMfg } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} + \beta_ {2} \text { AIApp } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} \times \text { IndSvc } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} + \beta_ {3} \text { ITApp } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} \times \text { IndMfg } _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}}
$$

$$
+ \beta_ {4} \text { ITApp } _ {\text { i,t }} \times \text { IndSvc } _ {\text { i,t }} + \beta_ {5} \text { IndSvc } _ {\text { i,t }} + \sum_ {j} \gamma_ {j} \text { Control } _ {\text { i,t }} ^ {j} + \text { Firm } _ {\text { i }} + \text { Year } _ {\text { t }} + \varepsilon_ {\text { i,t }}\tag{5}
$$

Ind $\operatorname { M f g } _ { \mathrm { i , t } }$ is equal to 1 if firm i in year t is a manufacturing firm and 0 otherwise. Also, IndSvc is 1 if firm i is a service firm and 0 otherwise.

## The Impact of AI Applications on Firm Labor Structure

To further investigate and determine the causal relationship between AI applications and the labor structure changes besides using PSM for sample assembly, we used a set of IVs to address potential endogeneity. After testing for validity in terms of over-identification and underidentification, we settled on a set of three IVs: the number of AI-related patents that the firm holds each year; the number of AI related research papers published in the industry that a firm belongs to each year; and the beta of the firm’s stock price. The latter is a measure of the volatility of a firm’s stock price that is a key driver of its cost of capital under the capital asset pricing model (CAPM). The number of AI patents indicate a firm’s intellectual properties that can directly lead to its AI applications. The number of research papers published on AI in the industry suggests its AI technology advances, which can also lead to AI applications in the firms. A firm’s beta may directly influence a firm’s technology adoption too [13]. We expect that this argument also applies to firms’ adoptions of AI powered applications. The data sources include: the CCER database (www.ccerdata.cn/) for the number of firm AI patents; CNKI (www.cnki.net/) and Web of Science (www.webofscience.com) for the number of AI research papers for the industry; and the CSMAR database (www.gtarsc.com/) for firm betas. ## Robustness Analysis and Summary

We conducted several robustness analyses for abnormal stock returns, alternative correlation threshold for keyword list, and missing data, which show the results are generally consistent with the results discussed above. (The robustness analyses and their related are in Appendix C.) Table 15 summarizes the hypothesis testing results of the fixed-efects paneldata analysis without the IVs in Tables 5 and 7–9 and with them in Tables 12-14.

The table reports evidence of positive and significant relationships between AI applications and overall employment as well as the employment of the non-academically-trained workers at the firm level based on both analyses. AI applications are not only positively associated with increasing overall employment and the employment of non-academically-trained workers at firms but also responsible for such increases. Collectively, these results provide the empirical evidence that AI application is a driver for the employment of workers without college degrees and an overall increase of the size of firm labor force.

The relationship between AI applications and the employment of academically-trained workers changed from insignificant to significant after adding the IVs. It suggests while AI applications indeed cause reduced demand for academically-trained workers at firms, we might not necessarily observe declining number of the academically-trained workers following more AI applications. This might be due to the existence of potential compounding or latent factors and endogeneity of AI applications and the employment of academically-trained workers.

As for the diference between the service and manufacturing sectors, the analyses with or without the IVs uncovered interaction efects between the service sector and AI applications. On the other hand, there are indeed diferences for how AI applications and the service and manufacturing sectors interact with each other. Such diferences may not have been caused by AI applications. More likely, the diferences are mainly driven by the sector diferences, and the inherent characteristics of their processes, industry structures, and business models.

## Discussion

We next discuss our findings and explore their relevant managerial and policy implications, as well as their limitations, and the directions for future research that they prompt.

## AI and Technology Deskilling Efect Theory

Our study contributes empirical evidence on the technology deskilling efects of AI applications, which include positive efects on the employment of nonacademically-trained workers and countervailing negative efects for academically-trained workers. The results suggest the impact of AI applications on labor structure is best explained by the technology deskilling efects theory rather than the SBTC theory or the USJP efects theory. AI efectively lowers the skill requirements of jobs by undertaking the tasks that used to require knowledge or skills developed through college education. It does not necessarily raise the knowledge or skill requirement as SBTC theory predicts. It also does not generate demand for high-skill employees (e.g., professional work) while reducing the demand for middleskill workers (e.g., manufacturing and routine ofice workers) and increasing the demand for low-skill workers (e.g., manual work and personal care) as the USJP efects theory would have predicted either.

Our study divides the workers into two groups, academically-trained and nonacademicallytrained. The high-skill workers are academically-trained in our framework. We found a negative efect of AI on their employment rather than a positive efect as predicted by USJP theory. The middle-skill workers can be either nonacademically-trained (e.g., a frontline assembly manufacturing job or front-desk receptionist ofice work) or academically-trained (e.g., a manufacturing system operator, or administrative ofice work). Thus, the impact of AI on middle-skill workers could be positive for those without a college education or negative for those with a college education based on our findings, not necessarily negative as predicted by USJP theory.

## Implications for Practice

The results from our study are consistent with findings from the global survey on AI by McKinsey mentioned earlier. This consistency suggests that the findings from our study of Chinese companies are not unique to China but represent a global trend in the changing labor structure of firms, following their AI deployment. For example, the survey found that the popular concern about AI causing workforce reduction:

“ . . . has largely not been realized. More than one-third respondents report less than a 3% change in their companies’ work size because of AI deployment, and only 5% of respondents report a change, whether decrease or increase, of greater than 10%.” And that “more respondents overall report job increases of 3% or more at their companies in the past year than report decreases of the same magnitude (17% and 13%, respectively).”

These quotations show that AI applications are linked to positive changes in the overall workforce size of the firms, as stated in H2 in this research. In addition, although the survey had no direct questions about hiring nonacademically-trained workers, it found that at least 83% of the companies using AI had retrained their employees to master skills to “work better alongside with AI” during the year before the survey and expected employee retention in the year after. As many as 38% of the companies in the survey expected more than a quarter of their workforces to be retrained eventually. The words “retraining” or “uptraining” were used without diferentiation, suggesting upward movement of the workforce skills following AI deployment, including for those at the bottom of the workforce skill pyramid, the nonacademically-trained, as stated in H1a.

An earlier study of 2,000 diferent work activities across more than 800 occupations by McKinsey, published in June 2018 [39], shared similar insights. It noted that almost all occupations will be afected by automation, but only 5% will be fully automated using the currently available automation technologies (including AI). This is consistent with one of our key assumptions for H2: job loss due to full automation should be low. The McKinsey study predicted significant labor demand increase by 2030 due to productivity growth and new occupations created by AI and other automation technologies. It pointed out that, when it comes to the ability for AI to create jobs for its own development and deployment, such as R&D, manufacturing, sale, operation and maintenance of its own devices and software, “technology itself has historically been a net job creator.” McKinsey also predicts that jobs will change dramatically in terms of their contents and format. As partial automation powered by AI prevails, machines commonly complement human labor in the workplace, and humanmachine interactions will reach unpresented scale and depth.

The findings of our study with evidence from China and the McKinsey AI global survey findings suggest that humans may not necessarily face a jobless future. But it is important to prepare for a transition or structural change of the labor force to work with AI side by side. Our finding shows the overall job posts may not change quantitatively in a negative way as many have feared. However, the qualitative aspect of the jobs will go through a complete overhaul to facilitate ever closer, more common, and deeper human-AI interaction. Firms, educators, policy-makers, and society-at-large need to work together to ensure a swift and smooth transition so the labor force is able to meet the new challenges.

Companies need to actively and creatively seek opportunities to reposition their employees to work with AI and to prepare and support them for new job roles with adequate incentives, training, and coaching. This may involve redesigning the workflow process, the workplace layout, the incentive system, the decision-making, and management structure, and even the organizational structure. With AI and human workers’ working side by side and sharing their knowledge and skills, an important question arises: Which of the two should have the power to make the final decision? While the workers can obtain knowledge and support from AI, less help or guidance will be sought from a human supervisor, which could lead to a simpler and flatter organization structure. Furthermore, as nonacademically-trained workers powered by AI take on jobs that used to be out of their reach, the traditional boundary between blue-collar and white-collar jobs will blur and the mobility of the workforce within and outside the firm will increase. As a result, the labor structure will have less hierarchy and become more agile and adaptive.

How can educators develop the supply of such a workforce? Most college graduates in the U.S. are working in jobs that are suitable for automation [21]. This demonstrates the diminishing returns to education there. The success of the Levi Strauss’s ML bootcamp indicates that those who hold a college degree are less necessary to work in jobs requiring certain analytical skills. Thus, our finding about the presence of the technology deskilling efect of AI confirms that this is not a singular case but an overall trend. How should the current higher education system respond to the risk of losing relevance for the majority of the workforce except for a handful of elites? Universities and colleges need to redesign their curriculum to focus on preparing future staf to work with AI. We need to identify the key characteristics, traits, skills, and qualities needed to make the partnership between human workers and AI a success. The list probably should include being agile and adaptive as well. We also need to rethink how and where higher education should take place. Closer interactions with practitioners, more deeply intertwined programs with industry, and learning-by-doing in the real world outside the college campus may be crucial. Knowledge and skill updates and life-long learning may become the new norm of education.

Policy-makers have a major role to play to lead this historic transition and transformation of the human labor force into the age of AI. While firms and organizations will be in the forefront of retraining or uptraining their workers and transforming their labor and organizational structure accordingly, the government can encourage such actions with financial incentives and tax breaks. It should also make retraining and uptraining resources available to those who don’t have alternative sources to turn to. One step further that policy-makers could consider is passing regulations that require firms to provide employee retraining and uptraining programs before they are replaced or let go if a change in skill requirements occurs. To best adapt to the imaginable and unimaginable future that AI might bring, firms, educators, and policy-makers need work closely together, and focus on growing and maintaining sustainable and agile human capital through life-long learning and training.

## Directions for Future Research

A limitation of our study lies with our selected measure for the key variable, AI applications, which is a proxy based on the count of AI-related terms in firms’ annual reports. The exact proxy used in our analysis is the ratio of AI terms to other terms in the reports. The underlying assumption is that such a ratio of AI terms indicates the relative amount or proportion of managerial attention and resources devoted to AI applications by firms. A potential problem with this proxy measure is that we cannot diferentiate whether an AI term mentioned in the firm’s annual report is for a built-out application or is just an idea to be implemented. It is possible for a firm to brag about their AI applications for products, services, or back-ofice operations to get market attention to support its stock price. This implies the ratios of AI terms in the reports, as our analysis proxies may have been inflated or higher than the number of those implemented.

The real number of AI applications might have been smaller than what the proxy indicated. In that case, the actual magnitude of the efect of AI should have been bigger than what we estimated based on the proxy. For example, the actual efects of AI on labor structure could have been stronger than what our data and analysis show. Thus, by using the proxy, we ran the risk of underestimating the efects of AI applications on labor structure. If we had used a direct measure rather than this proxy, we would not expect the directions of the relationships to change, but the magnitude of the correlations or efects could have become larger. Thus, like previous studies that used a similar proxy to measure technology applications in firms, we didn’t expect a change of direction or the validity of our hypothesis testing with such a proxy to be diminished. Still, there may be an underestimation bias. Thus, it is desirable to use more direct measurements for firms’ AI applications to avoid such biases in future research.

There are several directions for future research to extend or expand the scope of study in this area, especially for empirical studies. For example, our study focused on headcount changes of workers with diferent education levels at the firm level. It will be valuable to study the changes at specific job level to gain further insights on the role of the skill sets. Tasktechnology-fit (TTF) theory [26, 64] that focuses on the fitness of the task for the technology that is used provides a possible framework for such studies. Afordance theory [56, 57], which has been widely used in the IS literature to study organizational and individual adoption of technology, provides another potential perspective for such an investigation. In addition, our sample is limited to established publicly-traded firms. However, the impact of AI on labor structure in start-up firms could be very diferent. One pioneering work in this direction is Schulte-Althof et al. [48] which studies the role of AI in start-ups. Their study sheds light on how AI afects labor productivity and scaling at the firm level.

One future research direction is to investigate AI’s impact on labor structure in start-up firms that is built on their work. Such a study will fill the gap and enrich our understanding on the topics and questions we studied. In particular, during their start-up stage, firms usually have less rigid hierarchy and the workforce often must multi-task and thus is more adaptive and agile than in established firms, so we expect to observe even stronger upward movement of the nonacademically-trained employees in terms of skills and capabilities powered by AI. This should lead to an even stronger technology deskilling efect.

Our study has tackled a complex but important topic: the relationship between AI and firm labor structure. Our findings ofer empirical evidence of the technology deskilling efect of AI applications. However, besides enhancing our general understanding, the findings suggest eforts in academia, practice, and policy- making need to be made to address the implications of such efects of AI on firm labor structure.
