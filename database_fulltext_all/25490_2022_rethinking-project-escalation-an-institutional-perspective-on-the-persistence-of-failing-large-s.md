# Rethinking Project Escalation: An Institutional Perspective on the Persistence of Failing Large-Scale Information System Projects

Nicholas Berente <sup>a</sup>, Carolina Alves de Lima Salge <sup>b</sup>, Venkata K.P. Mallampalli <sup>c</sup>, and Ken Park<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>University of Notre Dame, Mendoza College of Business, Notre Dame, Indiana 46556; <sup>b</sup>University of Georgia, Terry College of Businesss, Athens, Georgia 30602; <sup>c</sup>University of Arkansas, Walton College of Business, Fayetteville, Arkansas 72701; <sup>d</sup>KP Consulting, 1619 Country Walk Trail, Lawrenceville, Georgia 30043

## ABSTRACT

Project escalation involves the continued, persistent commitment to a failing project. Through a qualitative meta-analysis of 15 published cases of large information systems (IS) projects in escalation situations, we develop an institutional perspective on IS projects in escalation situations. This perspective describes how project persistence emerges from a plurality of legitimizing institutional logics that decision-makers draw upon at diferent project stages to maintain and reduce their commitment to the project. Logics related to the project’s approval are not the same logics that guide decisions throughout the project. For example, while we find that innovation and economic logics of return on investment are salient before approval, economic costs tend to be more salient after approval, along with technical impositions and managerial concerns. We further find that managerial logics are particularly salient in reducing commitment to projects, and we detail the diferences and point out contextual triggers of external scrutiny and leadership changes that can contribute to reduced commitment to a project and eventual de-escalation.

## KEYWORDS

IS projects; project escalation; project deescalation; project legitimacy; project commitment; institutional logics; IT projects

## Introduction

Decision-makers often remain committed to investing in a troubled information system (IS) project even when the available evidence indicates that the project is poised for failure. This phenomenon is typically described as “escalation of commitment” – the continued commitment to a course of action despite negative feedback [100]. Escalation is an enduring problem in IS research [4, 61, 62] and organizational scholarship more generally [97, 98]. This persistent commitment to troubled projects is evident across the globe, including, for example, Canada’s “Phoenix Pay” system [5], England’s NHS paperless system [2, 3], Australia’s visa processing system [44], the U.S. Veterans Administration health records system [108], and Ghana’s customs systems [1]. Further, escalation continues to be a problem in “mega” projects [22, 32] that often have significant IS infrastructure components, as well as smaller scale corporate [e.g., 7, 28] and government projects [e.g., 19, 87, 123, 124]. In recent years researchers are finding evidence of escalation in new product development and digital transformation projects [57, 58, 116, 120, 121, 122].

At the heart of escalation of commitment is an apparent paradox [96], wherein presumably, rational decision-makers repeatedly remain committed to failing projects. This paradox has been explained through a variety of theoretical arguments rooted in human cognition, including expectancy theory [101], real options thinking [112], self-justification [100], prospect theory [114], and agency theory [42, 43]. Projects inevitably entail uncertainty involving equivocal information about project status, volatile requirements, and inadequate assessments of benefits [55], and existing explanations ofer a way to explain escalation rationally. Rooted in these explanations, the guidance for decision-makers to avoid escalation situations often involves practices that can enable them to identify, avoid, and exit the trap of unsuccessful projects – essentially avoiding “rationalizing” the continuance of unsuccessful projects [52, 55, 61, 70].

Often implied in this view, although not typically highlighted, is that something else is at play beyond the cognition of singular decision-makers. After all, decision-makers do not act in a vacuum; they have to justify decisions and influence others in ways that make sense in their organizational context [98, 99]. Projects can become taken for granted or “institutionalized” [100] in a way that goes beyond the decisions of individual managers. An institutionalized project has established legitimacy with momentum borne of a commitment from powerful actors [26]. In such situations, sticking with a highly legitimate project would be the norm, regardless of any individual manager’s short-term feedback and decision-making.

This paper emphasizes the importance of IS project legitimacy to reconceptualize escalation as an institutionalization process related to a project under duress. According to this formulation, escalation decisions are not the paradoxical exception associated with failing projects. In contrast, such decisions are the norm once a project is legitimized. This view does not contradict the cognitive views of decision-maker rationality. Rather, it contextualizes this rationality with respect to social legitimacy [99]. We draw upon the institutional logics tradition to develop our perspective [110]. Institutional logics, as a lens, is particularly well suited to linking individual cognition to broader, legitimizing institutions within which that rationality is embedded [9]. Organizational projects of any scale rarely have a single decision maker. Instead, they involve groups who must justify their actions according to some standard. There are typically multiple standards in an organization [9, 66, 80, 99]. In this paper we propose that the standards by which projects are evaluated in organizations are shaped, in part, by the institutions that decision-makers draw upon. Diferent decisionmakers draw on diferent institutional logics [9], and the interplay among these logics shapes the course of IS projects [41]. However, existing research does not distinguish among diferent logics or rationales across which IS projects gain, maintain, and lose legitimacy. Therefore, we ask whether and how diferent institutional logics legitimize decisions regarding project persistence at various points in IS project escalation situations.

We begin to answer this question through a qualitative meta-analysis [8, 75] of case studies that explicitly present escalation situations. Qualitative meta-analyses involve the cross-study synthesis of published qualitative case descriptions by recasting the findings from existing studies through a theoretical lens diferent than those applied by the original authors [75]. We re-analyze published case studies of 15 large-scale IS projects [6, 7, 29, 31, 54, 59, 67-70, 73, 77- 79, 88, 89], and find evidence of multiple, distinct legitimizing institutional logics that decisionmakers use to justify decisions in project contexts: economic, managerial, innovation, and engineering logics. Each logic acts as the standard by which specific decisions can be considered ‘rational.’ Diferent logics gain and lose legitimacy over time, and they take diferent forms in diferent stages of the project. Economic and innovation logics applied to the project are most salient pre-approval, yet managerial and engineering logics are more salient once a project is approved and accepted as legitimate. With this legitimization, IS projects can become largely taken for granted over time, even in the face of significant setbacks. In such situations, contextual triggers such as external scrutiny and leadership can lead to the managerial dynamics that reduce organizational commitment to the project (de-escalation).

## Escalation of Commitment

Escalation situations involve the continued commitment of resources for sustaining failing courses of action [97, 100, 106]. The phenomenon reflects a common problem in many IS projects [54, 56, 89, 112]. A pervasive assumption among the predominant theories that explain escalation is the idea that escalation behavior “runs counter to commonly accepted notions of rationality” [122 p. 313] that promote project abandonment or redirection as the prudent course of action.

Predominant escalation theories (see Table 1) consider psychological antecedents at the individual level and do not capture the dynamic organizational contexts that constitute the decision environments [12, 97, 98, 106, 112]. For example, Keil and colleagues [56] find that constructs from each prevailing escalation theory (self-justification, prospect theory, agency theory, approach-avoidance) significantly predicted escalation behavior across a large sample of IS projects. They point out that these findings suggest that existing escalation theories are best viewed as complementary rather than mutually exclusive [56, 97]. In general, explanations of escalation point to rational action in the context of specific goals, values, limited attention and given a detailed history [95]. But these perspectives do little to explore why organizational actors subjectively value specific projects.

Similarly, researchers describe decision-making in terms of self-interest in some form [43, 100, 114], yet they do little to investigate why some things are in their interest and others are not. Determinants of what individuals find to be in their interest are neither uniform across contexts nor idiosyncratic at an individual level. Organizational and institutional contexts matter to IS projects [8, 41, 111]. But these contexts are not explicitly theorized in escalation research.

There is indeed a rationale by which commitment takes place, but this rationale is embedded in specific contexts. While escalation theories imply this “embedded rationality” [113], escalation researchers foreground the “rationality” portion, while they often background the “embedded” part. Rational decisions are deemed reasonable only in relation to specific organizational cultures and values, socially conditioned norms, and historically (path) dependent situations that individuals draw upon to both arrive at and justify their decisions. All escalation decisions are embedded in a context and shaped by that context. Recently, Sleesman and colleagues [98] argued that characteristics of the milieu in which decisions are made have a substantial efect on the decisions. They suggest looking at context at various levels, from proximal to distal from the individual decision-maker. This approach is helpful to identify additional determinants arising from contextual factors that potentially afect the escalation of commitment. However, this still leaves open the question of how the determinants in diferent levels of context eventually influence the escalation decisions at the individual level.

In this research, we propose a view of project escalation that highlights the social context that does not abandon the psychological foundations of escalation theory. As such, it is not an alternative lens. Rather, in the spirit of Staw & Ross [104], the social lens complements our understanding of the cognition of decision-makers in escalation situations. The rationality of individual decision-makers is shaped by what is considered legitimate in the social context of an IS project. The notion of legitimacy is fundamental to an institutional view of organizational phenomena [103, 107], and the “institutional logics perspective” [110] is a way to tie individual rationality to broader social contexts [35].

## An Institutional View of Project Persistence

Institutional contexts shape IS projects [111], and the notion of institutionalization holds particular relevance for the topic of IS project persistence [54, 70]. Institutionalized systems are largely resistant to non-reinforcing stimuli [27, 28], including performance setbacks, equivocal measures of benefits, and environmental uncertainty. Thus, how projects become institutionalized might yield novel insights into the escalation phenomenon. Prior escalation research has certainly alluded to the notion of project institutionalization and the resulting momentum for commitment. Ross & Staw, for example, note that the failing Shoreham nuclear plant “became larger than the organization itself, involving forces beyond the organization’s boundaries” [86 p. 719], and was linked to the broader corporate vision.

Similarly, Keil [54] indicates that IS projects may become perceived as “sacred cows” that many are reluctant to challenge, even as negative feedback emerges. Others have acknowledged the “administrative inertia” [106] associated with “runaway” IS projects [54, 69].

Montealegre & Keil [70] suggest that commitment to the failing IS project at the Denver International Airport was more challenging to break because the project was institutionalized. The ultimate abandonment of the system resulted from the de-institutionalization of the project.

Thus, classic research on escalation has pointed out how projects are embedded in specific institutional contexts [85] but went little beyond this observation. Recent work in project management has begun to take the institutional context as a focus of study and unpack how institutional requirements shape projects of all sorts [99]. In particular, this research emphasizes how multiple decisions are made to establish and maintain legitimacy for the project, rather than some decontextualized version of rational action [26]. Projects often span institutional fields that have distinct logics, and project leaders somehow reconcile those logics [99]. Undoubtedly, project leaders draw on diferent logics in establishing legitimacy in diferent situations [40]. But there is little understanding of what these logics, specifically, are and when project leaders draw upon them to establish and maintain project legitimacy in escalation situations.

Although escalation is a social phenomenon [29] that can result from the institutionalization of projects [70], escalation research has not embraced institutional thinking beyond this general observation. This observation is not to say that escalation researchers have altogether ignored the potential influence of broader institutional pressures. Several studies, for example, emphasize the social determinants of escalation based on behavior modeling [13, 86] and cultural norms of consistency [54, 86, 106], which evoke the notion of isomorphic pressures underscored in institutional theory [27]. Additionally, several studies have identified multiple social, political, cultural, and environmental factors that may encourage escalation behavior [39, 54, 68, 69, 73]. However, this work has typically reflected an unspecified and rather vague view of the institutional environment, invoking institutional theory to explain the influence of a relatively narrow set of “environmental” or “political” factors not easily accounted for by assumptions of rational decision making. Moreover, the question of what constitutes legitimate action across diferent organizational contexts has not been explicitly addressed.

To link the individual cognition of decision-makers to their social context [35] and thus account for what is deemed legitimate at any point in a project, we adopt an institutional logics perspective [110]. The notion of institutional logics as a theoretical device for explaining decision-making rationality serves two general purposes. First, it allows for the potential identification of distinct logics – each delineated by unique objectives and causal assumptions – that reflect broader contextual influences of organizational environments. Logics serve as a standard set of motivating contingencies that may generalize across a variety of contexts. Second, it provides a basis for systematically exploring how projects become taken for granted and “infused with value beyond the technical requirements at hand” [92], which involves project legitimacy.

The concept of legitimacy lies at the heart of an institutional perspective. It highlights how multiple institutional logics constrain and enable action as referents for legitimate decision-making processes. Stakeholders are more likely to supply resources to enterprises that appear desirable, proper, or appropriate – linking the notions of legitimacy and persistence [103, 107]. Similarly, legitimacy may also influence the credibility of an enterprise by informing stakeholder perceptions of organizational worthiness, meaning, predictability, and trustworthiness [107]. There is no single way to maintain legitimacy; rather, diferent contexts draw on and constitute diferent institutional logics or rationalities for appropriate conduct [35, 110].

Institutional logics reflect the goals, values, and prescriptions of a specific institution. Individuals act rationally according to the logics “embedded in a context of goals and takenfor-granted assumptions that are a situated within a particular institutional context” [9 p. 5]. Institutional logics have two key dimensions [35, 109]. They act as organizing principles that guide activities, and thus embody the goals and values of the institution. Institutional logics are rooted in assumptions of specific causal means-end relationships. In the next section, we apply institutional logics as a framework for exploring the context of project persistence.

## Research Methodology

To explore how institutional logics are implicated in escalation decisions in IS projects, we examined 15 published case studies about commitment to troubled IS projects, and ofer a reinterpretation of the social context shaping project persistence for each (see Appendix).

Qualitative Meta-Analysis: Because of the widespread growth in qualitative research in recent decades, there has been increased attention to the value of analyzing published, qualitative studies as primary data for research studies across various scholarly fields [25, 81, 90]. Existing studies can be re-interpreted, and this interpretation can be synthesized with analyses of other published case studies [75]. The practice of using published qualitative descriptions as primary data for a new study has been described in many ways [25, 47, 75, 115]. We use the term “qualitative meta-analysis” in a way that is consistent with recent IS and organizational scholarship [8, 81]. This approach involves synthesizing the data portion of published studies in a way that does not need to be consistent with original study’s findings. Diferent published qualitative studies use diferent theoretical lenses to make sense of phenomena. The idea is not to attempt to reconcile their findings in terms of their conceptualization – this would be a traditional “aggregative” literature review that looks to preserve the findings of diferent case studies and generalize them through some synthesis [47]. In this research, since escalation research draws on various theoretical perspectives, we are not proposing to integrate the diferent perspectives in the literature. Instead, we foreground the social context that many studies use for background, reconstructing an interpretation of the data to build theory.

Qualitative meta-analysis can also be distinguished from traditional, quantitative metaanalysis [8]. Quantitative meta-analysis takes the constructs, methods, measures, and theoretical statements of previous studies into consideration to reinterpret results with each other [48]. In contrast, a qualitative meta-analysis involves original interpretations of detailed descriptions within published qualitative research not to integrate the findings of those studies, but rather the descriptions embedded in their write-ups [8]. Therefore, in our sampling procedure, we required thorough descriptions of the cases, including quotations directly from informants, to avoid misinterpreting the interpretations of the original study.

Three principles delineate qualitative meta-analyses from other forms of inquiry [75]. First, qualitative meta-analyses explicitly attend to case contexts rather than abstracting away from the context. Second, qualitative case studies must be synthesized with each other, using theoretical frames that are not necessarily related to the framing of the original researchers. In this way, interpretations can be standardized across case studies that use diferent theoretical framing. Third, the cases should be theoretically sampled for generalization to specific cases [90], enabling higher-order explanations. Sampling involves the pragmatic constraint of adequate presentation of data for reinterpretation. In a qualitative meta-analysis, the inclusion of additional cases is not always beneficial. Rather, what is helpful is the careful theoretically motivated sampling of cases with the bounds of inquiry and the contexts of generalization in mind [75].

Theoretical Sampling: We sampled various IS projects with these three principles in mind: context, translation, theoretical sampling. We began with the Web of Science Core collection to search for a pool of potential articles in the “basket<sup>1</sup>” of IS journals according to the Association for Information Systems (European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information Systems Research, Journal of the AIS, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly). We used combinations of the following search terms: “escalation,” or “commitment,” or “de-escalation,” or “escalation of commitment,” or “de-escalation of commitment,” or “persistence.” We also went backward by reviewing the citations of articles we initially identified to supplement our electronic search [119]. We used three selection criteria: (1) qualitative case studies that reported on escalation or deescalation of commitment; (2) where a case description was available together with interview quotes; and (3) the unit of analysis was the project. We conducted the original analysis on nine of the cases and added another six cases for the final round of coding and further validation. The final sample set yielded 15 unique cases from 14 diferent studies conducted between 1995 and 2012. All ofer longitudinal and rich data presentation describing new IT development projects involving escalation situations [102]. Ten of the cases (CONFIG, Centco, Alpha1, Taurus, Gamma, E-PRO, Alpha2, SmartCard, HRO, WTRS) were published as examples of escalation. Four (Middlebank, UKC, VUE, and DIA) illustrated deescalation. One case (QRS) tackled both escalation and de-escalation. Table 2 describes the sample. The cases involve large-scale organizational IS initiatives and a variety of project dynamics and outcomes.

## Coding & Synthesis Procedure

Consistent with qualitative meta-analysis conventions, we coded the data iteratively [75]. We began our analysis by coding each case independently. Figure 1 provides a summary. Note that each phase involved multiple rounds of coding.

Early in the analysis, we identified how diferent decisions seemed to use diferent standards by which they judged appropriate action (Coding Phase 1). We initially opencoded passages reflecting how stakeholders justified their project decisions. We looked to make sense of these standards through comparison with the literature. Here, we identified Townley’s [113] “rationalities” and the institutional logics perspective [109] to make sense of these rationalities (the two views are consistent with each other). We arrived at four distinct logics through an iterative process that involved multiple rounds. We found three that Townley [113] identified (i.e., economic, managerial, technical), but we also found a fourth that we describe as the innovation logic (Table 2). This is consistent with the logics that Hansen & Baroody [41] identified, indicating that these logics may be more generally applicable.

We then sought to clarify the relationship between the observed logics and project escalation (Coding Phase 2). We identified diferent phases in the lifecycle and found that diferent logics vary in their salience in diferent phases. We also noted that projects took on another character before and after approval, indicating that project approval likely impacts the standard for legitimate decisions around project persistence.

Next, we re-coded the data to look for patterns and relationships across the logics by focusing on decisions made in each phase (Coding Phase 3). This re-coding of the data began with the authors independently coding one case and then meeting to revise coding strategies, discuss results, and plan for the next case. Through this approach, we created and revised our coding strategy by cross-checking everyone’s code several times in face-to-face meetings to assure construct validity. During this analysis, along with a constant comparison with the literature, a variety of codes emerged. Following Staw & Ross [104], we coded relevant information regarding project phase (preapproval, post-approval), commitment decision (create, maintain, revise, reduce, redirect, or terminate), and feedback cycle (promise of future, receipt of questionable, receipt of negative, and receipt of highly negative)<sup>2</sup> We also coded contingent factors of each project (duration, system, and project type) and each decision (e.g., change in management at decision X in project Y). During this phase, we noted the distinction between public and private projects; leadership changes; external scrutiny and conflict; and IT imposition and deficiency (rooted in [106]). We noted “modal” distinctions in diferent economic and engineering logics we identified early and late in the lifecycle (e.g., economic as an emphasis on project return vs. project cost; engineering dealing with an imposition vs. deficiency).

Since much of our coding and many ideas emerged throughout this process, two authors started re-coding all the cases together (i.e., “pair coding” - Coding Phase 4). After identifying first-order codes, we clustered them into higher-order themes to compare respective categories (second-order codes). Here, we continued with our iterative and paired approach to coding, frequently meeting with the rest of the team to make sense of the emerging findings. When synthesizing the codes, we abstracted to those where we identified cross-case regularities. In the final stage of the analysis, we revisited the data from a theoretical perspective. Here, we traced linkages between logics and other themes, such as project context, to transform the static concepts we identified (four distinct logics) into a dynamic process model of project persistence. We used paragraph descriptions to synthesize the various cases by juxtaposing them in the process of de/escalation incorporating the four distinct logics motivating each project decision (see Appendix). We translated the cases into one another by comparing the logics in a specific project with the logics in the other projects using multiple visualizations, culminating in a cross-case diagram (Figure 2 below). ## Findings

We identified decisions across the two general stages in the project lifecycle delineated by key changes in the actions, attitudes, and perceptions of project stakeholders to understand what contributed to project legitimacy at diferent points in time. In general, the pre-approval stage includes the initial creation of commitment through an issue or opportunity identification, culminating in support of the project among key decision-makers and approval (indicating legitimacy). Following formal approval, the post-approval continuation phase reflects the sequence of iterative decision events involving discrete responses to repeated project feedback. These decisions afect how project stakeholders perceive their respective domains of activity, which in turn influences the mechanisms underlying project persistence. Some later decisions resulted in a reduction of commitment through project termination or redirection (de-escalation). Findings are organized as follows. First, we describe each logic and provide reference literature to aid in distinguishing the logics and linking them to appropriate reference institutions (Table 3). Then we identify broad patterns of institutional salience to illustrate the ebb and flow of standards for legitimacy throughout the projects. We conclude with contextual issues.

## Institutional Logics

We focus on the key dimensions of respective goals and assumptions regarding causal means-ends relationships from our analysis. Additional evidence for their occurrences, including example forms or “modes” in which the logics were manifest across the 15 cases, are provided in the Appendix. Note that the four logics emerged through constant comparison of case data with relevant literature – thus, we include key citations linking each logic to its institutional referent.

Economic Eficiency. We found evidence of the influence of the institutional logic of economic eficiency in all cases. This logic reflects decision rationality based on economic utility maximization [113]. Economic eficiency foregrounds cost as the standard by which decisions are made. It is rooted in scientific management and industrial capitalism. Industrial capitalism implies that eficiency is the foundational locus of competition [18]. For projects, this form of rationality was evident in two modes. Economic eficiency was often a fundamental concern used to justify the project and typically took the form of promised or experienced cost reduction. For example, the “highly favorable” results of early project feasibility studies at Centco indicated that the project was “expected to have a large payof” [73 p. 37], and these expectations appeared to have been instrumental for project approval despite resistance from users. In other situations, economic concerns were manifest through attention to cost overruns. The emphasis was no longer on the return on the investment but the cost of the investments themselves.

Managerial Rationalism. The cases also suggest the influence of the logic of managerial rationalism, which subsumes principles of rule-governed behavior, bureaucratic authority, and jurisdictional claims [113]. Although recent work in organizational research often conflates managerial rationalism and economic eficiency logics [e.g., 3, 20], our study identifies distinct diferences between the two. Economic eficiency is rooted in marketbased capitalism, whereas managerial rationalism emphasizes bureaucracy, rules, and decision rights [113]. Following the classic work by Danziger and associates [23], we use the logic of managerial rationalism to reflect the Weberian bureaucracy as manifest in organizational hierarchy and structure [118]. The logic of managerial rationality was manifest predominantly through diferent modes of authority, including decision rights of formal role authority and jurisdictional claims (who should have power over the project) and various issues around standards or rules for project control.

Innovation. A third logic relevant to project persistence in our cases focuses on innovation. Innovation requires diferent organizational structures and cultures than eficiency or managerially oriented goals [14, 60]. Innovative activity entails uncertainty and unpredictability, longer time horizons, and is often labor-intensive – all requiring a greater tolerance for risk and indeterminacy [46]. The reference institution is “entrepreneurial” capitalism [91]. The project at UKC, for example, was driven by a “strong desire” among members of the council to be “the first local council in the UK to purchase goods and services electronically” [79 p. 10]. This motivation appeared to create project momentum despite emerging problems related to system functionality. Decision makers draw on an innovation logic [41] when they look to generate novelty with IS projects.

Engineering or Technical Rationality. The fourth institutional logic influencing project persistence among the cases is the logic of technical rationality [113], which emphasizes project completion by leveraging the capabilities of internal and external technology professionals (e.g., users, developers, project leaders, consultants, etc.), rooted in the logic of the engineering profession. It could be manifest specifically in terms of the professional logic of IS engineers [41, 82] or a more general problem-solving logic [95]. According to this engineering logic, the imperative to deliver a working system is driven by confidence in technology-based solutions, and it often conflicts with cost eficiency and functionality considerations. This logic manifested as a response to either deficiencies or impositions of the system [106].

## Patterns across Cases

For all cases, every project decision (pre-approval, post-approval maintain commitment, or post-approval reduce commitment) was justified in a manner consistent with one or more of the institutional logics we identified. Decision-makers drew on a variety of logics across the same project in describing decisions. What is particularly interesting is that in every instance, the decision logics used to justify the project were not those drawn upon making decisions once the project was a going concern. The logics drawn upon during the project were, in turn, not the logics used to reduce commitment to those projects that were deescalated. Evidence of this is summarized in Figure 2 and detailed through the coding in the appendices.

We did identify patterns across the projects. Notably, decision-makers drew on logics diferently before and after approval. Also, logics identified to maintain commitment were diferent than those associated with reducing commitment. Next, we address these patterns.

Decision Patterns and Economic Logics. The logic of economic eficiency was prevalent in many of the cases throughout the projects. Still, the project’s cost savings and return-oninvestment potential were the specific modes of the economic logic early in the project. These were involved in decisions associated with generating commitment to the project. Mähring et al. [69] exemplify this by discussing the cost reduction rationale at VUE:

“In addition to saving taxpayer money by streamlining university administration, the new system would provide a means for evaluating institutional performance, which ultimately could be linked to financial incentives” [69 p. 470]

The flip side of cost reduction is a return on investment, and the economic rationale for approval decisions at Centco involved a large project payof.

“Blake, the president, decided that the potential benefits justified the risk of buying a system that was not yet working. It was implicitly believed that the system would help tum the problematic materials management situation around . . . [a] crucial [project determinant] here [was its] large payof” [89 p. 37]

The economic logic also became salient after approval but markedly diferent from preapproval, in general. Where pre-approval the economic logic tended to emphasize returns to the organization in the future, post-approval decisions involved in an economic logic that emphasized costs of the project in the present. As illustrated by Middlebank:

“At two steering committee meetings, the project manager presented substantial increases in project cost figures (projected cost was now €15 million, three times the initial estimate and twice the approved budget). Doubts increased . . . concerns were now quite widespread in the organization. As a result, the steering committee decided to review and reassess the project” [68 p. 252]

In many of the cases, decision-makers invoked substantial increases in project financial costs to legitimize a reduction in commitment. Table 4 summarizes these findings with examples.

Decision Patterns and Innovation Logic. In addition to the economic logic, we identified multiple instances of the innovation logic, a rationale formed by novelty-based advantage, in the pre-approval phase – particularly for public projects to create commitment and get formal approval from public institutions (UKC & DIA). Consider the following quote from Pan et al. [79] as an example, where they discuss the origins of the UKC case:

“The idea of e-government originates from the UK central government’s 1999 white paper, Modernizing Government, which . . . committed the government to the ‘use of new technology to meet the needs of citizens and business and not trail behind technology development’. . . . strong desire to be the first local council in the United Kingdom to purchase goods and services electronically” [79 p. 9]

The “strong desire to be first” and “modernizing government” encapsulate the logic of innovation that invokes the goal of novelty for legitimizing a project. DIA was similar:

“The initial project design did not incorporate an airport-wide [IT-based] baggage handling system . . . the project’s top managers began to recognize the potential benefits of [such] system. As a result, [they] develop specifications for [it] and the city sent out a request for a proposal . . . to develop the most complex baggage handling system ever built . . . In April 1992, after viewing a prototype of the proposed system, Denver oficials awarded BAE a \$175.6 million contract to build the system for the entire airport” [70 p. 423]

While the rationale for building “the most complex baggage handling system ever” was not used to legitimize the construction of the Denver airport from its very beginning, such innovation logic was involved in project approval decisions, soon after managers recognized the novelty-based advantages of an IT-based baggage handling system. Although the innovation logic was used to gain approval for the project and make decisions (i.e., hire a vendor or build a system), this logic did not appear to be used in decisions to maintain or reduce commitment.

Decision Patterns and Engineering Logic. Interestingly, innovation was not the main logic used to justify private projects in this sample. Instead, an engineering logic formed an important rationale for legitimizing private IS projects. The delivery of a working system was the goal, and technical concerns associated with existing legacy systems were a powerful motivator. Mähring & Keil [69], referring to how the Middlebank project was framed, illustrate this.

“ . . . the bank’s existing legacy deposit system was increasingly dificult to maintain and there were mounting concerns that it would malfunction as a result of the patchwork of modules added to the system over a 10-year period. As a result, a feasibility study for a NDS was initiated . . . During the feasibility study, concerns regarding the legacy system were elevated . . . These circumstances reinforced the idea that a NDS was a necessity” [69 p. 249]

The above statement shows that, in order to legitimize the project at Middlebank, technical problems were “elevated.” These problems were also used as an engineering/technical logic to legitimize the development of new IT systems at CONFIG and Alpha1:

In these cases, projects continue in many diferent ways. In some cases, decision-makers justified project continuance by increasing control and monitoring of established practices. Other cases persisted through role authority. Managerial rationalism was particularly implicated in decisions to reduce commitment to projects. Of course, all cases involved negative information regarding high costs and technical problems – they were all escalation studies, after all. But some involved decisions to reduce rather than maintain commitment. We identified two contextual situations that were “triggers” of managerial action to reduce commitment: external criticism and a change in management structures. Both situations acted as catalysts for managers to exert their decision rights to reduce commitment to a previously deemed legitimate project.

By whatever measures a project may be judged, high-profile criticism from outside the project team seemed particularly relevant for mobilizing action leading to decisions away from a continued commitment to the project (e.g., Middlebank, DIA, and UKC cases). This criticism comes from outside the established project team – from a diferent area within the organization itself or outside the organization. Criticism outside of the organization is particularly salient in public projects, though there is evidence of external criticism in private projects (e.g., external consultants). In all cases of criticism outside the organization, the scrutiny starts after the project is approved and underway, usually associated with negative information about the project in the public domain. For example, in the case of the VUE and DIA projects, the external criticism from the media was salient after negative information about the projects was exposed.

“Consequently, the auditor general’s report prompted considerable public attention and highly negative press” [69 p. 471]

In the VUE project, the external criticism was directed at the project’s cost, and the decision taken in response was correspondingly justified based on economic rationality logic.

“Through this measure, future costs for VUE were removed from the ministry’s budget, although VUE still had to report to the ministry. Additional funding would have to come from the university institutions and, in turn, institutions would be allowed to withdraw from VUE” [69 p. 471]

With the DIA baggage handling system project, the criticism focused on technical deficiencies.

The decision to delay the opening of the airport was justified using engineering rationality logic.

“Clearly, the automated baggage system now underway at DIA is not yet at a level that meets the requirements of the city, the airlines, or the traveling public,” the mayor stated. “There is only one thing worse than not opening DIA [and] that is opening the airport and then having to shut it down because the [baggage] system doesn’t work”” [70 p. 424]

In the case of Middlebank, this criticism came from sources internal to the organization, but external to the project - in part, from “intense pressure from the CEO-to-be to curtail overall IT costs,” and also from an oversight committee:

“ . . . committee members questioned whether it was at all viable to proceed with the project as currently planned” [68 p. 253]

Since the criticism in the Middlebank case was focused on project planning, the responses utilized managerial rationality for justifying project continuation decisions.

“The project manager rationalized continuation of the project by arguing that project planning had now advanced to a point where further delays were unlikely and that there was now “light at the end of the tunnel.” He also presented a newly developed plan designed to reduce system migration risks” [68 p. 253]

In Centco, the criticism was external to the project, yet it was not uniform in the organization:

“Graham, the VP of Operations, criticized the decision to some extent but was overruled by Blake, the president” [73 p. 37]

Criticism often focused on costs and technical issues, but the decisions for de-escalation inevitably involved restructuring, reporting changes, or other managerial action. In Centco, this external scrutiny was in part triggered by the organization’s financial situation as a whole. With a downturn in their business, diferent units began jockeying for scarce resources. In doing so, they made jurisdictional claims – in the case of Centco, MIS claimed ownership of the project. In response to external criticism of projects, managerial rationalism was the dominant logic driving de-escalation.

Another frequent contextual element in escalation situations involved project leadership changes. Whether voluntary or involuntary, leadership changes were often associated with project de-escalation based on managerial rationality by the assertion of role authority of the new leader. At Centco, the hiring of a new CEO and other top managers was associated with dramatic changes in the organization of the project. New managers replaced project leads:

“ . . . in January 1988, Bob appointed Trevor from marketing as the new project manager . . . the earlier VP, Richard, and several others had been removed from the company” [73 p. 41]

With the change in personnel came a dramatic change in the project.

“Upon assuming project leadership, the first thing Trevor did was to cancel all previous commitments, saying that the whole thing had to be rejustified with a new business case” [73 p. 41]

In the VUE and DIA cases, the leadership changes were subsequently followed by highlighting the negative information about the project. In both situations, strong proponents of the projects were no longer associated with them.

## Discussion

IS project escalation is a perennial issue that remains a vibrant domain for research. Consistent with the long-established research tradition into escalation, recent IS work emphasizes the cognitive issues associated with escalation decisions [4, 61, 63-65]. Although this tradition acknowledges the importance of external, institutional contexts in shaping escalation decisions [50, 70], the institutional context has largely gone untheorized in terms of its impact on escalation decisions. Our research is an initial step toward unpacking this institutional context in escalation decisions. Findings from this investigation are not conclusive but suggest how we can enhance research into escalation through an institutional lens. There are four main ways that this institutional approach contributes to work on IS project escalation. This approach can help to: (1) identify diferent logics at play as standards for legitimacy; (2) point out that these diferent logics are more or less salient at diferent points of a project; (3) show how maintenance of commitment to a legitimate project is the norm, or default, not some irrational exception; and (4) identify contextual triggers that reduce commitment to a project. Table 7 summarizes these four contributions. Next, we briefly discuss each and the implications for future research.

Multiple Logics: Consistent with past studies examining the role of multiple institutional logics and information systems in organizations [9, 41], we find evidence that each organization represented in our focal cases may be similarly characterized as pluralistic. All of the organizations examined here were influenced by logics consistent with rational economic principles such as eficiency, utility, and accountability. At the same time, other logics influenced project activity, including those related to political maneuvering, administrative authority, managerial persistence, engineering or technical rationality, and innovation. The interplay of multiple logics was apparent throughout each of the projects, from inception to eventual outcome. It is not a question of whether project commitment decisions are rational or not; rather, it is a question of by what standard, or logic, is a decision made.

The argument that escalation behavior is not inherently irrational is not new [11]; this assumption has implicitly driven the development of escalation theory for decades. Our institutional perspective contributes to this body of research by providing an approach for clearly distinguishing among the alternative, legitimate modes of rationality – embedded in the institutional logics that are legitimate in context – that potentially extend the life of projects despite emerging threats. We have long understood that escalation decisions are embedded in the institutional context of projects [85], but only recently has research pointed out that these institutional contexts can be quite pluralistic – particularly for project organizations [99]. Project leaders draw on diferent decision-making approaches based on what is deemed legitimate in a particular context [26, 40]. This study extends this work by drawing on institutional logics to situate decisions in escalation situations to reference institutions. We identify how general, identifiable logics are referents for legitimate decisions in a project. Future research can use this perspective to unpack further decisions related to project commitment to link specific types of decisions to specific logics. Our meta-analysis hints at how diferent logics matter diferently and are drawn upon for diferent decisions, but these findings are only suggestive. Future research can unpack this relationship more thoroughly to explore the relationship between specific decisions and the legitimating logics of those decisions.

Logic Salience: A key finding of this research involves the shift in standards of legitimacy throughout IS projects. We propose that these decisions are not a result of some universal rationality but instead reflect the ebb and flow of diferent legitimizing factors that change throughout the project lifecycle. What is a legitimate standard for making decisions about a project before approval – before it becomes taken-forgranted or institutionalized – is diferent than legitimate standards after approval. Formal project approval indicated that the overall project was a legitimate approach for addressing organizational goals. Following approval, the overall project adopts a taken-for-granted character, and the project components, in terms of specific technologies, become the focus of the activity of the project. In the cases examined, technical issues of the project overall emphasized the deficiencies of the old legacy situation before it was approved and on the new system’s deficiencies and impositions on how they do things once the project was taken for granted. Before approval, economic concerns were focused on the value generated, afterward the costs associated with the project. Before approval the innovativeness of the solution was often the focus, but afterwards managerial coordination among units and managers, jurisdictional disputes, and questions of authority were the focus. Early phases were characterized by eforts to reconcile diferences with respect to the choice of technology, development approach, and project management practices. After approval, decision-makers need to make it work and live with it. Once the project is deemed as legitimate, the focus moves from “theorizing” about the project – or envisioning what it will do overall – to “translating” the system to its organizational context [74]. This figure-ground (gestalt) shift is reminiscent of how Boland & Pondy [10] reconciled multiple perspectives of organizational activity. Specific logics are foregrounded while others are backgrounded, and this shift appears to occur as the project becomes increasingly taken-for-granted.

Diferent views of legitimacy may be more relevant at diferent stages of the project. Our institutional perspective of project commitment suggests that the process by which projects become legitimate creates a general climate of persistence that is facilitated by a distinct shift in project decision domains. However, as indicated above, this work is quite speculative and general in the way we link logics to decisions. Future research should unpack this relationship between the logics and the decisions to understand when and why diferent logics become more salient for decision-makers.

Persistence as the Norm: By conceiving IS projects through an institutional lens, we highlight how their taken-for-granted character shapes legitimate rationality around the project. Once it is legitimized, project participants do not question the project overall; rather, they assume it will persist. Persistence is not some irrational anomaly, but instead, the norm. Project persistence becomes a habit of human cognition. Once it is in the organizational budget, the project becomes part of the planning and accounting routines in the organization, which are a big part of the cognition of organizations [72].

In their seminal work, DiMaggio & Powell [27] indicate that institutionalization can be identified by formal approvals, increased interaction, information processing requirements, and mutual agreement in a common endeavor among those involved in an organization. Project participants appeared to meet these criteria as the project was generally underway. Selznick [92] had a two-stage institutionalization process that describes the emergence of a formal structure followed by the “thick institutionalization” of this formalized practice. The emergence of formal structure “provides an ‘institutional’ solution to problems of economy and coordination” [92 p. 235], which explains why economic logics and deficiencies of existing systems were important early. This process is followed by “thick institutionalization” resulting from, among other things, the further sanctioning of rules and procedures, the development of vested interests, and intensifying commitment to unifying principles. This thickening was accomplished through a logic of managerial rationalism, cost concerns, and technical fixes.

In the end, this observation of project persistence as the norm, rather than some unclear anomaly that must be explained, questions assumptions inherent in so much research on escalation. All else equal, an institutional perspective would start from the assumption that commitment to legitimate projects will persist as the default. Any reduction of this commitment would be an anomalous situation that would need to be explained. It is a subtle shift, but a critical one, nonetheless. The key question for future research is how do existing cognitive explanations change given the starting assumption of persistence?

## External Triggers

Once an IS project has gained legitimacy, is taken for granted, and the default becomes persistent, how do decision-makers reduce their commitment to a project? A legitimate project has a tremendous tailwind, and de-escalating is no small feat. Our qualitative metaanalysis indicates that it is not through some change of mind or cognitive discipline that inspires decision-makers to reduce their commitment to a project. Instead, there appear to be factors external to the project – in its institutional context – that trigger managerial action that reduces commitment. That these triggers originate outside of the project organization is perhaps not a major surprise once we adopt the assumption of persistence as the norm. Those close to the project by default do not tend to question the legitimacy of the project overall. External organizations such as the media or other departments bring attention to the issue and inspire project participants and other decision-makers stop to reflect on the project [55, 61]. Once the project is legitimate, questioning this legitimacy takes tremendous efort for those operating within the project – the external criticism must be clear and incontrovertible [98]. Together with external scrutiny and criticism are actual impositions the system puts on users. These impositions are felt by outsiders, such as users and other stakeholders. Typically, users are not full-on members of a project, and technical features may cause them to encourage this outside scrutiny.

Similarly, new leaders are notorious for changing personnel and reshaping organizational priorities to make their mark on the organization [38]. If project leaders depart, new leaders within the project or overseeing it may bring it under scrutiny. Diferent leaders may have conflicting views of appropriate action- they may draw upon diferent logics [93]. Triggers associated with external scrutiny are consistent with work on deinstitutionalization, which focuses on political, technical, and social antecedents [76]. This view emphasizes the contextual elements of IS projects and therefore complements the existing work on IS project de-escalation that emphasizes individual decision-making of managers within the project [55, 61, 62]. Future research can do more to identify, categorize and trace the causal paths of such external triggers.

Together these contributions question some assumptions in IS project escalation research and potentially contribute to work on project management more broadly, particularly institutional perspectives. Recently, scholars have pointed out that so much of the work on project management has historically focused on the managerial dynamics within a project. Still, they generally fail to unpack how the institutional environment matters significantly to project outcomes [99]. Further, research that does attend to this context points out that large projects, almost by definition, span a plurality of institutional orders, and project participants need to navigate this plurality to build the legitimacy to act [26, 40]. Our institutional perspective on project persistence contributes to this emerging perspective in unpacking how decision-making is made in reference to legitimating logics, which are more or less salient throughout the project.

Further, the context of IS projects is potentially important to institutional research more broadly. In this digital age, organizations of all sorts are undergoing digital transformations. Digital transformation involves fundamental, institutional change that is ushered in through large-scale projects [45]. The institutional perspective that we propose is a potential foundation for understanding the institutional change that marks contemporary digital transformation projects.

## Implications for Practice

Implications for practice associated with existing research on project escalation essentially focus on providing tactics for managers to identify, avoid, and exit the escalation trap [52, 55, 61, 70, 73]. This advice advocates for better risk assessment, project management, tracking, and structuring, and sensitizes decision-makers to the likelihood of escalation situations. Existing guidance is most certainly sound advice, and it is not the goal of this study to refute any of this. Instead, the practical implications of this study highlight the social grounds for legitimacy in diferent phases of a project. As such, the goal is to understand how to establish legitimacy for projects. Certain appeals drive diferent decisions at diferent stages of a lifecycle. Judging by our cases, novelty, economic return, and technical deficiencies of legacy situations are powerful means for establishing legitimacy for projects. Once a project is deemed legitimate, projects shift to economic costs, improve technical deficiencies, and increase project controls. Cases of project de-escalation will likely stem from leadership changes and external scrutiny. Our observations on de-escalation are consistent with existing guidance that emphasizes external scrutiny, such as consultants and task forces [70]. De-legitimization of a project can benefit from scrutiny outside of the project organization. This external scrutiny can act as a catalyst or trigger for the project changes. With our focus on legitimacy, however, we extend this guidance by emphasizing the notion of legitimacy and the diferent logics that are at play.

Specifically, our interpretations of the data suggest that, following the shift in the decision domain described earlier, tactical decisions aimed at problem-solving at the project component level can insulate the overall project from abandonment. As such, past research may inadvertently conflate the overall project with its component elements. Further, given that guidance generally advises managers to continually re-evaluate projects [106], in practice, this may involve continuously re-evaluating components or organization of the project, rather than project existence overall. This distinction between components and the overall project is a distinction relevant to understanding the scope of decision-making in institutionalized projects.

The question for practice then becomes a focus on context, and the logics that can be used to counter the legitimacy of runaway projects. These cases seem to show that by focusing on costs and impositions of the system, decision-making can move to be more critical of a project. But this is not enough. These rationales appear to be intermediated by powerful institutional actors that exert jurisdiction over the project and draw on the authority of their institutionalized roles. The managerial rationalism evident throughout all the projects post approval, points out how it is not simply decision-making cognition but also power dynamics around what constitutes legitimate authority over the project. Institutional theory can thus potentially help practitioners to understand the broader sources of persistence and change that influence their projects. The institutional conditions of organizational activity have decades of foundational work on these sorts of broader dynamics [27, 35, 92]. Practitioners would be well advised to attend to this tradition to truly understand their project’s external forces.

Also, it is important to note how so many large scale public IS projects are represented in both our sample [29, 31, 69, 70, 78, 79] and in the popular press with recent projects around the world [1, 2, 5, 19, 20, 44, 87, 108, 123, 124]. Government environments involve particularly pluralistic institutional contexts [9, 125] and therefore one would expect a perspective informed by institutional theory to be particularly relevant for public projects.

However, our research shows that private organizational projects appear to be just as pluralistic as the public projects over time, and they also show evidence of variety of diferent logics at play for creating, maintaining, and reducing commitment over the course of projects. This indicates that the institutional lens is broadly applicable.

## Limitations

It is important to note that this work is an initial, tentative institutional approach to IS project persistence. Our interpretations are essentially our constructions of a reasonable institutional explanation from data reported in the literature. This explanation is not a strong confirmation of our view. However, we chose only studies with detailed descriptions, so there is indeed data supporting our assertions. But, in the end, the explanations are speculative. Our research inherits the fundamental limitation of qualitative meta-analysis that has been described as the triple hermeneutic [8]. Organizational actors make sense of events and report them to researchers; researchers make sense of the explanations and selectively report the results of this research. We analyze these explanations to construct our explanation.

Nevertheless, this approach is justifiable given a broader reach – we include more case narratives in our sample than is reasonable for individual qualitative studies – as well as in detail – we attend primarily to quoted accounts in these studies. Thus, we establish some validity in our constructions by synthesizing cases with each other, and we can capitalize on the rich case narratives prevalent in the literature [75]. Future research might look outside of the academic journals for case studies into project escalation decisions to extend this meta-analysis.

Consistent with past research on escalation, the 15 cases emphasize decision events initiated in response to repeated negative feedback well into the project. In comparison, the data capturing early phases of project development are relatively limited. However, we believe that the diversity of contexts strengthens the generalizability of our conclusions compared to single case studies. The rich narratives, diverse contexts, and overall scope of our sample cases have provided a suitable canvas for theory generation; future research oriented toward an institutional perspective can clarify, extend, and test our initial eforts.

In particular, understanding decision-making in the diferent phases of a project is important, but we could not unpack these case narratives across the traditional PMBOK phases. We attempted to do this, but our sample’s case narratives did not provide enough information to do so. It would make sense to explore how the planning and executing of specific projects can potentially account for diferences in the patterns after approval further along with the execution. Still, it was not possible to bracket project phases in each case to be normalized across cases; identifying before and after approval was the best we could do.

Another limitation involves the timing of the sample. All the cases come from before 2015 – we could not find any rich case descriptions of IS project escalation published in the basket journals in recent years. This shortcoming does not mean that escalation is out of flavor – quite the contrary, there is still a vibrant discourse into project escalation using various methods in the IS field [e.g., 4, 61, 62, 64, 96, 98] and in organizational research in general, including, for example, work into corporate social responsibility [33], firm strategy and operations [84, 117], and distributed teams [71]. In particular escalation became an important topic in new product development [58, 116, 120]and research into infrastructural mega-projects [22, 32]. However, there is less qualitative, case-based IS research. This lack of qualitative work means that it is unclear whether contemporary digital innovation and transformation projects face similar dynamics. Recent studies do show that digital transformation projects do show evidence of persistence in the face of significant challenges [e.g., 121] and some are reporting that challenges are only now becoming realized in relatively nascent digital transformation projects [57]. To understand how escalation plays out in digital transformation contexts is an empirical question, but our institutional framework can potentially help with this.

Further, it stands to reason those contemporary approaches involving agile methodologies such as DevOps may impact escalation situations in some way. None of the papers in our sample expressly attend to the development methodology (i.e., agile or waterfall), although we might expect that more recent cases in our sample likely adopted more agile approaches. Existing research into agile development does indicate that agile projects do appear susceptible to escalation situations [30, 105], but there is virtually no empirical work in this regard. It is important to note that agile is not a single practice, it is a loose collection of practices that organizations adopt and tailor to their local contexts [16, 17, 49, 83]. Existing work simply does not attend to how the way that organizations adopt agile practices impacts commitment to troubled projects. Certainly agile practices may have some impact on commitment to troubled projects, but the research is silent on this. Future research should look to understanding how the institutional context interacts with agile development practices to influence project escalation situations.

## Conclusion

Traditional views of project escalation might view project issues such as high costs and technical problems as negative feedback about the project for decision-makers. These strong negative signals should kill the project at a certain point, but sometimes they do not. When they do not, we typically refer to this as an escalation situation. Our institutional perspective of project persistence does not substitute for such views but instead complements them by pointing out how social legitimacy factors into such situations. We reconceptualize escalation as an institutional process associated with IS projects under duress and describe mechanisms that maintain legitimacy despite the obvious challenges to the project. By emphasizing legitimacy and how decision rationalities are embedded in legitimizing logics, we explain how project commitment comes about and how the persistence of this commitment appears to be linked to the legitimizing logics in the institutional context. Decision-makers draw on diferent legitimizing logics in diferent phases of a project. Once projects are deemed legitimate, a diferent process to reduce commitment must unfold to accompany any de-escalation eforts. To bring about de-escalation, project legitimacy cannot be upheld – it needs to be compromised somehow. For highly legitimate projects, continued commitment is not the exception but the rule. In such cases, the continued commitment that we sometimes call escalation is not necessarily the result of some error of decision-making but is indeed the perfectly reasonable, default course of action.
