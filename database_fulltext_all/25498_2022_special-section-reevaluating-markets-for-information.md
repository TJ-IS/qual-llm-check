# Special Section: Reevaluating Markets for Information

Robert J. Kaufman<sup>a,b</sup> and Thomas A. Weber<sup>c</sup>, Guest Editors

<sup>a</sup>Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School, Frederiksberg, Denmark; <sup>b</sup>Singapore Management University, Singapore; <sup>c</sup>École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne, Lausanne, Switzerland

## Guest Editors’ Introduction

As the use of information as a productive and tradeable asset becomes more pervasive, its— often unintended—side-efects start showing. This prompts us to systematically recognize these efects and, once identified, to think about how to use them to our advantage or else mitigate them as much as may be economically and/or socially desirable. In this Special Section, we have assembled three research papers, which take a look at diferent interesting instances of this question, namely in the context of regulating peer-to-peer sharing markets, the substitution of knowledge workers (or their skills) by artificial intelligence (AI), and the dificulties in appropriating rents from information goods that can be pirated by its very consumers.

The deep infusion of information-based innovation into the manufacturing and service sectors of global economies and their electronic markets has continued apace over the past decade, resulting in efects that are both natural and expected. They include: information technology (IT)-induced price adjustments in computer retail [44]; manufacturing productivity and output quality gains [42]; new forms of business-network business-value cocreation [33]; a persistent global disaggregation of information-intensive services [3]; longanticipated shifts in the boundaries of large and small firms [26]; and flexible, digital supplychain sourcing in an increasingly borderless world of production and operations management [22]. In addition, we have witnessed changes in inter-firm collaboration via outsourced intermediate production [25] as well as how manufacturing plants [7] have leveraged process outsourcing to enhance their performance compared to their competitors.

Regarding unintended side-efects of the proliferation of information, a major emphasis in recent research has been on fake news, its externalities, and how to mitigate them [17, 35]. Researchers have also recognized that, given the extraordinary extent of fake news on the Internet, some “people believe what they want to believe [even] when it makes no sense at all” [41, p. 143]. The research has grown to encompass many diferent issues: the groundswell emergence of fake news on IT platforms; how it often spreads faster than real news; its dark-side efects in retail markets; how it can negatively shape consumer opinions about products and citizen views about political elections, and influence the debates on freedom of speech, gender, and racial equity; the selection of government agency, ministry, and judicial appointees; and social perspectives on resource sustainability in the wake of climate change. (See European Parliament [18] for an example.)

There also are Sector-related social-media communication anomalies that have resulted in biased information sharing. For example, there are now information-communication phenomena in financial markets that provide a new basis for irrational exuberance [49] and the paradox of rational irrationality [4, 12]—as well as irrational rationality [36]. An example is the GameStop stock meme on Reddit and the social-media-influenced stock price run-ups and short squeeze in January and February 2022 [45]. Such instances—increasingly observed—are changing the way we view the occasionally false wisdom of crowds, at least in the investment services and financial market context [14]. The conclusion many have drawn is that the abundance of information available today undoubtedly is a powerful force, both positive and negative in its efects, influencing consumer and producer decision-making and the behavioral patterns they exhibit [48]. Simultaneously though, it has afected the theory-based logic of firmlevel strategy formulation and our assumptions about what constitutes individual identity, social civility, and collective action [15].

This Special Section of the Journal of Management Information Systems showcases new empirical and theoretical research that informs the thinking and approaches, by academic researchers, industry professionals, and policy makers, toward peer-to-peer markets, machine intelligence, and the dificulties of capturing the value of goods that are entirely based on information. It also considers how to best channel their beneficial efects to create and appropriate value for the firm in services [31] and elsewhere when sector templates define the division of labor, asset complementarities, and factor mobility all support co-specialized production [30]. The research articles we developed for publication demonstrate why Economics and Social Science research on technology impacts is vibrant, insightful, and can even be counterintuitive in its contributions to the information systems (IS) literature. This is true for those of us who are involved in crafting their scientific underpinnings and balancing the rigor of the inquiry with the relevance of what can be learned. Emerging from the new digital ecosystems of technological innovation is the impetus for strategy and technology observers to determine its efects with existing theories, while they seek to move the theoretical foundations of the IS discipline to higher and less explored ground. The articles are intended to ofer insights to academics and advice to practitioners in several afected sectors. The empirical findings and theoretical perspectives that the authors present also deliver outcomes that will inform future decisions on sustainably generating rents and welfare from informational assets related to the peer-to-peer sharing economy, the substitution of knowledge workers by AI, and the selling of information goods in the presence of piracy.

## Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Rentals

We open with an empirical research article, entitled “P2P Rentals, Regulatory Policies, and Hosts’ Cost Pass-Throughs,” authored by Michelle Müller, Jürgen Neumann, and Dennis Kundisch. Their work focuses on peer-to-peer (P2P) rental services in New Orleans and other cities in the United States. The scholarship related to sharing economy issues and research directions, and the services ofered by Gig Economy firms have developed rapidly in the past decade [28, 34]. Numerous studies have contributed new theory and interpretations of how the P2P sharing economy has changed market demand for product and asset ownership, how dynamic pricing for economic transactions works in this service sector, and how the consequent impacts on consumer surplus and profit have been developing [57, 58].

Recent research has suggested that there are important bring-to-market costs for rental properties (e.g., rental property preparation, relevant labor and maintenance costs, and transactions costs) that support income generation which non-commercial hosts may not consider, but which nonetheless influence the returns on their property investments [16, 19]. There has been investigation of alternative regulatory approaches for P2P rentals in diferent kinds of cities, too, including liberal, moderate, moderate-collaborative, and protective cities.

● Liberal city regulation ofers generous regulatory frameworks, often due to Airbnb’s lobbying eforts as the key digital intermediary. It has been essential in creating a beneficial regime to support the intermediaries, enhancing their power in the local marketplace through control of rental and revenue data, and strengthening their economic and managerial positioning [50].

● Moderate city regulation typically indicates a willingness to work with residencesharing intermediaries, but only after the regulator studies how P2P rentals will afect the local housing market. Then, they will they establish regulatory restrictions (for purchases/sales and short-term rentals) and introduce covenants for tax rates and structure, as well as data monitoring or collection.

● Moderate-collaborative cities initially work with digital intermediaries that are contemplating new market entry, as the cities seek to bring the benefits that the digital intermediaries can ofer to their local markets. Later, they often modify the applicable rules as the local P2P rental market takes shape, and negotiations are required to properly apportion the generated market surplus.

● Finally, protective cities, which are often large and attractive for new market intermediation, maintain order in their housing markets through strict regulations, thus forcing the business-to-business (B2B) rental service intermediaries to adjust the way they seek to be profitable.

The authors’ quasi-experimental (QE) empirical research examines a large dataset of Airbnb P2P rental listings in New Orleans. The city enacted a ban on P2P rentals in specific neighborhoods near the city-center, along with regulatory restrictions on what property types and for how long owners could rent them. In addition, fee-based mandatory licensing was implemented, representing a new bring-to-market (BTM) cost for owners. New Orleans, in this research, is a treated moderate-collaborative city, where stricter regulations were newly promulgated. Data from New York, San Francisco, and Portland, in contrast, were used to jointly assess these control cities, where no new P2P rental regulations had been implemented. The authors use propensity score matching (PSM) so their econometric estimation could support causal inference for the tests of several hypotheses based on statistically-matched sampled listings [29].

Several surprising results were obtained. For example, owners of city-center listings, in spite of the municipality’s implementation of mandatory licensing fees, still were able to pass on some of their costs to P2P renters, who were not deterred by the fee-loaded, higher rental costs. Meanwhile, property owners reduced their prices in lower-demand areas, creating a new fork in their road to higher average property-level profitability, and achieved \$245 higher revenue on average over the first seven months. The authors concluded that new neighborhood-specific fees and city-level P2P rental regulations to promote afordable housing require more careful consideration of urban property and the efects for this service sector’s business than were previously recognized were necessary [8].

The authors also reported diferences in the responses of private and commercial hosts. They observed that commercial hosts passed on 100% of the new fees to their P2P rental guests, while commercial suppliers with multiple city-center listings did so only partially. They further point out that:

. . . commercial hosts behave mostly as predicted by economic principles, by adjusting their prices more frequently than non-commercial hosts in response to fluctuations in demand and supply. Non-commercial hosts are more likely to be subject to behavioral constraints, as they mostly act as inexperienced microentrepreneurs who lack economic knowledge about appropriate price setting behavior.

## And conclude:

our results suggest that in real-world market settings, where neither the supply-side nor the demand-side responds completely inelastically, the microeconomic model proposed in the literature (by Filippas et al. [19]) may not be directly applicable to non-commercial hosts.

The results of this research inform urban policymakers about the causal efects of regulation on P2P rentals and open up the possibility of more targeted attention toward conducting policy analytics research [32, 39].

## AI and Labor Substitution

A major point of discussion related to the growth of investments in AI is that, as a generalpurpose technology (GPT), AI is a major force of technological innovation leading to business-process disruption and the transformation of competition in the industry sectors it most impacts [24]. As Schumpeter [47] argued, economic growth is driven by the random occurrence over time of innovation, which results in firms and industry-sector evolution through a process of creative destruction, while making some forms of past industrial organization and interfirm agency relationships irrelevant [2, 15]. This perspective is in parallel with the writing of leading authors who have studied issues related to the impact of AI technology to weaken the contributions of human labor, reduce the tax base on workers replaced by robots, and impact the equality of workers in human society [53].

Numerous authors have reminded us that “[a]utomation concerns are not new to AI, and examples date back even to the advent of written language. In ancient Greece (ca. 370 BC), Plato’s Phaedrus described how writing would displace human memory and reading would substitute true knowledge with mere data” [20]. And “as with many technological developments throughout history, the advancement of AI has created fears that human workers will become obsolete” [51]. Yet, Acemoglu and Restrepo [1, p. 4] have taken a contrasting viewpoint:

Interpretation of the history of technology and a diferent way of thinking about the future of work—as a race between automation and new, labor-intensive tasks. Labor demand has not increased steadily over the last two centuries because of technologies that have made labor more productive in everything. Rather, many new technologies have sought to eliminate labor from tasks in which it previously specialized. All the same, labour has benefited from advances in technology because other technologies have simultaneously enabled the introduction of new labor-intensive tasks. These new tasks have done more than just reinstate labor as a central input into the production process; they have also played a vital role in productivity growth.

The future of work (e.g., job displacement, proactive steps that firms can take with retraining their employees, and the responsibility of educational institutions to be responsive to the change) is recognized now as being in the hands of organizations that operate on the front lines of ongoing change [38].

The second article in this Special Section reflects the actions of not one AI firm, but instead 1,387 publicly-traded companies in the People’s Republic of China from 2007 to 2018. The coauthors, Mei Xue, Xing Cao, Xu Feng, Bin Gu, and Yongjie Zhang, studied the links between AI application deployment in firms and the extent to which their labor structure changed. In 2021, the size of China’s AI market was already worth \$23.3 billion (¥150 billion) and is forecast to grow to \$61.9 billion (¥400 billion) by 2025 with further growth to \$154.6 trillion (¥1 trillion) by 2030 led by the government’s programs [37]. It will be a leading force for beneficial industry transformation, but “the pace in which AI will replace jobs will only accelerate, impacting the highly trained and poorly educated alike” [55, p. 3]. The economic and political consequences of such massive changes are likely to be enormous and unstoppable [60], and well justify the efort to study them. This is why the empirical baselines for changes in the skill intensity of labor in the presence of AI are important to track and compare against in the future.

The present research focuses on documenting and explaining the shifting intrafirm shares of employees who obtained formal university or college education or did not have such training. The authors used software to implement embedded-word analysis after segmenting, cleaning, and labeling terms and then counted the AI terms and created a ratio measure for them relative to all AI-related terms in the full text of annual reports for all the firms. This then enabled the authors to segment the resulting data to gauge the ratio of AI terms in the annual reports of firms in diferent industry subsectors. This supported their efort to conduct an initial econometric estimation of all data, then a reduced dataset estimation after removal of firms with missing data, and finally matchedsample estimation to address endogeneity through the use of PSM. This approach is frequently used in computational social science (CSS) [13] to support causal inference, as other articles in this journal have demonstrated [27, 59].

The authors report that they found evidence for a causal link in the presence of higherintensity AI investments proxied by the estimated AI applications of individual firms with their overall employment as well as higher employment of nonacademically-trained workers. However, their estimations suggested that academically-trained employees had lost out a bit, with relatively lower employment shares observed. They attribute their finding to technology-enabled deskilling theory, which is the opposite to the predicted labor share outcomes of the more traditional U-shaped job polarization efects theory [11, 21]. It explains why structural changes in firm-level workforces are diferently subject to changing demand for college graduates when the AI and robotic technologies they work with side-by-side are ever more capable and able to substitute for human knowledge. The research demonstrates the importance of new theory in IS to understand the underlying mechanisms at work.

## Digital Piracy and Bundling

The study of pricing and bundling of information goods has represented an important stream of research in IS [5, 6], economics [46], marketing [52], and strategy [23]. IS authors have studied pure bundling [54], mixed bundling [9], and customized bundling [10], among others, to improve the extraction of consumer surplus in the presence of heterogeneous valuations for diferent products, especially when those valuations exhibit a negative correlation. They typically would for diferent components of a productivity software package that includes word processing and data analysis, for example, as a function of the users’ particular preoccupations. Individuals in an organization who write a lot of text do not analyze a lot of data and vice versa, creating similar cumulative valuations for the bundle of both components, thus providing a profitable bundling opportunity.

In the past twenty years, however, new complications and “dark practices” in the market have increasingly challenged information goods vendors and strategists – as well as the economies in which they operate. The Organisation of Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) [43] reported that counterfeiting and piracy led to surprisingly large magnitude losses—estimated at \$200 bn in 2005, but by 2017, the global situation had become far grimmer.

In 2016 trade in counterfeit and pirated products amounted to as much as \$509 bn. This represent[ed] up to 3.3% of world trade. The share of fakes in world trade grew significantly. In 2013, up to 2.5% of world trade was in fake goods (up to \$461 bn). This growth was reported during a period of a relative slowdown in overall world trade. The intensity of counterfeiting is on the rise – big risk for the knowledge-based, open, and globalized economy. [43]

Moreover, Forbes reported in 2019 on digital entertainment goods:

The movie and TV production industry is an important aspect of the U.S. economy, . . . supporting 927,000 jobs and bringing in \$229 bn in revenue . . . A new report from the U.S. Chamber of Commerce’s Global Innovation Policy Center and NERA Economic Consulting has found that global piracy of movies and TV shows is threatening the industry’s growth and costing the U.S. economy at least \$29.2 bn in lost revenue every year. That is a conservative estimate and the research states that actual losses could be as high as \$71 bn annually. [40]

While the above reported piracy-induced losses may somewhat overstate economic reality, in the sense that much of the “lost sales” would have never occurred at the full sticker prices, it is clear that the problem presents a significant economic challenge to the firms whose intellectual property rights are being infringed upon. In this broad context, the final article of this Special Section examines “Piracy and Bundling of Information Goods,” authored by Chen Jin, Chengguang Allen Wu, and Atanu Lahiri. Their research opens a new direction for theoretical and empirical research contributions on best practices for information goods bundling, as the world becomes ever more susceptible to large-scale information and digital piracy. The incentive that criminals have today is a digital analog of a 1930s Great Depression line attributed to Willie Sutton, an infamous bank robber: “Why rob banks? Because that’s where the money is.” The authors ofer a diferent viewpoint, namely that big money has become available through the Internet due to the increasing sophistication of digital hacking and piracy.

For many years, the authors note, bundling has been viewed

as an efective pricing strategy for zero-marginal-cost information goods. Yet, in many information-goods markets, the efectiveness of bundling remains hard to ascertain. This is because information goods exhibit other characteristics as well, which can potentially interfere with a manufacturer’s bundling decision. For instance, they are also prone to piracy, and it is not obvious what impact, if any, piracy can have on the eficacy of bundling.

Piracy, they argue, has diminished the attractiveness of bundling because it may in fact increase a consumer’s incentive to engage in piracy, thus annihilating the traditional bundling benefits of homogenizing valuation diferences. This holds especially true when the illegal information products closely resemble those sold legally and the expected cost of being caught in the act of piracy is neither too high (so individuals prefer pirating just their preferred product) nor too low (so profits with and without bundling are similarly poor).

The authors’ results are developed as a function of product similarity (amounting to a valuation discount factor between 0 and 1) and (expected per-product) piracy cost, which provides a somewhat new perspective, as the set of parameters where bundling dominates becomes nonconvex—due to the newly discovered “wedge” of intermediate piracy costs and suficiently high product similarity. There unbundling leads to higher profits, for it limits the incentives of illegal copying. Based on a thorough analysis of the two-product base case, the authors examine a few interesting generalizations, including an extension to more than two products, endogenous piracy cost (with lobbying for enforcement), and interdependent (correlated) consumer valuations, which establishes that the findings are largely robust across these various scenarios. An interesting, empirically testable implication of the authors’ theoretical analysis is whether diferent enforcement regimes, which result in diferences of piracy cost, do result in the nuanced prevalence (or not) of product bundles on the local markets for information goods as it would be predicted by the model.
