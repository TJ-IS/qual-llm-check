# Digitalization of Loyalty: Impacts of Mobile Technology on Reward Redemption and Engagement Level

Yoonseock Son <sup>a</sup> and Wonseok Oh <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame, Notre Dame, IN, USA; <sup>b</sup>School of Business, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, Seoul, Korea

## ABSTRACT

Although digitalization is a prevalent strategy in reward programs, a minimal amount is known regarding the relationship between reward app usage and reward redemption behaviors. The enhanced availability and accessibility of information via mobile app can alter customers’ reward-redemption patterns and engagement level, highlighting the need for firms to adjust their reward-based service strategies. Using unique datasets that reflect individual consumers’ transactional and reward redemption behaviors, we conducted a series of analyses to capture the dynamics in the latent engagement state transitions of customers according to the type of reward program used. Results show that although reward app usage, on average, induces a more active engagement state, it polarizes the volatility of engagement state transitions. Furthermore, we find that greater volatility of engagement state transitions is significantly associated with a higher likelihood of churn. Finally, a follow-up survey and panel vector autoregression analyses are conducted to uncover the mechanisms that underlie the relationship. The results provide insights into how retailers can strategically design their reward programs in the emerging mobile-based omnichannel environment.

## KEYWORDS

Mobile apps; customer loyalty; reward redemption; customer engagement; reward programs; customer churn; online rewards

## Introduction

Reward programs (RPs) allow consumers to earn monetary incentives (e.g., reward points), which can be redeemed for future purchases. In recent years, such programs have become an essential part of firms’ management strategies for enhancing customer fidelity and increasing profitability. RPs can accelerate purchases and enable retailers to effectively understand customer behaviors and preferences [14]. In turn, these gains can be further leveraged to formulate effective customer acquisition and retention strategies [19]. Although RPs vary in several aspects, such as membership requirements and program communication, they share the primary intention of guaranteeing customers a wide range of services as well as stable retail management.

Recently, the interface between customers and firms has fundamentally changed as reward service deployment has shifted from nondigital channels to digital alternatives, ushering disruptive innovations into the retail environment [48]. As mobile devices are known for their incomparable ubiquity in temporal and geographical dimensions [3], mobile platforms have become a key driver of successful omnichannel retailing, with such digital programs integrating customer experiences in both online and offline channels [29]. The proliferation of mobile apps has likewise afforded retailers unprecedented opportunities to obtain insights that cannot be derived from traditional marketing environments [2, 33].

Given that reward points serve as economic and psychological incentives for customers to repeat their purchases [27, 46, 50], monitoring and understanding reward redemption patterns, given the switch to mobile platforms, enable firms to cultivate the ability to assess the extent of customer engagement over time [55]. This is especially critical because using mobile apps to check and redeem rewards may change the dynamics of customers’ engagement behaviors [48]. For instance, customers may purchase more actively and frequently as the use of mobile apps is associated with habit formation. Likewise, an appbased RP facilitates consumers to interact more actively with a firm and control how much information they reveal in their apps. Prior studies have punctuated the significance of vendor relationship management in the mobile technology environment, where consumers, rather than firms, manage bilateral customer-firm relationships [23, 44]. Reward apps may also inspire customer stickiness by reinforcing engagement through perceptions of high value and excellent brands and firm images [21].

Another favorable window open to both companies and consumers is the portability of mobile devices which can, among other advantages, facilitate active participation in mobile reward activities instead of limiting involvement to passive participation [24]. Such shifts in the RP paradigm are beneficial to both consumers and firms, given that increased engagement with a firm ensues from increased information accessibility. As a result, mobile technology has broadened the spectrum of customer engagement from a mere transactionbased occupation to an engagement-based activity (e.g., reward redemptions), in which the emotional bond between customers and firms is a critical factor. Nevertheless, the convenience and information accessibility made possible by app-based RPs may also engender negative outcomes (e.g., [48]). These emergent challenges highlight the need for firms to grasp the opportunities provided by mobile technology while acknowledging its potential drawbacks to avoid such problems.

Despite the immense potential benefits of such endeavors, academics and managers have paid limited attention to the use of mobile-driven RPs as an avenue through which firms can augment their understanding of customer engagement behaviors. Furthermore, in-app function usage distinguishes mobile app-based operation systems from conventional alternatives and is also highly related to reward redemptions [48]. Yet, limited research has illuminated customer relationship dynamics with respect to reward redemption behaviors. Further explorations are needed to thoroughly comprehend how mobile app usage affects consumers’ engagement levels.

To address these issues, we theoretically explore how the decrease in customers’ transaction costs from mobile app usage subsequently induces changes in their perceptions of rewards. For empirical validation, we acquire data on customers’ transactional and reward redemption behaviors from a large food and beverage service company in Asia with a wellestablished RP.

A challenge in investigating customers’ reward redemption behaviors and engagement levels is that they are mostly motivated by unobservable incentives [50]. To tackle the issue, we first employ a Latent Model (LM) to assess the relationship between the customer engagement level and the channel of RPs used while capturing the unobservable dynamics.

The latent Markov models are appropriate for examining the evolution of individuals’ behaviors over time through state transitions when their characteristic is not directly observable [5]. Furthermore, the measurement errors and time-varying unobserved heterogeneity are accounted for in the model, together with the effect of observable covariates on the response variables [40]. If a relationship exists between app-based RPs and customers reward redemption behaviors, our next interest lies in what the behavior change means to firms. Based on the state transition patterns from LM, we examine the relationship between consumers’ varying states and their churning propensities.

Our main results reveal that using a reward app, on average, increases customer engagement levels. However, reward app usage is also associated with more volatile engagement levels,<sup>1</sup> which in turn, may increase uncertainty in customer behaviors. The findings also suggest that reward app users with greater volatility of engagement levels tend to exhibit a significantly higher likelihood of churning. Hence, mobile apps afford firms unparalleled opportunities to capture customer engagement. At the same time, however, they can also place the firm’s retail efforts at risk, given the tendency to reward app users toward more unstable engagement levels. To extend our understanding of mobile app users’ volatile state transition behaviors, we conducted a follow-up survey to elucidate customers’ differential perceptions of reward points in accordance with the medium used. Furthermore, we also employed a panel vector autoregression (PVAR) model to examine how customers’ mobile in-app function usages are interrelated with their point redemption behaviors.

This study contributes to the growing body of literature on managing digital technology in reward redemption behaviors. In particular, it provides insights into the role of technology, in which researchers have overlooked the value of mobile devices in omnichanneldriven RPs that grant unparalleled opportunities for both customers and firms [29]. We also extend the literature by theorizing how the decrease in transaction costs from mobile app usage is associated with a change in consumers’ perception of reward points. Furthermore, we delved into how the adoption of mobile-driven loyalty initiatives affects how patrons interact with brands and make purchases. Although studies on mobile-driven contextual, temporal, and geo-locational targeting (e.g., see Lee et al. [30]) have adequately demonstrated the advantages of mobile platforms, they focused exclusively on particular spontaneous purchase events (e.g., coupon redemption) rather than on sustained efforts to foster customer–firm relationships. Customers were also accorded a passive role in these studies, having limited control over the relationships. Next, we extend existing literature that explores the effect of mobile apps in the RP context, and develop a latent model to delve into the dynamics of the internal state (i.e., engagement level) following reward app adoption. We not only the relationships between reward app usage and customer engagement levels, but also assess how the volatility of the engagement levels changes after reward app usage. Furthermore, we investigate the relationship between the dynamics of the internal states and customer-firm relationship (i.e., customer churn). These results provide important contributions to the literature as most RPs are noncontractual and hence, customer churn is not explicitly observed. Lastly, although the benefits of mobile technologies are well established in the literature (e.g., see Molitor et al. [34]), there is a paucity of understanding regarding the potential business-related hazards posed by such innovations. The digitalization of loyalty may yield countless tangible benefits, but retailers should nevertheless be alerted to potential threats that may adversely affect the consumer-firm relationship.

## Related Literature

## Reward Point Management

Reward point management is an important factor that fundamentally induces customers to perceive benefits [36] and ultimately drives engagement with a focal retailer. Nonetheless, researchers have inquired into the reward redemption mechanism only in relation to shortterm RPs in which members are given a limited time to reach a threshold and redeem a reward (i.e., buy 10 cups of coffee and get a cup free). Studies have explored the interrelationship between reward points and customer behaviors, which are represented as “points pressure mechanisms” [28] and “rewarded-behavior mechanisms” [51]. Kivetz et al. [28] used goal-gradient theory to illustrate that customers conduct themselves in a forward-looking manner and tend to purchase more frequently as they anticipate that they are closer to redeeming a reward. Taylor and Neslin [51] argued that reward redemption reinforces positive learning among customers by linking repurchases to rewards and ultimately increasing subsequent purchases.

Notwithstanding the insights provided by these works, however, only recently have researchers scrutinized reward point redemption behaviors and RP-related effects in a fashion similar to our context [16, 50], wherein customers decide on the timing and extent of redemption carried out. Dorotic et al. [15], for example, scrutinized the interrelationship between reward redemption and purchase and found evidence of an enhancement of purchase behavior pre- and post-adoption, regardless of the amount of reward accumulated. Stourm et al. [50] endeavored to elevate our understanding of why customers stockpile reward points amid the absence of incentives to engage in such conduct. They found that stockpiling behavior is mostly induced by nonmonetary transaction costs and the different valuation allocated to cash and rewards.

Although studies have clarified the mechanism of the redemption behavior itself, a gap remains in the literature with respect to illuminating the value of information on reward redemption behavior in predicting customer engagement [14]. To address this deficiency, our exploration centers on the customer engagement level according to the channel of RP used and customers’ reward redemption behaviors. Our study interrelates customers reward redemption behaviors to customer engagement levels and explores the dynamics of unobservable engagement states. Furthermore, we assess how the changes in customer engagement levels are related to customer churn.

## Mobile Reward Apps in Reward Programs

In nondigital RP schemes, customers are paradoxically regarded as endowed with the ability to make independent decisions, yet also restricted to a passive role in the firm-consumer relationship. Furthermore, although nondigital RP schemes allow retailers to electronically store and access consumer information, they do not lend themselves to interactive communication with patrons or provide customized marketing campaigns in real-time. This status quo is destabilized by reward apps by augmenting the power that consumers possess and consequently molding them into principal decision-makers in the firm-customer relationship. In the mobile environment, consumers take the reins in choosing when and how much to interact with brands. As such, mobile platforms have become a key driver of omnichannel retailing, with such platforms integrating customer experiences in online and offline channels via integrated data resources. The proliferation of mobile devices has likewise afforded retailers unprecedented opportunities to obtain insights that cannot be derived from traditional marketing environments [1].

Extensive studies have attested to the relationship between mobile devices (and apps) and consumer behaviors. The unique characteristics of mobile devices (e.g., portability, interactivity) facilitate high customer engagement [3, 62], leading to increased purchase intention and purchase frequency (e.g., see Bellman et al. [6]). Studies have also provided evidence of the unfavorable effects of mobile app adoption from the company perspective. For instance, Seeger et al. [45] showed that mobile devices negatively affect the accuracy of customers’ decision-making, driving increased product returns, and Son et al. [48] indicated that mobile apps facilitate cherry-picking behaviors of products on promotion and reduce store-specific loyalty.

Nonetheless, there has been limited understanding of the role that mobile apps play RPs and how they influence customers’ reward redemption behaviors. A perplexing issue is that the increasing prevalence of app-based LPs has not stimulated a corresponding comprehension of their business ramifications. The opportunities generated by mobile technologies have not motivated active inquiries into the effects and potential of reward app adoption in relation to RPs. These digital advancements have therefore been an overlooked dimension of relationship management. As reported by Kim et al. [26], reward app adoption increases reward points in RPs. Viswanathan et al. [55] looked into how disengagement in reward apps could have a significant long-term negative effect on purchase behaviors. Furthermore, Son et al. [48] found a positive relationship between reward app adoption and redemption behaviors, and also found evidence of potential adverse consequences such as reward app users’ deal-susceptible behaviors and reduced store-specific loyalty. These initiatives have motivated preliminary explorations of reward apps as one of the avenues through which to engage with customers and enhance customer-firm relationship in the RP context. Provided that reward redemption behaviors carry unobserved thought-processing with them [50], our study extends this nascent stream of research by delving into whether and how reward apps affect customers’ engagement levels and what the changes induced by a reward app imply for service providers. Furthermore, we provide additional insights as to the potential risks that reward apps may engender in the customer-firm relationship. We provide a summary of the comparison between our study and existing literature in Table 1.

## Theoretical Background

## Relationship Between Mobile App Usage and Engagement Level

A connection exists between the psychological distance that people create in relation to a target item (i.e., reward points), which ultimately influences how people think and behave [52, 54]. We argue that features associated with reward apps lower customers transaction costs and consequently alter their perception of rewards. Although point redemption is a critical LP element that induces perceptions of benefits and enhances engagement [36], redemption via conventional initiatives with limited IT features may ineffectively promote consumer interaction. Mobile apps play an essential role in engraving experiences in customers’ minds because of their unique features, such as ubiquity and associated information accessibility [3]. The upshot of these inventive attributes is a change in consumer behavior [48]. In turn, the mobile artifact-driven decrease in search costs eliminates geographical constraints and enhances the ondemand availability of information on specific products and deals both within and across stores [12]. The widened information accessibility and shopping convenience provided by mobile RPs may alter how customers perceive reward points, ultimately affecting their out-of-pocket spending and reward point usage. Mobile devices and apps also contribute to the formation of habit-checking [18]. Such cultivation is associated with reward values, including informational, awareness-related, and interactional [38]. The practice of checking is thus stimulated with greater intensity as customers increasingly access intrinsic mobile features (i.e., interactivity and convenience) [18].

In this respect, we theorize that the denser the information accessibility facilitated by a reward app, the higher the engagement level [16]. Differently put, we submit that the intrinsic features of mobile channels and the interactive components provided by mobile apps will change customers’ perceptions of the value of reward points, thereby enhancing the perceived benefits or the value derived from the rewards and the RP.

Given that reward points serve as economic and psychological incentives for customers to repeat purchases [46], and are highly related to customer engagement [50], understanding reward redemption patterns offers firms the ability to assess the extent of customer engagement over time [46]. Specifically, reward redemption behaviors are known to enhance customers’ perception of the RP’s benefits and enhance their engagement level [36].

In consideration of the increased perceived value of reward points, we expect the perceived fungibility of reward points to increase. Consequently, because customers are likely to appraise cash and reward points as having a similar value as a result of reward app usage, such perception enhances their engagement level. In this respect, we put forward the hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 1: Mobile app usage is associated with higher engagement levels.

When a customer redeems a certain number of rewards, the decline in reward balance may translate to a loss of resources [41]. As individuals are motivated to acquire, retain, and protect their valued resources [22], a natural consumer response to this loss would be to forgo reward redemption in future purchases to restore reward balance. When consumers cash in their accumulated rewards, they feel satisfaction emerging from economic savings. Simultaneously, however, they perceive “affective pains” caused by the loss of accumulated resources. Likewise, when consumers opt to stockpile rather than redeem rewards, they enjoy the sense of accomplishment from resource preservation but at the same time, experience the pain of missing out on discounts.

Such pain differs depending on how visible the depletion of resources is [11]. Given that customers can continuously check their reward apps anywhere and at any time, we anticipate an intensified desire to maintain a certain “threshold” in reward balance [50]. Hence, the psychological threshold of rewards is likely to change with greater volatility due to more active checking behaviors when using the mobile app than when using the nondigital medium. When the reward balance is low, and below a threshold, customers will tend to stockpile rather than redeem points as a means of reaching their desired cutoff. Conversely, they will likely redeem rather than stockpile when the reward balance is high and above a threshold.

Given this relationship between reward redemption behaviors and engagement level, we theorize that the easy accessibility of reward information will drive greater fluctuations in customer engagement levels. In other words, the controllability and accessibility proffered by reward app functions and the presence of psychological balancing thresholds render consumers susceptible to more volatile engagement levels intended to maintain threshold values. By contrast, redemption from conventional initiatives with limited informational features may ineffectively promote consumer engagement. This deficiency stems from the fact that non-digital RPs are established and framed exclusively from firms’ viewpoints, not from those of their patrons. Under nondigital initiatives, therefore, customers are less likely to be exposed to their reward balance due to higher search costs, and tend to discern rewards as secondary to cash as a source of purchase funds, leading to more static engagement levels. Thus, we formulate the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: Mobile app usage is positively associated with volatile customer engagement levels.

## Relationships Between Mobile App Usage and Customer Churns

Although technology reduces customers’ transaction costs, it has different effects on various customer segments, as not all customers are equal [25]. Previous studies have shown that a decrease in transaction costs causes a decline in customer loyalty for certain customer groups and that a reduction in information stickiness [56] from the usage of digital technology channels has a differential impact on customer churn according to a given customer’s engagement level [47].

In an RP context, customers strategically decide on how they make purchases and manage their rewards to maximize the surplus that they generate [14], and such behaviors are further facilitated by the low transaction costs associated with information technology (i.e., mobile) [63]. Importantly, however, a strategic customer approach translates to a significantly diminished perceived value of the benefits acquired from tactical actions [60]. On the basis of the literature, we posit that these volatile customer engagement level transitions reflect the strategic behaviors triggered by mobile app use in customers’ attempts to maximize utility for each purchase decision.

Digital channel usage may render customers more willing to switch to other service providers that offer more favorable conditions [10]. Zhang, et al. [63] introduced the concept of clumpiness, defined as “the degree of nonconformity to equal spacing” (p. 196), as an important metric to enhance the predictive power of customer churn. The basic premise is that while two customers may show identical behaviors in terms of their purchase records (e.g., purchase amount and frequency), their likelihood to churn will differ according to the volatility of their purchase behaviors. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 3: Customer engagement level transitions, resulting from reward app usage, are positively associated with a customer’s churn likelihood.

## Research Setting and Data

## Research Setting

We use individual-level transaction data from a large offline retailer with millions of customers. The retailer’s product portfolio encompasses more than 20 food and beverage brands, and its network comprises more than 18,000 stores nationwide. The firm’s RP was established in August 2008 and the focal RP had about 17.5 million enrolled users and yearly sales of about 40 million USD in 2015. Furthermore, the focal firm launched its reward app under the focal RP in 2014 and was the first to reach 10 million app users among RPs that revolve around food and beverages in the country.

Enrollment in the focal RP incurs no cost to customers. An enrolled customer can register their ID on either the web or mobile app or both; in each registration, a reward card number is allocated from which we could determine a customer’s enrollment date for each channel(s). Customers who opt for web registry are issued a plastic card within a few days, and the card can then be used to accumulate and redeem points. Those who register on the mobile app can accumulate and redeem points through this channel. When a customer enters a store and decides to purchase a basket of product(s), they will need to decide whether to settle the total cost in cash and accumulate reward points in return or redeem the accumulated reward points with a value equivalent to the basket size. If the entire transaction is made with reward points, no points will be accumulated. There are no differential economic incentives across the two mediums in terms of the percentage of cash expenditure accumulated as reward points. Customers enrolled in the RP can earn 5 percent of their expenditure as reward points, which can be redeemed once accumulated points exceed USD \$1 without a redemption cap (i.e., 5 cents worth of reward points accumulated from a \$1 worth purchase). When a customer decides to purchase a basket of product(s), he/ she will need to decide whether to expend the total cost by cash and accumulate reward points in return or redeem the accumulated reward points with equivalent value to the basket size.

## Data

Our dataset consists of consumers’ daily transaction data between October 2014 and December 2014. In the cleansing process, we excluded those who had missing values in their consumer data (i.e., age and gender). Next, we filtered out those who had fewer than three transactions during our data period, as these customers contribute a minimal amount of revenue to the focal retailer. We then obtained a random sample of 10,645 consumers (i.e., 1 percent of the filtered-out sample) who made their first purchase before our data period.<sup>2</sup>

We obtained both the cash and reward transaction data of each customer for each visit. Hence, we know when a customer purchased products, whether they decided to redeem or accumulate the rewards on a transaction, what kind of medium they used to redeem or accumulate the rewards. We also collect customer data such as demographic information and marketing channel opt-in information. Specifically, we use data on an individual’s age, gender, and whether they opted-in for email and Short Message Service (SMS) marketing. ## Methodology

Our objective is to understand the relationships between consumers’ mobile-based reward systems’ usage and their engagement level with the focal reward program. We first employ a propensity score matching approach to alleviate potential bias regarding the self-selection issue. We then delve into the dynamics of customers’ engagement level states according to the usage of the RP channel over time. Lastly, we extend our understanding of the effects of such engagement state transitions on customer churn and retention propensities.

## Propensity Score Matching

As the adoption of the reward apps is not randomized in our setting, our results may be biased due to the self-selection of reward app adoptions. That is, the differences between the reward app adopters and nonadopters may be the primary reason contributing to the different behaviors between the two consumer groups [17, 31, 58]. To mitigate such concern, we conducted propensity score matching (PSM) [43] between the reward app adopters and nonadopters to obtain two groups of consumers that resemble each other, hence, creating a statistical balance between the two groups.

## State Transitions and Unobservable Characteristics in Customer Engagement

This section examines customers’ engagement levels by examining the dynamics of purchase and reward accumulation/redemption decisions over time. To capture the unobserved dynamic changes in customers’ engagement levels, we implement the LM with covariates, which is a subset of latent mixture models. LM is widely used in longitudinal data analysis, where unobservables or latent states are specified as discrete variables [5]<sup>4</sup>. Furthermore, LM accounts for both the time-fixed dependence in the data and the timevarying unobserved heterogeneity, as well as the effect of observable covariates on the outcome variables over time [5].

The LM assumes a latent process that affects the distribution of the response variable and follows a Markov chain with a fixed number of latent states, the outcome of which depends only on the previous state. In the present framework, we employ LM to evaluate the observable outcome variables (i.e., reward redemption behavior and reward app usage) and measure the dynamic latent states in an unobserved latent construct.

Reward redemption is interrelated with customer engagement [50] and both are considered to be decided through unobservable psychological processes. Reward redemption is a critical RP element that induces perceptions of benefits and enhances engagements [36]. For instance, Taylor and Neslin [51] provided evidence that RPs can increase engagement through the point pressure effect, in which customers purchase more to earn rewards. Similarly, Smith and Sparks [46] contended that there is a rewarded behavior effect, wherein customers purchase more after receiving a reward. An important issue for consideration, however, is that a customer’s decision to stockpile (i.e., accrue without redemption) may indicate a deferment of redemption for future purchases or reduced engagement, signaling potential customer churn [20].

Such a relationship between customer engagement and reward redemption allows us to use LM in modeling the effects of reward app usage on customer reward redemption behaviors and, ultimately, on customer engagement, which is an unobservable psychological state. Furthermore, engagement is a dynamic and iterative process that occurs through interactive customer experiences [7]. Hence, we model customer engagement level as the focal unobservable latent state, which stochastically determines the observable reward redemption behaviors and subsequent latent state of customers.

According to customer engagement theory [39], the focus of customer management has evolved from a transaction-based orientation to a relationship-based concentration, with customer engagement at its core. Although no general consensus has emerged regarding the notion of customer engagement, researchers typically regard it as a multidimensional concept that embeds emotional, behavioral, and cognitive attachment within a brand or a company [7]. Customer engagement behavior results from motivational drivers, thus going beyond purchase [53]. Brodie, et al. [7] defined it as heavily dependent on brandinitiated experiences, in conjunction with consequent dynamic and iterative interactions between customers and firms, ultimately captivating customers by satisfying their needs and values. A number of designated engagement consequences are likely to affect subsequent engagement behaviors, reflecting the iterative features of the engagement process [8].

Against this backdrop, we employ the following three categorical response variables: (a) making purchases and redeeming rewards for immediate benefits; (b) making purchases and stockpiling rewards for future benefits; and (c) making no purchases. We use the aforementioned categorical response variables, with each time period representing weekly visits. For instance, assume that a customer belongs to a certain reward redemption state in period $t - l ,$ which is represented by a latent process with an assumption to follow a firstorder Markov chain. Then, based on the type of customer-firm interaction—whether the customer used the mobile app or plastic card—along with the time-constant covariates in time $t ,$ a transition of relationship state in the next period will be determined. Consequently, given the customer engagement state in time t, the customer’s probability of choice at time t +1 will be determined. Thus, a customer can reside in one state at a given time period and make a transition to another unobservable state through an alternative purchasing or reward redemption behavior.

In the present framework, we observe r categorical response variables denoted by $Y _ { j } ^ { t }$ labeled from $0$ to $c _ { j }$ where $j = 1 , . . . , \mathrm { ~ r ~ }$ at the t th customer-firm interaction and where $t = 1 , . . . , \mathrm { T }$ . The response variables are collected in the random vector $Y ^ { t }$ for each interaction. Also, $X ^ { t }$ is a vector of covariates corresponding to $\pmb { Y } ^ { t } \mathrm { a t }$ the t th interaction. We denote the latent process by $\mathbf { U } = ( \mathbf { U } ^ { 1 } , . . . , \mathbf { U } ^ { T } )$ , which affects the distribution of the response variables. Note that local independence is assumed within the process in which response variables are conditionally independent given the latent variable [4].

## Optimal Number of States

An important initial step in LM is to choose an optimal number of latent states. We used the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) on the basis of the following indices [64]: $\mathrm { A I C } = - 2 \hat { l } ( \pmb { \theta } ) + 2 f$ and $B I C = - 2 \hat { l } ( \pmb { \theta } ) + l o g ( n ) f$ where $\hat { l } ( \pmb \theta )$ is the maximum log-likelihood of the model estimated in Equation (W.2), and f denotes the number of free parameters in the model. We compared models with different numbers of states defined a prior and selected the one with the minimum AIC and BIC values. As shown in Table 4, the three-state model exhibits the minimum value requirement, confirming that dynamics potentially underlie the relationship between customer engagement and channel usage.

Table 4 reports the estimated parameters that affect the transition probabilities. As our interest is directed toward channel usage, we focused on the MobileApp parameter. Reward apps offer the advantage of convenience and involvement, and continuously expose users to reward points [48]. In consequence, consumers will be motivated to monitor their reward points via reward apps, which reinforces the positive effects of reward points. Such behavioral changes will ultimately have a positive effect on the average engagement level. Hence, we expect a greater transition probability from a passive or inactive state to an active state when the reward app is used.

The results are shown in Table 5. Note that we used multinomial logits to estimate two models, with the inactive and passive reward redemption state being the baseline for Panels (a) and (b), respectively. The results of these models can be interpreted in relation to the findings of the baseline state. Furthermore, the coefficients of PlasticCard and MobileApp are relative to the baseline category (i.e., nonvisits). As presented in Panels (a) and (b) of Table 6, the usage of mobile apps shows a stronger tendency to switch from inactive to passive, and from passive to active states. This lends support to H1.

Next, Table 7 shows the transition probability matrix used to compare the effects of plastic cards and mobile app usage. As we hypothesized that reward app usage would result in a greater valuation of the reward point due to the intrinsic features of reward apps, customers will experience a greater level of affective pain from the loss of resources. Hence, we expect that reward app usage will facilitate information accessibility and lead to a more volatile engagement state dynamics to maintain the reward point balance above a threshold. The results show that mobile app usage is associated with a higher probability of movement from inactive and passive states to the active state, compared with plastic card usage. However, the mobile app also shows a higher probability of inducing movement from passive to inactive engagement states. Conversely, plastic card usage tends to show a less volatile state transition behavior, with customers occupying the same engagement state as that occupied in the previous time period. Thus, H2 is supported. This finding suggests mobile app users exhibit a high-risk, high-return behavior in terms of the dynamics of customer engagement levels. In the mobile app-driven RP platform, then, highly customized and efficient customer management strategies are critical to capture the benefits allowed by mobile technology.

## State Path Decoding

In this analysis, we decode the sequence of customer engagement states based on the observed data to identify when and how an individual’s state has changed. We first aggregate the individuals into two groups according to the type of reward channels used and assess how the sequence of state transitions for mobile app usage differs from those of plastic card usage [5]. Details of the maximum likelihood estimation and state path decoding are provided in the Online Supplemental Appendix 2.

We then conduct a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and t-test to assess whether there are statistical differences in the state transitions between the two set of transition probabilities in Table 7. In doing so, we operationalized a binary variable that equals 1 if there was a state transition for an individual i at time t, and 0 otherwise. The p-value of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test between the two groups is 0.827 which indicates a dissimilar distribution of state transition between the two groups. Similarly, the t-stats of the difference between state transition incidences between the two groups is 14.79, which also provides support for the idea that the two groups are significantly different in terms of the state transition incidences. Hence, these results provide further support for H2.

## Partial Defection Behavior

In this section, we first examine the distribution of interpurchase time ratio for plastic card users and mobile app users, which is an important factor in operationalizing a partial defection behavior [9]. In a noncontractual setting, as is the case with most RPs, customers can terminate their relationships with a firm at any time without notice. Hence, it cannot be explicitly determined whether customers have churned or have simply become disengaged. To address such ambiguity, the previous literature proposed the idea of a partial defection of customers in terms of interpurchase time [9]. Specifically, the ratio of the standard deviation of interpurchase time to mean interpurchase time was used, with a low ratio indicating higher potential valuation from customers. The literature has also emphasized the value of interpurchase time in defining loyal customers [37], with interpurchase time usually premised to have a proportional relationship with transaction size, especially for regularly purchased products [32].

We formally examine the effects of unstable state transitions on partial defection and how the usage of reward app moderates this relationship. We employ a logistic regression, per the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \ln \left(\frac {\operatorname* {P r} (\text {Partial Defection} _ {i} = 1}{1 - \operatorname* {P r} (\text {Partial Defection} _ {i} = 1)}\right) & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Mobile} _ {i} * \text {State Transition} _ {i} + \beta_ {2} \text {Mobile} _ {i} \\ & + \beta_ {3} \text {State Transition} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\mu} \text {Control} _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

where i denotes each customer. In line with Buckinx and Van den Poel [9] recommendation, we operationalize PartialDefection<sub>i</sub> as a binary variable that equals 1 if a customer’s interpurchase time ratio exceeds the median interpurchase time ratio. As presented in Figure 2, mobile app users tend to show a more dispersed ratio distribution than do plastic card users, implying a higher potential of partial defection.

StateTransition is operationalized by dividing the number of incidences in which there was a transition to a different engagement state for each individual, by the number of weeks in the data period. The denominator includes both incidences in which there was a transition to a different engagement state and those in which the engagement state remained the same. Hence, the variable reflects the magnitude in which an individual experienced a state transition. The variable ranges from 0 to 1, with a higher value indicating higher volatility in regard to transitions to different states. Furthermore, we construct Mobile<sub>i</sub> which is a binary variable that indicates whether a user is a mobile app user or not. We also include the interaction between $M o b i l e _ { i }$ and StateTransition to investigate the moderating role of reward apps. Control represents a vector of timeinvariant individual-level control variables and $\varepsilon _ { i }$ is the error term.

Table 8 shows the results of our logistic regression analyses. We employ a series of models: column (1) consists of the main variables, column (2) adds the interaction term between the main variables, column (3) adds the demographic and customer information as control variables, and column (4) adds the purchase behavior-related control variables. The results of all the models are consistent and suggest that although reward app usages tend to have a lower likelihood of churning, those associated with high state transition volatility tend to show a significantly high likelihood of churning. This result supports H3. Thus, mobile app users’ unstable transition behaviors both benefit and harm the customer-firm relationship, accentuating the demand for more discrete customer relationship management for mobile app users. We also conduct additional analyses with different thresholds in operationalizing the PartialDefection<sub>i</sub> and the results remain qualitatively consistent. The details are provided in the Online Supplemental Appendix 3.

## Additional Analysis

## Additional Survey

We have shown how customers engaged in different mediums of RP show different internal state transition patterns—mobile app users show more volatile state transitions. A potential concern with our previous findings may lie in the underlying psychology of consumers in their perception of reward points depending on the engaged medium, as the models previously employed only use observed behavioral measures to measure customer-firm relationships. Hence, we conduct an online survey to assess our theoretical arguments regarding the following aspects: whether the valuation of the reward point increase for reward app users, as we hypothesize through prior literature, and whether and how the benefits of reward apps (i.e., convenience and involvement) enhance customer engagement level.

## Design

To address such questions, we conducted a survey in which we asked questions related to loyalty points and purchases to the members of the RP in our research context. The survey was conducted in collaboration with a leading market research firm in the country and an affiliate of Gallup International, the leading global research network. Because the quality of Gallup’s unique set of panel data is stringently managed and because it yielded substantial panels that could represent the target population, we found the research firm to be an appropriate partner in our endeavors. The survey was administered online, and we obtained 396 completed questionnaires from respondents who are actual members of the focal RP in this work. Of the 396 respondents, 295 were reward app users and 102 were plastic card users of the focal RP. We provide the descriptive statistics of the sample in the Online Supplemental Appendix 4.

## Results

First, we ask both groups regarding several aspects related to their perception of rewards in the RP. As shown in Table 9, reward app users showed higher awareness of their point inventory and their future purchase intention was more likely to be affected by the reward points than their counterparts, even after controlling for individual-specific information. Thus, our survey provides support to our previous findings that reward app users are more likely to have a higher valuation of the reward point. Furthermore, the reward points reflect mobile app users’ purchase behaviors with a higher degree than plastic card users’ purchase behaviors, which could potentially affect the volatility of customer behaviors. Furthermore, when mobile app users were asked if their interest in loyalty points of the focal retailer had increased after mobile app usage, more than 70 percent of the respondents gave positive answers, while only 4 percent gave negative answers.

Next, we further delve into the psychological aspects of customer engagement via structural equation modeling (SEM) using partial least squares (PLS) [13, 42]. The two pillars of customer engagement are satisfaction and emotional attachment [11], which are moderated by customer involvement and convenience (Figure 3). Following our conceptual framework, we measured the five variables in our follow-up survey and used the constructs with the suggested items. Our main interest in this analysis lies in whether the benefits of reward apps (i.e., convenience and involvement) positively moderate the reward app users customer engagement level. We provide the details of the construct measurements and SEM (e.g., rotated component matrix, psychometric properties, interconstruct correlation) in the Online Supplemental Appendix 3.

## Interrelationship Between In-App Function Usage and Customer Behaviors

Next, to understand how reward app usage can reduce customer churns, we explore how different types of in-app functions are associated with customer behaviors. In doing so, we focus on the consistency of reward timing granted from in-app function usage, which is one of the important causes of habitual IT-mediated state-tracking [18]. Habit formation is known to positively affect customers’ subsequent purchase behaviors, leading to a lower likelihood of churn. We focus on two influential and representative types of apps. The first type features inconsistent reward timing (e.g., promotional information and reward-giving events such as coupons) in which customers do not know in advance when the expected reward will be provided. For instance, customers cannot predict when and which promotions or coupons will be offered by brands. The second type features consistent reward timing (e.g., free rewards after regular check-ins) in which customers are aware of when rewards will be provided when conditions are met. We posit that the degree of consistency of reward timing from engaging with the mobile app is likely to result in different consequences of reward redemption behaviors. To investigate the interrelationship of reward predictability and redemption behavior, we employ panel vector autoregression (PVAR). This approach allows us to treat the variables of interest (i.e., usages of different inapp functions, and reward redemption) as endogenous and to examine the dynamic interdependent feedback among the variables [49].

Our results of the PVAR analyses indicate that use of both in-app function types has differential impacts on reward redemption behaviors. Specifically, functions with inconsistent reward timing are related to less active reward redemption behaviors (i.e., stockpiling points), whereas consistent reward timing is associated with more active redemption behaviors. The findings also indicate that in-app functions with inconsistent reward timing are more effective for customers with greater engagement length (i.e., tenure) and depth (i.e., purchase level). These empirical patterns may provide retailers with insights that would allow them to create more commanding strategic options and operation designs for implementation in in-app-based promotions and reward schemes. Table 11 summarizes our key findings, and the details of the PVAR analyses are provided in the Online Supplemental Appendix 5.

## Implications for Research

Mobile-based RPs have provided retailers with unprecedented opportunities and benefits that are infeasible through non-digital mechanisms. Although consumer engagement is a strategic position that changes infrequently once established, our results suggested that consumer engagement in the mobile era is a phenomenon in flux rather than a stationary occurrence that continually confers strategic and competitive advantages to firms’ operational efforts.

Notwithstanding the availability of extensive literature and the renewed interest in RPs [57], it is difficult to draw inferences regarding customer motivations on the basis of reward redemption and accumulation behaviors. This holds true, for example, in cases wherein customer motivation to redeem and stockpile reward points is driven mostly by unobservable cognitive and psychological incentives [50]. A noteworthy attempt at shedding light on unobservable dynamics is that of Netzer et al. [35], who examined the movement between unobserved relationship states throughout a course of interactions. Nevertheless, these authors overlooked the role of digital technologies in moderating these changes in states, especially in a RP context.

Programs designed to reinforce customer fidelity enables sellers to collect data on individual members and accordingly enhance the quality of understanding of customer visit and spending patterns through the securitization of reward redemption behaviors. Despite the immense potential benefits of such endeavors, however, academics have paid limited attention to the use of reward points as an avenue through which companies can augment their understanding of customer-firm relationships, especially with respect to mobile app-based schemes. Numerous studies (e.g., see Taylor and Neslin [51]) have validated the positive effects of RPs on purchase behaviors and revenues, yet no research has illuminated customer engagement dynamics with regard to reward point redemption behaviors and the informative value of such dynamics in understanding consumer reward redemption behaviors.

This work contributes to the growing literature about the role of mobile apps in the RP context. Prior studies have discussed the positive impacts of reward app adoption in regard to purchase and reward redemption behaviors (e.g., see Son et al. [48]). Our study extends these works by examining how customers’ engagement levels fluctuate after reward app usage and what consequence such behaviors have on customer-firm relationships. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to provide an understanding of the potential negative impacts of reward app adoption and calls for attention to how firms can use reward apps to prevent unfavorable customer behaviors.

Most previous studies have relied primarily on purchase data as grounds for inferring levels of consumer engagement [61]. We deviate from convention by using reward redemption patterns as bases for unraveling dynamic changes in the consumer-firm interface. The results showed that for firms, customers’ reward redemption behaviors could function as important strategic resources for understanding customer behaviors, particularly in the omnichannel environment where mobile-based RP schemes can drive traffic to offline stores and accelerate the purchase cycle.

To sum up, a minimal amount is known about consumers’ redemption choice decisions and the consequent effect on firms, especially on mobile platforms. Stourm et al. [50] argued that cognitive (i.e., nonmonetary transaction costs) and psychological (i.e., differential valuation of points and cash) factors motivate consumers to stockpile rather than redeem points even though the former may be economically detrimental. Chun et al. [14] found that RPs can act as buffers against uncertainty in which the value of points can be affected by firm’s operating performance. Our findings on mobile RP users exhibiting more volatile engagement level transitions can be explained partly by the mobile RP schemedriven reduction in cognitive costs. The nonmonetary transaction costs associated with redeeming rewards are significantly lowered under a mobile RP system. Because mobile payments have become increasingly pervasive alternatives to cash, consumers’ psychological costs that arise from different perceptions regarding cash and rewards may be substantially reduced. Furthermore, the different functions and features unique to mobile RPs can influence consumers’ redemption behaviors. By illuminating these issues, our findings provide a fresh account of how mobile RP systems can influence consumers’ redemption choice behaviors and affect firm’s customer management strategies.

## Implications for Practice

Our results present several important practical implications for managers. To increase consumer engagement, managers should provide incentives to consumers who have yet to enroll in their mobile RP programs. However, because app-based RP users are given abrupt changes in customer engagement level, retailers should carefully monitor their behaviors by keeping track of purchase and reward redemption patterns. Various management strategies should then be offered to consumers whose engagement levels show rapid changes. For instance, special marketing inducements and promotions should be offered to consumers whose engagement states rapidly change as they are likely to eventually churn. Although reward app users are likely to show a more active engagement level on average, retailers should not be deceived by such customer behaviors.

Because customer churn is not explicitly observable in the context of most RPs, RP providers should consistently scrutinize the changes in the reward app users’ engagement level, as well as their interpurchase time with the focal RP. With greater information accessibility facilitated by reward apps, RP providers should be aware that customers are likely to appreciate the value of reward points more and that their reward balance could affect the volatility of their engagement levels. Collectively, despite the greater benefits granted by the digitalized RPs, RP providers need to be cautious of multiple aspects in order to fully capitalize on the technology.

Retailers are likewise advised to frequently observe and analyze consumers’ online activities and leverage their observations in balancing potential redemption/stockpiling disequilibrium. To minimize the damage associated with excessive engagement level changes, retailers can encourage the usage of certain in-app features. Ascertaining whether customers are in the redemption or stockpiling phase presents equally important practical implications, such as identifying price or value seekers, which enables retailers to design enhanced intervention strategies contingent on the type of RP that consumers adopt.

In sum, managers of retailers should be aware that customers who are disengaged from mobile app-based reward schemes are more likely to leave the program than their plastic card-based counterparts. Nonetheless, successful customer marketing in a mobile appbased program will result in a stronger customer relationship and this can be facilitated by utilizing the information on customers’ reward redemption behaviors and engagement levels.

Our study provides valuable insights into the interrelationship between mobile loyalty app usage and reward redemption behavior, but it also has a number of limitations. First, although the focal RP has millions of enrolled customers, we use a relatively small randomized sample for the analyses. Although the randomized sample resembled the customer base, future studies could employ a larger sample size to ensure the robustness of the results. Second, because our context is a non-contractual setting, we could not identify whether customers actually churned or not. Although we used a measure that reflects partial defection behaviors from prior literature, future research can be conducted in a contractual setting or can employ alternative measures of customer churn. Third, our data on in-app functions were limited to two different types of functions. Future research can expand the scope by examining more granular in-app function usage data and detailed individual-level mobile clickstream data. Fourth, our models did not take local store competition (e.g., store density) into consideration. Future work could explore how the effects of reward app usage differ according to the degree of local store competition in a customer’s residential area. Notwithstanding the aforementioned limitations, our study provides important knowledge to managers in an industry that is experiencing radical shifts due to the emergence of mobile innovations and the advent of the omnichannel revolution.
