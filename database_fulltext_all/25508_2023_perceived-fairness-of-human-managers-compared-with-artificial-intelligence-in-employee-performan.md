# Perceived Fairness of Human Managers Compared with Artificial Intelligence in Employee Performance Evaluation

Shaojun (Marco) Qin<sup>a</sup>, Nan Jia<sup>b</sup>, Xueming Luo<sup>a</sup>, Chengcheng Liao<sup>c</sup>, and Ziyao Huang<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Marshall School of Business, University of Southern California, Los Angeles, CA, USA; <sup>c</sup>Business School Sichuan University, Chengdu, P. R. China

## ABSTRACT

Human managers are increasingly challenged by artificial intelligence (AI) technologies in performing managerial functions. We undertook a field experiment that used AI vis-à-vis human managers to perform structured, data-intensive evaluations of employee performance. We generate two sets of insights. First, employees considered AI to be both fairer and more accurate in evaluating their performance than the average human manager. Second, to catch up with AI, human managers’ fairness perceived by employees played a first-order role by (a) helping human managers, to a greater extent than those managers’ evaluation accuracy, to close the performance gap of the employees evaluated by them compared with that of those evaluated by AI, and (b) constraining the efect of human managers’ perceived accuracy of evaluations on employees’ performance. Thus, facing the competition from AI, it is all the more important for human managers to treat employees fairly and build positive interpersonal relationships with employees.

KEYWORDS

Artificial intelligence; employee performance; field experiments; performance evaluation

## Introduction

Rapid technological advancement in organizations, particularly the burgeoning applications of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies in the workplace, has created an urgent need for scholars and practitioners to rethink the future of work and workplace dynamics [7, 9, 17, 21, 35, 57, 62, 75]. Researchers have applauded AI for its strong capabilities in conducting data analysis and making statistical predictions, resulting in higher accuracy in carrying out data-intensive tasks in managing organizations [70] and thus threatening to outperform human managers [2, 43, 46]. Recent research provides evidence of algorithm appreciation wherein humans follow algorithmic advice to a greater extent than human advice [30, 76]. How can human managers catch up with AI at those tasks—or should they even try? Conventional wisdom dictates that human managers need to improve the accuracy of their data analysis (where AI currently has an edge), by either strengthening their own data analytical skills or leveraging the private information that AI does not (yet) have [15, 41]. However, there exist strong reasons to be pessimistic about beating AI at its own “game” of data analytics because of humans’ long-known cognitive limitations in making statistical predictions compared with algorithms [19, 53] and because of continuous technological improvement of AI’s capabilities [45].

Theories on interpersonal dynamics within organizations may shed light on a new path for human managers to compete with AI, at least when performing employee-facing organizational functions. A common goal of managing employees is to increase their job performance, the achievement of which frequently occurs through changing employees behaviors at work. Organizational, psychological, and sociological theories have taught us that humans’ willingness to be influenced by others is determined not only by the “message” itself, but more importantly, by how they feel about the “messenger” [13]. Specifically, fairness, or treating employees without bias or discrimination, critically determines employees’ willingness to accept feedback on their job performance provided by the evaluator. Indeed, perception of the advice giver matters critically to how much individuals are willing to adopt the advice [30]. However, theoretical uncertainty remains over how AI compares to human managers in terms of perceived fairness [69], because of the debate over algorithm aversion [19, 40, 58, 59]. Thus, we lack understanding about whether perceived fairness can indeed constitute a new, feasible path or play a more important role than perceived accuracy for human managers to catch up with AI in managing employees.

Here, we aim at contributing to this emerging and burgeoning conversation by both demonstrating higher accuracy and fairness of AI relative to human managers in managing employees, and, importantly, establishing that the perceived fairness of human managers vis-à-vis AI plays a first-order role, compared with the relative perceived accuracy, in determining the performance of the “horse race” between the average human manager and AI in managing employees. We provide causal evidence of our theory by conducting a field experiment in a large fintech firm whose employees make phone calls to collect overdue payments of peer-to-peer (P2P) loans from individual borrowers. The firm’s managers routinely listened to samples of the calls made by employees to customers, assessed them, and provided feedback to employees on the mistakes they had identified in these calls and the corresponding guidance on how to correct these mistakes. The firm licensed an AI-based algorithm that was successfully commercialized in the same industry to perform the same managerial task. We randomly assigned 654 employees to be evaluated by the AI technology and multiple managers of the firm.

Our study generated interesting findings. First, employees perceived AI to have assessed them with a higher level of fairness (i.e., holding less bias against them) and a higher level of accuracy, than an average manager. Second, employees’ perception of fairness and accuracy of human managers and AI’s evaluations affected their performance. Notably, perceived fairness of human managers versus that of AI plays an important first-order role in reducing the performance gap of the employees evaluated by managers to those by AI. That is, while employees who received evaluations from AI achieved higher performance, in terms of the payment collected, than those from human managers, this performance gap narrowed for employees evaluated by human managers who were considered to hold less personal bias against the evaluated employees. Interestingly, we find that human managers’ perceived fairness constrained the effect of their perceived accuracy: perceived accuracy helped in closing the performance gap only for employees evaluated by managers who are perceived to have a high level of fairness. Moreover, we find that the magnitude of the contribution of human managers’ perceived fairness for closing the performance gap exceeds that of the perceived accuracy. In other words, this employee performance gap was affected to a lesser extent by the perceived accuracy than by the perceived fairness of the evaluations by human managers relative to AI.

We also probed underlying mechanisms by conducting a full mediation analysis. We found that when AI and human managers conducted evaluations, they differed in the content of the feedback provided in terms of the breadth and depth, which shaped employees’ perceptions of the feedback, and in turn affected employees’ behavior at work as captured by the amount and productivity of work based on the audio data of the calls. We found that all mediators were indispensable, and they collectively fully mediated the performance gap generated by AI versus human managers’ evaluations. Moreover, we found that the employees evaluated by AI made fewer job mistakes during the calls— while spending less time on the phone and making fewer calls, suggesting improved productivity.

This study makes several contributions. Recent work in information systems provides evidence of actual application of AI in management, such as Bai et al. [5] which used AI to examine how jobs can be allocated to warehouse employees. Tong et al. [70] shows that contemporary AI technologies have the capability to deliver feedback of superior quality. Consequently, AI surpasses human managers in effectively conducting employee performance evaluations. Contributing to this stream of research, we further unpack AI’s relative advantage over human managers in terms of its fairness in employee evaluations and the first-order role of fairness in empowering human managers to close the performance gap to AI. The key is to understand the relative advantages and disadvantages of human managers in performing a given managerial task in the race with AI, rather than the verdict of who will win the race. This knowledge will provide a necessary basis to assess the areas in which AI and human managers are substitutes or complements for each other [15, 25, 38, 57].

Moreover, we find that human managers’ perceived fairness (i.e., being perceived to hold less interpersonal bias against the evaluated employee) helps them catch up more effectively with AI than their perceived competence (i.e., being perceived to provide accurate evaluations). This novel fairness-based dimension of AI substantiates the general idea that interpersonal skills become increasingly important as AI’s data analytics capabilities continue to improve [20, 65]. Furthermore, it provides a theoretical basis to further understand how AI can be augmented [74]. Importantly, this insight sheds light on a new path for human managers to compete with AI, specifically for performing employee-facing organizational functions. Facing the competition from AI, it is even more important for human managers to treat employees fairly and build positive interpersonal relationships with employees. What enables human managers to beat AI at the game of performing dataintensive managerial tasks is not necessarily by playing the game per se (i.e., enhancing data analysis skills). Rather, it is by strengthening the seemingly unrelated feature of interpersonal affect based on fairness toward employees.

## Related Literature

Our research goal is to comprehend the innovative utilization of AI technologies, in contrast to human managers, in conducting employee performance evaluations, with a specific focus on employees’ reactions to the process. To achieve this, we must draw upon valuable insights from various strands of literature, each contributing to the development of our theory. The relevant literature encompasses the managerial function of employee performance evaluation, the comparison between AI and humans in performing organizational tasks, with a particular focus on how humans, particularly employees, perceive AI within organizational contexts.

## Literature on Employee Performance Evaluation

Because we focus on the context of AI application in conducting employee performance evaluation, we need to evaluate employees’ performance and providing feedback on how to improve their performance is considered a critical managerial function that significantly contributes to firm value [55]. For evaluations and feedback to improve employees’ performance, they need to change employee behavior [70]; this means that they need to be absorbed, internalized, and learned from by the receivers [4, 18].

What features of a piece of feedback or advice affect employees’ willingness to accept it? Two related strands of literature sheds light on this question. The first literature pertains to the interpersonal perception that has been established in psychology, sociology, and organization research. There are two dimensions of interpersonal perceptions [13] <sup>1</sup> are critical in shaping a person’s willingness to be influenced by another person: the intellectual desirability and social desirability of the latter. Intellectual desirability, or competence of the target, refers to the perceived abilities of the target to carry out activities of interest. In our context, perceived competence pertains to employees’ perceptions of the evaluators abilities to generate accurate evaluation and feedback for them. Social desirability, or benevolence, refers to the perceived intent of the target for good (as opposed to ill) and is generally termed “positive interpersonal affect.” In the context of employee evaluation, a good intent in evaluating others is to generate the evaluation fairly, with objectivity, and without discrimination [68]. Perceived fairness pertains to evaluators’ carrying out evaluations without interpersonal bias against the relevant employee matter critically to whether algorithms are welcomed or rejected [46]. Thus, employees are more likely to adopt the feedback that they consider to be accurate and fair, and change their behaviors in accordance with the feedback [60].

The second relevant literature, which also pertains to employees’ receptiveness to feedback, examines interpersonal trust, defined broadly as an individual’s willingness to be vulnerable to another person [66] A well-known perspective in this area is the “ABI’ framework, which suggests that individuals are more likely to trust others when they perceive them to possess ability, benevolence, and integrity [51]. Consequently, employees are more inclined to trust the feedback they receive if they perceive the feedback provider as capable of generating accurate evaluations, having good intentions in providing the feedback, and demonstrating integrity by doing what is right in the appropriate manner.

## Literature on AI vs Humans in Performing Organizational Tasks

The rapidly growing literature has shown how AI transforms multiple functional areas of business, including production, operations, marketing, customer service, and international trade [6, 8, 49, 54]. Yet, research on how AI shapes firm management is still in its infancy. Possibly owing to the novelty of such AI applications, research on this topic mainly comprises theoretical developments [7, 17, 57, 62] and empirical studies of employees’ responses to hypothetical scenarios in lab experiments [58], instead of actual deployment of the technology<sup>2</sup>. Recent studies, such as Tong et al. [70], use data from real-world implementations of AI in actual firm management and a field experiment to demonstrate that current AI technologies can provide better quality feedback, and thus, outperform human managers in performing employee performance evaluation.

## Literature on Employees’ Attitudes Toward Algorithms

How employees react to various applications of AI in organizational context is closely related to the literature on human beings’ attitudes toward algorithms. On the one hand, there exists a long-standing literature of algorithm aversion [19, 40] and it continues to expand rapidly. Most recent systematic reviews aiming to synthesize this sizable body of literature include [28, 43, 46, 50]. In organizational contexts, such algorithm aversion is manifested as employees’ skepticism of and resistance to the adoption of algorithms in performing organizational tasks [22, 23, 58, 70], which is determined by characteristics of the algorithm, users, tasks, and environment [50]. Employees may be concerned that the use of algorithms generates systematic errors [22], lacks procedural justice [58], deprives them of the workplace autonomy [58], threatens their power at work [44], threatens their job security [70], creates discrimination and bias [43] and other ethical concerns [26].

On the other hand, however, researchers have document algorithm appreciation by finding that people, including employees, may prefer algorithms to human judgment[14, 23, 29, 47, 67, 76, 77]. Based on meta-analysis, [39] provides a benefit-cost analysis of when human beings demonstrate algorithm aversion or appreciation. Studies show that algorithm aversion may be lessened for tasks that consist of a larger objective component [14] and for individuals with greater expertise [22, 39]. In particular, for data-intensive tasks such as performance evaluations, employees’ aversion to AI may be lessened [30]. The debate over algorithm aversion or appreciation is an important part of the ethical issues [26, 43].

The existing literature on algorithm aversion has yet to establish a connection with AI fairness or bias, despite the pivotal role they play in employee performance evaluation. Notably, frontier research on AI fairness reveals that while algorithmic bias does exist, its magnitude is comparatively smaller than human bias [16]. Previously, discussions on algorithm appreciation and AI fairness were treated as separate streams of research. However, we aim to demonstrate in this paper that by incorporating discussions on AI fairness, we can generate novel insights regarding the appreciation or aversion to the algorithm, particularly in relation to perceived fairness.

## Theory and Hypotheses: Artificial Intelligence Vis-À-Vis Human Managers in Evaluating Employees

Our research aims to examine the crucial factors that influence the comparison between AI and human managers in employee performance evaluation. Drawing on the literature highlighting the significance of perceived accuracy and perceived fairness in shaping employees’ reactions to evaluations, our goal is to analyze how employees perceive AI versus human managers along these two dimensions. We first develop theoretical frameworks to assess employees’ perceptions of accuracy (H1a and 1b) and fairness (H2a and H2b) in evaluations conducted by AI versus human managers. Next, we compare the relative importance of these dimensions in shaping employees” reactions, leading to the discussion of Hypothesis 3. To further understand this relative importance, we investigate the interaction between perceived fairness and accuracy, demonstrating that the impact of perceived accuracy is influenced by the level of perceived fairness (H4). To provide an overview, we present Figure 1, which visually presents all hypotheses in a conceptual framework, and subsequently, we will present detailed arguments to establish each element depicted in Figure 1.

## Perceived Accuracy

A critical scope condition of our analysis pertains to the nature of the tasks at hand because task characteristics critically shape both the actual performance and employees’ evaluation of AI vis-à-vis human managers[39, 46, 50]. The extent of “structuredness” of the evaluation matters critically to this comparison [10]: When there exist quantifiable goals to achieve (on our research context, they pertain to evaluating employee performance), a well specified set of information to draw on (on our context, it pertains to the raw data on employees’ work behavior to be used for evaluation), and a clear understanding of what to look for in the raw data to conduct (in our research context, it pertains to specific rules over how to assess work behavior), AI demonstrates much stronger abilities than humans to generating accurate results [70]. Accordingly, employees “feel more comfortable with algorithmic advice in domains that feature a concrete, external standard of accuracy” [47]. By contrast, if the task of evaluation is less structured as lacking any of the features specified above, then much of the advantage of AI over human managers may be eroded.

In performing structured, data-intensive tasks, AI technologies are known to have stronger technological capabilities than humans in processing big data and generating accurate predictions using data [1], for three reasons. First, AI has higher analytical data capabilities. This means that compared to humans, as the amount of data on employees’ behavior and performance increases, AI’s accuracy increases with respect to using the data to identify mistakes and strengths of employees, with fewer type-1 errors (rejecting true positives) and type-2 errors (accepting true negatives). Second,

AI’s greater predictive power to elicit highly accurate data for each individual case enables it to generate more relevant feedback for employees. For example, the feedback is more suitable for each employee’s idiosyncratic situation, whereas human evaluators tend to use templates of assessing average cases to compensate for their lower cognitive skills [36]. Third, AI is capable of storing a larger knowledge bank of best practices and can more efficiently retrieve relevant suggestions from this knowledge bank to remedy identified mistakes. Therefore, employees are more likely to perceive AI to generate more-accurate evaluations than human managers in conducting structured and quantifiable forms of job performance evaluations. We thus hypothesize this as follows:

Hypothesis 1a (H1a): In conducting structured and quantifiable evaluations of employees’ job performance, human managers are perceived by employees to generate less accurate evaluations for them than is AI.

Jointly, H1a and the first stream of literature reviewed above on how employees accept performance evaluation and change their work behavior accordingly suggests that AI evaluations, because of the higher perceived accuracy, will increase employee performance more than human managers’ evaluations do. However, there exists substantial heterogeneity among human managers in data collection and analytical skills, such that not all managers are equally inferior to AI in terms of accurately evaluating their employees. In some cases, AI may have incomplete information as inputs. For example, Choudhury et al. [15] and Kesavan and Kushwaha [41] argued that humans sometimes have more private information that has not yet been used to train AI. Meanwhile, additional information is constantly added to the training data of AI such that AI learns continuously [1]. Therefore, if employees perceive human managers to reach a higher level of accuracy, they will accept managers’ evaluation to a greater extent, then those human managers have greater opportunities to catch up with AI in terms of using evaluation to increase employee performance. This is given as not all human managers achieve an average level of accuracy in evaluation, but their capabilities in generating accurate performance evaluations and feedback vary significantly. Therefore, we hypothesize this conclusion as follows:

Hypothesis 1b (H1b): The more accurate a human manager is perceived by employees in generating employee performance evaluations, the smaller the gap between the job performance of employees evaluated by the human manager versus those evaluated by AI.

## Perceived Fairness

A critical determinant of “good intent” in evaluating employees is to conduct evaluations objectively and without much interpersonal bias against them. Some researchers caution that while AI does not have intentions as humans do, the technology can in effect “inherit” bias against certain groups of human beings from its human developers as machine learning algorithms “learn” human bias through their training data [43, 63]. However, note that much of this research uses the “absence of human bias” as the baseline of comparison. By contrast, compared with human bias, algorithm bias is of a lesser extent [16], more straightforward to diagnose [42], and easier to remove or reduce [16, 48]. For example, Zhang et al. [77] shows that an AI algorithm that makes pricing recommendations decreases racial disparities in the marketplace compared with when these recommendations are made by humans.

Data-based performance evaluations may be seen as relying heavily on external standards [24], thus AI, as a technology, holds no ill intent towards employees, whereas human managers may inevitably have innate personal preferences for or against certain employees. This knowledge may also lead an employee to recognize that there is no particular reason for AI to develop a personal favor or disfavor for their or peer employees. Thus, we argue that AI achieves a higher level of perceived fairness than the average human manager in evaluating employees. We hypothesize this conclusion as follows:

Hypothesis 2a (H2a): In conducting structured and quantifiable evaluations of employees’ job performance, human managers are perceived by employees to generate less fair evaluations for them than is AI.

Although employees consider managers in general to be less fair than AI, the extent to which employees perceive human managers to hold personal bias against them varies by the manager—it is determined by the interpersonal relationships between employees and a given manager [13]. As established in the literature review section, for evaluations generated by AI or human managers to change employee behavior and enhance employee performance, the evaluation receivers (employees) need to be willing to accept and learn from these evaluations [4, 18]; and employees are more likely to adopt and learn from evaluations provided by evaluators who employees consider to hold more-positive intent or to be fairer. Therefore, we argue that the fairer a human manager is perceived by employees —thus making the manager’s perceived fairness to be “closer” to the level of perceived fairness of AI, the more likely it is that employees adopt his/her evaluations and feedback to improve performance, thus approaching the level of employees’ acceptance of AI’s evaluation. As a result, employees receiving evaluations from such managers will be able to achieve performance that is closer to the performance achieved by those evaluated by AI. This conclusion is captured by the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2b (H2b): The fairer a human manager is perceived by employees in generating employee performance evaluations, the smaller the gap between the job performance of employees evaluated by the human manager versus those evaluated by AI.

## Perceived Fairness Plays a More Important Role Than Perceived Accuracy

We have argued thus far that both perceived accuracy and fairness of employee evaluations provided by human managers and AI can increase the likelihood that employees accept the evaluations and, thus, improve their performance (i.e., close performance gap of the employees receiving evaluation from human managers relative to that from AI). However, the two dimensions do not play equal roles. First, in terms of the relative importance, the perceived fairness of the evaluation itself may play a more important role than perceived accuracy in enabling human managers to close the performance gap to AI. Behavioral research suggests that individuals resort to affective judgment, that is, to rely on positive or negative feelings to reach a judgement about an issue [30]. Positive feelings toward an issue lead to greater legitimacy, which further prompt individuals to support the issue [31, 71]. The theory of legitimacy suggests that upon receiving their performance evaluations, employees are more likely to consider evaluations to be appropriate, that is, “legitimate” [71] if they have positive feelings about the evaluator. In other words, they perceive the evaluators to hold good intent. Negative feelings about the ill-intent of evaluators will reduce employees’ perceived legitimacy of the evaluations, thereby reducing the likelihood that they will accept them. For example, employees are more likely to act upon their affective judgment of the legitimacy of evaluations, rather than to act upon an objective assessment of the content of evaluations. In this process, the accuracy of the evaluations matters to a very limited extent. In our context, this arises from the perceived fairness of evaluation of human managers overpowering the actual content of evaluations in affecting human judgment about the subject matter [37]. Therefore, we argue that human managers’ fairness perceived by employees is more important than their perceived accuracy in reducing the gap between the job performance of employees evaluated by human managers versus those evaluated by AI.

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Human managers’ fairness as perceived by employees in conducting employee evaluations is more effective than their perceived accuracy in reducing the gap between the job performance of employees evaluated by human managers versus those evaluated by AI.

## Perceived Fairness as a First-Order Condition Moderates the Effects of Perceived Accuracy

Furthermore, we argue that as employees make important decisions over whether and how much to accept the received feedback, they are less likely to accept the feedback if the evaluation is not perceived to be fair, that is, it is conducted with good intent. This makes the accuracy of the content of the feedback irrelevant. We make this assertion based on the behavioral theory of self-regulation, which suggests that whether individuals feel that others have good or ill intent plays a fundamental role in motivating them to take action [12]. Positive (negative) interpersonal affect stimulates (inhibits) activities towards the individuals’ goals [11]. Employees are known to make decisions on whether to accept their performance evaluations and follow the feedback received to change their work behavior. This implies that the perception of whether evaluators are fair determines whether employees will act upon the former’s evaluations and feedback. Consistent with this view, research on feedback-seeking behavior conceptualizes that interpersonal affect plays a moderating role in how competence shapes interpersonal interactions. Casciaro and Lobo [13] argued that when employees perceive a colleague with negative psychological effect, they are less likely to seek feedback from the colleague. This behavior diminishes the relevance of a colleague’s competence in affecting the relevant employees’ feedback-seeking behavior.

Therefore, the first-order effect of perceived evaluation fairness on whether the feedback is accepted creates an important scope condition: higher perceived accuracy of evaluations of human managers will contribute to greater employee performance, thus empowering those employees to close the performance gap relative to peers evaluated by AI only when employees are willing to follow the evaluators’ suggestions. When employees do not act on the suggestions of evaluators who are seen as holding ill-intent or being unfair, the human managers’ perceived accuracy becomes irrelevant to employees’ performance. The firstorder effect of evaluation fairness on whether the feedback is accepted is even more important in our context of performance evaluation which is to identify employees weaknesses and mistakes; moreover, negative feedback is more likely to be feared and rejected than positive feedback [56]. Thus, the perceived fairness of human managers evaluation exerts a scope condition: higher (lower) perceived fairness enables (constrains) the effect of the human managers’ perceived accuracy of evaluation to shape employee performance, to close the performance gap to AI. The following hypothesis captures this conclusion.

Hypothesis 4 (H4): The effect of perceived accuracy of human managers on reducing the gap between the job performance of employees evaluated by human managers versus those evaluated by AI (H1b) is only present among those human managers who are perceived by employees to have a high level of fairness.

## Empirical Design and Data

## Field Experimental Background: Firm, Employees, and AI

Delta Corp (pseudo name) is a medium-to-large-sized fintech firm in Asia. At the time of the experiment, the firm had hired 654 employees to make calls to individual borrowers of P2P loans to collect overdue payments. These were small personal loans of 10,000-15,000 units in the local currency (which at the time of the experiment was equivalent to approximately US\$1,485 to US\$2,777). On every working day, a randomly generated list of customers was given to each employee to call to ensure fairness of the job assignments, a practice that persisted before, during, and after the experiment. This action alleviated alternative explanations concerning heterogeneous call lists. Each employee was required to collect a minimum number of payments each day. They also had the choice of working more hours after reaching the minimum collection level.

Successful local collection of payments required trained skills. Moreover, this training incurred high costs. The employees were trained with call scripts and various voice control techniques when they were hired. Furthermore, they continuously received on-the-job training from managers. For on-the-job training, managers had to frequently sample employee calls to customers, evaluate them to identify mistakes and provide evaluations and suggestions to employees to correct these mistakes and help them improve their probability of more successful collections in future calls.

Delta Corp considered these managerial tasks to be of the utmost importance for two reasons. First, the company considered routine performance evaluations and feedback provision to be vital for improving employee performance. Second, because Delta Corp gave employees high-powered incentive contracts (discussed next), such on-the-job training became one of the few remaining tools, other than terminating employment, available to managers to influence employees’ behavior at work. Importantly, note that Delta Corp did not use this performance evaluation to determine employees’ compensation or career rewards (both were primarily determined by employees’ performance, that is, the amount of payment collections). Rather, bonuses constituted over 70 percent of employee compensation, determined solely by the number of payments collected.

In the three months prior to the experiment, employees’ monthly compensation ranged from 4,470 to 19,400 units in the local currency (US\$704 to US\$2,881, respectively). An average employee made approximately 7,900 units in the local currency per month (approximately US\$1,173). In contrast, the average starting salary of undergraduates from the best university in the same region in the year prior to the experiment was approximately 4,000 to 6,000 units in the local currency (US\$593-\$891). Therefore, unlike the conventional perception that working in call centers is a low-skill job, collecting delinquent loan payments were not low-paying jobs; rather, individuals required significant job skills that needed to be honed regularly.

AI was applied extensively in the overdue loan collection industry. At the time of the experiment, an industry expert that we interviewed estimated that more than 80 percent of professionals in this industry were keenly aware of AI and its applications. Delta Corp licensed an AI technology whose algorithm was developed by a high-tech firm for overdue loan collection. This machine-learning-based algorithm drew on established basic technologies to process audio data that recorded conversations between employees and customers over the phone. This included the following: (1) the speech-to-text technologies developed by a dominant provider of voice-recognition technologies in the local language; (2) the deep learning and neural networks method of Word2Vec in a Python package to quantify the similarity of words and generalize the context of conversational topics; and (3) the hierarchical softmax model to simplify the neural network structure to reduce the vector dimensionality and scale up computing. The machine learning algorithm aimed to predict the probability of successful or unsuccessful collection efforts based on different scripts. Importantly, it could identify the scripts that reduced the likelihood of successful collection. It extracted alternative scripts that increased the likelihood of successful collection from its knowledge bank, which are essential components of performance evaluation and feedback provision. The training data of this algorithm came from other firms of the same industry and not Delta Corp. This alleviated the concern that existing biases of managers at Delta Corp were captured by AI. Prior to the adoption by Delta Corp, the algorithm had successfully powered loan-collecting AI for multiple other firms in the same industry.

## Experimental Design

The field experiment was conducted at Delta Corp in the first month when it implemented the AI technology. The experimental design included three stages. The first stage occurred immediately prior to the experiment. First, 196 employees were randomly assigned to receive performance evaluations from AI, accounting for approximately onethird of the 654 employees in the firm. The remaining two-thirds of the employees were randomly assigned to five human managers, with each manager receiving a similar number of employees that matched their normal workload. We initially asked the CEO and the Human Resource department to evaluate all eligible managers along the two dimensions of accuracy and fairness. We then carried out a mean split based on the scores on each dimension and divided the managers into four categories accordingly. We then randomly selected managers from each category. This is a stratified sampling approach. Before the experiment, no employees had received evaluations directly from those managers, because those managers’ jobs were to train the lower-level managers who conducted routine performance evaluations for employees and to provide training to employees. However, all employees knew all managers through direct experience of interacting with them as trainees and/or indirect experience of hearing about them from colleagues. Second, all employees were notified of the experiment and their group assignments.<sup>3</sup> Short surveys were conducted to ask employees how much they expect their assigned evaluators would conduct evaluations with accuracy and without interpersonal bias against them. 4

The second stage of the experiment occurred during the month of the AI implementation. For each employee, the calls that they made were randomly sampled and passed to their assigned managers or AI for performance evaluation and feedback. To ensure that the same number of calls made by each employee were sampled and that these calls were distributed evenly throughout the entire period, the month was divided into ten three-day windows and 10 calls made by each employee were sampled in each three-day window. This sampling scheme generated a workload that was consistent with the relevant managers normal workload at Delta Corp.<sup>5</sup> This design alleviated the concern that managers might not have had the capacity to process the calls assigned to them during the experiment. This approach essentially constrained AI in this “horse race” because AI had the capacity to process a much larger number of calls than humans could within the same time frame.

If an employee was scheduled to work for three days in the three-day window, four calls on the first day and three calls on the second and third days were randomly sampled. If an employee was scheduled to work for two days in the three-day window, five calls were randomly sampled for each day. In the case of an unexpected absence, more calls were randomly sampled in the previous or subsequent days to ensure that 10 calls from the same person in the three-day window were sampled. This sampling method resulted in a total of 100 calls made by each employee throughout the month. Each sampled call needed to be longer than 15 seconds to provide enough information for analysis. During the month of the experiment, the sampled calls were relayed to AI and managers every day. AI analyzed the calls using audio analytics. Conversely, managers were required to click on each call, and the system did not allow them to “fast forward” to ensure that they listened to all assigned calls.

On the morning of the day after each three-day window, each employee received an email from the assigned manager or AI. This email contained the feedback on the calls that the employee had made in the previous three-day time frame. This feedback followed the same format of first, reproducing the scripts that the manager or AI detected as containing mistakes, followed by a few suggested corrections to correct the mistakes. Generally, a mistake occurred when what the employee articulated to the customer violated the knowledge bank of scripts, company rules, or regulations, or if they insulted the customer. Mistakes reduced the likelihood of loan collection, as we demonstrate later. Online Supplemental Appendix 1 contains an example of identified mistakes and corrections emailed by AI to an employee.

In summary, during the second stage of the experiment, AI and managers listened to the same number of calls that were made by each assigned employee. They followed the same sampling scheme and provided feedback with the same frequency, timing, and format. They were allowed to differ in the content of the feedback. For example, one manager or AI may detect and identify a larger number of mistakes. For each mistake, one could also provide more suggested corrections than the other.

The third stage occurred immediately after the experiment was over. Before employees were informed of their performance of the month (the total collection amount), short surveys were simultaneously conducted to ask employees the extent to which they felt that their assigned evaluators conducted evaluations with accuracy and without interpersonal bias against them. <sup>6</sup> We assured the employees of the confidentiality of the survey results.

## Results

## Employees’ Perception of AI and Human Managers

We examined whether and how employees from AI and human manager groups perceived the accuracy and fairness of their evaluators differently. Table 4 presents the results. Models (1) and (2) use the perceived accuracy and fairness as dependent variables. The coefficient of the indicator of human vs. AI is negative and significant in Model (1), showing that employees considered the AI to have generated more-accurate evaluations than human managers did. The coefficient of this variable is negative and statistically significant in Model (2), indicating that employees also thought the AI as fairer (i.e., hold less bias against them) in conducting performance evaluations than human managers on average. These results thus lend strong support for H1a and H2a by demonstrating that employees consider AI to be both more accurate (H1a) and fairer (H2a) than the average human manager in conducting performance evaluation.

## Performance Comparison between AI and Average Human Manager

Table 2 shows that the daily collection amount was higher in the AI group than in managers groups, and this difference was statistically significant $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ , consistent with the literature [70].<sup>9</sup> On average, employees evaluated by AI collected payments of 9,233 units in the local currency (US\$ 1,371) per day. This was 626 units more than the daily payment collected by employees evaluated by an average manager, or an increase of 7.27 percent. Figure 3 shows that the cumulative density function of the performance (daily collection amount) for employees evaluated by AI (dash line) lies to the right of that of the performance of the employees evaluated by managers on average (solid line). This pattern indicates that the former group systematically achieved higher levels of performance than the latter group. A Kolmogorov-Smirnov test rejected the equality of the two distributions $\left( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ . Results generated by regression analysis also confirmed this pattern, which is discussed next.

To test H1b and H2b, we regressed the daily collection amount on an indicator of whether the employee was evaluated by any human manager instead of AI, the interaction term of this indicator with perceived accuracy, the interaction of this indicator with perceived fairness of the evaluations, and a series of variables that capture various employee characteristics. We use measures capturing employees’ perception of the accuracy and fairness that are collected immediately after the experiment was completed. The results are reported in Table 5.

Model (1) of Table 5 shows that employees evaluated by human managers, in general, achieved lower performance than those evaluated by AI $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ , controlling for their characteristics. This result confirmed the model-free comparison discussed previously. To test H1b, in Model (2), we added employees’ perceived accuracy of the evaluations received from their assigned evaluators and its interaction term with the indicator of being evaluated by human managers as opposed to AI. First, the estimated interaction effect of “Human Manager (vs. AI) \* Perceived Evaluation Accuracy” was positive $\left( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 \right)$ . This suggests that the marginal effect of perceived accuracy on employee performance was larger in human managers’ groups than in the AI group. Further, we aim to test whether perceived evaluation accuracy helps managers reduce their performance gap compared with AI. Therefore, instead of examining the difference of the marginal effect of evaluation accuracy on performance between the two groups, we focused on the combined effect of evaluation accuracy for human managers, that is, the total, combined effect of “Perceived Evaluation Accuracy” and “Human Manager (vs. AI) \* Perceived Evaluation Accuracy”. This combined effect was also positive, albeit of a smaller magnitude; an F test showed that the combined effect was statistically different from zero $\mathrm { ( p < 0 . 1 ) }$ . This finding confirmed that higher evaluation accuracy indeed helped employees evaluated by managers to reduce the performance gap with those evaluated by AI, thereby supporting H1b.

The estimated interaction effect itself was positive $\left( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 \right)$ , indicating that the marginal effect produced by perceived evaluation fairness on employee performance was greater for employees in human managers’ group than those in the AI group. Again, we focused on the combined effect of perceived fairness of human manager on employees’ performance; that is, the total estimated effect of “Perceived Evaluation Fairness” and “Human Manager (vs AI) \* Perceived Evaluation Fairness”. The sum of the these two coefficients was positive and statistically significant, according to an F test $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This suggests that the fairer (i.e., less biased) the employee considered their assigned human manager to be, the closer their performance was to that achieved by employees evaluated by AI. These results strongly support H2b.

To test H3, we used the estimated marginal effects generated in Models (2) and (3) of Table 5. We plotted the marginal effects of human managers’ evaluation accuracy and fairness on employees’ performance in Figures 4a and 4b, respectively. On average, a single standard deviation improvement in a human manager’s perceived evaluation accuracy reduced the performance gap with the AI’s group by about 160 units in the local currency for each employee. The corresponding effect for perceived fairness of a human manager was approximately 200 units in the local currency. These results suggested that the marginal effect of managers’ perceived fairness on the performance gap between them and the AI was greater than that of perceived accuracy of managers’ evaluation. These results lend support to H3. Interestingly, when either perceived accuracy or perceived fairness was one standard deviation above their respective mean value, the performance of an average employee in the human group was not statistically different from that of an average employee in the AI group. That is, the performance gap between human managers and AI was completely eliminated.

Finally, we examined H4 using two approaches to explore the role of perceived evaluation fairness in moderating the effect of evaluation accuracy on employees’ performance. Consequently, this offers a test of H4. In Models (1) of Table 6, the sum of the coefficients for “Perceived Evaluation Accuracy \* Perceived Evaluation Fairness” and the three-way interaction terms were all positive and statistically different from zero (p < 0.1). This suggests that the effectiveness of a human manager’s perceived evaluation accuracy in reducing the performance gap with AI can be further enhanced by a higher level of their perceived evaluation fairness, which corroborates H4.

This result showed that the perceived fairness of managers constituted a scope condition for the effect of perceived accuracy of evaluation on reducing the performance gap, and thus, was consistent with H4.<sup>10</sup>

## Probing Underlying Mechanisms

We further probe the underlying mechanisms that enable employees who are evaluated by AI to ultimately outperform those evaluated by human managers. Following the sequence of activities that occurred during the experiment, we construct a conceptual framework as captured by Figure 6. After the AI or human managers evaluate employees, they provide feedback whose content varies; upon receiving the content and knowing the identity of the evaluator, employees form their perceptions of the accuracy and fairness of the evaluation; these perceptions shape employees’ behavior at work, as captured by the amount of efforts exerted and the productivity of their work.

![](/api/attachments/M9ACT5FT/fulltext/images/54128aeb5bb69a86d4bb0f58bbe4a7f24c0f6d1d2bada7822c8e79db4cae58ba.jpg)  
Figure 6. Conceptual framework of underlying mechanisms. Abbreviations: AI, artificial intelligence.

We measure each level of the mediators with multiple variables. First, the content of the feedback is measured by Feedback Breadth, defined as the average number of mistakes identified by the assigned evaluator in the feedback report), and Feedback Depth, defined as the average number of suggestions following each identified mistakes provided by the assigned evaluator in the feedback report. Second, employees’ perceptions of the accuracy and fairness are measured along two dimensions through survey questions, as discussed in the main analysis above. Third, we capture employees’ behavior at work along two dimensions. The first dimension pertains to the amount of work (extensive margins), which we capture with two variables including Number of Calls (the average number of calls made daily by the focal employee) and Call Length (the average length of the calls made by the focal employee). The second dimension pertains to the productivity of employees’ work (intensive margins), which we proxy by Improve Rate, defined as the proportion of mistakes in the first week of the experiment that were no longer present in the last week of the experiment.

Because each level of the mediators is measured with multiple dimensions, we customize Hayes’s Process model to allow for both serial and parallel mediation in our empirical analysis [33,34].<sup>11</sup> The estimation results are summarized in Figure 7. The standard errors are calculated with 5,000 bootstrap samples. For clarity, we only report in the figure the coefficients associated with the paths of the indirect effect that are statistically significant. More detailed results about the estimation and the indirect effect are presented in Online Supplemental Appendix 2.

We generate three main conclusions from these results. First, when all mediators are included in the regression, the effect of using AI versus human managers to generate employee evaluation on employee performance becomes insignificant, suggesting a full mediation of the treatment effect.

Second, the differences of the content of the feedback provided by AI and human managers alone do not fully explain employees’ perceptions of feedback, work behavior, or ultimate performance. Instead, employees’ perceptions of the evaluation (and subsequently, work behavior and performance) are also critically shaped by who provide the evaluation—that is, the evaluator’s identity as AI or human manager. This result bolsters the basis of our main analysis, by highlighting the importance of studying employees’ perception of their evaluators in understanding of their performance differences.

![](/api/attachments/M9ACT5FT/fulltext/images/3975e09d95d2a16d9cb74899bc2d76601057a51c0460bde3ba8170c84827d73c.jpg)  
Figure 7. Summary of mediation analysis results. Abbreviations: AI, artificial intelligence.

Finally, we reveal an interesting result that being evaluated by AI does not improve employee performance through increasing the extensive margins of employees’ work, as none of the indirect effect paths that go through number of calls or average call length are statistically significant. Thus, employees evaluated by AI achieved higher performance not because they worked longer or more. Instead, the higher performance of employees evaluated by AI is only achieved through their behavior of making fewer mistakes, evidence of them “working smarter.” This finding is promising as it shows that using AI improves employees’ productivity, one of the most important drivers in production function.<sup>12</sup>

## Discussion

AI technologies are bound to transform managerial activities. While managers increasingly face the challenge of AI technologies, they can build on the extant knowledge developed by organizational research to develop new insights on how to effectively manage with AI. In this study, we present two sets of results. First, we demonstrated that employees considered AI to be both more accurate and fairer in conducting an evaluation for them than an average human manager. Second, we provided causal evidence based on a field experiment in a fintech firm to show that while performing a data-intensive managerial function (frequent employees performance evaluation), AI outperformed the average human manager. However, human managers’ perceived fairness in conducting the evaluation played a first-order role (a) by constraining the effect of their perceived accuracy of evaluation on narrowing this gap and (b) outweighing perceived accuracy in the magnitude of the effectiveness in closing this gap. Interestingly, to catch up with AI on this data-intensive task, the most effective tool was not to make managers better at the task per se (i.e., achieving higher task competence) but to strengthen managers’ interpersonal affect with employees. While this insight is new and may be counterintuitive in AI research, it is rooted in various established theories about interpersonal dynamics, or what makes people (employees) more willing to be influenced by others.

## Contributions

Our study contributes to the fast-growing research on economic activities in the era of AI. There exist multiple streams of expanding literature in various disciplines and fields on how AI reshapes functional areas in modern businesses, including production, information systems, operations, and marketing. The research of AI in organizations and management has emerged quite recently [7, 17, 38, 57, 58, 62, 70, 76]. We contribute to this emergent stream of literature by showing that human managers’ skills in interacting with employees that contribute to their perceived fairness in evaluation critically add to their relative advantage vis-à-vis that of AI. Moreover, we demonstrate that such skills pertaining to building favorable interpersonal relationships with employees are even more important than manager’s skills of achieving evaluation accuracy based on data analysis—even though the “race” in our study was about a data-intensive managerial task. This insight is novel because the literature has focused primarily on how to enable managers to catch up on data skills, such as by using private information or knowledge that is (currently) outside the scope of AI technologies (e.g., [15, 41]).

A better understanding of the relative advantages of AI and human managers contributes to a solid basis for a discussion of whether AI and humans can become complements rather than substitutes for each other, also known as “augmented intelligence” [74] or “AI-human assemblage” [25]. Without the knowledge of the first-order effect of managerial skills in building interpersonal relationships with employees, we would be wrong to think that the only way for human managers to catch up with AI is to strengthen their hard data skills. This will be a “lost cause” for human managers for two reasons. First, human managers have inherent cognitive limitations compared with fast-improving algorithms. Second, as we show, managers’ accuracy of evaluation matters only for managers who are considered to be fair in conducting the evaluations. In this case, AI clearly emerges as a threat to managerial jobs. However, a more sophisticated picture emerges given that the perceived good intent of managers plays a first-order effect and that there exists ample room for human managers to develop positive interpersonal relationships with employees. On the one hand, managers who are perceived as the ones holding interpersonal bias continue to face the threat of being replaced by AI which can be scaled up cheaply. On the other hand, managers who are perceived as holding less personal bias against the employees remain competitive. Thus, this finding provides the basis for leveraging the interpersonal skills of managers and data skills of AI to create complementarity [38]. Delineating the key mechanisms deepens our understanding of why humans are more willing to be influenced by advice provided by algorithms than by humans [30, 52, 61, 64, 72, 73, 77].

Furthermore, this study demonstrates the value of integrating theories of organizations and that of Csaszar and Steinberger [17] proposed several directions to think about how to use many ideas in the field of AI to develop ideas in organizational theory. The reverse could also be fruitful, that is, theories developed in studying organizations in the pre-AI era can help researchers with the advanced AI tools; specifically, they can help assess how AI can be applied more effectively to create more value for the organizations and the society (e.g., [35]). We have demonstrated that to develop our theory, we needed to draw on both the extant literature on interpersonal dynamics and AI. While AI may be a novel phenomenon for employees, such that we need to understand how employees respond to it [47, 58], the logic of interpersonal dynamics and relationships within organizations may still be at work. As such, the existing knowledge about organizations and organizing are useful building blocks for a new theory about AI in management.

While many are concerned about the future of jobs in the era of AI [8], our study demonstrates that applying AI may improve the welfare of employees. The immediate effect is that employees feel that they receive more accurate and less biased feedback from AI than from an average human manager. Importantly, employees achieve higher actual performance—which directly increases their bonuses—by working more productively and for less time. In this sense, AI aids and facilitates employees at work by making them more productive. This contributes to the emerging conversation on how AI and humans can become complements to each other to create value [1, 3].

Finally, this paper may also contribute to the literature on organizational learning and the transfer of explicit and implicit knowledge [4, 17], in the era of AI. In our research context, the evaluations were well codified and followed standard format, which differs from know-hows which commonly remains in the form of tacit knowledge. Tacit knowledge transfers much more effectively through stronger interpersonal social connections, whereas codified knowledge transfers equally well regardless of the strength of interpersonal connections [32]. Thus, for more-complex evaluations that involve much greater tacit knowledge, it is reasonable to expect that inter-human interactions to be more effective than AI-human interaction. Therefore, to the literature on organizational learning and knowledge transfer, this paper contributes a new insight that using AI strengthens effective transfer of codified knowledge to human employees, above and beyond what human managers can achieve on their own. However, this paper also opens up novel questions that AI may challenge human managers to a much lesser extent in transferring tacit knowledge that requires much deeper interpersonal understanding and trust.

## Boundary Conditions and Future Research

All theories have their boundary conditions. We identify three sets of them to encourage future research to probe when and to what extent the current theory holds. The first set of conditions pertain to the nature of the task. In our context, AI was used to evaluate employee performance using detailed objective data on employees’ behavior at work. However, evaluations may be more complex for other tasks, such as those for which objective data are incomplete such that subjective evaluations are necessary (e.g., [27]). Moreover, the performance evaluations in our context were highly structured such that employees did not require further explanations. By contrast, performance evaluations are commonly used in other contexts as part of mentoring activities to more effectively convey the messages and change employees’ behavior. For evaluation tasks that require more subjective assessments and interpersonal interactions such as mentoring, AI’s advantages can be weakened, a topic worthy of further examinations.

The second set of conditions is related to the performance of AI. In this context, AI has demonstrated superior ability to provide feedback to employees, compared to an average human manager. As the quality of the feedback has a significant impact on employees perceptions of fairness and accuracy, it would be interesting to examine whether our theory and predictions still hold in scenarios where AI’s performance is hard to assess, or weaker than that of human managers. Additionally, previous research has demonstrated that people are more likely to avoid an algorithm when they see it make mistakes [22]. Future studies are encouraged to examine whether employees’ perceptions will be impacted when they see AI make mistakes.

The third set of conditions pertains to the type of employees receiving the evaluation. First, employees in Delta Corp were young and likely familiar with technologies. Furthermore, they were commonly aware the use of AI at least in their industry. Moreover, applications of AI in Asia are widespread [45]. These characteristics may explain the employees’ generally positive attitudes toward the application of AI. Subjects in other studies tend to have quite different profiles [58], thus calling for examining the theory in other industries and regions. Second, employees in our context exhibited a stronger shortterm transactional mindset, shown by their lack of teamwork design, and the prevalence of high-power contracts and high turnover. Future research can examine how AI performs in contexts in which long-term relationships are valued, such as where teamwork is a necessary factor of employee success.

## Practical Implications

We have two specific suggestions for human managers in the modern era of AI. First, with AI technology developments, firms emphasize training managers for or selecting managers with greater skills for building interpersonal relationships than data analytics skills. This is because the former skills are more effective in enabling human managers to better compete with AI. Second, while building complementarity between AI and human managers, firms should recognize that managers who are perceived by employees as being fair are in a much stronger position to collaborate with AI. Leveraging this fact can help enhance employees willingness to accept the evaluation feedback.

## Conclusion

AI possesses advantages over human managers in performing managerial tasks that require data analysis. One such task is evaluating employees’ performance based on data on their work activities. However, human managers who are perceived by employees as being fairer in conducting performance evaluations have a greater ability to catch up with AI in terms of leveraging these evaluations to improve employees’ job performance. Additionally, the fairness exhibited by managers is even more significant than their ability to generate accurate evaluations when it comes to achieving this objective. Hence, when carrying out employee-facing organizational functions such as employee evaluations, the key for human managers to catch up with AI lies not solely in achieving higher evaluation accuracy through superior data analysis, but in treating employees fairly to establish a stronger interpersonal influence.
