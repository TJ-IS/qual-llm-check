# Discovery of Technological Innovation Systems: Implications for Predicting Future Innovation

Junho Yoon<sup>a</sup>, Gautam Pant<sup>b</sup>, and Shagun Pant<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Business Analytics, Tippie College of Business, The University of Iowa, Iowa City; <sup>b</sup>Department of Business Administration, Gies College of Business, The University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Champaign

## ABSTRACT

In contrast with the accelerating trend of boundary-spanning (horizontal) technological innovation, the current Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) scheme applies a hierarchical (vertical) structure to innovation output in terms of patents. For this reason, we argue that the CPC can be complemented with dynamic technological innovation system (TIS) discovery through machine learning that accounts for horizontal relationships across seemingly disparate technologies. Using a design science approach, we propose a framework to discover boundary-spanning TISs by leveraging the textual information from millions of patents. We validate our framework in terms of the ability of discovered relationships to predict future innovation quantity and quality in diferent technology classes. Our novel TIS-based innovation metrics that leverage patenting activity in related technology classes are significantly associated with future innovation intensity in focal technologies. We conduct experiments with machine learning models to further tease out the predictive utility of our TIS discovery framework.

KEYWORDS technological innovation system (TIS); machine learning; patents; predictive analytics

## Introduction

Innovation is increasingly boundary-spanning and interdisciplinary. Boundary-spanning research can reveal new frontiers at the intersection of disciplines for solving problems, and ultimately lead to breakthrough innovation. Collaborative research spanning two or more disciplines is predominant in both natural and social sciences [26] and provides a fertile ground for ground-breaking ideas [43]. These interdisciplinary trends have obscured the traditional boundaries and relationships between technology sectors leading to the formulation of hybrid technologies. Firms and research institutions have blended existing disparate technologies and spawned new ecosystems of technologies [3]. Each such ecosystem of related technologies can be referred to as a technological innovation system or TIS [10].

TIS benefits from modular components derived from different technologies that can be combined in various permutations. The escalating trends of interdisciplinarity with modules combined together [7] enables recombinant innovation [18, 59] by creating novel products from independently designed subsystems that cohesively function as a unified entity [7, 17]. As introduced in Baldwin and Clark [7], such interdisciplinarity or modularity allowed the computer industry in the late 1990s to experience immense technological growth.

In recent years, several prominent instances of Technological Innovation Systems (TISs) featuring modules that bridge disciplinary boundaries have been observed in various products and services. For example, smart TV presents a convergence of various technology components, such as display, wireless communication, and digital applications, which were once considered separate technologies. Likewise, autonomous vehicles operate with the help of computer vision, security, and wireless connectivity. As implied in these examples, the boundary-spanning recombination of technologies cultivates brand-new TISs. The potential space for such innovative TISs is combinatorial as technology inventors find new ways to combine different technologies. Consequently, the blurred boundaries make siloed disciplinary structures such as the Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC), which is used by US and European patent offices (i.e., USPTO and EPO), less useful for discovering new TISs with technology components that cut through the vertical CPC structure with horizontal relations. In fact, the fast-paced boundary-spanning innovation that now abounds is creating challenges for specialized patent examiners relying on CPC’s hierarchical structure to review patentability [66].

A technological innovation system (TIS) provides a systems view around a focal technology by considering its relationship with various components, which could be social (e.g., firms, governments) and/or technological (e.g., other technologies) [10]. Such a systems view could provide an understanding and prognosis of technological trajectory. We focus on the discovery of TIS around a focal technology so as to identify an ecosystem of related technologies that have interactions and inter-dependencies with the focal technology [8, 10]. Motivated by the biological ecosystem analogy, a stream of literature in technology management and innovation has focused on the inter-dependencies and interactions of technologies as opposed to analyzing technologies in isolation. For example, in the information systems (IS) literature, Adomavicius et al. [3] provide a conceptual model of technology evolution that is based on an ecosystem view focused on the dynamic interactions between interrelated technologies. This ecosystem view is in concordance with the notion of TIS introduced by Carlsson et al. [10] when the components of TIS are focused on technologies. It has been argued that a systems view of related technologies facilitates diagnosing vulnerable spots in a TIS, providing policy guidelines such as incubating particular technologies [27]. Similarly, identifying a TIS could lead to a better prognosis and prediction for the future evolution of a set of technologies.

In this work, we utilize the design science approach [25] in crafting our artifact for TIS discovery, subsequently evaluating its practical utility for businesses and other organizations within the sphere of innovation. The design science paradigm has emerged as a prominent approach in contemporary IS literature [41, 45, 57]. Accordingly, we propose a specific framework for the automated discovery of TIS (construction phase). Following which, we demonstrate that such a discovery can indeed serve as a tool for more accurately predicting future innovation intensity and quality in a technological class (evaluation phase).<sup>1</sup> Such predictions can be beneficial for various stakeholders of a technology innovation system, such as firms, research labs, governments, and universities. The practical utility of our proposed framework aligns well with the goal of design science research [25]. A firm may consider allocating resources towards a technology class that shows potential for steady progress in innovation, both in terms of quantity and quality. This decision can be informed by insights gained through our TIS discovery framework, which also identifies complementary technologies worth considering for investment. In fact, through empirical analysis, we find that firms that focus their patent portfolios on related technologies based on TISs discovered through our framework show greater future value ascribed to their patents by the financial markets. Hence we observe a clear practical validity of the proposed TIS framework for firms.

Despite the interdisciplinary trends, many research organizations and fields still rely on hierarchical disciplinary structures that are often derived through expert knowledge, and the pace of change in these structures is slow [16]. One challenge with defining a new TIS that spans across vertical innovation structures such as the CPC is delineating its boundaries. Such delineation has been done in the past on a case-by-case basis using manual expertise. However, the reliance on such human expertise limits the scale of TIS discovery across technologies and over time. We provide a scalable solution as a design science artifact to the problem by proposing a TIS discovery framework that applies unsupervised machine learning on text from millions of patents to operationalize technology relatedness. Our proposed framework involves deriving a horizontal topical innovation structure that crosses the boundaries under the popular hierarchical technology classification system (i.e., CPC), which is vertical in nature. The innovation topics in the horizontal structure are used to identify, for each focal technology class, its related classes. We thus define a group of associated technologies (i.e., focal and related) as a technological innovation system (TIS).

Before we discuss the details of the proposed framework, it is instructive to briefly review the CPC system used by the USPTO and EPO. The CPC structure starts from nine main technology sections at the highest level, and then it drills down into more granular technology categories in a tree-like hierarchical structure (see Figure 1). Each node in the tree has a CPC code associated with it (e.g., H01C for “Resistors”), which contains characters that identify it uniquely along with its parent categories (e.g., H01 for “Basic

In essence, each technology category identified by a CPC code is dependent on the higher-level categories. Such a vertical scheme is likely inadequate to capture a TIS with increasingly horizontally related technology components.

In response to this gap, to discover the horizontal relations within a TIS, our study leverages the textual information from millions of patents to derive an alternate topical innovation structure. As opposed to the top-down and expert-derived CPC scheme, the textual information in a patent describes the invention as is. It is first-hand information from the inventor(s) without coaxing it into a disciplinary structure. In other words, such textual information provides cues on boundary-spanning relations within a TIS that are absent in the vertical CPC structure. Our study uses BERTopic, a state-of-the-art neural network-based topic modeling technique that uses Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) to discover the topical innovation structure from the patent text data. In the topics that emerge from our ML-based analysis, we find evidence of boundaryspanning innovations. For example, B60R (“Vehicles”) and F16H (“Gearing”) are found to be topically related while they belong to two different top-level sections of the CPC (B is for “Performing Operations . . . ” and F is for “Mechanical Engineering . . . ”). Subsequently, we discover tangible TISs with the help of this topical innovation structure. Specifically, the text-driven structure is used for a focal technology to identify its related technologies with high relatedness over a reasonable threshold. The clique of technologies thus identified can be framed as a TIS.

After we provide a TIS discovery framework, the next question we ask is: how do we validate the TISs that are discovered by such a framework? This particular question carries significant importance in design science research, where the artifacts must undergo rigorous evaluations to assess their practical utility [25]. To answer this question, we utilize the theory on technological spillovers as a kernel theory governing our artifact development, where technologies (and more generally, knowledge domains) build over each other, affecting inter-sectoral innovation [31]. The importance of such externalities between technologies is well-documented in the literature [65], some of which quantify the knowledge flow to study the impact of technological spillovers on economic growth and productivity [40]. More recent literature focuses on the role of bridging technologies that enhance technological spillovers and drive synergies out of distant technologies, thereby facilitating new innovation ideas [14].

Drawing from the technology spillover literature, we can expect innovation spillovers across related technologies in a TIS. In other words, the intensity of innovation in one technology within a TIS is likely to be associated with future innovation in related technologies within the TIS. This observation provides a method to validate our proposed framework for TIS discovery. A TIS must present evidence of innovation spillover across related technology classes. Hence we validate the associations of the TIS components by estimating their utility in predicting future innovation intensity in technology classes (four-character CPCs such as B60R for “Vehicles”). In particular, we suggest several innovation metrics for a given focal technology class based on the past innovation output in the related classes. We find positive and significant associations of the metrics that capture the innovation in related technology classes with the future innovation in the focal class with respect to both quantity and quality of innovation. This result is found to hold after controlling for technology class heterogeneity and alternate specifications. We also find that greater levels of boundary-spanning innovation, which have been associated with greater synergy through the spillovers, signal a higher level of aggregate innovation in the technology class [15]. These results, based on explanatory empirical modeling, validate our TIS discovery framework as they indicate expected spillovers across technologies in discovered TISs. We also perform a firm-level analysis to demonstrate the utility of our framework as a guide for firms to shape their patent portfolios. The results show that firms that focus their patent portfolio on the related technologies, as indicated by our discovered TISs, achieve a greater future financial value from their portfolio.

We further validate the utility of TIS discovery and consequent innovation metrics using popular machine learning models. With these models, we find a significant predictive utility of our innovation metrics on two prediction tasks relating to future patenting volume and citations for a technology class. Specifically, our experiments show that a neural networkbased time series prediction algorithm (Long-Short Term Memory or LSTM), as well as several tree-based methods, significantly benefit from our proposed innovation metrics with consistent results across varying evaluation metrics.

In summary, the topical innovation structure we derive using patent data complements the extant hierarchical CPC scheme in that it realizes the explicit discovery of TISs with horizontal components and also provides a new perspective for understanding the multidisciplinary aspects of innovation. Our study provides the following contributions to the literature:

(1) While the extant literature, including that in the IS area [2–4], has highlighted the importance of a systems view toward related technologies, it stops short of the automated discovery of a TIS that is focused on related technologies. We propose a generalizable framework to facilitate such discovery with horizontal components that cut through the vertical CPC structure that is currently imposed on innovation output (i.e., patents). Our framework is completely automated, utilizing unsupervised ML on patent text with no reliance on vertical dependencies of the CPC system or human examiners’ inputs. We present several examples of boundary-spanning TISs with the components originating from disparate CPC sections. (e.g., Figure 2). Our text-driven TIS discovery framework presents clear evidence of the boundaryspanning nature of current technological innovations. We expect that the management and innovation literature will utilize our automated TIS discovery framework to remove current constraints on the number of TISs or technology ecosystems that can be manually derived and hence used to test theories [4, 60].

(2) In practice, firms can take advantage of our framework for identifying focal technologies with high levels of future innovation potential as well as a set of related technologies that could be part of their diverse technology portfolio. Such diverse portfolios may lead to breakthrough innovations and a higher market value of patent stock [34]. Specifically, rather than investing in technologies in isolation, a firm’s research and development (R&D) costs could be amortized faster if spent on candidates from a TIS due to the spillovers and synergistic growth. In fact, firms can be expected to appropriately invest in R&D of technologies as they can capture the value of spillovers within a TIS [27], thereby improving R&D outcomes by utilizing our IT artifact [47, 51]. A firm-level analysis in our study reveals that prioritizing TIS technologies in their patenting indeed leads to high patent capital for firms in the future. In a similar vein, our framework also could provide useful information to policymakers and research institutions (e.g., NSF) for more efficient research funding allocations. With the help of our framework, the innovation stakeholders can first discover topically associated technology classes. Then, they can design a long-term plan for fostering a technology ecosystem with investments in both a focal technology class (e.g., Semiconductors) as well as its related classes (e.g., Plasma technique and Single-crystal growth). In summary, our TIS discovery framework can provide useful intelligence for innovation stakeholders in both the public and private sectors in terms of efficient research and technology portfolio plans. We illustrate such a use case based on our TIS discovery framework in Figure 5. The use case presents a scenario of an R&D manager at a firm (i.e., Intel) that can identify related technologies (based on our TIS discovery framework) to their current portfolio of patented technologies. The use case presents the possibility of identifying related technology classes for future investments. Such a tool would also benefit public research agencies (e.g., NSF) for the same purpose.

We then present empirical results that support our discovery framework. This is followed by the results of the predictive modeling tasks. We conclude with the main implications of our study.

## Related Literature

The importance of boundary-spanning channels that allow knowledge flows among otherwise siloed communities has been asserted in the sociology literature [64]. The focus is on social capital (e.g., novel ideas or performance improvement) produced by individuals that are connected across heterogeneous groups in organizations [62]. More specific to innovation, human capital engaged in technology developments, such as inventors and engineers, are spurred to form inter-sectoral collaborations to discover original ideas or artifacts otherwise unseen from intra-sectoral collaborations [44].

Collaborations among technical experts from diverse domains can lead to the emergence of new technologies, and hence such collaborations have drawn the attention from innovation stakeholders (e.g., firms, universities, and federal research agencies) for their potentially impactful outcomes [63]. In fact, the National Science and Foundation (NSF) has funded interdisciplinary research initiatives since the mid-1980s in order to nurture impactful technologies, and some of them did materialize into new products or services (e.g., smart TV and autonomous vehicle) [32]. In terms of patents, such increasing interdisciplinarity in technology development has also been observed in the gradually widening topical distance (based on patent text) between co-cited patents over the years, i.e., recent patents are more likely to cite more distant pairs of patents than in the past [66]. As a result, there is an increasing focus among both researchers and professionals to encourage innovation through such technological reflections and resulting combinations. [54].

The innovation literature in management and economics has found empirical evidence that the recombination of distinct knowledge can engender breakthrough innovations. Ahuja and Lampert (2001), through an example from the chemical industry, demonstrate that firms can create breakthrough inventions by incorporating technologies they are previously unfamiliar with [5]. Singh and Fleming (2010) show that experience diversity among patent inventor groups can play a role in reducing patenting failures [59]. Such exploratory R&D practice encourages experimentation toward novelty, potentially driving impactful innovation outcomes [52]. Although spanning technological boundaries entails the trade-off between many failures and some breakthroughs [48], it has been seen as a way to bring technological innovations over the recent decades.

As a result of such boundary-spanning (i.e., horizontal) trends, however, the boundaries between technologies have become more ambiguous than in the past. In contrast, the current Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) system used by USPTO and EPO has a hierarchical (i.e., vertical) structure like a tree with separate branches that clearly delineate technology classes. Although the patent examiners can assign multiple CPC codes to a patent based on its content (e.g., patent claims), the subjectivity of the individual examiners undermines the consistency of the evaluations [16] and a substantial portion of patents map to just a single CPC technology subclass [66]. The increasing trend of interdisciplinary patents delays the examiners’ patent review process and thus may distract their attention [66], leading to potential inconsistencies. The shortcomings of the CPC system have prompted the use of patent text to measure the boundary-spanning nature of patents [66].

The broader literature on text analysis of patents can be subdivided into three strands. The first strand, which accounts for the majority of this literature, quantifies the novelty or impact of patents using their text [6, 29]. The second strand computes the similarity between patents using text [19], and the third strand categorizes patents into subgroups based on their text [33]. Our study is focused on using the patent text (title and abstract) to quantify the relatedness between technology classes. While our work does not directly fit into the above-mentioned strands in the literature, it could be seen as being related to the second and third strands. However, our work is the first to propose the automated discovery of TISs with boundary-spanning technology classes. We do so while using the text of the patents in different technology classes.

Our research proposes an IT design artifact to identify TISs. Hence it is related to the design science literature in information systems. The current literature in design science regarding technological innovation has mainly focused on specific industries, crafting artifacts to gain insight into particular technological advancements. Adomavicius et al.’s IT ecosystem model [3] is a notable example aiming at facilitating practitioners’ IT investment decision [4]. The artifact that they propose is validated specifically in digital music and wireless networking industries. Recently, the emphasis on innovation within design science research has transitioned towards blockchain technologies, a shift underscored by Rossi et al. [49]. In alignment with this direction, Guggenberger et al. [22] have employed a design science paradigm to explore how blockchain can improve information sharing in vendormanaged inventory.

Our study differentiates itself from previous innovation-related design science research in two primary ways. Firstly, we address extensive technological domains based on the Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) scheme, rather than concentrating on a specific technology or industry. This approach allows us to explore a broader range of technological domains. Secondly, we employ machine learning to uncover intersectoral relationships among technologies using text data extracted from millions of patents. This contrasts with the related Information Systems (IS) literature, which typically uses empirical approaches with event data for technology relatedness [4]. By leveraging machine learning, our study can identify and analyze complex relationships among technologies more effectively. In summary, the scope of our findings and the novel methodology employed set our study apart within the realm of design science research.

## Technological Innovation System (TIS)

A technological innovation system (TIS) can be defined by its technological components or classes analogous to a biological ecosystem that is described by various organisms that are part of it [10, 38]. The innovation literature that adopts the component-based standpoint considers technologies in a TIS as mutually associated in terms of their evolution or external interventions (e.g., government policies). Hence identification of a TIS can help to diagnose vulnerable spots in a TIS or facilitate policies such as investment recommendations for technology components [8, 27].

For example, Stephan et al. [60] apply the value chain perspective to their study on the lithium-ion battery (LIB) innovation system. In particular, on the production side of LIB, the raw materials obtained by the mining technologies are refined by chemicals, metals, and electronic technologies. The downstream sectors process the intermediate artifacts, and the manufactured batteries are then used in the final applications (e.g., laptops or electric vehicles). By their definition, the LIB innovation system comprises the chained connection of technologies, spanning the areas of mining, chemicals, metals, electronics, and downstream applications.

A few studies in the information systems (IS) literature have adopted the ecosystem analogy in the information technology context. Adomavicius et al. [2] propose a framework that helps understand the IT landscape based on three roles of technologies - 1) product, 2) component, and 3) infrastructure. The work finds that technologies in an ecosystem affect one another through a visible path. As a result, they discover explicit patterns of evolution in the technology ecosystems.<sup>2</sup> In a follow-up paper, Adomavicius et al. [4] empirically validate the framework using two case studies on 1) digital music and 2) wireless networking technologies. They find the pragmatic utility of their framework through interviews with IT industry experts.

The focus of the above-mentioned empirical studies is on specific manually-identified TISs based on case studies. In contrast, we present a framework to automatically identify TISs around a focal technology utilizing large-scale patent data with ML. We then show that the TISs discovered through the framework show bonafide relational cues about future innovation in the focal technology. Hence we fill a research gap in the literature that has until now relied on manual identification of technology ecosystems or TISs. The research gap may be explained by the limited popularity of ML and data science methods in the innovation literature as well as a lack of consensus on how to set the boundaries and hence the size of a TIS. The choice of TIS size in the literature seems ad-hoc and can vary depending upon research questions [38]. We provide a systematic data-driven procedure for delineating TISs based on text-based relatedness between technology classes.

A critical concern about an identified TIS (whether manually or automatically) is how to consistently validate the within-TIS associations. In other words, the TIS validation method used in one study may not be applicable to a different TIS in another study. For example, patent citation-based validation, as suggested in [60], may not be applicable to [4]. We address the issue with a straightforward premise based on expected spillovers across technologies in a TIS. Specifically, we find strong evidence that the future innovation in a focal technology class (e.g., Semiconductors in Figure 2) is positively associated with the past innovation outputs of related technologies in a TIS (e.g., Capacitors, Micro-chemical device, Fusion reactor, etc.). In doing so, we validate that our TIS discovery framework finds a group of technologies that are not just associated with the focal technology in terms of topics but also in terms of actual innovation output.

## Data

To operationalize our design artifact, we leverage comprehensive patent information to extract the topical innovation structure from patents. Subsequently, we utilize this horizontal structure to discover TISs. Based on the TISs, we then propose innovation metrics to predict the future innovation intensity and quality for a large number of technology classes. Patents have been widely used in the prior literature as a proxy for innovation measures at different levels, such as firms or countries since only inventions with enough novelty materialize into official patents. We collect patent data as of 2021 from the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) because it has been widely employed in prior studies within the fields of information systems and innovation. The USPTO database is renowned for providing high-quality data that serves the research purpose effectively [12, 50]. In addition, the USPTO patent data allows us to use the patent-firm matching data offered by Stoffman et al. [61], thereby enabling firm-level empirical analyses.

The database contains the following patent information: patent publication numbers, filing dates, grant dates, patent titles and abstracts, country codes, citations, CPC codes, and assignees of patents. For this study, we use patents granted by the USPTO that were applied between 2000 and 2017. In the innovation literature, it is a common practice to focus on the patent application date rather than the issue date for the following reasons: 1) the application date is the earliest time point when the inventions are ready for patent review, and 2) the time lags between application, and issue dates may vary between patents. Also, following Hall et al. (2001), we assume a four-year gap between the two dates to control for truncated filings; hence our dataset ends in 2017 (4 years before the data collection time). The unit of analysis of our study is a technology class (four-character CPC) which forms a coherent technology field [36], thereby facilitating the systematic categorization of patents based on their knowledge content [11]. Hence, the technology class is seen as a unit granular enough to conduct empirical studies in the innovation literature (e.g., [11]). As a result, our study uses data related to 4,265,824 patents.

## TIS Discovery Framework

In accordance with the paradigm of design science [25], the main goal of our work is to propose a data-driven framework for discovering TISs and the validation of the artifact. We first elaborate on how we build the design artifact as illustrated in Figure 3. We note that the unit of analysis of our study is a technology class (four-character CPC), e.g., “H01L” (Semiconductors). Although we start with the individual patent text, patents are subsequently aggregated to the technology class level. There are three stages in the framework that we describe next.

## Stage 1 - Topical Innovation Structure using Neural Topic Modeling

To derive a text-based topical innovation structure using patents, we employ BERTopic [21]. BERTopic is a state-of-the-art neural topic modeling technique based on the bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT), a groundbreaking natural language processing (NLP) model developed by Google in 2018. This technique allows for more effective analysis and understanding of textual data by leveraging the advanced language capabilities of BERT. Such sentence embedding-based topic modeling methods have been widely used in the recent literature for identifying latent topics ground up from a set of documents (e.g., [39]).

Figure 3 Our proposed framework for the TIS discovery. In Stage 1, BERTopic is used to derive the latent innovation topics from the patent text. In Stage 2, technology classes (four-character CPC) are mapped onto the innovation topics, and then technology relatedness is measured. In Stage 3, for the TIS component search, a focal class and its related classes over the 90th percentile are identified. The focal and related classes together form a TIS. Lastly, the relatedness in TISs is validated via explanatory and predictive modeling.

The use of BERTopic with patent text enables the discovery of latent innovation topics that are transparent (i.e., topic categories can be described through the top words in them). The transparent nature of the topics also allows us to easily label them for ease of interpretation. Then, the identified innovation topics can be used to measure the interdisciplinarity of technology classes. Given the transparent nature of topics, it is easy to manually validate the mapping of technology classes to innovation topics (see Table 1 for examples). Finally, given the need to build a framework that can use millions of patent text as inputs, the stable and seamless implementation of BERTopic is an added benefit.<sup>3</sup> Hence, it is appropriate to use BERTopic for the current instantiation of our framework. However, the proposed framework is also flexible to using other topic models.<sup>4</sup>

In our framework, the input documents used by the BERTopic are patent texts. In particular, we use the combination of a patent’s title and abstract as its text. A patent’s title and abstract are expected to contain some of the most topically relevant innovation terms related to the patent and hence should provide high-quality topical information to BERTopic. Specifically, BERTopic is a topic model that enhances traditional techniques by utilizing a class-based version of TF-IDF to extract meaningful topic representations. It creates document embeddings using pre-trained transformer-based language models (e.g., BERT), clusters these embeddings, and then generates topic representations using the classbased TF-IDF process. In our implementation based on BERTopic, patents between 2000 and 2007 (N = 1,647,042) are used for training the topic model (we use these “learned” topics to subsequently predict innovation in the period of 2008 to 2017). We suggest using recent patents (e.g., from the previous decade) for topic modeling in order to capture the timely innovation topic structure. With the output from BERTopic, we categorize patents into latent innovation topics based on their text. The discovered innovation topics are characterized based on the topic-term distributions.

A main hyperparameter for topic modeling is the number of latent topics. We experiment with different numbers of topics, and the normalized pointwise mutual information (NPMI) coherence scores are computed for each choice of the number of topics. The advantage of using the NPMI score is that it captures semantic relatedness between words in a topic based on their co-occurrence in a text corpus [21]. In other words, it measures the degree to which words within the same topic co-occur in the same contexts, which is often a good indicator of whether those words form a coherent, semantically meaningful topic. However, a larger number of topics reduces interpretability and causes overfitting. Hence we identify a reasonable number of latent innovation topics by considering the elbow of the curve in the plot of coherence scores against the number of topics (see Figure 1 in Appendix A of the online companion). As a result, we pick six topics to categorize patent terms with a coherence score of 0.208. The first column in Table 1 (“Main terms”) shows the top five most representative terms appearing in patents for the six innovation topics. We note that each of the six topics has a distinguishable set of most representative terms. Therefore, each latent topic represents a distinctive innovation topic. However, we do find supporting evidence for such topic labeling in the following discussion.

## Stage 3 - Discovery of TIS

This section describes the use of the topical innovation structure for measuring relatedness between technology classes (i.e., four-character CPC codes) and discovering specific technological innovation systems.

Technology Relatedness and TIS Component Search: To discover specific TISs, each technology class s is represented as a discrete probability vector $\boldsymbol { w } _ { s } = [ \mathrm { P } _ { c 1 } , \mathrm { P } _ { c 2 } , . . . , \mathrm { P } _ { c 6 } ]$ over the six innovation topics $( c 1 , c 2 , \ldots c 6 )$ based on the patent frequency in each topic. The vector representations are then used to measure pairwise technology relatedness between technology classes (s1 and s2) by employing the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) measure [35].

$$
J S D \left(w _ {s 1} \| w _ {s 2}\right) = H \left(\frac {w _ {s 1} + w _ {s 2}}{2}\right) - \frac {H \left(w _ {s 1}\right) + H \left(w _ {s 2}\right)}{2}\tag{1}
$$

where $H ( w _ { s } )$ denotes the Shannon entropy for the probability vector $\boldsymbol { w } _ { s } .$ . In essence, for two identical probability distributions, i.e., ${  { \boldsymbol } w _ { s 1 } } = {  { \boldsymbol } w _ { s 2 } }$ , the JSD value is 0. Given that the JSD is bounded by 0 and 1, we let $1 - J S D ^ { \frac { 1 } { 2 } }$ indicate the relatedness between a pair of technology classes, which yields:

$$
\text { Technology   Relatedness } _ {w _ {s 1}, w _ {s 2}} = 1 - J S D (w _ {s 1} \parallel w _ {s 2}) ^ {\frac {1}{2}}\tag{2}
$$

We refer to this as technology relatedness. For a given focal technology class, we can sort all other technology classes by their technology relatedness with the focal class. This facilitates the search for TIS components.

Thresholding and TIS: We want to retain a collection of related classes with reasonably high relatedness to the focal technology class while deriving a TIS around the focal class. For this purpose, only the related classes above the 90th percentile on the technology relatedness metric are identified as TIS components. Finally, the focal and its related classes together form a TIS, as discovered by the framework.

## Innovation Metrics

We now validate the efficacy of the TIS discovery framework by estimating its usefulness in predicting future innovation intensity both in terms of quantity (i.e., patents) and quality (i.e., citations). Specifically, we operationalize several innovation metrics based on the past patenting output in the related technologies to predict future patenting in and citations to a focal class. The proposed metrics build on the previously discussed spillover expectation that innovation in a focal class should be associated with the innovation taking place in the related classes (i.e., positive externality across related technologies). If the TIS and the consequent innovation metrics are valid, they should provide support for this association. If the association indeed exists, then we can argue that the discovered TISs are an effective way to capture horizontal relations between the technology classes from the vertical CPC tree.

We also come up with a boundary-spanning innovation metric of a focal class over the six innovation topics to quantify interdisciplinarity. It is important to control the boundary spanning at the technology class-level given its potential role in innovation output. The existing literature emphasizes the positive impact of boundary-spanning on innovation output [55, 66]. However, there is some evidence suggesting that boundary-spanning innovation may also face challenges and be less productive due to collaborative difficulties and cost [18, 32, 48]. Therefore, the overall effect of boundary spanning within a technology class remains an empirical question. In addition, motivated by Acemoglu et al. [1], we create a citation-based predictor to model inter-class spillovers where the technology classes are linked through citations and use it as a control variable in estimations. The citation-based predictor also serves as an important benchmark from the literature for machine learningbased prediction experiments.

We now describe the operationalization of several innovation metrics. The proposed innovation metrics serve as predictors for predicting future innovation quantity and quality (outcome variables) in a technology class.

● Related-Tech Innovation (RTI): We use the normalized technology relatedness (as weights) to compute a weighted average of the past patenting intensity in related technology classes (from TIS) for a given focal technology class.

$$
\text { Relatedness   Weight } _ {j, j ^ {\prime}} = \frac {\text { Technology   Relatedness } (j , j ^ {\prime})}{\sum_ {k \neq j} \text { Technology   Relatedness } (j , k)}\tag{3}
$$

Here, j refers to the focal class (four-character CPC) for which we are predicting, and $j ^ { \prime }$ denotes one of the related technology classes (k). Each pairwise relatedness is then normalized by the sum of the relatedness for all pairs. The normalized weight is combined with the patenting volume in a related technology class for the past five years as below.

$$
\text { Related } - \text { Tech   Innovation } (R T I) _ {j, t - 1} = \sum_ {k \neq j} \text { Relatedness   Weight } _ {j, k} \sum_ {t - 5} ^ {t - 1} P _ {k, i}\tag{4}
$$

$P _ { k , i }$ is the patenting volume in a related class k of the focal class j in a year i. Consequently, this weighted average becomes the Related-Tech Innovation (RTI) predictor for a focal technology class.

● Boundary-Spanning Innovation (BSI): To measure the boundary-spanning nature of a technology class, we calculate information entropy for each technology class with the probability distribution over the six innovation topics.

$$
\text {Boundary Spanning Innovation} (B S I) _ {j, t - 1} = - \sum_ {C = 1} ^ {6} P _ {j, C, t - 1} \times \log_ {2} P _ {j, C, t - 1}\tag{5}
$$

In this metric, $P _ { j , C , t - 1 }$ is a probability of a patent in the class (j) to be assigned to the innovation topic C in the past 5 years (until t− 1). A higher entropy is indicative of a higher dispersion over topics and hence indicative of the boundary-spanning nature of a technology class.

● Related-Tech Innovation Quality (RTIQ): Citations to a patent are seen as a measure of its impact or quality [23]. Such utility of patent citation data has been demonstrated over the last two decades in the innovation literature [28]. Hence the quality of innovation in a technology class can be measured by accounting for the sum of citations to the patents in the class. As a result, we propose the Related-Tech Innovation Quality (RTIQ) metric for future citations using the relatedness weight in the same manner as Equation 4.

$$
\text { Related } - \text { Tech   Innovation   Quality } (R T I Q) _ {j, t - 1} = \sum_ {k \neq j} \text { Relatedness   Weight } _ {j, k} \sum_ {t - 5} ^ {t - 1} C _ {k, i}\tag{6}
$$

$C _ { k , i }$ is the citation volume scaled by the annual average in a related class k in year i. Consequently, this weighted average serves as a predictor of future innovation quality of a focal technology class.

● Citation-based Related-Tech Innovation (CRTI): Patent citations can also be used for modeling the associations between technology classes in terms of knowledge spillover. In fact, Acemoglu et al. [1] show that citation records are associated with future innovation. Based on their work, we utilize a measure analogous to RTI based on intertechnology citations instead of TIS. The weights between classes are then based on patent citations instead of technology-relatedness.

$$
\text { Cite   Weight } _ {j \rightarrow j ^ {\prime}} = \frac {\text { Cite } _ {j \rightarrow j ^ {\prime}}}{\sum_ {k \neq j} \text { Cite } _ {j \rightarrow k}}\tag{7}
$$

The citation weight is based on the normalized count of citations from class j to class j<sup>0</sup>. It intuitively quantifies the relative importance of j<sup>0</sup> to j.

$$
\begin{array}{l}\text { Citation   -   based   Related   -   Tech   Innovation(CRTI) } _ {j, t - 1}\\= \sum_ {k \neq j} \text { Cite   Weight } _ {j \rightarrow k} \sum_ {t - 5} ^ {t - 1} P _ {k, i}\end{array}\tag{8}
$$

This weight is then combined with the past patenting volume (as a weighted average) to create a citation-based related-tech innovation metric (CRTI) in a manner similar to the related-tech innovation metric (RTI). As in Equation 4, $P _ { k , i }$ is the patenting volume in a related class k in year i. Hence, we are accumulating patenting activity in a related technology class in the past 5 years. CRTI is used as a control (or benchmark) to account for an alternative way (based on literature [1]) to model inter-technology relatedness that does not use text semantics.

## Explanatory Modeling

We now evaluate the validity of our design artifact (i.e., TIS discovery framework) as suggested by [25]. For this purpose, we specify the explanatory empirical models that we use to validate the TIS discovery framework and the consequent innovation metrics. We do so by estimating the associations of the innovation metrics derived using the TISs with the future innovation quantity (patent volume) and quality (patent citations) of the focal technology class. We account for technology class-level heterogeneity through fixed effects. Table 2 describes the two panels used for estimations. We note that the purpose of the explanatory empirical models is to transparently tease out the association between TISbased innovation metrics and future patenting in a technology class. While our proposed metrics are guided by spillover theories, we are not attempting to identify a causal relationship as this is peripheral to the main goal of validating our TIS framework.

## Innovation Quality (Citations)

For the innovation quality dimension, we model the volume of citations to future patents (FC) in a focal technology class for the next 5 years (from year t). We specify FC in terms of the related-tech innovation quality (RTIQ) and other innovation metrics as follows:

$$
F C _ {i, t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \cdot P C _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \cdot R T I Q _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} \cdot B S I _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {4} \cdot R T I Q _ {i, t - 1} \cdot B S I _ {i, t - 1} + \phi_ {i} + \epsilon_ {i, t}\tag{10}
$$

The time window (t) for our analysis begins in 2008 and ends in 2012. We allow for five years of citation lags (e.g., considering citations of a 2016 patent until 2021). Hence, we consider patents filed until 2016 for Equation 10 while accounting for their citations till the end of our data in 2021. More specifically, given the 5-year time horizon for prediction as measured by FC, predicting in 2012 (t) includes accounting for the future citations to patents filed in 2012-2016.

As a result, it is reasonable to end our time horizon (t) in 2012. The decision to consider five-year lags is based on a conservative estimate from the innovation literature.<sup>7</sup> In addition, to account for the cumulative nature of citations over time,<sup>8</sup> we scale the actual citation counts of patents by the annual average for patents filed in a given year. We note that the citation-based related-tech innovation (CRTI) in Equation 8 is excluded from our current analysis on innovation quality as CRTI was based on a work in the literature [1] that argued for citation-based downstream effects on innovation quantity, but not quality.

We focus on $\beta _ { 2 }$ , the coefficient on the related-tech innovation quality (RTIQ), the metric of innovation quality in the TIS-based related technologies. A positive and significant coefficient would show that high-quality innovation in related technologies is associated with more high-quality innovation in a given focal class. This is a result that we would expect from a valid TIS discovery that has the ability to predict the consequent knowledge spillovers between related technologies. $\beta _ { 3 }$ , corresponding to boundary-spanning innovation (BSI), would measure the association of interdisciplinarity within a technology class on innovation quality. ## Predictive Modeling

Our explanatory empirical analysis helped to tease out the utility of TIS discovery and the consequent innovation metrics for estimating future innovation intensity and quality within technology classes. While transparent and helpful for isolating individual associations, the explanatory empirical models are constrained in terms of their predictive power. This is due to the constraints in the functional form (i.e., linear) as well as the inability to utilize bias-variance trade-offs for improving out-of-sample predictions. Machine learning models are not bound by these constraints as their focus is squarely on prediction quality. Hence to understand the full utility of our TIS discovery and the proposed innovation metrics, we now build predictive models using machine learning, wherein inputs to a model are the innovation metrics (e.g., RTI, BSI) and past patent volume (PP) or citation count (PC) for a technology class.

We perform two separate prediction tasks for estimating future innovation quantity and quality for a technology class. The target variables in the two prediction tasks are the number of patents in the next year (Task 1) and the number of citations in the next year (Task 2), for a technology class. The predictor/input variables are still based on cumulative innovation volume for the past five years. The setup of the target variable (i.e., annual patents or citations) is partly driven by the need to create a separate hold-out data set (also called test data) for evaluation, which is necessary to measure the prediction quality. As a result, future information beyond the training period is not available for modeling the target variable. For example, when predicting for 2017 (test set), information is available only until 2016 (training set); hence, the training set cannot include cumulative future innovation volume (i.e., 2017 and onward) in the target variable. Also, from a purely predictive analytics standpoint, the prediction of the immediate future is likely to be more practical and useful.<sup>9</sup> Table 6 describes specific dataset configurations in terms of years (and size) of data used for training, validation (for tuning hyperparameters), and testing (for performance measurement) for each of the two tasks.

The current experiments utilize a linear model with L1-regularization (commonly referred to as LASSO), in addition to a variety of decision tree-based algorithms, which include Classification and Regression Trees (CART), Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Moreover, Long-Short Term Memory (LSTM), a class of recurrent neural networks commonly deployed for time series forecasting, is also employed. These models are popular in the machine learning literature due to their strong performances across a variety of datasets as well as easily available and robust implementations. Each experiment is conducted 10 times with bootstrapped instances in the training set, and the resulting test set performance is used to estimate the average and standard error.

## Task 1 – Innovation Quantity

In Task 1, we train models to predict future patenting volume (FP) in a given year and technology class. We evaluate the trained models based on their ability to correctly predict for the year 2017 (the hold-out year whose data is not used for training). Starting with the baseline feature of the past patenting volume (PP), predictors are incrementally added to the models based on their type. In particular, we are interested in the incremental predictive utility of innovation metrics that we propose.

The resulting performances are evaluated on three metrics - root mean squared error (RMSE), root relative squared error (RRSE), and coefficient of determination $( R ^ { 2 } )$ . RMSE is a popular evaluation metric in machine learning and is commonly used in various predictive modeling tasks, including time series forecasting. RMSE is a good measure of how accurately the model predicts the response, and it is especially useful when large errors are particularly undesirable. We also examine RRSE in addition to RMSE because it can be relatively easily interpreted; an RRSE value greater than 1 indicates that the model underperforms the mean predictor (i.e., using mean value as the predicted outcome). We also provide $R ^ { 2 }$ for completeness, despite its limitations in capturing the performance of complex machine learning models as it does not directly assess the prediction error.

Table 7 shows the performance of the four models (columns) and different sets of predictors (rows) tested on the hold-out data on patenting volume from 2017. The results with RMSE clearly show that the proposed innovation metrics significantly contribute to the performance of LSTM and the tree-based models (i.e., CART, RF, XGBoost). These are also the models that achieve the best performance overall, indicating the need to utilize the non-linearity that neural network models and tree-based methods do by design.

In particular, for the LSTM model, using the proposed innovation metrics (Past+Cite +Innov in Table 7) yields a 40.11% improved average RMSE (p-value < 0.0001) than using only the past innovation in the technology class and the past innovation in related classes based on citations (Past+Cite in Table 7). Past patenting (Past or PP) and Citation-based Related-tech Innovation (Cite or CRTI) are important benchmarks based on previous literature [1]. A similar pattern of statistically significant better performance is observed with respect to RRSE when we use LSTM using all of the proposed innovation metrics. To a lesser degree, a similar pattern is also noted with $R ^ { 2 }$ . Hence, the most effective approach is to build an LSTM model with all of the proposed metrics.

## Task 2 – Innovation Quality

In Task 2, we train models to predict future citation volume (FC) for patents filed in a given year and technology class. We evaluate the trained models based on their ability to correctly predict for patents filed in the year 2016 (hold-out year whose data is not used for training). The baseline feature is the past citation volume (PC) in a given technology class for the previous five years. We are again interested in the incremental predictive utility of the proposed innovation metrics based on TIS. We note that the citation-based related-tech innovation (CRTI) is not included as a feature in Task 2, because it is based on a theoretical argument on the role of citations in determining the downstream effect on innovation quantity (not quality) [1]. ## Discussion and Implications

From a managerial standpoint, a key contribution of our work is in proposing symbiotic technology candidates in a TIS to be considered for developing firms’ patent portfolio strategy [34]. An efficient budget allocation in the R&D portfolio is important since stakeholders evaluate portfolio compositions (i.e., types of technologies) and synergy between constituents. A positive assessment of the portfolios drives more investment and cash flow to the firms, leading to a virtuous cycle that brings competitive advantages to them [37]. More specifically, firms can strategize their patent portfolios by adequately combining interrelated TIS technology components into their baskets, which is possible as a result of our TIS discovery framework. Such well-structured portfolios could create more technological spillovers in patenting output than incoherent diversification, i.e., spending on unrelated technologies.

To examine the role of the technological innovation systems (TIS) within a patent portfolio context, we posit that innovating in a cluster of related technologies integrated within a TIS can constitute a more valuable patent portfolio for a firm. To test this empirically, we employ the patent capital metric, measured by Woeppel [67]. The patent capital metric estimates the market value of a firm’s patent stock based on the 3-day idiosyncratic return following the announcement of newly granted patents.<sup>10</sup>

For the firm-level analysis, we take firms’ patent capital [67] as the dependent variable while introducing the main independent variable of interest, termed TIS score. This metric computes the number of a firm’s patents in related technologies based on TIS discovery as a fraction of all patents of the firm. As a result, the TIS score ranges from 0 to 1.

$$
T I S \text { Score } _ {i, t} = \frac {\text { Related   Tech   Patents } _ {i , t}}{\text { Patent   Stock } _ {i , t}}\tag{11}
$$

Related Tech $P a t e n t s _ { i , t }$ refers to the cumulative patent volume of firm i that is in related technology classes based on discovered TISs over a five-year period up to year t, similar to Equation 4. Patent $S t o c k _ { i , t }$ includes the total patent volume of firm i over the same five-year span leading up to year t. In simpler terms, the TIS score is akin to a portfolio alignment score, wherein firms prioritizing TIS technologies exhibit higher TIS scores.

A positive coefficient would indicate that firms prioritizing TIS technologies in their current patenting tend to have higher patent capital in the following year (i.e., a higher market value of patents in the future). Such a positive coefficient would underscore the role of TIS technologies in acting as a synergistic patent portfolio for firms, leading to positive innovation performance in the future as measured by the increased market value of their patent stock [67]. To rigorously tease out the association between the TIS score and patent capital in the future, we control for patent stock size [30].

The results suggest that firms focusing on TIS technologies during their current patenting activities tend to experience an increase in patent value in the subsequent years. Therefore, our TIS discovery framework could assist firms in designing their patent portfolio. This benefit, observed in the rise in a firm’s patent capital, is possibly a result of innovation spillovers that emerge from the interplay of related technologies incorporated within the firm’s patent portfolios.

Another implication for managers is through our predictive modeling that provides an ML-based technique to identify technology classes that are expected to show strong innovation intensity and quality in the near future. This can help them better prioritize their investments across a candidate set of technologies. In Figure 5, we demonstrate a practical application of our TIS discovery framework. This use case outlines a scenario where an R&D manager at a company, such as Intel, leverages our framework to identify technologies related to their existing portfolio of patented innovations. It illustrates the potential to pinpoint relevant technology classes for strategic investment. Additionally, this tool could prove useful for public research organizations, such as the NSF, in pursuing similar objectives.

Institutional investors such as endowments, hedge funds, or actively managed mutual funds can make investment decisions in firms based on the composition of their patent portfolios. Additionally, firms in the market for merging with and acquiring other firms can evaluate patent portfolios of possible target firms utilizing our TIS discovery framework.

Our framework has the potential to be useful to various organizations, including federal research agencies like the US National Science Foundation (NSF) and the US National Institutes of Health (NIH). These agencies might find our TIS discovery framework and predictive models helpful as one of several tools to enhance their decision-making process regarding the allocation of research funding. The funding agencies source from taxpayers to provide financial support to researchers; therefore, the budget allocation needs to be efficient so that it maximizes social good. In line with this expectation, the agencies can set up a long-term goal of fostering a promising focal technology (e.g., Semiconductors) along with its related technologies in a TIS (e.g., Plasma technique) discovered by our framework for the purpose of a collective funding process. In summary, our study can provide R&D investment intelligence for both public and private innovation stakeholders.

In terms of implications for academic research, our study answers the call for the automated identification of TISs [8, 10]. The previous TIS literature has been limited to specific TIS cases that are identified through manual expertise [4, 60]. Our automated TIS discovery framework could allow researchers to easily obtain additional test beds for further theoretical validations. In this manner, research findings can become more generalizable as they are corroborated with TISs that span a wide variety of sectors and time periods. For this purpose, we detail the entire TIS discovery framework as illustrated in Figure 3. We would also like to emphasize that the data (e.g., USPTO Database) and analytic tools are publicly available and updated on a regular basis, which should ease the follow-up use by other studies. More broadly, our TIS discovery framework utilizing patent text finds strong evidence of horizontal innovation that cuts through the vertical CPC structure. This supports the literature that sheds light on the boundary-spanning nature of current technological innovation, e.g., [66]. This could motivate the need for proper and timely CPC modifications to capture the horizontal relations and address challenges in the patent review process caused by boundary-spanning trends.

## Conclusion and Future Work

Utilizing over four million patents, this study develops a design artifact for the automated discovery of TISs and provides its empirical validation through explanatory and predictive models. Our proposed framework begins with deriving the topical innovation structure and subsequently using it for finding specific TISs. We propose several innovation metrics that capture the innovation activity in related technology classes of a TIS. The fact that these innovation metrics are significantly associated with future innovation intensity and quality substantiates our framework. We also tease out the significant predictive utility of the innovation metrics with popular machine learning models using a hold-out dataset. Accurate prediction of future innovation intensity in different technologies can allow managers and investors to allocate capital efficiently.

By proposing the TIS discovery framework, this study answers the long-existing need for the easily scalable identification of specific TISs [10]. In future studies related to the TIS, it may be worthwhile to empirically study how technologies in a specific TIS are responsive to inducement (e.g., federal R&D subsidies) or blocking (e.g., the rise of competing new technologies) mechanisms. We expect future empirical studies using our framework to locate a particular TIS of interest and delve into more sophisticated innovation mechanisms in that TIS. Such research would be conducive to the long-term R&D policies of innovation stakeholders. There also exist future opportunities to create metrics of alignment between actual and predicted investments in the TIS framework at the firm level and study the effects of such alignment on various measures of firm value such as Tobin’s Q [46]. Also, there may be other potential methods, such as advanced deep learning (DL) methods for discovering TISs that are different from our framework [53], and we expect this study to motivate such follow-up works.
