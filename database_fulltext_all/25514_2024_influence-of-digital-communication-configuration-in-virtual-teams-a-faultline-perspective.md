# Influence of Digital Communication Configuration in Virtual Teams: A Faultline Perspective

Sut I Wong $^{a}$ , Limei Zhang $^{b}$ , Matej Černe $^{c}$ , and Nils Brede Moe $^{d}$

$^{a}$ BI Norwegian Business School, Department of Communication and Culture, Nordic Centre for Internet and Society, Oslo, Norway; $^{b}$ School of Management, Xi'an Jiaotong University; $^{c}$ School of Economics and Business, University of Ljubljana; $^{d}$ SINTEF Digital, Trondheim, Norway

## ABSTRACT

The recent and swift move to virtual working has highlighted a need for greater understanding of how to best communicate remotely for work, how well team members are able to identify the correct communication mediums for their tasks and how team members align on how to complete these tasks. Drawing on Media Synchronicity Theory (MST), we examine communication-based subgroup dynamics in terms of faultline strength—the degree to which a team is hypothetically divided by lines of communication media they use—and subgroup imbalance regarding the influence of communication-based subgroup dynamics on virtual team sociability, and subsequently, team coordination. Results from a field study (Study 1) with 292 team members belonging to 37 virtual teams, and an experiment (Study 2) with 385 participants provided support for the hypotheses. Our findings contribute to existing theory by underscoring the idea that it is not simply virtuality, but the misalignment of the use of communication mediums among team members that plays a key role in the development of communication challenges in virtual teams. The study takes a novel approach by addressing how faultline strength within virtual teams that can cause communication challenges. Consequently, organizations are encouraged to pay attention to guidelines of the use of virtual communication tools and team composition to enhance communication and collaboration within virtual teams.

## KEYWORDS

communication-based fault lines; subgroup imbalance; team sociability; team coordination; virtual teams; group dynamics; team fault lines

## Introduction

In the past two decades, we have witnessed the rise of virtual teams, teams consisting of members who are geographically dispersed and heavily reliant on digital communication technology to coordinate $[50]$ . Virtual team coordination refers to the process by which team members orchestrate the timing and sequencing of interdependent activities that enable the achievement of their common goal $[68]$ . The general rise of virtual teams and virtual coordination has occurred for various reasons, including remote work enabling strategic goals via increased employee autonomy and flexibility $[23,24,75]$ , greater access to tools that support virtual teams, and thus supporting access to distributed employee talent without geographic restraint $[26, 27]$ , and trends towards globalization $[52]$ . While the shift to virtual teams is not new, the recent acceleration due to the COVID-19 pandemic foretells a future workplace being increasingly reliant on technology, driving the need to better understand how team members coordinate in such a context $[42]$ . Due to limited in-person interactions, achieving goals heavily depends on how team members use digital communication technologies, such as team meetings, instant messaging, emails, etc., to coordinate activities $[48, 49, 50]$ . This upward trend in the use of digital communication tools gives rise to challenges, as current technologies are inadequately supporting the growing need for human communication in virtual environments.

The coordination of virtual team members is highly reliant on their willingness and effort contributed to share knowledge and avoid knowledge hoarding $[1, 2]$ . Given that distributed teams utilize electronic media rather than face-to-face interaction, the only behavioral evidence available to team members to establish rapport is the communication behaviors of other members $[72]$ . Individuals have shown to perceive digital environments as more risky in general than face-to-face environments when approaching communication, inherently reducing opportunity to develop trust via communication $[71]$ . Moreover, remote team members often leverage multiple communication tools with diverse media synchronicity to complete their teamwork, largely or exclusively in accordance with their own personal needs and preferences $[74]$ . There is no guarantee that team members share similar preferences and levels of engagement with various digital tools available $[26]$ . These barriers associated with digital teams, in addition to lacking a shared social context, make coordination a large concern $[72]$ . The (dis)similarities in media reliance are important to consider, because different communication technologies provide different informational value $[3]$ . According to Media Synchronicity Theory (MST), which considers the extent to which a communication medium is able to support group members to achieve synchronicity, team communication is influenced by the success of orchestrating various media used in the team $[21]$ . Furthermore, team members simply being aware of one another's communication practices has shown to improve coordination and feelings of social presence within the team and willingness to work harder $[30, 58]$ . More recent digital communication research underlines the impact of how team members use different media to communicate from a network perspective [e.g., 12, 26]. In particular, some of these studies explore how to establish awareness and access to subgroups within a team to facilitate managers' enable-ment of their employees, subsequently increasing performance and the employee experience. That said, the way in which multi communication media is configured across subgroups, rather than the richness of a single medium used [e.g., 34, 50], remains under-explored and is important to address in research endeavors.

Therefore, in this study we aim to explore how the degree of team communication-based faultline strength influences virtual team coordination. Based on faultline research, we define team communication-based faultlines as hypothetical dividing lines that split a group into relatively homogeneous subgroups $[41]$ based on the team members' communication pattern alignment across various digital communication tools. In other words, members of the same subgroup show a synchronized use of media, such that they display similar extents of reliance on similar communication tools, while members across subgroups have distinct patterns of media reliance. The level of faultline strength describes the dissimilarity between members separated by faultlines $[41]$ . The stronger the faultline the greater the distinction between media utilization patterns of members across subgroups.

We suggest that strong faultlines indicate fragmented interdependence and synchronicity within a team, thus hindering team members' abilities to socialize, and subsequently leading to weaker coordination. When communication-based subgroups divided by strong faultlines are imbalanced, those from larger subgroups may dominate interactions within the team, marginalizing the voices of minority members from team communication $[15]$ . This marginalization, in turn, decreases team sociability and coordination. Accordingly, we propose that the negative relationship between team communication-based faultline strength and team coordination via sociability will be stronger when such communication-based subgroup dynamics are imbalanced (i.e., unequally sized subgroups) than when they are balanced (i.e., subgroups are equally sized).

By combining faultline and media synchronicity theories, our study first intends to expand the understanding of virtual team communication. Dimensions of virtual team communication that are commonly studied primarily include frequency, quality, content (e.g., relation vs task-oriented, unique vs common information sharing), climate/style (e.g., psychologically safe communication climate, openness of communication) and medium (e.g., synchronic vs asynchronous, the degree of virtuality). These themes vary, but largely align with the idea that the teams with members who agree or adhere to similar patterns tend to be more successful. We attempt to address this underlying idea by focusing not on one specific communication factor, such as frequency, but on the overall (dis)similarity of digital communication media reliance and its impact on the extent to which team members coordinate. By employing a novel approach to analyzing faultline strength of digital media configuration and how it relates to effective coordination, we first aim to contribute to the literature on teamwork in virtual settings and MST $[21]$ . Second, we aim to shed light on the effectiveness of communication media and refocus the discussion on “which pattern of digital communication media benefits team functioning” rather than “which medium is effective in a specific task.” By showing how this relationship between communication pattern and team functioning benefits from more balanced communication-based subgroups through team sociability, these findings can be translated to practical insights on how communication technology can be used to coordinate effectively and improve virtual teamwork.

## Theory and Hypotheses

## Digital Communication Configuration and Virtual Team Coordination

The use of virtual teams, which refer to geographically dispersed teammates working together via computer-mediated communications, has rapidly increased in popularity in recent years $[16, 21]$ . Benefits of working in a virtual team compared to a traditional face-to-face team include more flexibility in when and where work is carried out $[23]$ , more autonomous work, the possibility of connecting in new ways $[76]$ , and enhanced job performance $[28]$ . Moreover, researchers have found that virtual teams, compared to face-to-face teams, can better overcome challenges associated with increased team size $[45]$ , due to computer-mediated communication tools. Instant chat, video conferencing, online forums, and digital enterprise social networking tools (e.g., Slack and HipChat) likely make collaborating and knowledge sharing in virtual teams easier and less bureaucratic, as well as promote increased transparency, team awareness, and spontaneous informal communication [72]. However, not all communication media are equally rich and efficient in serving various information and social needs [10, 20].

Media Richness Theory (MRT) argues that media vary in information richness based on capacity to facilitate shared meaning in a given time interval $[18]$ . Accordingly, media richness is determined based on the availability of immediate feedback, multiple cues, language variety, and personal focus $[37]$ . The premise underlying MRT is that organizations and leaders should select the most appropriate medium by matching media richness to a particular task's informational needs $[18]$ . However, researchers of the impact of media in dispersed teams found that teams communicate effectively or poorly regardless of the media used, which indicates that potential boundary conditions might exist $[32, 35]$ . In their literature review, Gilson and colleagues found that the choice of technological communication tool per se has either an impairing or no effect on the performance of virtual teams $[27]$ . More likely, it is how these different media are being used in the teams that matters. Gibson, Dunlop, Majchrzak, and Chiad (2022), for example, found that the most effective teams are effective at matching communication medium to task, recognizing when a communication media change is needed to support information sharing $[25]$ . This work highlights that successful virtual teams adapt their use of communication technologies based on their knowledge management activities, recognizing the importance of evolving their communication technology configurations in a way that less successful teams fail to do $[25]$ . Other researchers highlight boundary-spanning individuals who share communication across sub-groups $[41]$ . These insights provide valuable context for the different ways in which configurations of communication technology have been studied in virtual team contexts.

MST builds upon MRT, arguing that the fit of a particular type of media to a particular type of task influences how well the media is able to work for a team, and in turn, influences a team's communication performance as a whole $[22, 32]$ . This theory does not wholly subscribe to the idea that richer media equates to better media, but instead to the idea that certain media are more appropriate for certain activities $[31]$ . Rather than relying solely on how rich any specific communication medium is, MST focuses on how well the medium is able to be utilized by team members to communicate their points and have their points understood by team members. These two areas of communication, conveyance and convergence, require both information transmission and information processing, often in different proportions $[21]$ . With this more dynamic viewing lens for communication media, it is likely that multiple mediums are appropriate for use within a team for different activities.

In contrast to previous researchers who examined one medium at a time, in this study, we apply the faultline analytic approach to capture the configuration of team members' multiple media reliance. In our case, faultlines separate virtual team members into relatively homogenous subgroups based on their choices of using various communication media $[75]$ . Faultlines can take multiple media into account when studying the (dis)similarity effect of these different media on team coordination. The strength of a faultline refers to the distinction between members as separated by faultlines. That is, the stronger the faultline is, the more distinct the communication patterns are between the subgroups $[54]$ , which, in a remote setting, can entirely separate workers with different preferred communication mediums. In addition, these subgroups could be similar in size (balanced) or different in size (imbalanced). ## Team Sociability as a Mediator between Digital Communication Tool Faultline Strength and Virtual Team Coordination

Increased team virtuality can disrupt work routines and thrust employees into unfamiliar situations, in which their old experiences make them shocked and challenged by their new work setting $[48]$ . Thus, employees need to adjust to their new environment as it relates to shared experiences, which involves the creation of tacit knowledge on communication processes in forming social relations between team members $[57]$ . Indeed, social interaction is a dominant factor impacting collaboration and performance $[42]$ and can be challenging in virtual team arrangements when communication channels are limited to mostly digital media $[47]$ .

Team sociability refers to the extent to which team members can facilitate a social space where affective work relationships and a strong sense of community are present $[44]$ . Members of a team with high sociability engage in communication processes related to acquiring information and behaviors through socializing $[4]$ . Researchers have found that the social effects of knowledge sharing in virtual teams positively influence trust and collaboration $[2]$ . We therefore argue that the existence of strong communication-based faultlines, with less cohesive digital communication tool reliance patterns among team members is likely to hinder team sociability. A weaker team communication based faultline would indicate team-wide and inclusive patterns of interaction, more synchronized use of media, and are likely to result in better shared experiences for team sociability.

Conversely, differences in team members' communication patterns can hinder social interactions across subgroups [63]. For instance, all members equally engaging in an instant chat app is more likely to form a collective social environment compared to some members engaging in the app and others communicating via email. This is particularly important for virtual teams, because unlike face-to-face teams, there is not the opportunity for in-person interactions to compensate for poor digital communication. Further, a strong team communication based faultline (i.e., a less cohesive team digital communication configuration) is likely to hinder members from socializing. Subsequently, we argue that virtual teams that have less ability to socialize due to unsynchronized and uncoordinated communication are likely to experience coordination problems. Therefore, we posit that:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Virtual team sociability mediates the negative relationship between communication-based fault line strength and virtual team coordination.

## Communication-Based Subgroup Imbalance as a Moderator of the Mediated Relationship

Another way to understand the configurational characteristics of communication in virtual teams is to consider the balance of subgroups divided by faultlines. The degree of balance in subgroups refers to the composition of subgroups in terms of majorities and minorities $[15]$ , and in this context, communication-based subgroup (im)balance. As examples of (im)balance conditions are shown in Table 1 or Figure 1, teams with balanced subgroups (Team 3 & Team 4 in Table 1) have similar numbers of members across subgroups. Conversely, teams with imbalanced subgroups (Team 1 & Team 2) have different numbers of members across subgroups. Balanced subgroups allow for different sources of knowledge to be included and shared, preventing groupthink and amplifying the sharing of task-relevant information $[14, 15, 76]$ .

The information flow between team members can also provide insight into the social fabric of a team $[52, 53]$ . Some researchers have shown that norms and shared mental models prescribing the use of specific communication technology are socially constructed over time $[62]$ , for example, via leader briefings or training $[51]$ , while other researchers found that individuals adapt the way they use media in a target- and situation-appropriate manner $[31]$ . These findings indicate that jointly created norms guide the use of media in teams. Thus, when communication-based subgroups exist and are imbalanced, we expect that those from stronger subgroups may dominate interactions within the team. Given that team members have different asynchronous communication patterns across subgroups, the minorities cannot effectively exert their voices towards the majority and are excluded from team communication $[15]$ , which subsequently hurts team sociability. Conversely, when subgroups are more balanced, it is likely that various subgroup members are more equally represented at the team level and jointly contribute to forming team norms, resulting in higher collective norms and sense of belonging, in contrast to imbalanced subgroups $[15]$ . The negative impact of different synchronous and asynchronous communication patterns within the team is likely to be attenuated, having less of a negative impact on the shared social experiences and subsequently, team coordination. Therefore, we posit that:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Subgroup imbalance moderates the mediated relationship between digital communication tool fault line strength, virtual team sociability, and team coordination, such that the negative relationship will be strengthened when the subgroup imbalance is high.

## Methods

## Participants and Procedures

To test our hypotheses, we recruited 394 team members working for three companies in Norway between May and June 2020. The first company is in the maritime sector, delivering B2B products. This company has 3700 employees and is globally distributed with a headquarter in Norway. The second company is in Norwegian fintech, which delivers B2B and B2C products. The fintech organization has over 600 employees and is owned by an alliance of banks. The third company is a Norwegian technology and investment company with 80 employees delivering B2B and B2C products. The companies were all part of a research project on teamwork, digital transformation and innovation in turbulent contexts. Each team had personnel with different roles, such as software developers, testers, designers, user experience, team leads, and product owners. The teams worked with different agile methods depending on what problems they were addressing, and they had considerable freedom in how they worked. Most teams used a Kanban variant with Scrum elements and several deliberation practices, such as backlog meetings, team meetings, and daily stand-ups. Some teams also followed Lean Startup principles. The teams used a suite of digital collaboration tools to support work within and across teams, including the project-management platform, Jira, the software version control services, GitHub and Maven, Confluence for knowledge sharing, SharePoint and Google docs for sharing documents, Trello for task management, Slack for instant messaging, Snapchat groups for social sharing, and Microsoft Teams, Zoom, and Anywhere for videoconferences and chat. These tools were readily available and team members had a high degree of autonomy in deciding which tools to engage with to go about their work. All participants were informed of the voluntary nature of their participation, procedures for completing the questionnaires, and the confidentiality of their responses. We asked team members to report their reliance on various communication tools and assess team sociability and team coordination.

A total of 320 team members (81.22 percent response rate) responded, and 10 responses were excluded due to unusable responses (completed less than 20 percent of the survey). Because our hypotheses address team behaviors and subgroups, we dropped five teams from which only one member responded and three teams with less than three employed members. ## Measures

Unless otherwise noted, all measures used a Likert response scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). All survey questions were presented in Norwegian. We followed the translation and back-translation procedures advocated for in previous research when translating the English items to Norwegian [9]. All the items are presented in Online Supplemental Appendix 1.

Digital Communication Tools Reliance. We measured reliance on digital communication tools using Gibson and Gibbs' scale to capture the degree to which individuals were dependent on various computer-mediated communication tools to stay in touch with their team members for going about their work $[26]$ . We asked participants to indicate their reliance on email, chat, phone, teleconferencing, and collaborative software or shared online documents, as well as electronic communication in general, on a scale of 1 (not at all) to 5 (to a very great extent). A sample item was “To what extent do you rely on e-mail in your daily work?”

Faultline Strength and Imbalance of Subgroups. We calculated fault line strength and identified members as part of their respective subgroups based on the average silhouette width (ASW) algorithm, a cluster-based approach that splits team members into subgroups according to their similarity in terms of which digital communication tools and to what extent they relied on them, to obtain subgroups with maximum internal homogeneity and maximum between-subgroup heterogeneity $[54]$ . Researchers have adhered to ASW, as it is the only diversity fault line measure that has favorable attributes and accurately determined subgroup membership in the presence of more than two subgroups and more than ten members $[54]$ . Compared to the most frequently employed Fau measure of fault line strength $[54, 78]$ , ASW does not assume the existence of two subgroups, and detects the number of subgroups and their homogeneity in a given team with a cluster-analytic procedure. Furthermore, even though the subgroup algorithm (SGA) can detect multiple subgroups based on the calculation, it is impossible to obtain a reliable subgroup size for teams with more than ten members utilizing SGA $[54]$ . As our sample contains teams with more than ten members, the ASW algorithm is more suitable for the sample in this study $[54, 55]$ .

We identified the subgroup members and calculated the corresponding fault line strength according to employees' six types of communication tools, including email, chat, telephone, video conferencing, collaborative software or shared online documents, and other communication mediums used to engage in teamwork. For each team, the algorithm returned information on the subgroup configuration: the ASW value (fault line strength), the number of subgroups, which team member was placed into which subgroup (member-to-subgroup), and the size of the given subgroup.

The ASW values range from 0 (i.e., a weak fault line, suggesting no homogeneous subgroups exist) to 1 (i.e., a strong fault line, suggesting that all identified subgroups are completely homogeneous). The imbalance of subgroups was calculated based on the standard deviation of subgroup sizes such that less deviation in subgroup sizes results in a greater balance of subgroups [15]. In this sample, the fault line strength ranged from 0 (i.e., a weak fault line strength) to .52 (i.e., a strong fault line strength) (Mean = .30, SD = .10). The imbalance of subgroups ranged from 3.90 (i.e., subgroups are unequal in size) to 0 (i.e., subgroups are equal in size) ( $Mean_{team} = 1.37$ , $SD_{team} = 1.06$ ; $Mean_{individual} = 1.81$ , $SD_{team} = 1.15$ ).

Team Sociability. We assessed sociability with a ten-item scale developed by Kreijns and colleagues $[44]$ . Example items include “This environment enables me to easily contact my teammates” and “This environment enables me to develop good work relationships with my teammates.” To support the between team analysis, we calculated the F-values, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), and $r_{wg}$ values. In the sample, F = 1.56, p < .05, ICC(1) = .08 and ICC(2) = .36. The mean and median $r_{wg(j)}$ values were .91 and .94, respectively $[8, 46]$ , which suggests adequate inter-rater reliability (agreement) of group members’ ratings on team sociability. ICC(1) is the proportion of variance in ratings due to the group membership, which indicates the proportion of between team variance in the total variance. ICC(2) is the reliability of the group mean, which indicates whether group means can be used to reliably differentiate between groups. The high $r_{wg(j)}$ and significant team variance (F = 1.56, p < .05) $[43]$ , supports the between group analysis ( $\alpha = .88$ ).

Team Coordination. Team coordination was rated by team members using a five-item scale from the Transactive Memory System scale developed by Lewis [47]. A sample item is "Our team worked together in a well-coordinated fashion." To support the between team analysis, we calculated the $F$ values, ICCs, and $r_{wg}$ values. The mean and median $r_{wg(j)}$ values were .94 and .95, respectively [8, 46], which suggests adequate inter-rater reliability-(agreement) of group members' ratings of team coordination. In the sample, $\mathrm{F} = 1.53$ , $\mathrm{p} < .05$ , $\mathrm{ICC}(1) = .08$ and $\mathrm{ICC}(2) = .35$ . As $\mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{wg(j)}}$ is high and team variance is significant ( $\mathrm{F} = 1.53$ , $\mathrm{p} < .05$ ) [43], the between group analysis is supported ( $\alpha = .73$ ).

Control Variables. First, we controlled for team size as it may influence sociability and team coordination. Second, members' familiarity may influence their sociability and team coordination. We therefore controlled for the average team tenure of members. Third, we controlled for the means and dispersion (SDs) of the communication tools used to identify communication-based faultline strength in this study $[69]$ .

## Analysis Strategy

We employed multi-level analysis to test our theoretical model. Contrary to the ordinary least square (OLS) analysis, which requires individual-level data aggregation, multi-level analysis separates within-group variance and between-group variance of sociability and team coordination and tested theoretical hypotheses based on the between-group variance $[29]$ . Results would support our hypothesis when the independent variable significantly explains the between-group variance of the outcome variables (H1 and H2). Moreover, to estimate the indirect effect (H2) and conditional indirect effects (H3), we calculated the compound coefficients, which are not normally distributed and may produce Type I errors $[73]$ . We therefore adopted Monte Carlo simulation procedures (i.e., parametric bootstrap with 20,000 repetitions) with R to calculate 95 percent confidence intervals (CIs), as this does not assume that the compound terms (indirect effects and moderation effects) are normally distributed and “yield asymmetric CIs that are faithful to the skewed sampling distributions of the product term” $[67:223]$ . Specifically, the parametric bootstrap uses parameter point estimates and their asymptotic variances and covariances to generate random draws from the parameter distributions. In each draw, the product terms (indirect effects and conditional indirect effects) are computed and replicated a number of times (i.e., 20,000 parametric replications in this research). The resulting simulation distribution of the indirect effect or conditional indirect effect is used to obtain a percentile CI around the observed indirect effects by identifying the values of the distribution defining the lower and upper 2.5 percent of simulated statistics in this research $[67:223]$ . We check whether the 95 percent CIs includes zero to test the significance of the indirect effect and conditional indirect effect.

## Preliminary Analyses

We used Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) procedures to assess the distinctiveness of the two measures based on maximum likelihood (ML) estimation through Mplus 7.4 [59]: sociability and team coordination. CFA results suggested that the data had a modest fit ( $\chi^{2}$ (89) = 265.32, p < 0.05; RMSEA = .09; CFI = .89, TFI = .87, SRMR = .06), thus providing support for the distinctiveness between measures.

Before hypotheses testing, we adapted several methods to test the influence of common method bias. First, following the procedures proposed by Podsakoff et al. [64] and by Williams et al. [81], we tested the influence of common method variance with a separate

CFA model wherein the self-reported items of team coordination and sociability loaded both on their respective factors and on a method factor. The average variance explained in the items by the method factor was 10.96 percent, which is below the 25 percent median reported by researchers for studies using self-reported variables [81]. This result suggested that the method factor lacks a significant influence [60]. Meanwhile, Harman's single-factor test indicated that the first factor takes account for 38.59 percent, which is lower than the principle of .50 [29]. This result also suggested that there is no observable significant common method bias in this study.

Furthermore, common method bias is typically associated with the use of a self-reported approach, which is less vulnerable to the relationships tested in this study. This reduced risk exists because the variables in our model were assessed and operated by different methods: we separated the self-reported perceived team sociability and team coordination to within and between subgroup variances and tested theoretical hypotheses based on the between-group variance, and the independent variable (i.e., fault line strength) and moderator variable (i.e., the imbalance of subgroup) were calculated based on a complex composition score that is based on the individual media reliance. Therefore, we conclude that common method bias is not likely to explain any statistically significant effects observed in this study and we are confident in the validity of our results due to the construct validity evidence for our measures $[17]$ .

## Methods

Findings indicated that fault line strength decreases team coordination via sociability. Furthermore, these relationships were strengthened by the imbalance of subgroups. Notwithstanding, relying on self-reported questionnaire-administered measures of our studied phenomena is likely a limitation, although mitigated to some extent by applying the ASW algorithm to split team members into subgroups according to their digital communication tools used. Moreover, Study 1 is correlational in nature and does not sufficiently establish causality. To address these concerns and adhere to the needs to drive this field forward $[80]$ , we conducted a vignette-based experiment (Study 2).

## Sample and Procedures

We conducted an online experiment and recruited 688 participants through Prolific. The average participant age was 37.91, with 48.4 percent being women, and 70.6 percent having a bachelor's degree or above. We excluded 303 members who failed the attention check, such as responding to the survey without enough duration of time and/or failing to correctly answer the attention check question, retaining a sample of 385 participants. Among the excluded participants, 158 employees responded the survey without enough time, and 244 employees did not correctly answer the attention check "How many members does your team have?". There are no systematic differences in gender ( $M_{retained} = 1.52$ , $SD_{retained} = .55$ ; $M_{excluded} = 1.50$ , $SD_{excluded} = .52$ ; $F(1, 576) = .31$ , p = .576) and education ( $M_{retained} = 4.70$ , $SD_{retained} = 1.24$ ; $M_{excluded} = 4.71$ , $SD_{excluded} = 1.17$ ; $F(1, 576) = .00$ , p = .957) between the participants who were retained and those who were excluded. Meanwhile, the ANOVA results indicated no systematic differences in gender ( $F(8, 366) = .60$ , p = .778) and education ( $F(8, 366) = .71$ , p = .679) between participants across nine conditions. Eventually, we retained a sample of 385 participants with an average age of 38.83, 48.9 percent women, and 70.5 percent having a bachelor's degree or above.

The experiment included a 2 (weak fault line strength vs. strong fault line strength) × 2 (balanced subgroup vs. imbalanced subgroup) × 2 (posited in majority subgroup vs. posited in minority subgroup) (+ control condition) between-subjects design. Whether individuals sat in the majority subgroup or minority subgroup may influence employees' perceived sociability and coordination. Accordingly, although we do not have any hypotheses related to employees' majority or minority, we still employed a $(2 \times 2 \times 2 + \text{control condition})$ design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of nine conditions.

In each condition, we established a different pattern of media use (virtual conferences vs. emails) among team members to manipulate the team's communication-based fault line strength (i.e., strong vs. weak) and subgroup (im)balance. Specifically, in the strong fault line conditions, members were told that they spend 90 percent (or 10 percent) of their time in coordination with team members based on email (or virtual conferences) and 10 percent (or 90 percent) of their time in virtual conferences (or email), while in the weak fault line conditions, members were told that they spend 55 percent (or 45 percent) of their time in coordination with team members based on email (or virtual conferences) and 45 percent (or 55 percent) of their time in virtual conferences (or email). For the balanced subgroup condition, members were evenly distributed in the different patterns of media, with each pattern having four members. For the imbalanced subgroup condition, members were unevenly distributed in the different patterns of media, with two members having a media pattern and six members having another media pattern (for more information, see the Online Supplemental Appendix 2).

## Measures

A five-point Likert scale was used to measure participants' responses (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). We used the same scales as Study 1 (see Online Supplemental Appendix 3) to capture sociability and team coordination. ## Results

## Manipulation check

Table 5 presents the analysis of variance (ANOVA) results used for testing manipulation validity and hypotheses.

We used one item, “Please indicate the extent to which you agree that your team became divided due to the distinct patterns of media use” to check the validity of our manipulation of greater fault line strength. For greater fault line strength manipulations, ANOVA tests revealed that participants in the greater fault line strength condition reported higher scores (M = 3.88, SD = .99) than those in the weak fault line strength condition (M = 3.06, SD = 1.16) and the control condition (M = 2.71, SD = .81, F(2, 377) = 33.14, p < .001).

We used one item, “Please indicate the extent to which you agree that members with different patterns of media use are equal in number/size” to check the validity of our manipulation of subgroup balance. For subgroup balance manipulations, ANOVA tests revealed that participants in the subgroup balance condition reported higher scores (M = 4.63, SD = .68) than those in the subgroup imbalance condition (M = 2.04, SD = 1.26) and the control condition (M = 3.15, SD = .82, F(2, 374) = 307.02. p < .001).

## Hypothesis Testing

In support of H1, we conducted ANOVA analyses and found that participants in the strong fault line strength condition reported lower perceived team coordination (M = 3.26, SD = .89) than those in the weak fault line strength condition (M = 3.74, SD = .77) and the control condition (M = 3.74, SD = .70, $F(2, 373) = 15.81$ , p < .001). Therefore, H1 was supported.

To test H2, we first conducted ANOVA analyses and found that participants in the strong fault line strength condition reported lower sociability (M = 3.43, SD = .77) than those in the weak fault line strength condition (M = 3.78, SD = .62) and the control condition (M = 3.81, SD = .57, $F(2, 373) = 12.27$ , p < .001). Furthermore, we conducted a bootstrap approach using PROCESS [36] to test the indirect effect of fault lines strength on team coordination via sociability after controlling for the subgroup imbalance, age, education, majority in the imbalanced condition, and control condition via SPSS.24. $^{1}$ The results showed that strong fault line strength was negatively related to sociability (B = -.35, SE = .07, p < .001) and sociability was positively related to team coordination (B = .91, SE = .04, p < .001). In addition, the indirect negative effect of strong fault line strength on team coordination via sociability was significant (indirect effect = -.32, bias-corrected 95 percent CI [-.45, -.20], excluding zero). $^{2}$ Therefore, H2 was also supported.

H3 proposed that the conditional indirect effects of communication-based fault line strength on team coordination via sociability varies according to the levels of imbalance associated with fault line subgroups. We first conducted an OLS analysis via SPSS.24, entering the fault line strength, the subgroup imbalance, age, education, majority in the imbalanced condition, and control condition via SPSS.24. The interaction term of strong fault line strength and subgroup imbalance was significant on team sociability (B = -.29, SE = .15, p = .050). Furthermore, we adopted a bootstrap approach [36] to test the conditional indirect effect of fault lines strength on team coordination via sociability after controlling the subgroup imbalance, age, education, majority in the imbalanced condition, and control condition via SPSS.24. The results showed that the indirect effect of communication-based fault line strength on team coordination via sociability was negative when subgroups within the team were balanced (indirect effect = -.20; bias-corrected 95 percent CI [-.39, -.02], excluding zero). This relationship was strengthened when subgroups within the teams were imbalanced (indirect effect = -.46; bias-corrected 95 percent CI [-.65, -.28], excluding 0). Therefore, H3 was also supported.

## Robustness Analysis

First, we re-tested the moderation effect after removing the control condition. The interaction term of strong fault line strength and subgroup imbalance was marginally significant on team sociability (B = -.29, SE = .15, p = .052). Second, we re-adapted a bootstrap approach $[36]$ to test the conditional indirect effect after removing the control group via SPSS.24. The results were the same as the original. Specifically, the indirect effect of communication-based fault line strength on team coordination via sociability was negative when subgroups within the teams were imbalanced (indirect effect = -.46; 95 percent CI [-.64, -.27], excluding zero) and negative when subgroups within the team were balanced (indirect effect = -.20; 95 percent CI [-.38, -.02], excluding zero). Third, we reanalyzed the model after removing the participants who did not finish the survey (with less than 100 percent progress). The results were the same as the original.

## Supplementary Analysis

We conducted the supplementary analysis to test whether individuals positioned in the majority subgroup or minority subgroup may influence employees' perceived sociability and coordination. We found that in the imbalanced subgroup condition, minority members experienced lower levels of sociability and coordination compared to majority members in the strong fault line condition. Specifically, majority members in the strong fault line condition experienced significantly higher levels of sociability (Mean = 3.62, SE = .63) and team coordination (Mean = 3.58, SE = .79) than minority members in the strong fault line condition (Mean = 3.28, SE = .76; diff = .34, SE = .15, p = .024) and team coordination (Mean = 3.16, SE = .84; diff = .42, SE = .18, p = .021). However, we did not find a significant difference in sociability and coordination between majority and minority members in the weak fault line condition. The majority members in the weak fault line condition experienced similar levels of sociability (Mean = 4.08, SE = .61) and team coordination (Mean = 4.02, SE = .66) to the minority member in the weak fault line condition (Mean = 3.83, SE = .63; diff = .25, SE = .15, p = .106) and team coordination (Mean = 3.80, SE = .80; diff = .22, SE = .18, p = .238).

## Discussion

Our proposed hypotheses were consistently supported by a multi-level study of 292 team members in 37 teams (Study 1) and an experimental study of 385 participants (Study 2). The findings of both Study 1 and Study 2 showed that participants in teams with strong communication-based fault line reported low levels of coordination (H1). Such a negative effect of strong fault line on team coordination was mediated by team sociability (H2) and was enhanced in teams divided by fault lines in uneven ways (i.e., imbalanced subgroups in the team, H3). Therefore, the combination of Study 1 and Study 2 provided external and internal validity in explaining our proposed model.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our study offers important contributions to the research of virtual teams, teamwork generally, and MST. First, we contribute to the literature on virtual teams by specifically investigating the fault line perspective of communication. We add this contribution by using fault line analyses and examining how strong communication-based fault lines involve diverse asynchronous patterns of digital communication reliance and coordination, which indeed harm virtual team coordination. In this way, we extend prior research on fault lines that has thus far mainly focused on demographics [see review and 75] with a clear focus on fault lines as a method to investigate subgroup dynamics with the use and configuration of different media. Scholars highlight that all dimensions of diversity can influence communication preference and team functioning $[21, 74]$ . Previous researchers of both traditional teams and virtual teams mainly focused on how demographic fault lines influence team interactions and processes. These conventional demographics, such as race, gender, and age $[34]$ have shown to be potential fault lines $[45]$ , but other demographics such as personality, values, team tenure, and ideology have also been found to be potential fault lines $[34, 45]$ . These various potential demographic fault lines have shown to impact fault line strength to varying degrees (e.g., some researchers have found sex and race to impact fault lines more heavily than functional and educational background, and tenure) if activated [45, 56].

When presented with certain task-types, these potential fault lines are more likely to be activated, highlighting that the mere presence of differing demographics is only one hurdle when establishing processes to limit fault line activation. Task type should be considered in the context of a team when developing communication norms and shared mental models. With the increasing use of diverse digital tools in the workplace, fault lines based on the digital tools used provide additional context to explain a different type of dynamic in teams. Future researchers are encouraged to operationalize fault lines beyond traditional demographics, exploring areas such as rank or team effort $[11]$ , and various ways fault lines can activate and present themselves to impact team dynamics and synchronicity $[34]$ .

Second, our study extends the general understanding of communication aspects of virtual teams and adds to the existing literature MST. This line of research has been made extremely relevant in the recent pandemic-facilitated move to (and drastically scale up) distributed and virtual work, which calls for additional research $[5, 38]$ . Contrasting research on top-down imposed information technology and digital communication channels that enable virtual team communication $[6, 69]$ , our study explores a bottom-up approach that spotlights the degree of similarity of digital communication use between team members to achieve synchronicity. Specifically examining fault line strength and subgroup imbalance interplay and showing that the indirect effect of communication-based fault line strength on team coordination via team sociability is negative for imbalanced subgroups provides important insight into the black box of communication configuration for virtual team sociability. These findings further inform MST by showing which mediating factors, such as team sociability, communication media preferences, and fault line strengths contribute to virtual team synchronicity.

Our findings, supported by both the field survey and the experimental study contribute to understanding the gap between intended and implemented management practices, such that narrowing the gap is not so much about what tools to provide, but rather how they are used, and in our case, what type of team (i.e., regarding subgroup balance or imbalance) a manager is attempting to enable. Specifically, our findings, highlighting that equally-sized groups can mitigate coordination problems that arise from increasing fault line strength, are consistent with those of O'Leary and Mortensen $[61]$ . In addition, Polzer and colleagues $[65]$ found that two subgroup teams (composed of two equally-sized subgroups of three collocated people each) experience more conflict than members in three equally-sized subgroup teams (composed of three equally-sized subgroups of two collocated people each), which in turn experience more conflict than fully dispersed teams. However, these findings counter Harrison and Klein $[33]$ , who theorized that equally-sized subgroups are more likely to provoke conflict than imbalanced subgroups, where the larger subgroup dominates.

In conjunction with Harrison and Klein's [33] study, we highlight the need for future researchers to offer a more nuanced understanding of how (un)equally-sized subgroup configuration impacts team coordination. Furthermore, our use of an experimental design and mixed method approach calls to expand the methodology that many researchers in this area have used to further specify antecedents to fault lines, coloring the explanation for why fault lines do or do not activate [103]. Future researchers are encouraged to adopt methods that can further imply causality and increase the precision of practical implications. The findings of the current and future studies may well inform team structuring in a practical sense, while also differentiating avenues for more specified research based on team size and implications of different respective fault line impacts. For example, in the (im)balanced condition, team coordination can vary according to the number of subgroups that are (un) equally sized.

Lastly, MST emphasizes that team communication should not be studied using one single medium. However, previous researchers postulated the use of a specific medium for all communication tasks and found contradictory results about the effectiveness of the specific medium used (e.g., [31]). Given the rapid growth of diverse digital communication tools used in coordinating teamwork, focusing on the effectiveness of a single medium may fail to clearly explain the influence of media in team functioning. Thus, in this research we focused on how the use of diverse media, instead of a single medium—often utilized based on personal preferences of individual team members (rather than agreed upon team norms)—influences team coordination and team sociability. This approach aligns with other researchers who have found that task/communication medium matching is more important than agreeing to one medium or communication approach to achieve synchronicity as stated in MST [30, 105]. Te'eni [77:251] highlighted the need for “a balance between relationship and action, between cognition and affect, and between message and medium,” underlining the multiple variables at play in determining the success of team communication. Specifically, we found that fault line strength decreased team coordination through virtual team sociability. This strength might be sustained by other factors, such as social categorization and ingroup bias [11, 90, 79]. These findings further debunk the idea that a specific media type is beneficial due to alleged richness alone. Complementing appropriate communication structure [70], the ability to move from one appropriate medium for one task to another appropriate medium for another task is the underlying necessary skill to empower these teams [25]. By examining the use of a set of media used in teams, our research supports the argument for different types of media for appropriate tasks. We open a door for exploring the most appropriate medium to use in specific team workplace settings and thereby elaborate on MST theory to understand how team members’ media communication patterns and use of media tools are relevant to achieve synchronicity.

## Practical Implications

Virtual teams entail diverse knowledge and experience from geographically distant individuals, making it challenging for team members to establish sociability and coordination, coupled with limited but ever-changing communication tools to use in accomplishing these tasks. Our findings demonstrate two avenues to provide practical insights on how communication technology can be used to coordinate more effectively and improve virtual teamwork.

The first avenue is related to the use of communication technology and media, adhering to the concept of facilitated collaboration, which highlights the effective use of guidelines for communication and task completion in the development of trust and effective teamwork $[16, 77]$ . When initially establishing these guidelines, researchers advocate for the assessment of team readiness to adopt and perceived benefits of adopting new tech-driven practices $[13]$ , and eventually introducing guidelines as a new team expectation. Successful coordination is associated with positive perceptions of the available digital tools $[80]$ and team members with a shared understanding of task-related work, which is influenced by media configuration in the team $[46]$ . When media are used specifically for a particular task, this approach displays a shared knowledge of combining task requirements with specific media. Such cohesive media configuration has shown to allow for effective coordination $[20, 16]$ . Managers and virtual team members should thus direct particular attention to align a specific task (based on task characteristics, the nature of the work, information to be shared) with a channel to be used for its communication and collaborative execution. High performing managers, for example, have shown to be more sensitive to the social and informational media requirements of various tasks than lower performing managers, displaying different media use for tasks that require more social or more informational exchange $[19]$ . Moreover, managers should tune into patterns of communication, such as habitual “rules” that team members apply to specific communication mediums, as these may need to be purposefully adjusted or replaced. These rules also have the potential to be leveraged for optimal communication and coordination according to task $[7, 8]$ .

The second avenue is perhaps more challenging to achieve, as it deals with the composition of subgroups in terms of majorities and minorities. Specifically, our findings further exacerbate the need to prevent fault lines in virtual teams and attempt to avoid having imbalanced subgroups in virtual teams. A climate of psychological safety, voice, and inclusion should be stimulated, as this has been found to prevent the formation of subgroups in virtual teams $[23]$ . Emotional and affect management interventions, such as online training sessions to improve employee affect management skills have been highlighted by the extant research $[23]$ to help prevent strong fault lines from forming, subsequently preventing team subgroup imbalances, and could be useful in improving virtual team communication and coordination.

## Future Research Suggestions

Future researchers are encouraged to further explore how communication fault lines in teams develop, are enabled, and importantly, how they can be addressed by appropriate communication types. Exploration of leader behaviors, for example, may show that certain approaches, such as initiating structure or servant leadership, are more effective in establishing communication norms $[60]$ . Further, it may be fruitful to examine how different types of teams (e.g., consultants, financial professionals, engineers), but with similar fault line patterns, for example, are able to work via specific communication media. Specific types of team members may present as boundary-spanning individuals who may be critical to improving team communications $[41]$ . Conversely, examining different industries or backgrounds may highlight differences in virtual team communication and effectiveness [e.g., 50]. This exploration could better enable the construction of virtual teams by identifying the most useful communication tools by task type. Moreover, future researchers could investigate team members' actual communication synchrony, combined with the communication configuration within the team to construct a robust understanding of how communication configuration influences team processes. These communication patterns, as well as preferred mediums, may impact how information is processed $[55]$ . Potentially important contingency variables could be included in future research endeavors, such as those that we identified as salient in the practical implications section (i.e., psychological safety, climate of inclusion, or voice climate in virtual teams), which likely act as boundary conditions to our studied relationships.

Future researchers could also focus more explicitly on additional factors that affect collaboration patterns, such as the structure of work tasks or the nature of the tasks and existing work dependencies. Communication and collaboration in virtual teams do not happen in a vacuum, and existing collaboration technologies likely mirror the interdependence of tasks and role structure within the team, as particular team members may require more collaboration and alignment than others to accomplish their work $[49]$ . Another area of existing research also explores how communication patterns align with task structures within teams $[70, 49]$ . These bodies of work emphasize that decisions about collaboration are influenced by the nature of the work being done, with socially-oriented tasks aligning with different mediums of communication than complex knowledge sharing $[70]$ . Future empirical work that would tackle a complex interaction between the nature of work, task interdependence in virtual teams, as well as virtual team coordination simultaneously and in a more nuanced manner is thus needed. As the mere presence of fault lines is not inherently problematic, with activation of those fault lines perhaps representing a stronger challenge, future researchers could delve specifically into this matter.

## Strengths and Limitations

The current research has various strengths, one being the multi-pronged approach. We used a combination of field (Study 1) and scenario-based experimental studies (Study 2) to corroborate our results. The field study helped in establishing external validity and the scenario-based experiment provided causal evidence and increased the robustness of our results. Thus, the two studies were more rigorous in providing internal and external validity in elucidating the effect of fault line strength on team coordination.

As any, our study is not without limitations. First, despite our hypothesis that digital communication configuration would influence communication synchrony, we did not assess actual communication synchrony $[56]$ . The findings of our study, however, provide a fruitful avenue for future researchers to expand our understanding of virtual team communication configuration. Second, Study 1 may suffer from common method bias, given that all of the variables were measured at one time point. It is possible that common method variance inflated the relationship between team sociability and team coordination. However, the results from the experimental study (Study 2) support the findings we observed in Study 1. Third, we did not account for the role of task structure in impacting communication coordination and medium choice. Future researchers are encouraged to not only acknowledge this influence but incorporate this in testing. Fourth, in this research, we employed MST to explore the negative impact of communication fault lines on team coordination. According to social categorization theory $[45]$ , fault line activation could potentially serve as an underlying mechanism for this negative effect. Specifically, the needs for team coordination during daily work make the communication differences and divisions among team members salient and trigger fault line activation, ultimately leading to decreased team coordination.

## Conclusion

The present studies explored media richness and media synchronicity theory, showing that communication-based subgroup dynamics in terms of fault line strength and subgroup imbalance influence virtual team sociability, and subsequently, team coordination. Indeed, fault lines and imbalanced subgroups should be avoided in virtual teams, as taken together they significantly impair virtual team coordination via team sociability. This research further expands our understanding of virtual team communication and coordination, and subgroup dynamics, especially considering the recent pandemic-facilitated push towards (even) more virtual and distributed work.
