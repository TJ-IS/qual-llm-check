# Partnering for prosperity: small IT vendor partnership formation and the establishment of partner pools

N Melike Findikoglu <sup>a</sup>, Chandrasekaran Ranganathan<sup>b</sup> and Mary Beth Watson-Manheim<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Management Information Systems, Ozyegin University, Istanbul, Turkey; <sup>b</sup>Information and Decision Sciences, College of Business Administration, University of Illinois, Chicago, IL, USA

## ABSTRACT

Small IT vendors increasingly establish intra-industry collaborative arrangements with other technology providers. Despite the criticality of this strategy, there is little research that provides insights into partnership formation. Our study attempts to close this gap.Building on resource dependency theory (RDT) and resource-based view (RBV), we posit that, depending on external market and internal resource considerations, small IT vendors either supplement or complement their IT resources and capabilities via partnerships. When seeking to expand the scope of their resource portfolio by accessing dissimilar resources, vendors are engage in complementary partnerships (goal: improving the scope of IT resources). However, if they seek to expand the scale of their portfolio, they engage in supplementary partnerships (goal: extending the scale of IT resources). Using a qualitative approach, we examine the partnership formation practices of seven small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms. We propose a conceptual framework with <sup>fi</sup>ve constructs that illustrate dynamics underlying these IT service partnerships, i.e., External market considerations, Internal resource con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, Partner considerations, Partnership exploration, and Partnership development. We <sup>fi</sup>nd variations in partnership practices depending on the supplementary or complementary nature of resources being sought. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd small IT vendors form and manage partner pools to mitigate risks associated with partnerships.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, as demand for sophisticated and complex information technology (IT) products and services continues to grow (Basole & Rouse, 2008), IT vendors<sup>1</sup> face increased challenges to meet client expectations and sustain business growth. IT vendors confront a highly demanding and <sup>fi</sup>ercely competitive environment that is fragmented, complex, technologically uncertain, and volatile. To improve the business value from IT, client <sup>fi</sup>rms demand innovative solutions (Oshri et al., 2015), <sup>fl</sup>exible infrastructures, and systems (Benitez et al., 2018), with stringent intellectual property requirements (Chen et al., 2017) that often necessitate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant rerepresentation of requirements (Oshri et al., 2018) and distribution of IT work to multiple vendors (Su & Levina, 2011; Wiener & Saunders, 2014). Clients are changing their traditional approaches increasing the pressure on IT vendors to rapidly adapt (Aubert et al., 2016; N. Su et al., 2016). Many IT vendors, both big and small, repeatedly <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves facing disruptive changes (Kaltenecker et al., 2015; Lewis et al., 2011; F. Su et al., 2014). The problems are more acute for small IT vendors who have limited human, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, and technological resources. Small IT vendors constitute over 80% of overall IT services industry (Swinarski et al., 2012) and often compete against many others including o<sup>f</sup>shore vendors and established multinationals. They face several challenges including market uncertainties, cost pressures, tighter delivery schedules, increased wages as well as high rates of personnel attrition (Swinarski et al., 2012; Wang & Kaarst-Brown, 2014).

In response to such challenges, small IT vendors are increasingly establishing di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of intraindustry collaborative arrangements with other providers. They seek to share business risks, gain quick access to needed resources, and e<sup>f</sup>ectively manage client expectations. IT vendors, in general, have shown increased proclivity to form partnerships: approximately 95% of the publicly traded software <sup>fi</sup>rms in the US have formed alliances (Lavie, 2007). Further, research and technology alliances in the IT industry accounted for about of 41% of worldwide alliances reported during 1990–2004 (Schilling, 2009).

Compared to the wealth of scholarship on client– vendor relationships in IT outsourcing, we know much less about IT vendors and their strategies for ful<sup>fi</sup>ling service obligations (Currie & Seltsikas, 2001; Lewis et al., 2011; Su, 2013). In<sup>fl</sup>uential studies on IT service providers include Feeny et al. (2005) that identi<sup>fi</sup>ed twelve key capabilities for them, and Levina and Ross (2003) that highlighted three strategic capabilities for IT vendors viz. client relationship management, methodology development & dissemination, and personnel development. Building on these higher-level capabilities, Jarvenpaa and Mao (2008) focused on operational level capabilities and learning mechanisms that small and mid-sized IT vendors needed to develop in order to be e<sup>f</sup>ective in subcontracted work a large IT vendor <sup>fi</sup>rm – delineating the issues in mediated outsourcing model. Other studies on IT vendors have examined sequential strategies followed by these <sup>fi</sup>rms (Lewis et al., 2011) and their internationalisation strategies to enter foreign markets (Su, 2013), importance of IT integration capabilities (Ceci et al., 2019) and crowdsourcing (Nevo & Kotlarsky, 2014). More recently, Ruckman et al. (2015) examined how IT vendors imitate their rivals’ decisions to recon<sup>fi</sup>gure service o<sup>f</sup>erings and delivery. It should be noted that most prior research on IT vendors implicitly assumes that these organisations use internal resources to meet their service obligations while ignoring their e<sup>f</sup>orts to address resource de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies through external partnerships. However, the prevalence of partnerships among IT vendors suggests this mechanism to be a salient feature of their organisational strategies.

In this paper, we seek to explore the partnership arrangements that small IT vendors form with other providers. We examine how small IT vendors choose their partners and manage the partnership formation process. Closing resource de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies is especially salient for small IT vendors in order to grow and compete in a dynamic environment. Resource limitations force small IT vendors to focus on building specialisation in certain core areas and to engage in partnerships with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms to acquire resources and capabilities that are needed to meet IT service obligations (Findikoglu & Watson-Manheim, 2015).

Small IT vendors need to carefully engage in IT service partnerships as there are several risks. The need to access, acquire, and quickly utilise a partner’s capabilities may force them to join partnerships under less favourable conditions that could make the alliance unstable or prone to complete failure. Further, as in any alliance, there is risk of opportunistic behaviour by the partner. In addition, these partnerships could also expose small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms to competitive risks. Small IT vendors who operate under the umbrella of larger IT <sup>fi</sup>rms as “certi<sup>fi</sup>ed” partners might face additional challenges due to the sheer size and power associated with the larger partner <sup>fi</sup>rm. How can small IT vendors use partnerships productively, yet mitigate the associated challenges? There is a strong need to empirically assess these issues underlying IT service partnerships.

Addressing the above-mentioned gaps, our primary research objective is to explore how small IT vendors form IT service partnerships. We begin with the premise that small IT vendors enter into partnerships to address their resource de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies and gain access to two types of resources (1) complementary resources that are dissimilar to what they already possess and (2) supplementary resources that are similar to their current resource endowments. We posit that, depending on market and resource considerations, small IT vendors may either complement or supplement their IT resources and capabilities via these partnerships. When seeking to expand the scope of their resource portfolio by accessing dissimilar resources, small IT vendors are more likely to engage in complementary partnerships. However, if they seek to expand the scale of current resources by adding similar resources, they are likely to form supplementary partnerships. Invoking the resource-based view (RBV) on alliances, we analyse whether small IT vendors show diferences in formation practices of complementary and supplementary partnerships.

Based on qualitative data analysis from partnership practices of seven small IT vendors, we <sup>fi</sup>nd distinct di<sup>f</sup>erences in how IT vendors approach complementary versus supplementary partnerships. Vendors in our sample clearly di<sup>f</sup>ered in their motivations for the two types of partnerships. We conduct further crosspartnership analysis, comparing supplementary and complementary partnership formation practices. We <sup>fi</sup>nd support for variations in partnership formation practices depending on the supplementary or complementary nature of resources sought via the partnerships. Furthermore, departing from the dominant thinking in extant literature, we <sup>fi</sup>nd small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms create a “partner pool” with a set of selected providers before entering into formal contractual agreements. Small IT vendors rely on this pool to draw potential partners and this helps them reduce search costs and coordination issues typically faced in alliances.

We synthesise our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to propose a conceptual framework with <sup>fi</sup>ve constructs to understand IT service partnerships: External Market Considerations, Internal Resource Considerations, Partner Considerations, Partnership Exploration and Partnership Development. We believe this framework helps us better understand the dynamics underlying IT service partnerships formed by small IT vendors.

Our study makes important contributions to the research and practice on IT services and outsourcing. First, our study throws light on partnerships among small and mid-sized IT vendors who form a considerable chunk of IT industry. Moving beyond client–vendor relationships that have been extensively studied by IT outsourcing scholars, we extend the research to the realm of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm relationships among IT vendors. Second, our study describes how small IT vendors approach and engage in partnerships. Small IT vendors with similar resources can pool their resources to enhance the scale of resource endowments whereas those with dissimilar resources can help extend the scope of resource pool. Third, our study directs attention to di<sup>f</sup>erences in partnership formation practices of small IT vendors. Fourth, our novel <sup>fi</sup>nding on partner pool creation and maintenance also contributes to the work on strategic alliances. Finally, we respond to recent calls (Abbott et al., 2013; Findikoglu & Watson-Manheim, 2015) for a better understanding of strategies followed by IT vendors to survive and succeed in hyper-competitive environments. Our study has important managerial implications for decision makers in small and midsized IT <sup>fi</sup>rms who seek business growth via IT service partnerships. Managers should carefully consider collaborating with other <sup>fi</sup>rms based on whether they need to access a diverse pool of IT resources and expertise (for complementary partnerships) or if they need to scale up the resource levels (supplementary partnerships). When seeking complementary partnerships for customised IT services, managers should look for partners who are of similar or smaller size who are located in closer geographic proximity to them. This can provide them with <sup>fl</sup>exibility and control over newer partner resources. For supplementary partnerships, if the project involves noncommoditized IT services, seeking local partners will be more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. Managers will also greatly bene<sup>fi</sup>t by maintaining a pool of potential partners who can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and developed through referrals from trusted a<sup>fi</sup>liates. The partner pool helps in minimising search costs and also help in providing timely access to required resources.

Partnerships are crucial vehicles for <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, yet formation and management of IT service partnerships can be costly and resource-intensive for small IT vendors. Hence, understanding the nuances in partnership formation based on resource con<sup>fi</sup>gurations and adapting the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s practices accordingly are of critical importance for resource-constrained small IT vendors.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: we present theoretical background on IT service partnerships and summarise <sup>fi</sup>ndings from prior literature of strategic alliances to form the theoretical foundations for our work. Next, we present our research setting, data collection, and analysis methods. We present our results and a framework for understanding IT service partnership formation, including the relationships and mechanisms uncovered in our analysis. Afterwards, we discuss our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in light of the literature and highlight our contributions. Finally, we conclude with implications for practice, address the limitations of the study, and o<sup>f</sup>er ideas for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. IT service partnerships

Partnerships or alliances refer to collaborative arrangements between <sup>fi</sup>rms that typically involve exchange, sharing, or co-development of products, technologies, or services (Gulati, 1998). These arrangements can facilitate access to markets, technologies, and resources, help cope with market uncertainties, aid innovative pursuits, decrease delivery timespan, share risks, facilitate organisational learning via knowledge exchange, and optimise costs (Gulati & Singh, 1998; Ireland et al., 2002; Stuart, 2000). Via collaboration, the partners jointly create relational rents that can only be generated through their respective idiosyncratic contributions (Dyer & Singh, 1998). Building a portfolio of partnerships, <sup>fi</sup>rms can enhance innovativeness (Van de Vrande, 2013) and increase their overall business performance (Jiang et al., 2010). Partnerships typically involve contractual agreements through the terms could be loosely or very tightly de<sup>fi</sup>ned based on the nature of collaboration (Khan & Khan, 2017).

In the context of IT industry, partnerships have proved to be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for IT <sup>fi</sup>rms (Lavie, 2007). Particularly, partnerships with similar software <sup>fi</sup>rms that are in the same layer of the software stack have generated more market value for IT <sup>fi</sup>rms that formed alliances (Gao & Iyer, 2009). Delivery of IT products or services typically entails integration of several interdependent components. In contemporary settings, these components are often not designed, developed, implemented, or maintained by a single IT <sup>fi</sup>rm, but are outcomes of collaboration among multiple IT <sup>fi</sup>rms (Grover & Kohli, 2012). IT <sup>fi</sup>rms jointly create value under di<sup>f</sup>erent arrangements (Ceccagnoli et al., 2014; Findikoglu & Watson-Manheim, 2015; Han et al., 2012; Sarker et al., 2012). For instance, software <sup>fi</sup>rms collaborate with other packaged-software providers, & co-create value through customisation and implementation of add-on modules (Chellappa & Saraf, 2010; Sarker et al., 2012). There are also open innovation alliances where technology is co-developed by multiple members. Such collaborative arrangements di<sup>f</sup>er from traditional alliances in terms of membership and governing principles, such as open membership, transparency, self-regulation, and selfmonitoring (Han et al., 2012).

Unlike multi-<sup>fi</sup>rm, or open alliances, IT <sup>fi</sup>rms frequently form dyadic collaborative arrangements with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms to ful<sup>fi</sup>l certain service delivery obligations to their clients. The scope of such partnerships typically covers various phases of service provision including analysis, design, development, customisation, implementation, integration, and maintenance of IT solutions (Findikoglu & Watson-Manheim, 2015). Using these partnerships, IT <sup>fi</sup>rms complement or supplement their existing resources and competencies. In turn, they can enhance their IT service o<sup>f</sup>erings substantially. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne such partnerships formed between IT <sup>fi</sup>rms for service provision as IT service partnerships and analyse the formation of this particular type of partnerships in this research. Our speci<sup>fi</sup>c focus is on those partnerships formed by small and mid-sized IT service providers.

## 2.2. Key considerations for IT service partnership formation by Small IT vendors

In the strategic management literature, two theoretical perspectives are popular to understand collaborative arrangements between <sup>fi</sup>rms: resource dependence theory (Drees & Heugens, 2013; Hillman et al., 2009) and resource-based view (Das & Teng, 2000). Resource dependence theory (RDT) focuses on gaining the required resources to survive and the resourcebased view (RBV) focuses on synergies achieved by collaboration. Both the RDT and RBV view <sup>fi</sup>rm resource conditions as primary precursors to partnership formation, i.e., the propensity of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to enter into a partnership will be a function of their need to acquire external resources. We use both the RDT and RBV theories to understand two important considerations that small IT vendors have when they seek alliances: external market considerations and internal resource con<sup>fi</sup>gurations.

## 2.2.1. External market considerations

According to RDT, organisations depend on a reliable supply of resources from the external environment to meet their goals (Hillman et al., 2009). Any uncertainty in the supply of resources diminishes a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to control the resources and imposes adaption challenges. RDT advances the notion of environmental uncertainty to be a key driver for partnership formation. A fundamental premise of RDT is that <sup>fi</sup>rms who are faced with external uncertainty will establish partnership arrangements as a response to environmental uncertainty and inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm dependence. The issue of environmental uncertainty is much pronounced in case of small and mid-sized <sup>fi</sup>rms which typically tend to operate under greater constraints of scarce resources.

Environmental uncertainty refers to the di<sup>fi</sup>culty in predicting changes in the external market conditions (DeSarbo et al., 2005). Changes in technology and client markets are the two primary sources of uncertainty (Bestieler, 2005). Technological uncertainty refers to the inability to completely comprehend or predict some aspect of the technological environment (Song & Montoya-Weiss, 2001). This uncertainty becomes stronger with increases in technological complexity and development of IT services. When small IT vendors operate in areas that utilise technologies that are fast changing, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s technological level and status could become unreliable, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm would need to increasingly rely on external partners to obtain relevant technological information or know-how. Moreover, the need for developing customised IT solutions can increase the complexity and uncertainty associated with the project as compared to projects that involve developing commoditized solutions. Small IT vendors that work in fast-changing technological environments might need to constantly upgrade their know-how or face the risk of failure. Market uncertainty refers to the characteristics of clients or prospective clients and their behaviour patterns. When faced with high market uncertainty, small IT vendors need to be proactive in understanding client demands. Small IT vendors will also need to expend e<sup>f</sup>orts in looking for newer clients in order to reduce market uncertainty and sustain their growth. High technological or market uncertainty forces small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms to be more adaptive to environmental changes (DeSarbo et al., 2005).

When small IT vendors form partnerships, they do so on the basis of certain native attributes that make it easier for them to derive value from the partnership. When faced with increased technological and/or market uncertainty, small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms seek partnerships to reduce the environmental uncertainty. Such partnerships would provide them with <sup>fl</sup>exibility and also enable adapting to changes in the market.

## 2.2.2. Firms’ resource con<sup>fi</sup>gurations

RBV has been concerned with managing a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s internal resources and capabilities for gaining superior advantages (Barney, 2001; Grant, 1991). Since resources are heterogeneously distributed across <sup>fi</sup>rms, certain resource de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in a <sup>fi</sup>rm can be e<sup>f</sup>ectively addressed through external sources via partnerships. Through partnerships, <sup>fi</sup>rms can seek resources such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial capital, technological capabilities, managerial acumen, and other relevant assets (Hitt et al., 2000), which would otherwise be unavailable. Moreover, the partnerships in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of value-creating resources or contribute to resource homogeneity by facilitating exchanges between partners (Lavie, 2006). Hence, partnerships enable a <sup>fi</sup>rm to achieve an optimal resource con<sup>fi</sup>guration (Eisenhardt & Schoonhoven, 1996; Das & Teng, 2000). Firms seeking to engage in partnerships tend to search for alliance partners with speci<sup>fi</sup>c resources and capabilities that they can leverage and integrate in order to create desired synergies (Lin et al., 2009).

Building upon the RBV perspective on alliances (Eisenhardt & Schoonhoven, 1996; Das & Teng, 2000), we argue that small IT vendors will choose partners based on their need to extend the scale or scope of their resource portfolio. Depending on the nature and extent of resource gaps, an IT vendor can <sup>fi</sup>nd one or more partners with similar or dissimilar resource endowments as appropriate. Partners with dissimilar resources can help extend the scope of the resource pool, whereas partners with similar resources can help enhance the scale of resource endowments. Hence, the similarity or dissimilarity of resources becomes a key determinant in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s choice of partners.

The extent of compatibility of resources between two alliance partners is referred to as resource complementarity (Harrison et al., 2001). When resource endowments of partners are su<sup>fi</sup>ciently di<sup>f</sup>erentiated, their combinations can provide complementary advantages. For instance, when a software development <sup>fi</sup>rm which lacks su<sup>fi</sup>cient expertise in user experience design seeks an alliance with an IT <sup>fi</sup>rm specialising in that area, this alliance can bring in added capability, thus expanding the range of existing services of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. In such complementary IT service partnerships, alliance partners bring in non-overlapping, dissimilar, and supportive resources (Chung et al., 2000; Das & Teng, 2000) that can be utilised at di<sup>f</sup>erent stages of their IT service delivery process. Alternatively, when the partners combine similar resources, they can add or augment the combined resource pool, thus providing supplementary bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. An example of this kind of partnership is when a web development <sup>fi</sup>rm works with a small IT sta<sup>fi</sup>ng <sup>fi</sup>rm to bring in additional resources such as web developers to work on a client <sup>fi</sup>rm’s project. In this type of arrangement, partners pool similar or identical resources to ful<sup>fi</sup>l the task requirements (Dussauge et al., 2000). We refer to these arrangements as supplementary IT service partnerships. Appendix A provides description and examples of complementary and supplementary IT service partnerships.

Scholars examining strategic alliances have debated if one type of partnership provides better value than the other, though the results have been largely inconclusive (Jiang et al., 2010; Wassmer & Dussauge, 2011). It can be argued that for a small IT vendor, complementary IT service partnerships with dissimilar, diverse partners are more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial than those with similar partners, as they provide access to di<sup>f</sup>erentiated resources and capabilities currently unavailable to the <sup>fi</sup>rm, at lower cost (Wassmer, 2010). However, managing a set of diverse partners can be challenging and may increase the overall costs of coordination (Goerzen & Beamish, 2005) for small IT vendors. Further, collaborating with diverse partners can also impose a steep learning curve to the small IT <sup>fi</sup>rm adding to the complexity of managing the alliances.

On the other hand, supplementary IT service partnerships could be easier to manage due to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s familiarity with the resource base. For instance, small IT service <sup>fi</sup>rms often face the need to expand their human resource base (Lacity et al., 2012) and tend to augment skilled IT sta<sup>f</sup> from other <sup>fi</sup>rms. Such supplementary partnerships might be relatively easier to manage as the IT vendor retains dominant control over the acquired resources and the similarity of resources makes it easier for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to quickly acquire and utilise them. Depending on their needs, small IT vendors need to carefully choose a portfolio of complementary and supplementary partnerships in order to e<sup>f</sup>ectively meet their service obligations.

## 2.3. Partnership formation by small IT vendors

The literature on strategic alliances has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two distinct phases in partnership formation – partner selection based on a set of criteria, and partnership development where <sup>fi</sup>rms enter into formal working relationships. The success of any inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm partnership is largely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the selection of partners. Partner selection entails astute assessment of potential partners to choose the ones with best skills and/or resources for provisioning the particular IT service under consideration. Strategic alliance literature has documented several partner considerations that <sup>fi</sup>rms take into account when forming alliances. Building on prior research, we focus on four speci<sup>fi</sup>c partner considerations that are relevant to our research context: location, size, competitive overlap, and prior ties.

In technological alliances, the location of partner <sup>fi</sup>rm has is a key consideration in selecting partners (Reuer & Lahiri, 2014). As information asymmetries and the risk of adverse selection tend to rise with geographic distance, small <sup>fi</sup>rms prefer to work with partners who are geographically closer (Franco & Haase, 2015). For small IT vendors involved in knowledge-intense IT services, geographical proximity with partners can reduce uncertainty and enhance coordination (Capaldo & Petruzzelli, 2014). Another key consideration is the size of potential partner. Alliances with large IT <sup>fi</sup>rms could provide small IT vendors with opportunities for accessing wider pool of resources, technical know-how, besides bringing reputational advantages (Kude et al., 2011). However, these relationships are asymmetric due to power disparities, and small IT vendors face the risk of exploitation by larger <sup>fi</sup>rms. On the other hand, small-sized partners might provide IT vendors an equal platform for negotiations and appropriations in the partnership. However, smaller partners may not provide the same resource and reputational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as larger <sup>fi</sup>rms. Hence, the size of partners becomes a careful consideration for small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms when choosing partners.

Competitive overlap refers to the similarities in the services o<sup>f</sup>ered by the partner <sup>fi</sup>rms and the markets they serve. Competitive overlap becomes a key consideration in IT vendor partnerships as it can bring both advantages and disadvantages (Inkpen, 2000; Rogan, 2014). Advantages include development of enhanced knowledge and skills in the operating domain as well as positive information externalities. At the same time, competitive overlap brings disadvantages including increase in potential for information leakages, risk of opportunistic behaviour by the partner, and potential substitutability of suppliers by the client.

Prior studies on strategic alliances have investigated the value of prior ties in partner selection (Gulati, 1995; Lioukas & Reuer, 2020) Building on information asymmetry lens, these studies have argued that prior experience with partnerships can decrease <sup>fi</sup>rms information disadvantages and can help mitigate the risk of adverse selection and opportunism. In case of IT service partnerships, we expect that prior partnerships can o<sup>f</sup>er <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained information on each others’ technological capabilities and management processes. Prior partnership experience can also help small IT vendors better understand and manage the operational activities in the partnership.

Partnership development entails a gamut of activities that make a partnership operational, such as search, assessment and selection of partners, design and negotiation of the cooperation structure, commitment of resources, and engagement in the relationship (Das & Teng, 2002). During this phase, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm should assess the uncertainties associated with the partnership, the partner’s trustworthiness, and potential e<sup>fi</sup>ciencies gained through formal bargaining and informal sense making. Following the negotiations, the partners commit the necessary resources and the partnership becomes operational. We argue that a small IT vendor’s partnership formation practices are shaped by its current resource con<sup>fi</sup>gurations and the task’s resource requirements. The similarity or dissimilarity of resources between the partnering service providers becomes a key criterion in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s choice of its partners. We empirically examine how small and mid-sized IT vendors choose a portfolio of complementary and supplementary partners and balance these arrangements. In the following section, we present our study.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Study rationale

Our study is motivated by the recognition that many small IT vendors are increasingly establishing di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of intra-industry collaborative arrangements with other technology providers, i.e., vendor–vendor relationships. While the establishment of the client – vendor relationship is fairly well understood, the conditions that lead to the establishment of more collaborative relationships and alliances between IT vendors are not. Our objective is to analyse partnership formation strategies of small- and medium-sized IT <sup>fi</sup>rms for collaborating with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms in service provision. This research provides an opportunity to extend existing theory in a relatively under-studied context.

To investigate this phenomenon, we employ a qualitative approach to gather context-rich data from <sup>fi</sup>rms (Miles et al., 1994). We use a multiple case study design to collect data from seven small IT vendors to explore the process used by these <sup>fi</sup>rms while engaging in partnership formation with other vendors to acquire necessary resources. Our research approach is most accurately described as soft positivism (Kirsch, 2004; Ravishankar et al., 2011) or a hybrid approach (Leidner et al., 2009). We start our investigation with a priori expectations based on prior research as is expected in positivist research (Dubé & Paré, 2003) while also remaining open to uncovering unexpected constructs consistent with interpretivist approaches (e.g., Klein & Myers, 1999; Sarker et al., 2013). Prior theorising of partnership formation as well as a resource-based view provides the foundation for our research.

We enter the <sup>fi</sup>eld with a theoretical set of constructs which we assume are relatively stable (Leidner et al., 2009) but have not been adequately studied in this setting. Accordingly, we remain open to identifying emergent constructs and relationships that cannot be untangled from the small IT vendor–vendor partnership context, as would be expected in an interpretive approach (Sarker et al., 2013). In the following sections, we <sup>fi</sup>rst explain our case selection approach by elaborating sampling and recruitment methods, we then elaborate our data collection and analysis methods (Pan & Tan, 2011).

## 3.2. Case selection

Guided by Eisenhardt’s (1989) framework, we adopted a theoretical sampling approach rather than random or strati<sup>fi</sup>ed (Yin, 2003). Our aim was to select <sup>fi</sup>rms that o<sup>f</sup>ered enough similarities yet possessed comparable as well as distinct properties (Miles et al., 1994) so that the cases can contribute more to theory development (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). The cases were drawn from the population of small- and mediumsized Chicago IT <sup>fi</sup>rms that were engaged in service partnerships with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms. Our selection criteria included <sup>fi</sup>rm size (max. 250 full-time employees) and <sup>fi</sup>rm age (min. three years old).

We further constrained the sample to a single segment within the IT industry, that of software development <sup>fi</sup>rms and IT consulting <sup>fi</sup>rms with a focus on development, customisation, implementation, or integration activities. We chose this segment due to the prevalence of small <sup>fi</sup>rms and the abundance of IT partnerships pursued by them. Further, a singlesegment study provides greater control over the sources of extraneous variation within the sample due to segment-speci<sup>fi</sup>c or environmental factors (Yin, 2003). In order to control for economic, social, and cultural contexts in which the <sup>fi</sup>rms operate, we narrowed the geographical scope of the study to Chicago Metropolitan area. The Chicago area is an ideal site for this research. A citywide technology strategy was launched in 2013 focusing on technology innovation and startups leading to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increase in tech companies and jobs in the area.<sup>2</sup>

We promoted our study via emails, periodic announcements on social networking sites (such as Chicago Technology Network, Technology Leaders Association in LinkedIn, Tech Cocktail) as well as through technology associations (such as Illinois Technology Association, Society for Information Management). Interested <sup>fi</sup>rms were validated according to the sample criteria above. Ultimately, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 7 software development <sup>fi</sup>rms that <sup>fi</sup>t our sample criteria.

Our primary mode of data collection was through semi-structured interviews with senior executives from the seven <sup>fi</sup>rms, such as founders, partners, CEO, CIO, and COO, who were responsible for and involved in development and implementation of partnership strategies. In the partnership context, it is important to obtain data from key informants who are knowledgeable about partnership decisions (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). Where appropriate, we also interviewed managers who were engaged in day-to-day partnership operations, such as project managers, to collect data for our study. In some cases, the founders of the <sup>fi</sup>rms were actively participating in management and decision-making, shaping <sup>fi</sup>rm strategies and behaviours and thus were important informants. The demographics of the interviewees are presented in Table 1.

## 3.3. Data collection and analysis

For data collection, we created a semi-structured interview protocol consisting of <sup>fi</sup>ve sections with open-ended questions aimed at developing better understanding of the conditions that lead to the establishment of partnerships between IT vendors and the role of resource con<sup>fi</sup>guration in the shaping of partnership formation practices. In developing our questions, we were guided by established partnership formation practices found in the previous research as discussed in the previous theoretical background section. The interview guide is provided in Appendix B.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst section of the guide contained questions about interviewee demographics, <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>le such as size, age, location, founders, organisation structure, core business, products, and services, technologies and customers. In the second section, we asked open-ended questions to gain knowledge of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s partnership rationale and strategies with regard to resource acquisition, and preferences on partner pro<sup>fi</sup>le found in the previous literature such as size and location. The third section inquired about the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s existing IT service partnerships and their characteristics in terms of resources acquired and partner pro<sup>fi</sup>le. Finally, the fourth section investigated partner search, selection, and negotiation activities as well as di<sup>f</sup>erences in these activities based on the type of resource acquired from partner. Nevertheless, the questions were tailored during the interviews, depending on the interviewee’s role in partnerships. The interview protocol was reviewed and re<sup>fi</sup>ned by peer researchers and practitioners before data collection. The demographics of the interviewees are shown in Table 1. ## 4. Field observations and framework development

In this section, we present our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on partnership formation activities found in our sample <sup>fi</sup>rms and summarised in the conceptual framework presented in Figure 1. The conceptual framework presented is an elaboration and extension of the key stages of partnership formation found in the previous literature (see Table 2). In particular, our analysis indicated that external market consideration continues to be important but we also uncovered the key role of internal resource con<sup>fi</sup>guration. We further identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a new aspect of partnership selection which we refer to as partnership exploration: the role of partner pools, or the set of potential vendor partners.

Thus, our framework is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by <sup>fi</sup>ve major constructs that underlie the dynamics of smalland medium-sized IT service partnerships: (i) External market considerations that consist of market uncertainty and technological uncertainty that serve as drivers of partnership, (ii) Internal resource configurations – complementary versus supplementary resources that motivate and are sought through the partnership, (iii) Partner considerations that are taken into account when small IT vendors engage in partnerships, (iv) Partnership exploration where small IT vendors create a partner pool and maintain it and (v) Partnership development, which constitutes the activities pertaining to partner assessment, selection, negotiation, and structuring of partnership. The framework also includes a learning efects construct that indicates the dynamic nature of partnering activities among small IT vendors.

## 4.1. External market considerations

When asked about key drivers for entering into partnerships, all participants emphasised the strategic role of IT service partnerships for <sup>fi</sup>rm competitiveness and growth, and considered external market considerations, i.e., market uncertainty and technological uncertainty.

## 4.1.1. Market uncertainty

Interviewees reported Demand Management to be a key consideration for seeking to engage in a partnership with other vendors. Respondent <sup>fi</sup>rms faced di<sup>fi</sup>culty in forecasting the timing and extent of client demand for their services; thus, a key motivation for partnering with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms was the uncertainty in customer demand. Retaining slack resources was not feasible for the small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms; therefore, they utilised service partnerships to manage “peaks and valleys” in service provision.

It[Partnership] gives us the ability to always have access to up-talent, the right talent at the right time. Generally speaking that gives us ability to find price points, to look at the companies that are willing to work at diferent price points. That gives us a lot of flexibility not having to carry the bench; we don’t have to be using our bench to define our business delivery. Lambda, 14, Principal

Some interviewees also looked to partnerships as a growth mechanism for potential future business opportunities. Thus, Market Development was a key consideration in some cases. In our interviews, executives from two <sup>fi</sup>rms stated that their decision to engage in partnerships with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms was motivated by sales generation opportunities. They considered partnerships a contact with the customer for future sales. As noted by Sigma’s founder:

One would be lead generation. One would be to help us get the foot in the door at clients that they have, to ofer either a complementary service or to fill the gap that that partner has with that client. Sigma, I2, VP

## 4.1.2. Technological uncertainty

Technological uncertainty changes with the nature of IT services that are sought through partnerships. When the desired IT service involves a combination of technologically complex solution with distinct user needs, uncertainty becomes higher. On the other hand, when a fairly common technological solution is sought, uncertainties are low. Our respondents viewed technological uncertainty in terms of speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics of the IT service to be provided, which were cited as a motivation for engaging in partnerships. The services provided by the <sup>fi</sup>rms included software development and implementation, user experience design, hosting services, infrastructure design and implementation, and product integration. Interviewees distinguished attributes of the services to be provided as “commoditized” or “customized”. In commoditized services, there is very little di<sup>f</sup>erentiation, and advantages accrue primarily due to low costs of service provisioning through economies of scale. However, a customised IT service involves providing solutions that are tailored to speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs and requirements. Customised IT services are typically more complex and are associated with higher levels of technological uncertainty. Our respondents indicated that customised IT services need more careful approach to partnerships as compared to commoditized ones. As illustrated by the following quotes, interviewees considered the nature of IT service – commoditized or customised when they sought partnership approach to service provisioning.

What I would consider a commoditized development service, meaning that there is no really core expertise that is needed at the ground, something that we could ship to an ofshore partner to build not expensively. In those cases, typically, the components of the projects that are well defined, from a business requirement perspective there is no ambiguity there, there is no technical risk, it is pretty straightforward what needs to be done. Sigma I1, President

We are first and foremost a software development consulting firm, a custom software development as opposed to an integrator who might work with primarily packages and do integration. Our specialty is building software from the ground up. Rho I3, Principal Architect

## 4.2. Internal resource considerations

All participants also emphasised internal resource considerations as key drivers for partnership formation. One of the main conditions that drove our respondents to engage in an IT service partnership was a deficiency of resources critical for ful<sup>fi</sup>ling their service obligations. By acquiring resources from partners, these small <sup>fi</sup>rms could simply focus on their core competencies. Our respondents consistently reported that they could o<sup>f</sup>er additional services, or meet service obligations, by <sup>fi</sup>lling out the de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in their resource endowments through partnerships. Hence, via IT service partnerships, the <sup>fi</sup>rms could increase both the depth and breadth of service o<sup>f</sup>erings:

It [Partnership] increases the number of services we can provide to our clients. So for example, if we are building a new custom software development application and there is a large BI component to it, we don’t have a lot of in-house BI expertise, but we have a partner that does provide a lot of those, focusing on those services. So we can bring in them for that component of the project, still deliver the holistic solution while being able to focus on the things that we are good at. Sigma, I1, President

Throughout the narratives, we found choices pertaining to the nature of partnerships to be centred on the RBV-based a priori categories: to supplement or complement existing resources, skillsets, and capabilities. All <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample are software development companies, whose core business is custom solutions development (see Table 1). Thus, we characterised supplementary partnerships as those where the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm reported engaging in an alliance with another software development <sup>fi</sup>rms to obtain resources and competencies that were similar to their own, i.e., additional software development capabilities, while complementary partnerships were those where the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm was seeking to acquire di<sup>f</sup>erent internal capabilities, i.e., non-software development capabilities. Table 4 summarises our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by number of partners and acquired resource type, i.e., supplementary or complementary, for each <sup>fi</sup>rm. We next discuss each type of partnership in more detail.

## 4.2.1. Supplementary IT service partnerships

All <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample reported engaging in supplementary partnerships with other IT vendors. The aim of supplementary partnerships was to enhance the resource con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the focal partner. In our sample, supplementary partnerships were those where the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm was acquiring software development capabilities by increasing or augmenting current capabilities. When increasing capabilities, focal <sup>fi</sup>rms could enlarge their current development capabilities to ful<sup>fi</sup>l temporal or excess demand, e.g., access skilled labour for a particular software language. The following quote from an executive at Rho illustrates the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s partnership with another software vendor to acquire additional Ruby on Rails<sup>™</sup> skills.

There is a company that specializes in one technology, one of the many that we work in, [which] happens to be Ruby on Rails. So, at time we only had 3-4 people with those skills out of the 36 we have on staf. They are good guys and if we had some excess demand that we could not meet for Ruby on Rails development, we would certainly use them. Rho, I4, CEO

We also found <sup>fi</sup>rms forming partnerships with other development <sup>fi</sup>rms to augment their software development capabilities in related domains. In other words, the development service being provided by the <sup>fi</sup>rm required a speci<sup>fi</sup>c development capability that the <sup>fi</sup>rm did not adequately possess. The <sup>fi</sup>rm then engaged in a supplemental partnership to acquire the skill. For example, a senior manager from Rho (the company had considerable database expertise including some expertise in ORACLE products) described how they acquired supplemental ORACLE expertise:

We are not an ORACLE shop. So, let’s say we are doing a development project that involves some ORACLE expertise. We connect with a third party firm that has experience and expertise in ORACLE; we partner with them. Let’s say a development team of 5 people, 4 come from our company and one comes from the third party that provides the ORACLE expertise. Rho, I1, COO

## 4.2.2. Complementary IT service partnerships

Six of the seven software development <sup>fi</sup>rms in our study reported forming complementary service partnerships with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms for service provisioning. Through these partnerships, <sup>fi</sup>rms aimed to gain new capabilities to enhance their software development o<sup>f</sup>erings. Examples of such complementary capabilities reported by our interviewees were user experience design, hosting services, infrastructure design and implementation, and product integration. As summarised by the senior manager of Beta:

We realized probably 6-7 years ago that we can’t do everything. In certain places we just decided not to develop [internal] capabilities. Primarily in two: Hosting, the idea of deploying our software under a stable web based environment, we decided a partner for hosting services and we decided to partner with companies that do design and usability testing and brand integration, marketing communication kind of work. We decided not to staf graphic designers. So we built a variety of partnerships with both of those types of organizations. Beta, I1, Managing Partner

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>ve <sup>fi</sup>rms (Beta, Rho, Omega, Sigma, and Gamma) complemented their Web development services with user experience design competencies. Two <sup>fi</sup>rms (Gamma and Lambda) formed partnerships with local infrastructure and network <sup>fi</sup>rms. Gamma and Beta reported partnering with hosting <sup>fi</sup>rms. Only one <sup>fi</sup>rm, Psi, did not report complementary service partnerships with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 4.3. Partner considerations

In addition to the resource endowment of the partner, respondent IT small and mid-sized <sup>fi</sup>rms consistently reported four major partner considerations they took into account in identifying potential partners. The location of the partner, the relative size of the partner <sup>fi</sup>rm relative to the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, competitive overlap of the <sup>fi</sup>rm with the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm (i.e., whether the partner was a direct competitor to the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm with overlap customer market segments) and prior partnership experience of the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

## 4.3.1. Potential partner location

While participants generally expressed a preference for local (Chicago-based) partners due to convenience, our analysis of their descriptions indicated a more complex picture. Firms engaged with both local and non-local, i.e., domestic (outside of Chicago but in the US) and international (India), <sup>fi</sup>rms to form partnerships. All <sup>fi</sup>rms reported engaging partners from the local Chicago area and most <sup>fi</sup>rms (Omega and Sigma are exceptions) also reported domestic partners, i.e., US-based <sup>fi</sup>rms that are outside of the Chicago area. With the exception of Omega and Beta, all <sup>fi</sup>rms reported at least one o<sup>f</sup>shore or international partnership.

4.3.2. Potential partner size relative to focal <sup>fi</sup>rm Interviewees reported taking relative potential partner size into account in their search for potential partners. In some cases, size was associated with the stability of the potential partner.

Ofshore I needed a bigger firm, because I needed to be able to go ofshore with some stability. A small ofshore firm of 30 or 40 people may be in business today, may not be in business tomorrow. I needed a bit of stability You needed to be a little bigger when ofshore. Psi, II, Executive VP

In general, <sup>fi</sup>rms preferred similar or smaller-sized partners to build relationships, and thus trust, directly with the head or owner of the partner <sup>fi</sup>rm. In addition, they could increase control over the collaboration and the partner’s resource commitment. Gamma’s chairman elaborated the situation as follows:

At the smaller companies, we can deal with the owners. So we can build a level of trust with the owners of those organizations. They have a vested interest in putting energy into the relationship. A larger company, we are just not driving the volume of business to make it worthwhile to make it a serious partnership. Gamma, I1, Chairman

## 4.3.3. Competitive overlap with focal <sup>fi</sup>rm

Respondents emphasised that they were unlikely to partner with direct competitors. In particular, if the potential partner competed in multiple segments and provided software development services in competition with the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, interviewees expressed concern for the potential for leakage of proprietary customer information, as pointed by Beta’s senior manager:

We make a policy; . . . we never partner with somebody who is a competitor, who has competitive services. So for example, I would not partner with a design firm that does software development. Here I think it is impossible for them to shield competitive services on that [customer] account. Beta, I1, Managing Partner

## 4.3.4. Prior partnership experience

Another important consideration recounted by respondent <sup>fi</sup>rms was the prior experience of potential partner <sup>fi</sup>rms in working with other IT <sup>fi</sup>rms. Our respondent <sup>fi</sup>rms reported a preference for partners with such a track record. The main rationale for this preference is to reduce overhead in establishing and maintaining an e<sup>f</sup>ective collaborative work relationship with the potential partner. This consideration would also reduce any learning curve in instituting a “collaborative culture” to work towards shared goals. The potential partner’s values, such as being honest and transparent, keeping commitments, not competing, and endangering <sup>fi</sup>rm’s customer ownership, were also emphasised. As explained by one of our respondents:

So, when we try to qualify a partner, we ask them the questions that based on our experiences with other clients before, challenges that they faced with other partners, and how they react to it, it is one way to qualify them. Now the credibility part is do they have the same culture as we do. If we commit to do a deliverable for a client, do they have the same level of commitment to deliver that because we promise that this partner is an extension of our firm, right? So if they don’t deliver as we do, then they are not the same. Beta, 13, Director

## 4.4. Partnership exploration

The Partnership Exploration stage is comprised of the establishment and maintenance of a partner pool, or a group of vetted potential vendor partners. Firms in the pool are not involved in an active partnership with the focal <sup>fi</sup>rms but the focal <sup>fi</sup>rms view them as viable and ongoing relationships.

## 4.4.1. Partner pool establishment

Our respondents reported that that they did not engage in partner search and selection at the onset of every new customer project. Instead, they created a small partner pool consisting of a few IT service providers to complement or supplement their resources and competencies as necessary. As a respondent from Sigma explained:

## We have partners in our back pockets in diferent areas of complementary services. Typically, the relationship with the firm has been created before the need arises. Sigma, I1, VP

The respondents reported that they assess potential IT service partners according to their resource needs and other consideration criteria discussed in the previous section (see Partner Considerations). They identify and vet candidates and <sup>fi</sup>nally selected those to be added to the pool. Focal <sup>fi</sup>rm respondents considered partnerships to be those that were active at the time of the interview and involved in an ongoing project, as well as those in the pool. When a new project opportunity arises, <sup>fi</sup>rms refer to the pool to identify the most appropriate partner for the speci<sup>fi</sup>c project and then activate the partnership.

To identify a potential partner to be added to the pool, the <sup>fi</sup>rms relied on two main sources. First, they took into account referrals from known and trusted a<sup>fi</sup>liates such as customers, employees, and partners. The “stamp” of a known a<sup>fi</sup>liate was perceived as a strong endorsement in favour of the prospective partner and a validation check that saved partnership formation e<sup>f</sup>orts. As narrated by Psi’s senior manager:

They have to have sort of that stamp that “we are recommending this partner for your partnership because we think they will add value to your business model.” And I am sure those partners who are calling me unsolicited will add value, but I don’t know them and that adds higher risk as well as too much work to establish a partnership. Psi, I1, Executive VP

Second, the respondents reported utilising various technology-focused associations and a<sup>fi</sup>liations, such as user group meetings, local technology communities, conferences, or technology ecosystems (e.g., Microsoft Partner Platform), as a source for identifying potential partners:

We are also active in ITA {Illinois Technology Association} and user groups, technology groups where people meet on regularly to learn about specific technologies, we are very active in those communities. That is where people kind of get to know us. And sometimes the partnerships grew up out of that. Omega, I3, Director

## 4.4.2. Partner pool maintenance

All respondents emphasised the need for long-term relationships with the potential partner <sup>fi</sup>rms, reporting multi-year relationships in many cases. Participants stated that this approach saved project-speci<sup>fi</sup>c search e<sup>f</sup>orts, thus, minimising partner search and selection costs and enabling timely response to service provisioning requests.

One of the relationships we have, we have been working on and of together for almost ten years, another one has been two years but we expect it to continue. Gamma, I1, Chairman

Yes, think about it, for a company like ours to construct a partnership and to maintain it, this is investment. . . . So, obviously it is on our best interest to create partnerships that persist. Because the long term is that the more we have to pay, give the time and money, or whatever, to build all those partnerships, and test out what will work for us. Beta, I1, Managing Partner

Participants reported that they nurtured and managed relationships with pool members at the senior management level. For example, managers’ social a<sup>fi</sup>liations (such as residency in the same neighbourhood, membership in the same alumni association) o<sup>f</sup>ered opportunities to foster the relationship and create bonds between partners. These a<sup>fi</sup>liations were utilised not only to socialise but also to strategize with partners, i.e., to exchanging expectations, aligning business perspectives, further developing joint service o<sup>f</sup>erings, and targeting new customers. As a senior manager reported:

It is really just to keep in touch with what, where they are headed, what are their expectations, what their satisfaction is with the partnership. The partner company, that’s a living real organism that changes and has its own ideas, objectives that may change quarterto-quarter or year-to-year. Beta, I1, Managing Partner

These pool maintenance activities were performed with the foreign partners as well, despite fewer opportunities to interact due to the distance. The participants narrated that they spent signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ort to stay engaged with distant partners, share project prospects and keep communication channels open.

One of the owners of that ofshore firm, I talk with them every 2-3 weeks. And it is not really the projects. We talk about the projects, as well as we talk about business and opportunities, challenges just to make sure we communicate the channels are open. Psi, I1, Executive VP

While participants deemed IT service partnerships as crucial arrangements for competitiveness and growth, they reported relatively few (less than 6) active partnerships. The participants stated that they could initiate only a limited number of new IT projects in a given period; hence, the scale would not satisfy the volume requirements of multiple partners. Identifying partners, negotiating with them, and fostering the relationships were laborious activities for these resource-constrained <sup>fi</sup>rms. Therefore, they tended to concentrate on a few complementary and supplementary service partnerships, which they considered as investments. Beta’s senior manager explained that strategic choice:

Think about a company like ours. To construct a partnership and to maintain it, this is investment. Sometimes, it is intellectual bandwidth, how we design this. We have to invest in money too. We sent out emails, lots of combined oferings. So it is investment in all of these dimensions. So, obviously it is on our best interest to create partnerships that persist. Beta, I1, Managing Partner

## 4.5. Partnership development

Once an opportunity for a new project arose and the need for a partner was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, the partnership is materialised around the project initiation. We label this phase as Partnership Development. The <sup>fi</sup>rms reported two primary activities: Partner Assessment and Selection, selecting a partner from the pool based on project resource need, i.e., based on complementary or supplementary capabilities as discussed previously, and Negotiation and Structuring of the Partnership, or formally negotiating a structure for the partnership that is enforced through contractual arrangements We next discuss the latter activity in more detail.

## 4.5.1. Negotiation and structuring of partnership

As a norm in the software industry, all <sup>fi</sup>rms signed a non-disclosure agreement (NDA) with their supplementary and complementary partners at the initial contact. Additionally, at the beginning of each project, they furnished a Statement of Work (SOW) or a Service Level Agreement (SLA) to clarify the project details, deliverables, methodologies, timelines, billing scheme, and responsibilities. Additionally, Gamma and Lambda signed master partnership agreements to determine partnership terms with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c partner in general and then attached each project-level document to the master one. As summarised by Gamma’s senior manager:

Any contractor or partner, even a firm that’s selling services to us, typically we have a master services agreement what we call a master consulting agreement that would define the terms of how we are going to work together, what are the termination rights, what are the nondisclosure terms, what is the, what are the things that might cause a breach of contract. Those are things spelled out in the contract. And there is usually a statement of work that says “We are going to work from this month to that month, this is going to be the rate, these are the people and the nitty gritty details.” One of them, the master agreement will govern the relationship. The statement of work will govern the specific project that is laid out. Gamma, I3, Client Partner

## 4.6. Learning e<sup>f</sup>ects

At the close of an active partnership project, all <sup>fi</sup>rms reported informally and subjectively evaluating partners’ technical competencies and trustworthiness to determine whether the <sup>fi</sup>rm was suitable for future projects. We label the knowledge gained from this assessment as Learning E<sup>f</sup>ects (see Figure 1). All of the participants reported that they preferred to work with previous known partners when a partnership opportunity arose.

Like I said if there is a chance for us getting a project now and we have to actually go out and find a brand new partner at this point is very, very rare. We are pretty much already have <partners> lined up. We know who to go in certain situations; we probably always have worked with them before. Lambda, 14, Principal

Working with a partner in a project allowed the <sup>fi</sup>rm to gain knowledge of the partner’s technical prowess as well as standards or behaviour. In general, the interviewees described partnership formation with metaphors such as dating, getting married, and making a friendship, emphasising a rather cautious, experimental, and risk-averse style. Respondents often chose the initial project for a partner carefully to learn about and assess the working relationship as exempli-<sup>fi</sup>ed in the following quote:

It is similar to dating. Just like when you date someone for the first time, you want be careful. You want to schedule something that might not be a super high-risk engagement. You want to start with something to get to know [each other]. So, you both get to know each other. You set a rhythm, and then later on the more you feel comfortable, it is easier to engage, the expectations are set, you don’t have to reestablish the same baseline every time. I would call it a kind of dating. Gamma, I3, Client Partner

In these technology-based relationships, trust in partner’s technical competencies is necessary but not suf-<sup>fi</sup>cient. Our respondents indicated that having trust in partner’s values (such as being honest and transparent, keeping commitments, and avoiding opportunistic behaviour) is more fundamental.

Trust in that they are representing themselves fairly and honestly. Trust that they would do what they say they are going to do. Trust during the contract negotiations. Lambda, I3, Principal

None of the <sup>fi</sup>rms implemented formal, structured partnership evaluation activities. The main evaluation criteria were the partner’s technical performance, customer satisfaction for the particular project, and the perception of the partner’s general conduct and attitude such as evidence of opportunistic behaviour. Based on the evaluations, the <sup>fi</sup>rms decided whether to retain that particular partner in the pool or not.

We don’t have formal evaluation process. But if one of our partner firms starts to fail in projects or have some perpetual problems, then we might want to stop and say “What is the pattern here? Is there something going wrong and are they still in a position to be a partner we continue to work with?” Gamma, I3, Client Partner

It is very important that they <partners> are long term because we build trust. Because in IT you will have some projects that are going to be successful, and there are some that are not going to successful. And on the not successful ones, where you end up go over the budget or scope changes that trust is very important because then you have the customer’s or client’s best interest, both you, meaning us and the partner. And we both cannot walk away, we have to finish it up. Psi, I1, Executive VP

## 4.7. Variation in partnership formation activities by partnership type

In this section, we report the results of our crosspartnership analysis. As discussed previously, we grouped partnerships categorised as supplementary and those categorised as complementary and compared the reported partnership formation activities. We next discuss the results, which are also summarised in Table 5.

## 4.7.1. External market considerations

We observed no di<sup>f</sup>erences in the reported external market considerations as <sup>fi</sup>rms considered the need for partnership engagement.

## 4.7.2. Internal resource considerations

As discussed previously, we found choices pertaining to the nature of partnerships to be centred on the RBV-based a priori categories: to supplement or complement existing resources, skillsets, and capabilities.

## 4.8. Partner considerations

We observed that three of the four considerations that were emphasised by interviewees, i.e., location, size, and competitive overlap, were weighed di<sup>f</sup>erently depending on whether the focal IT <sup>fi</sup>rm sought a supplementary or complementary partnership and in some cases, the nature of the service. We further elaborate on each of these key considerations when discussing partner characteristics in the following section. We found no di<sup>f</sup>erence in the consideration of prior partnership experience; all respondent <sup>fi</sup>rms reported a preference for partners with such a track record.

## 4.8.1. Potential partner location

Interestingly, our analysis indicates that the type of service being developed in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the location preference of partners, regardless of whether the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm was acquiring a supplementary or complementary resource. Participants reported supplementary partnerships with non-local partners in software development projects that entailed less complex, wellde<sup>fi</sup>ned components and featured low ambiguity and minimal customer interactions. The participants characterised these projects as “commoditized development”. Likewise, <sup>fi</sup>rms engaged in supplementary partnerships for packaged software implementation and integration such as ERP, Microsoft, or own products (like Lambda and Io) could also partner with non-local (across the US) software <sup>fi</sup>rms thanks to well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned components, and integration and implementation activities, hence, low ambiguity.

However, for custom development e<sup>f</sup>orts, <sup>fi</sup>rms preferred supplementary partnerships with local IT <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Another diferentiator of ours is the business logic intensive type of work that you need to keep close to home for collaboration reasons. We don’t do the commoditized stuf. We do the stuf that requires the business logic, and really understanding the business and staying close to the business. Sigma, I2, VP

The design and usability guys, they have to work closer to the client, they have to understand their needs, their psychology. ## 4.8.2. Potential partner size relative to focal <sup>fi</sup>rm

In complementary partnerships, the choice was di<sup>f</sup>erent; the partner’s size was a decision criterion for complementary partners. In general, <sup>fi</sup>rms preferred similar or smaller-sized partners to build relationships, and thus trust, directly with the head or owner of the partner <sup>fi</sup>rm. In addition, they could increase control over the collaboration and the partner’s resource commitment. Gamma’s chairman elaborated the situation as follows:

At the smaller companies, we can deal with the owners. So we can build a level of trust with the owners of those organizations. They have a vested interest in putting energy into the relationship. A larger company, we are just not driving the volume of business to make it worthwhile to make it a serious partnership. Gamma, I1, Chairman

Our analysis indicated that <sup>fi</sup>rm size and location are sometimes intertwined. Firms engaged in international supplementary partnerships favoured mediumor large-sized software <sup>fi</sup>rms. This preference was due to the belief that medium to larger size <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to provide consistency in the partner’s resource commitment and overall stability across projects. As explicitly stated by the senior manager of Psi:

Ofshore I needed a bigger firm, because I needed to be able to go ofshore with some stability. A small ofshore firm of 30 or 40 people may be in business today, may not be in business tomorrow. I needed a bit of stability. You needed to be a little bigger when ofshore. Psi, I1, Executive VP

## 4.8.3. Competitive overlap with focal <sup>fi</sup>rm

All respondents emphasised that they were unlikely to partner with direct competitors, particularly when acquiring supplementary resources from software <sup>fi</sup>rms active in the Chicago Metropolitan area. This was not a consideration when acquiring complementary resources unless the potential partner competed in multiple segments and provided software development services in competition with the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. This consideration was due to the potential for leakage of proprietary customer information, as pointed by Beta’s senior manager:

We make a policy; . . . we never partner with somebody who is a competitor, who has competitive services. So for example, I would not partner with a design firm that does software development. Here I think it is impossible for them to shield competitive services on that [customer] account. Beta, I1, Managing Partner

## 4.8.4. Prior partnership experience

Our analysis revealed no di<sup>f</sup>erences across <sup>fi</sup>rm partnerships in the consideration of partnership experience regardless of the complementary or supplementary nature of the partnership. Interviewees uniformly preferred potential partner <sup>fi</sup>rms to have prior partnership experience.

## 4.9. Partnership exploration

Our analysis indicated di<sup>f</sup>erences in Partner Pool Establishment activities but little di<sup>f</sup>erence in the Partner Maintenance activities.

## 4.9.1. Partner pool establishment

We previously identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two primary sources interviewees reported using to identify potential partners: (1) referrals from trusted a<sup>fi</sup>liates among customers, employees, or existing partners, (2) associates in local technology communities or other groups in the local technology ecosystem. In our cross-partnership analysis, we found that <sup>fi</sup>rms relied on di<sup>f</sup>erent sources for identifying supplementary and complementary partners.

While searching for a complementary partner, <sup>fi</sup>rms limited the source for potential partners to referrals from known and trusted a<sup>fi</sup>liates among customers, employees, or existing partners. They did not choose <sup>fi</sup>rms based on relationships in associations communities as candidate partners unless this <sup>fi</sup>rm was referred by a trusted colleague. For the candidate partners, the <sup>fi</sup>rms gave most weight to a candidate’s trustworthiness and reputation and “word of mouth” evaluation of a<sup>fi</sup>liates. This view is exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed in the following quote:

Typically, I only search for partners that are referred or recommended by people I know. So, the people I know might be a client, could be an employee or could be an existing partner. They [complementary partner] have to come through one of these channels. Psi, I1, Executive VP

On the other hand, to identify a potential supplementary partner, the <sup>fi</sup>rms relied on referrals from trusted a<sup>fi</sup>liates as well as recommendations from less wellknown acquaintances from technology-focused associations or a<sup>fi</sup>liate groups. Assessment of a supplementary partner among candidate <sup>fi</sup>rms was relatively more technical and objective than that of complementary partners. Respondents reported higher levels of control and more comfort in supplementary partner assessment. For supplementary partners, internal IT employees such as system architects, engineers, developers, and project managers could e<sup>f</sup>ectively vet candidates via background checks, perform technical interviews, and review prior project deliverables and case studies. Due to familiarity and knowhow about the software development industry, the <sup>fi</sup>rms could also correctly identify and avoid competitors, if any, and hence minimise the risks associated with partner’s opportunistic behaviours.

For o<sup>f</sup>shore supplementary service partnerships, partner identi<sup>fi</sup>cation activities were similar, yet more in-depth; via detailed due diligence, the <sup>fi</sup>rms could evaluate o<sup>f</sup>shore partner’s service provision model, value proposition, and expertise level. The assessment often included visits to o<sup>f</sup>shore <sup>fi</sup>rm’s premises and face-to-face interactions. The presence of a local representative o<sup>fi</sup>ce in Chicago enhanced candidate’s visibility and credibility; thus, simplifying the search and selection e<sup>f</sup>orts and saving related costs. As narrated by Gamma’s Chief Information O<sup>fi</sup>cer:

It was more background information to understand how the company positions itself, what type of brand they present to customers, are they internally focused or are they externally focused, are they solution vs. skill set based, do they move in to being a trusted advisor or <are> they really more of a vendor of services. . . . I invited them here. I looked for whether their story was credible – for whether their story, whether they walked the walk meaning how they acted and how they engaged – was it reflective of what they said they were going to do or was it nonreflective of what they said they were going to do. So, we call that walking the walk. And we looked at their methodology to see whether it complemented how we do our work. And those were the final criteria. Gamma, I2, CTO

## 4.9.2. Partner pool maintenance

We found very little di<sup>f</sup>erence in partner pool maintenance activities when comparing the type of IT resource, with one exception: none of the participants reported the need to develop and foster bonds with the hosting partners.

## 4.10. Partnership development

4.10.1. Negotiation and structuring of partnership Our analysis revealed variances in partnership negotiation and structuring between supplementary and complementary partnerships. As discussed earlier, all <sup>fi</sup>rms signed partner-speci<sup>fi</sup>c agreements, e.g., NDA, and project-speci<sup>fi</sup>c agreements, e.g., SOW, with all partners. However, for supplementary partnerships, the <sup>fi</sup>rms reported designing more detailed agreements as they were more knowledgeable about the technical details and nuances in software development. Moreover, they could more con<sup>fi</sup>dently govern the partnership via formal and precise agreements.

Alternatively, for complementary partnerships, although the <sup>fi</sup>rms could frame the partnership and partner’s responsibilities, it was challenging to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the services to be rendered by the partner in detail. Therefore, respondents reported that they had to rely more on informal trust-based mechanisms for governance, characterised as a “handshake” by one respondent:

## We have nothing in place with them [design partners] with them it is just a handshake. Sigma 12

In sum, we observed some di<sup>f</sup>erences in terms of partner preferences and partner identi<sup>fi</sup>cation activities across IT service partnership types. In supplementary service partnerships, the <sup>fi</sup>rms reported employing more <sup>fl</sup>exible strategies with variety of options due to the fact that they were acting in “familiar waters”, namely within their own business domains with known players, practices, methodologies, and norms. The more commoditized, thus the less customised, the service o<sup>f</sup>ered by the partner, the less constrained were the partnership formation practices and choices. On the contrary, when assessing complementary partners, the <sup>fi</sup>rms were performing the vetting outside of their core business domain under higher level of ambiguity about prospective partners and their competencies. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rms tended to rely heavily on trusted a<sup>fi</sup>liates’ referrals and evaluations in search and vetting activities; hence their practices and choices were more limited.

## 5. Discussion

IT service partnerships are arrangements between <sup>fi</sup>rms involved in the production and delivery of IT services in a collective way. These partnerships are gaining in popularity as the demand for increasingly sophisticated and complex IT services continues to grow. Much of the existing IT literature addresses partnerships in a client– vendor relationship, particularly in an outsourcing context, while IT vendor partnerships have received little attention. Despite the growing interest among IS outsourcing researchers to understand vendor capabilities (Feeny et al., 2005; Levina & Ross, 2003), most of these studies implicitly assume that IT vendors primarily use internal resources and do not take into account the collaborative arrangements made by the vendors with other service providers to address resource requirements (Nevo & Kotlarsky, 2014). Jarvenpaa and Mao (2008) highlighted the relevance of the mediated outsourcing model by focusing on the relationship between the primary IT vendor and sub-contractor IT <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Our study analyzes collaborative IT service partnerships between small and medium-sized IT <sup>fi</sup>rms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we investigate partnership formation practices of seven Chicago-based small to mediumsized IT vendors. We <sup>fi</sup>rst explore how small IT vendors form IT service partnerships and next examine whether small IT vendors show diferences in partnership formation mechanisms depending on the nature of the resource needed, i.e., supplementary or complementary.

Building upon RDT and RBV perspectives, we propose a theoretically based framework that delineates a number of key issues related to the formation of IT partnerships to guide our investigation. Our study underscores the importance of forging inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm partnerships among IT vendors as a viable organisational strategy. All <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample employed service partnerships as the key part of their IT serviceprovisioning strategy. Our results suggest IT service partnerships are especially salient to resourceconstrained small IT vendors who are competing in highly dynamic markets. Importantly we identify a new stage in partnership formation: the development and maintenance of a partner pool. In the following paragraphs, we re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the implications of our results for theory and practice. We also discuss limitations that may be overcome through future research.

## 5.1. Contributions to IS research

## 5.1.1. Framework for understanding IT vendor service partnership formation for IT SME <sup>fi</sup>rms

We began our study with a review of the literature on partnership formation to guide our <sup>fi</sup>eld research. A set of interlinked constructs critical to partner choice and partnership formation activities emerged from our analysis. We propose a conceptual framework on IT service partnership formation to further throw light on key variables that underscore the dynamics of partnership formation among small IT vendors. This framework (see Figure 1) is comprised of four sets of activities: (i) External market considerations and Internal resource con<sup>fi</sup>gurations that serve as key drivers of IT service partnership formation; (ii) Partner considerations or salient factors taken into account by small IT vendors; (iii) Partnership exploration and assessment where pre-partnership activities take place; and (iv) Partnership development, which encompasses the actual selection of partner and structuring of partnership.

In our framework, IT service partnerships among small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms are described as a product of two motivating forces that serve as key considerations for IT service partnership. Resource de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, market uncertainties, and technological uncertainties serve as key drivers stimulating the partnership arrangements. Since small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms are often more resource-poor and less socially embedded than established IT <sup>fi</sup>rms, they are usually at a disadvantage as compared to established IT <sup>fi</sup>rms in forming partnerships. Partnerships not only o<sup>f</sup>er a means to address current resource gaps but also a way for potential future business growth.

As a theoretical foundation for our study, we argued that the small IT vendors will choose service partners based on their need to extend the scale or scope of their resources for IT services provision. In other words, small IT vendors will engage in partnership formation to obtain access to supplementary resources and competencies, forming relationships with <sup>fi</sup>rms o<sup>f</sup>ering similar services to the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm, or complementary resources and competencies, forming relationships with dissimilar <sup>fi</sup>rms. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support this argument.

Our framework also highlights key external and internal considerations for small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms in pursuing partnerships and how these considerations drive partnership formation. Though external considerations drove small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms to pursue partnerships, the internal resources played a key role. As shown in Table 4, six of the seven <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample reported engaging in both supplementary and complementary partnerships. The partnerships could be distinguished based on resources needs, i.e., supplementary partnerships were intended to increase the scale of resources, such as programming and software development capabilities, while comple mentary partnerships were employed to gain user experience design, network design and implementation capabilities.

As illustrated in Table 5, we found that the need for supplementary or complementary resources was a key criterion in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s choice of partners and partnership formation practices. Managers reported di<sup>f</sup>erent preferences for the geographic location of the partner depending on the objective of the partnership. All <sup>fi</sup>rms engaging in complementary service partnerships reported having partners from the same local community, i.e., the Chicago Metropolitan Area, whereas <sup>fi</sup>rms engaging in supplementary service partnerships were more likely to collaborate with partners located outside of the Chicago area, either in the US or o<sup>f</sup>shore. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the importance of location as a key consideration and preference for local partners by small IT vendors mirror similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the SME strategic alliances literature (Franco & Haase, 2015).

We found that the preference of partner <sup>fi</sup>rm size relative to the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm varied based on the nature of resources needed. In supplementary partnerships, <sup>fi</sup>rms collaborated with IT <sup>fi</sup>rms of micro to medium size whereas they strictly preferred smaller-sized partners in complementary partnerships. We attribute these preferences to the need for greater control over partners providing resources outside of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s “comfort zone” and domain knowledge (Das & Teng, 2001). When small vendors partner with larger <sup>fi</sup>rms, the arrangements tend to be driven by the dominant larger partner. For complementary partnerships, though large IT <sup>fi</sup>rms might be in better position to provide required resources, we <sup>fi</sup>nd small IT vendors to prefer to partner with other smaller IT <sup>fi</sup>rms. This preference could stem from their desire to have greater control over the partnership and also possibly to enhance their absorptive capacity to assimilate the external expertise that they currently lack. Moreover, partnering with smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms would also help the vendors to monitor and control any potential opportunistic behaviour by the partner. Prior experience with partnerships was also a key consideration for small IT vendors in the partnership formation process. Small IT vendors may look for partners that seem generally competent at inter-organisational coordination so that sustenance and maintenance of relationships become easier over the lifecycle of the partnership. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings from strategic alliance studies (e.g., Shah & Swaminathan, 2008) that have reported process manageability (i.e., degree of interaction, communication, control, and coordination required for the alliance tasks) and prior partnership experience (Gulati, 1999) to play a key role in partner selection.

Our framework also highlights how small IT vendors engage in dyadic relationships and develop the partnership, i.e., search for and selects partners and structure the collaborative arrangement. Alliance building activity – including partner search, selection, negotiating, and structuring – has proved to be particularly challenging for SMEs. Our research throws light on speci<sup>fi</sup>c alliance formation practices that small IT vendors follow.

## 5.1.2. Partner pool creation and maintenance

A key <sup>fi</sup>nding in our research is the existence of “partner pool” creation and maintenance activities that we found to be key to partnership formation for all <sup>fi</sup>rms in our study, regardless of the type of partner. Instead of initiating a new systematic search at the onset of each project, small IT vendors created in advance a small “back pocket” consisting of a few, reliable and technically competent IT <sup>fi</sup>rms, including the o<sup>f</sup>shore ones, to which they would refer at each customer project. We posit that partner pool creation helps the IT vendors optimise search and selection e<sup>f</sup>orts and minimise relevant costs, hence, increasing the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of resource allocation and management. Moreover, consistent with strategic alliance literature (Kumar, 2014), we expect that such partner pool creation could greatly reduce di<sup>f</sup>erent ambiguities that arise at di<sup>f</sup>erent stages of alliance formation and management. Strategic alliance literature has highlighted the need for <sup>fi</sup>rms to maintain and sustain an alliance portfolio (Wassmer, 2010) where focal <sup>fi</sup>rms form and manage a network of partnerships. The idea of alliance portfolio has also been documented in the context of SMEs as well. (e.g., Moon & Lee, 2017), but the formation of a “partner pool” with no formal contractual obligations has not been studied. Our <sup>fi</sup>nding regarding the “partner pool” highlights an important and distinct activity that precedes the construction of a partnership portfolio among small IT vendors.

According to RDT, IT service partnerships are instruments for reducing power imbalances and for managing mutual dependencies (Drees & Heugens, 2013) between the focal IT vendor and its potential partners. The idea of creating a partner pool helps small and mid-sized IT <sup>fi</sup>rms cope up with resource uncertainty that can minimise their costs of exploring and assessing partners. Small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms can reduce search costs, mitigate potential power imbalances, and decrease the challenges posed by uncertain environments by forming partner pools that can be easily accessed as and when a need arises. Creation of partner pool also helps small IT vendors gain legitimacy among the key stakeholders such as clients, investors, and regulators (Drees & Heugens, 2013).

Our respondents alluded to the dynamic nature of the partner pool, with di<sup>f</sup>erent potential partners entering the pool at di<sup>f</sup>erent points in time. Further, partners are likely to migrate from the pool to an alliance portfolio depending on the nature of engagement, and based on the “learning e<sup>f</sup>ects”. Respondent <sup>fi</sup>rms in our study reported engagement in numerous partnerships, highlighting the importance of this practice to gain added value in a highly competitive and rapidly changing environment. Indeed, we found that the <sup>fi</sup>rms in our study were engaged in multiple partnerships with other IT vendors. They also reported dormant partners in the project pool.

While our research uncovered pool creation activities as a key aspect of IT service partnerships, some respondents highlighted the importance of pool maintenance activities. For example, regular communication with pool members was important not just to maintain the relationship but also to understand any strategic changes being undertaken by the partner. We suggest that maintaining an alliance portfolio for IT service partnerships, and managing issues that come up during the course of partnership – such as collaboration continuity, assessment of outcomes, con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution, and mitigation will be another area where SME’s must develop competencies in order to compete e<sup>f</sup>ectively.

## 5.1.3. Variations in partnership formation activities based on IT service partnership type

Our respondent managers reported di<sup>f</sup>erences in the partner search, assessment, and governance practices depending on the partnership type. In general, comple mentary partner acquisition practices re<sup>fl</sup>ected a substantial reliance on trust-based relationships and individualised, subjective assessment while managers were more likely to rely on more objective criteria and formalised practices in supplementary partner acquisition. For example, managers reported assessing supplementary partners via background checks, technical interviews, and reviews of prior projects while evaluation of complementary partners was conducted via recom mendations and assessment of trustworthiness and reputation from “trusted third parties.” Likewise, supplementary partnerships were governed by formal agreements while managers reported more informal trust-based governance practices, e.g., “hand shake and trust,” for complementary partnerships. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reinforce the interlinkages and trade-o<sup>f</sup>s between risk control, and trust in inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm alliances (Das & Teng, 2001). One possible explanation for these di<sup>f</sup>erences may be that since supplementary partnerships augment and extend existing <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities (such as software development skills), managers have experience and knowledge on how to acquire, allocate, manage, and monitor these resources, and therefore face less uncertainty in managing similar capabilities across <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries. Alternatively, in complementary partnerships, the <sup>fi</sup>rm gains access to dissimilar capabilities that extend the scope of their resources. Thus, managers have little experience in managing these resources and no developed practices that can be extended across <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries, thus they are likely to experience consider able uncertainty.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

Our study has important implications especially for project managers and business development professionals in IT service <sup>fi</sup>rms. These professionals need to carefully search for partners and make alliance decisions based on the resource similarity among the partners. They will need to expand their roles to understand and learn from complementary partners. They will need to implement stronger set of controls in order to protect their own intellectual assets in supplementary IT service partnerships. Balancing a portfolio set of supplementary and complementary partnerships is often di<sup>fi</sup>cult. Our study points to the importance of developing capabilities to e<sup>f</sup>ectively build and manage a service partnership portfolio.

Our study also suggests that managers in small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms seeking complementary partnerships for customised IT services should look for local partners who are of similar or smaller size. This can provide them with <sup>fl</sup>exibility in partnership arrangements, in addition to o<sup>f</sup>ering better control over any potential opportunistic behaviour of the partner. For those seeking to add and supplement to the current IT resource pool, partnerships from medium and large-sized partners, especially in o<sup>f</sup>shore contexts, may be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. Managers will also greatly bene<sup>fi</sup>t by maintaining a pool of potential partners who can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and developed through referrals from trusted a<sup>fi</sup>liates. The partner pool helps in minimising search costs and also help in providing timely access to required resources.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

There are of course limitations to our study. We acknowledge that our study is based on a sample of small IT vendors in one geographic location, i.e., Chicago metropolitan area, thereby limiting the generalisability of the results. However, this setting allows us to control for economic, social, and cultural contexts in which the <sup>fi</sup>rms operate. Location is an in<sup>fl</sup>uential construct on <sup>fi</sup>rm behaviour, o<sup>f</sup>ering an intermediary space for individual-, <sup>fi</sup>rm-, and institution-level interactions (Shaw and Gilly, 2000). These interactions are shaped by the economic, social, and cultural contexts of the spatial setting, in which they are situated. Future studies examining a similar sample in di<sup>f</sup>erent metropolitan locations will increase our understanding on the phenomenon.

In addition, we focused on small and mid-sized IT vendors as they are the engine of a national economy and often produce innovative IT services and products. Future research could investigate the role of <sup>fi</sup>rm size in partnership practices. For example, if the <sup>fi</sup>rms are of unequal size where one <sup>fi</sup>rm is signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly larger than the other, the dynamics of the partnership and the power relationships may lead to di<sup>f</sup>erent practices from what we observed. It should also be noted that it is a common practice among small IT vendors in industry to seek “certi<sup>fi</sup>ed partner” status or “preferred partner” status by entering into agreements with larger IT vendors. Our study did not explicitly examine the dynamics of control by larger IT vendors on small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms and this could be a fruitful extension of our work.

Recent research has highlighted the emergence of an ecosystem or interdependent networks of <sup>fi</sup>rms that drive delivery of complex IT products and services. Our research is intentionally constrained to the alliance formation between a focal <sup>fi</sup>rm and supplementary and complementary partners, a dyadic approach. Future research could examine the SME partner formation from a network perspective. For example, are there implications for the construction of IT service Partnerships (Figure 1) when there are interdependencies across a number of <sup>fi</sup>rms in a network?

Our data collection relied primarily on interviews with the key informants who were decision makers in focal <sup>fi</sup>rms initiating the partnerships. Our data is analysed from the perspective of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm and does not investigate the target <sup>fi</sup>rm in the partnership. Future research could investigate the partnership choices and formation strategies of the target <sup>fi</sup>rm.

As with most interview-based data collection, the memories of the individuals may not accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect actual conditions. As a remedy and to increase the rigour of the study, we interviewed multiple respondents at each <sup>fi</sup>rm. Moreover, our research method allows us to gain rich insight into an important aspect of the increasingly complex inter-organisational processes required for delivering IT services (Sarker et al., 2013), that of partnership choices made by vendors to jointly provide services to a client. Yet, our study has a cross-sectional design; it would be worthwhile to gather data longitudinally to analyse the evolution of the IT SMEs’ partnership formation practices.

Our analysis focused on types of resource endowments as the contingency factors that shape partner acquisition and service partnership formation. Researchers might explore other organisational, taskrelated or partner-related contingencies, and analyse the impact of the “value” of the IT service partnership on partner choices and formation practices. Last, but not least, our propositions call for testing; the researchers might derive hypotheses from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and test them via surveys based on larger samples.

## 6. Conclusion

Our study makes important contributions to literature. Contemporary <sup>fi</sup>rms in the IT service sector are increasingly using varied IT service partnerships to access and gain knowledge and other resources for successful execution of their client <sup>fi</sup>rms’ IT projects. Despite their importance, partnerships between IT vendor <sup>fi</sup>rms are an understudied phenomenon in the complex and highly volatile IT industry. Our study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to investigate this type of relationship, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally we investigate how small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms choose and engage in partnership arrangements with other small IT <sup>fi</sup>rms.

All <sup>fi</sup>rms in our study engaged in multiple partnerships to enhance both scale and scope of their service o<sup>f</sup>erings. Thus, our study calls attention to the importance of the portfolio of partnerships being used by IT <sup>fi</sup>rms to address resource de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. We draw upon the RBV and the notion of resource complementarity, to <sup>fi</sup>nd support for the notion that the resource con-<sup>fi</sup>gurations and task requirements of the <sup>fi</sup>rm play a key role in an IT <sup>fi</sup>rm’s choice of partners and the partnership formation process. In other words, IT <sup>fi</sup>rms may either supplement or complement their capabilities via partnerships and the objective of the partnership in<sup>fl</sup>uences the choice of partners and partnership formation practices. We also uncover creation and maintenance and the utilisation of partner pools by small IT vendors to mitigate risks associated with IT service partnerships. Accordingly, in the increasingly complex technological environment, it is important that these <sup>fi</sup>rms improve their alliance management capability to e<sup>f</sup>ectively build and manage a service partnership portfolio.

## Appendix B. Partial Interview Protocol

## Background Information

Interviewee Demographics: Provide information about yourself: Describe your role in the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Firm Demographics: Provide information about the <sup>fi</sup>rm (e.g., number employees, date and place of establishment, business locations, organisation structure)

Founder Information: Provide information about the founders and their current role.

IT Services and Products Information: Describe IT services and products o<sup>f</sup>ered by your <sup>fi</sup>rm, and technologies utilised to provide these services.

Customer Information: Describe your <sup>fi</sup>rm’s primary customer segment (e.g., size, industry, and location).

## Partnership Strategies

Explain the role and importance of IT service partnerships in your business model. Why does your <sup>fi</sup>rm form IT service partnerships?

Describe your <sup>fi</sup>rm’s preferences in terms of partner characteristics (e.g., size and location). What other considerations do you have in selecting partner <sup>fi</sup>rms?

## Partnership Portfolio

List all of your IT service partnerships and provide information about them (e.g., type of resource acquired, partner’s service scope, location, and size).

## Partner Search, Selection, and Structuring

Tell us about how your <sup>fi</sup>rm identi<sup>fi</sup>es its partners. What are the selection criteria? How does your <sup>fi</sup>rm negotiate resource acquisition? (e.g., agreements) Are there any di<sup>f</sup>erences depending on the type of resources acquired or the type of partner?
