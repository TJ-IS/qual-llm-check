# Curbing cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng: studying general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ects with <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence

Przemysław G. Hensel and Agnieszka Kacprzak

Faculty of Management, University of Warsaw, Warsaw, Poland

## ABSTRACT

Although the General Deterrence Theory has frequently been employed to study the prevention of misconduct associated with computer use, the common reliance on survey data makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure the general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ects. We use one-group pre-testpost-test quasi-experiment based on data from a monitoring system covering 230 employees for nine months to study general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c e<sup>f</sup>ects of two interventions: the reminder about possible punishment and the actual punishment. The interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) with ARIMA modelling employed to study the longitudinal e<sup>f</sup>ects of the interventions allowed to deliver novel <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Punishing the violators of organisational policy a<sup>f</sup>ected both the punished and unpunished employees (decrease in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng by 41% and 24%, respectively). The e<sup>f</sup>ect was maintained for three months, that is until the end of our dataset. The punishment had a stronger e<sup>f</sup>ect on unpunished employees located closer in the organisational structure to the punished ones than on the remaining unpunished employees. Moreover, we show that reminder about the possibility of punishment brought no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect, likely due to the earlier experience of punishment avoidance. We discuss implications for theory and designing organisational policies intended to curb cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng.

## 1. Introduction

When organisations punish employees for misconduct, they do it rarely just because they want to rectify transgressors’ behaviour. Such punishments are often meant to act as a deterrent, that is a signal sent to other members of the organisation that the threat of sanction is real. This is vital as organisational codes of conducts and policies are frequently nothing more than a window-dressing (Edelman, 1992; MacLean & Behnam, 2010), and they are often decoupled (Meyer & Rowan, 1977) from the actual organisational practices. Thus, sanctions-as-deterrents are essential for communicating to the employees which norms and policies will be enforced and which will be treated with some leeway.

In this study, we focus on punishing cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “any voluntary act of employees’ using their companies’ internet access during o<sup>fi</sup>ce hours to surf non-job related Web sites for personal purposes and to check (including receiving and sending) personal e-mail” (Lim, 2002, p. 677). Cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng constitutes a perfect case of small-scale misconduct as a single employee spending half an hour a day on a social network is not causing any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant harm to the organisation. Moreover, short pauses in work might even be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the case of knowledge workers, who need cognitive breaks for maintaining su<sup>fi</sup>cient productivity (Coker, 2013; Epstein et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2014; Lim & Chen, 2012). However, when thousands of employees do the same for prolonged periods of time, the total economic and social cost can be tremendous. According to estimates, the cost of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng to the US economy alone extends 85 USD billion annually (Zakrzewski, 2016). This issue is only going to gain signi<sup>fi</sup>cance as the increasing share of the global economy is made of information workers while it has been estimated that about 80% of them engage in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (Garrett & Danziger, 2008). At the same time, 77% of employees declare that they use social networks at work (Olmstead et al., 2016). Thus, studying the e<sup>f</sup>ects of punishment of such misconduct is likely to bring understanding that is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant not just to organisations but – due to its scale – also to society as a whole. What is more, given that cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng is a common phenomenon, the sanctioning policies towards such behaviour – if unwarranted by the e<sup>f</sup>ects they bring – could potentially cause unjusti<sup>fi</sup>ed discomfort to a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of people. For instance, it has been found that computer monitoring (a pre-requirement of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng punishment) may lead to increased rather than decreased levels of computer abuse, due to perceived injustice (Posey et al., 2011). Moreover, since some evidence suggests that low levels of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng may be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to employees’ performance, <sup>fi</sup>ne-tuning policies intended to reduce such work-place behaviour seems warranted (Coker, 2013; Epstein et al., 2016;

Kim et al., 2014; Lim & Chen, 2012). Accordingly, studying the scope and strength of the deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect of such policies can contribute to better organisational practice.

While the phenomenon of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng deterrence garnered considerable attention over the last decade, the research on that issue is associated with at least four challenges. First, the majority of studies is based on survey data. They are useful for numerous reasons, but at the same time are tainted with problems associated with self-reporting on sensitive issues (Dalal & Hakel, 2016; Podsako<sup>f</sup> & Organ, 1986). Second, studies based on <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence are often focused on the introduction of technologies to curb misconduct (Glassman et al., 2015), rather than on imposing punishment, thus the speci<sup>fi</sup>c deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect (impact of punishment and punishment avoidance on the punished employees) and general deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect (impact of punishment and punishment avoidance on those, who were not punished) cannot be studied. Third, studies of technical solutions to the problem of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (like the application of internet <sup>fi</sup>lters) are associated with one more problem – the used technology makes it impossible for the employees to continue their engagement in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. Hence, such studies have no data on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng after the technology was introduced. Fourth, the rare studies based on <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence related to cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng and other forms of unsanctioned IT usage employ data for just one (Silic et al., 2017) or only several points in time (Shepherd & Mejias, 2016). Such data, while useful, disallow to grasp the full dynamic of behavioural response to the punishment of misconduct over time.

In the current paper we use unique <sup>fi</sup>eld data that allows studying speci<sup>fi</sup>c and general deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ects. We employ a one-group pre-test-post-test quasiexperimental design (Shadish et al., 2001) with multiple pre-tests and post-tests (time series). The data we use spans nine months and was collected by an organisation-wide computer activity tracking system. The study was conducted in a mid-size public administration organisation in Northern Poland. Three months after the data collection began the employees were reminded that overusing the internet connection for private purposes will be sanctioned. Another three months later fourteen people were punished for unreserved abuse of their time spent online (above 2 hours a day on average for four weeks). Using interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) we study how the reminder and the sanction a<sup>f</sup>ected both the o<sup>f</sup>enders and the non-o<sup>f</sup>enders over the following months.

Employing this kind of data in a quasi-experiment allows addressing at least some of the challenges associated with the study of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. First, the collected data re<sup>fl</sup>ects actual use of computer devices, rather than a declaration of such use. Second, it allows studying e<sup>f</sup>ects of actual punishment rather than studying responses to hypothetical scenarios of misconduct and punishment, which are commonly used in the research on curbing cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. Third, application of organisational rather than a technological solution to cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (i.e., the punishment rather than the internet <sup>fi</sup>lter) allows to collect pre- and postintervention data, which can be used to estimate the direct and indirect e<sup>f</sup>ects of punishment. Fourth, our data spans nine months, thus allows to establish a credible before-the-intervention baseline and measure e<sup>f</sup>ects of such intervention in the subsequent three-months period.

Before we delve deeper into the study of cyberloafing a caveat is due. The popular literature often portrays cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng as a bane of the o<sup>fi</sup>ce world that brings costs for the companies without o<sup>f</sup>ering any advantages. We beg to di<sup>f</sup>er: while excessive levels of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng might be harmful, moderate levels of this behaviour can bring bene<sup>fi</sup>cial consequences for the employees. Engagement in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng allows taking a cognitive break and “unwinding” before proceeding to the next task (Coker, 2013; Epstein et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2014; Lim & Chen, 2012), dealing with the workplace stress (Anden et al., 2019), and managing the border between work-related and non-workrelated responsibilities (Konig & de la Guardia, 2014). Thus, the intention behind any sanction policy should be not to eliminate cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng entirely but rather reduce the level of the behaviour while still allowing for the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial consequences of cyberloafing to occur. Taking an opposite stance, that is reducing cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng to zero would most likely produce an unbearable workplace, particularly for younger employees.

## 2. General deterrence theory

The origins of deterrence theory can be tracked to eighteen-century works of Cesare Beccaria and Jeremy Bentham (Tomlinson, 2016). Simply put, the theory maintains that sanctions deter individuals from committing crimes (Gibbs, 1975; Paternoster, 2010). This thesis is closely associated with the basic premise of the rational choice theory: the prospective o<sup>f</sup>ender is understood to calculate the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the crime, the probability and cost of possible punishment to arrive at a decision if engagement in crime is pro<sup>fi</sup>table (Wenzel, 2004; Williams & Hawkins, 1986). Deterrence can be disentangled into two e<sup>f</sup>ects: speci<sup>fi</sup>c and general. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is associated with the personal experience of punishment that deters individuals from engaging in further crimes. For instance, drivers punished for driving while intoxicated are less likely to re-o<sup>f</sup>end (Lee & Teske, 2015) and cause fatal crashes (Wagenaar & Maldonado-Molina, 2007). The second is associated with the e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment on those, who were not punished but are deterred from committing a crime by seeing the ordeal of the punished individuals. For instance, introducing new countermeasures against driving under in<sup>fl</sup>uence of alcohol creates a generalised threat of sanction and leads to decrease in injuries and fatalities in drinking-related collisions (Byrne et al., 2016). In a similar vein, when penalties for common tra<sup>fi</sup>c o<sup>f</sup>ences (speeding, nonuse of seat belts) are increased, the number of o<sup>f</sup>ences decreases (Elvik, 2016). A more recent model of general deterrence theory shows that deterrence is also a<sup>f</sup>ected by previous experience of punishment avoidance (Sta<sup>f</sup>ord & Warr, 1993), that is cases where the law or norm was violated but the transgressor was not punished.

The deterring e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment depends on three major variables (Nagin & Pogarsky, 2001; Paternoster, 2010; Paternoster & Piquero, 1995; Wenzel, 2004). First, the severity of the punishment can a<sup>f</sup>ect the intent to engage in crime, with higher severity often assumed as having a stronger deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect. Second, the certainty of punishment a<sup>f</sup>ects the cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, with many scholars suggesting that it constitutes the central deterring factor (Paternoster, 2010; Wenzel, 2004). Finally, also the celerity (swiftness) of punishment is considered to a<sup>f</sup>ect the strength of the deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect. The research in the organisational setting also reveals that the way in which the punishment was administered (contingent or non-contingent on employees behaviour) impacts the e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment (Podsako<sup>f</sup> et al., 2006).

While the general deterrence theory certainly does not provide anything close to a complete explanation of causes of deviance (non)engagement, it remains a central framework focusing on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of punishment. It has been used to explain human and organisational behaviour in variety of contexts including criminal o<sup>f</sup>ences (Paternoster, 2010), world politics (Nalebu<sup>f</sup>, 1991), institutional maintenance (Lawrence & Suddaby, 2006), doping in sport (Overbye, 2017), and cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (see Moody et al., 2018 for review).

## 3. Deterrence of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng

In this paper, we focus on general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. Below we overview the extant literature considering the issues related to the impact of severity and risk of punishment on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng.

By engaging in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng employees expose organisations not just to considerable <sup>fi</sup>nancial costs but also to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant security threats. Due to these facts, the research on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, violation of accepted use policies, and information systems security policies has been blooming over the last twenty years. General deterrence theory remains one of the major approaches used to explain engagement and non-engagement in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng and violation of such policies (Moody et al., 2018).

The research so far tends to focus on two aspects of the problem. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one is the e<sup>f</sup>ect of monitoring on engagement in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. For instance, Straub (1990) surveyed 1211 organisations to <sup>fi</sup>nd that those which have formal computer security policies and technological controls have a lower incidence of computer abuse, suggesting that such measures deter possible transgressors. Also, Kankanhalli et al. (2003) found that the e<sup>f</sup>ort spent on computer security correlates with IS security e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. In a similar vein, the use of computer activity monitoring tends to limit personal use of work computer devices (Li et al., 2010; Urbaczewski & Jessup, 2002).

More central to our study is the research on the deterring e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment. Following the tradition set in the research on crime deterrence these studies tend to focus on two major issues: the impact of sanctions (penalties) in general and the impact of two constructs associated with sanctions, that is the perceived severity of punishment and the perceived probability of punishment. However, there is little agreement between studies on the role of each of the constructs in deterring cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. For instance, a study of 395 employees of three Finnish organisations revealed that the relationship between formal sanctions and intention to violate IS security policy has not achieved signi<sup>fi</sup>cance (Siponen & Vance, 2010). A survey of 440 employees from four organisations located in the US, Croatia, and France, combined with analysis of the actual use of forbidden software at the workplace revealed that neither formal nor informal sanctions have a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the use of such software (Silic et al., 2017). However, a survey study of 238 employees of a Finnish company indicates that the perceived penalties are negatively associated with attitudes leading to intent to cyberloaf (Moody & Siponen, 2013).

What is more, there appears to be mixed evidence on the relative impact of the perceived severity of punishment and perceived certainty of punishment. A survey study on relationships between awareness of sanctions and the intent to misuse IT resources (D’Arcy et al., 2009) found that perceived severity of sanctions has a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on the intention to misuse IT. However, such an e<sup>f</sup>ect was not observed for the perceived certainty of sanctions. In a similar vein, the study of 185 employees revealed that the perceived severity of sanctions has an impact on intention to violate IS security policy, while the perceived certainty of sanction turned out as not having a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect (Cheng et al., 2013).

These results stand in stark contrast to numerous studies which show that it is the certainty rather than the severity of punishment that deters potential o<sup>f</sup>enders (Paternoster, 1987). Accordingly, a survey study of 230 employees of Chinese companies (Cheng et al., 2014) found that the perceived detection certainty has a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on the intention to cyberloaf, while the perceived sanction severity has no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect. Likewise, in an online survey of 246 participants, it was shown that the perceived detection probability is positively related with compliance intention, while a relationship between perceived sanction severity and compliance intention was non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (Li et al., 2010). By the same token, a study of 2000 subjects indicates that announcement of formal controls of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng makes the perceived risk of detection a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of intention to cyberloaf (Khansa et al., 2017). A survey using scenarios administered to 228 employees of seven organisations and to MBA students (D’Arcy & Devaraj, 2012) found that the threat of formal sanctions remains to have a negative impact on intention to misuse computer resources, even when the informal sanctions are factored in. A survey of 163 MBA students revealed that the likelihood of sanctions correlates negatively with the intention to cyberloaf (Henle & Blanchard, 2008). What is more, a survey of 312 employees from 78 organisations found that punishment severity has a negative impact on intention to comply with computer security policy, contrary to what was hypothesised, while the certainty of detection was found to have a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on intention to comply (Herath & Rao, 2009).

Still other studies suggest that it is both perception of severity and perception of certainty of punishment that a<sup>f</sup>ect the intention to cyberloaf. For instance, an experimental study indicates that perception of the severity of punishment and perception of certainty of punishment a<sup>f</sup>ect the intention to violate IT security policy (Chen et al., 2012). On the basis of an experimental survey of 150 employees and students, Ugrin and Pearson (2013) found that the severity of sanctions a<sup>f</sup>ects the intention to engage in most kinds of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, while this e<sup>f</sup>ect is moderated by the increased certainty of detection and by evidence of past enforcement for certain kinds of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng.

Of particular interest in the context of our paper are two studies that are to some extent based on <sup>fi</sup>eld data with each of them providing a di<sup>f</sup>erent appraisal of the impact of punishment. As it has already been said, a survey of 440 employees from four organisations combined with analysis of the actual use of forbidden software at the workplace revealed that neither formal nor informal sanctions have a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the use of such software (Silic et al., 2017). At the same time, a study focused on deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect of administrative rather than technological methods of curbing cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng revealed that reminding the employees what constitutes acceptable use of computer devices allows decreasing non-work related use of the Internet (Shepherd & Mejias, 2016). The study used data logs rather than self-reports, thus it allowed to measure the actual extent of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng and the impact of reminders on it. Importantly, a more severe reminder led to instant 33% decrease of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng that remained at the pre-treatment level for three weeks, while a milder reminder resulted in 12% instant decrease, which however disappeared one week later.

## 3.1. Summary of the extant research

In general, three points can be made about the literature to date. First, the results on the impact of punishment on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng are mixed. Studies do not agree on which aspect of deterrence – the severity or the certainty of punishment – impacts the behaviour of employees, and if there is an impact at all, although the balance of evidence suggests that such e<sup>f</sup>ect exists. These puzzling di<sup>f</sup>erences in results can likely be explained by the impact of context variables (Johns, 2006, 2017, 2018). Each study is inevitably situated in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c national, cultural, industrial, and organisational context which is often left uncontrolled for (Bamberger, 2008). Context variables were shown to a<sup>f</sup>ect plethora of job-related behaviours and attitudes, including reward preferences (Chiang & Birtch, 2007), turnover intentions (Wong et al., 2017), leadership practices (Dorfman et al., 1997), and job satisfaction (Markovits et al., 2014). The recent theorising in the information systems literature shows similar impacts of context on IT-related behaviours (Davison & Martinsons, 2016; Hong et al., 2014). It is thus possible that the same deterrence strategy would deliver a di<sup>f</sup>erent result in, for instance, the collectivistic culture of China and individualistic culture of US.

Second, since the majority of research is based on survey data, it tends to study the awareness of possible punishment rather than the experience of actual punishment as an independent variable. This means that it is close to impossible to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate between the speci<sup>fi</sup>c and the general e<sup>f</sup>ects of punishment. Thus far we have no methods at our disposal, that would allow conveying to the study subjects the subtle di<sup>f</sup>erence between the awareness, that they can be punished and the awareness that they were punished. Hence, the research to date focuses on the general e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment but not the speci<sup>fi</sup>c one.

What is more, the use of surveys rather than <sup>fi</sup>eld behavioural evidence related to actual punishments may lead to the underestimation of the impact of punishment. As Ugrin and Pearson have argued, “AUPs [acceptable use policies] and Internet monitoring will be relatively ine<sup>f</sup>ective at reducing behaviours like personal emailing and social networking unless employees know about of others who have been caught and punished severely” (Ugrin & Pearson, 2013, p. 818). A similar message was conveyed in a recent test of the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Model of Information Security Policy Compliance (Moody et al., 2018, p. 308): “If there are no examples of punishments, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of deterrents is argued to be marginal”. In a nutshell, responses to hypothetical scenarios of punishment presented in survey studies can be di<sup>f</sup>erent from responses to actual punishments administered to individuals that the studied employees know personally. Thus, the research so far can be understood to focus primarily on responses to policies of punishment rather than on responses to actual sanctions.

Third, the extant research tends to study intentions to cyberloaf rather than actual cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng as the dependent variable (although there are rare exceptions), which brings two de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant possibility remains that survey participants’ responses regarding their intention to engage in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng di<sup>f</sup>er from their actual behaviour. This di<sup>f</sup>erence does not necessarily result from the intention to cheat, it may just as well ensue from di<sup>fi</sup>culty in selfestimating the time spent on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. Moreover, estimating the dynamics of behaviour over time is impossible with the use of one-time surveys. In our knowledge, to date, there is just one study available that analyses deterrence of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng with use of multiple measurements over time (Shepherd & Mejias, 2016). However, that study focuses on the impact of warnings (reminders) related to violation of IS security policy, rather than on the impact of punishment and does not use time-series data but only one preintervention and three post-intervention readings. ## 3.2. Hypotheses development

Even though certain studies on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng overviewed in the previous section disagree with the thesis that punishment deters further misconduct, the balance of evidence seems to point to the existence of such e<sup>f</sup>ect. Thus, on the basis of the General Deterrence Theory (Gibbs, 1975; Paternoster, 2010) applied in the extant studies of curbing cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (Cheng et al., 2014; D’Arcy & Devaraj, 2012; Henle & Blanchard, 2008; Khansa et al., 2017; Li et al., 2010; Moody & Siponen, 2013; Ugrin & Pearson, 2013) we hypothesise that punishment will deter both punished and unpunished employees from engaging from further cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we expect that after the punishment is administered, we will observe signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lowered levels of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng.

## H1: The punishment has a deterrence efect on employees that were punished (specific deterrence efect).

## H2: The punishment has a deterrence efect on employees that were not punished (general deterrence efect).

While the unpunished individuals need to deal only with the prospect of being sanctioned in the future (Nagin & Pogarsky, 2001; Paternoster, 2010; Paternoster & Piquero, 1995), the punished employees have to face a broad range of consequences. First, they are a<sup>f</sup>ected by a direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment, such as being reprimanded, losing part of the salary, or even having their jobs terminated. Second, they are also exposed to indirect e<sup>f</sup>ects of punishment, such as shame (Silic et al., 2017), being seen by their superiors as not contributing to the wellbeing of the organisation, and having diminished chances for promotion. They may also be forced to deal with negative social valuations extended by their co-workers, who suddenly realise that their workload is likely heavier because of cyberloafers work avoidance. All of the above suggests that the deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect for punished individuals will be stronger than the same e<sup>f</sup>ect for unpunished individuals.

## H3: The deterrence efect is stronger for those that were punished, as compared to those that were not punished.

To date, only one study focused on measuring the dynamic of deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect over time (Shepherd & Mejias, 2016), but it was targeted on impact of reminders of acceptable use policy rather than on the impact of punishment and it was based on four readings (one pre-intervention, three post-intervention) rather than on time series. However, it allowed establishing that more severe reminder has longer-lasting deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect than a milder reminder. Since actual punishment in the form of termination and withholding bonuses is more similar to severe reminder than to a weak one, we hypothesise that both the general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment will have consequences in the form of decreased levels of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng till the end of the data set we study, that is for three months.

H4: The punishment has a long-term deterrence efect.

The aforementioned work of Shepherd and Mejias (2016) shows that reminders regarding what constitutes acceptable use of computer devices tend to deter cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. Since the authorities of the studied organisation issued a reminder that excessive cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng will be met with severe penalties, including termination of employment, we hypothesise that this reminder will have a similar e<sup>f</sup>ect to severe reminder studied by Shepherd and Mejias, that is it will lead to substantially decreased levels of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng.

H5: The reminder of the intent to punish the ofenders has a deterrence efect.

Since the general deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect depends on the non-o<sup>f</sup>enders ability to learn about punishment imposed on o<sup>f</sup>enders (Paternoster & Piquero, 1995; Sta<sup>f</sup>ord & Warr, 1993) we hypothesise that the proximity to the punished individuals moderates the strength of the deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect. Individuals located in proximity to the punished o<sup>f</sup>enders (i.e., employed by the same organisational unit) will react stronger to punishment than the individuals employed by the remaining organisational units.

H6: The deterrence efect is stronger for employees located in the proximity of the punished employees (i.e., employed by the same organisational unit) than for the remaining employees.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data

The <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence we use in this study was obtained from a mid-size public administration o<sup>fi</sup>ce, which acquired an organisation-wide computer activity monitoring system <sup>fi</sup>fteen months before the data set used in this study began and had been using it since. The system registers all the uses of computer devices and resources accessed (e.g., applications, websites, internal databases) for each employee, regardless if they use Ethernet or Wi-Fi connection. As soon as the system was operational, all the employees had to consent in writing to the accepted use policy, which speci<sup>fi</sup>ed that the private use of company infrastructure is not allowed.

Due to our previous research at the focal organisation, we learned from its authorities that at a certain moment in time employees were warned that excessive engagement in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng would be sanctioned and that eventually certain number of employees were punished for such misconduct. We asked for permission to use the data covering the before- and after- punishment period for academic purposes and the permission was granted. The data set obtained contained logs for 539 accounts of which 39 were not associated with any speci<sup>fi</sup>c person (e.g., “customer service workstation”), thus were excluded from further analysis. Also, four days before the punishment was administered, the IT department of the organisation rolled out an update to about half of the workstations, which – due to a glitch in the monitoring software – resulted in activity on these workstations being not logged. This left us with 230 accounts (individuals) for which we have data for the entire focal period (that is three months of data before the reminder, three months of data after the reminder but before the punishment, and three months of data after the punishment).

General research model.

Apart from obtaining the data from the computer activity monitoring system, we also conducted seven interviews within the focal organisation. We interviewed the managing director of the organisation, the director of the IT department, the specialist within the IT department responsible for the administration of the computer activity monitoring system (two interviews), the director of the HR department and the mid-level manager of HR department (two interviews). The data acquired in these interviews allowed us to reconstruct what happened at the studied organisation, that is when and how the employees were warned about the prospect of being punished, how the employees were selected for punishment, when and how o<sup>f</sup>enders were punished and how the punishments were administered to the employees. For instance, we learned that the punished employees were given a chance to explain their behaviour. However, because the content of their online activity could not be categorised as job-related, regardless of the de<sup>fi</sup>nition employed, none of the individuals was able to provide a valid excuse for their actions. The interviews also allowed us to establish that there were no other events in the focal period that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the levels of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (no change in organisational regulations/policy, managerial positions, amount of work, nature of tasks, location, and the level of employment). Finally, we could ascertain that the sanctioning policy was based on the best available scholarly evidence. The authorities recognised the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that short periods of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng may bring to employees, such as dealing with stress or taking cognitive breaks. For this reason, the interventions described below were not intended to eliminate cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng entirely. Instead, they were targeted only at the employees whose engagement in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng was excessive, with the intention of showing what levels of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng are unacceptable.

## 4.2. The interventions

In this study, we analyse the general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect of two interventions. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one amounted to reminding the employees about the incoming punishment of those that transgress the accepted use policy for computer devices. The second intervention was the actual punishment of the employees that engaged in misconduct in the form of excessive cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (more than 2 hours a day on average for four weeks period).

## 4.2.1. Intervention 1: the reminder

Three months after the dataset for our study begins, the managing director summoned all 67 managers of the organisation to a meeting devoted to the issue of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. The director reminded the participants of the meeting that the organisation has a formal regulation forbidding the private use of computer devices and that all the employees consented in writing to that policy. He informed the managers that the IT department would be analysing the logs from the computer activity monitoring system to see if the employees engaged in the unsanctioned use of the internet. What is more, he also announced that a preliminary analysis of the data from the system had revealed that the employees used the internet connection for cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng for 54 minutes a day on average. He also warned that the punishment for abusers would be severe, including termination of employment, if justi-<sup>fi</sup>ed. The director asked the managers to relay that reminder to all of their subordinates. Our interviews with the managing director and the managers of the HR department allowed to establish that the reminder was indeed passed to the line employees during the meetings organised in all the departments on the same day.

## 4.2.2. Intervention 2: the punishment

In line with the reminder described above, three months later, the o<sup>f</sup>enders were selected for punishment. Since the authorities believed that short periods of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng might be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for the employees and the organisation (by providing the opportunity to relax), they decided that only the most extreme o<sup>f</sup>enders would be punished. The IT department provided the authorities of the organisation with a list of 50 employees having the highest average time spent online for private purposes. Next, consultations with the managers overseeing these employees were conducted with the intent of checking if there were any speci<sup>fi</sup>c reasons that could justify the use of the internet for apparently private purposes (for instance, the manager might have asked the employee to browse social media to <sup>fi</sup>nd certain information relevant to the organisation). Finally, the authorities adopted a cut-o<sup>f</sup> point of 2 hours per day on average for four weeks period spent on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng for shortlisting 14 employees targeted for punishment.

Next, the punishments were administered. First, the employment of the two workers was terminated. Each of them spent on average over three and half hour daily on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, which constituted nearly 70% of their daily computer use time. Second, two employees were reassigned to di<sup>f</sup>erent positions, where they still worked with computers but could be easily seen by their supervisors. What is more, their wages were permanently lowered, although – due to legal restrictions – we were not able to obtain any details about the size of the decrease in wages. Third, ten more employees were disciplined in two ways. First, they were summoned to the managing director’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce, where they received an oral reprimand. Second, their bonuses were withheld for six months. Importantly, all of the above actions, that is the termination of employment, reassignment to new positions, reprimands and bonus cut announcement were administered on the same day. The other employees were informed that sanctions were imposed on heavy cyberloafers, however, the identity of sanctioned employees was not disclosed. Nevertheless, in cases of termination and job reassignment, the sanctions were easily visible, while in the remaining cases it was easy to infer the identity of sanctioned coworkers because all of them were summoned to the managing director’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce shortly before the information about sanctions was issued.

## 4.3. Measure

Given the observational nature of this study we use just one measure to establish the consequences of interventions in the studied organisational setting, that is the measure of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. Cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng amounts to any unsanctioned use of work computers and company-sponsored internet access for personal purposes (Lim, 2002). Obviously, each organisation may de<sup>fi</sup>ne unsanctioned use di<sup>f</sup>erently, since it depends on the nature of the jobs performed at a given organisation and on the authorities’ vision of what is an authorised and unauthorised use. Thus, rather than trying to categorise the computer-related behaviour of employees ourselves, we have resorted to using the categorisation developed by the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT department in cooperation with the organisation’s authorities. According to the company policy communicated to the employees, all the computer-related behaviour are divided into two categories: workrelated and forbidden. We used the time spent on activities belonging to the latter category as our measure of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. No changes were made to the categorisation of computer activities over the period of our study.

Importantly, the data gathered by the system allows for precise measurement of the extent of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, rather than the declarations about the intent to cyberloaf. While employees may have shifted some of their non-work related behaviours to personal devices (such as smartphones) use of such devices does not fall within the scope of de<sup>fi</sup>nition of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng adopted in this study, which maintains that it amounts to “any voluntary act of employees’ using their companies’ internet access during o<sup>fi</sup>ce hours” (Lim, 2002, p. 677, emphasis ours), unless they were connected to the company Wi-Fi network, in which case they were monitored and re<sup>fl</sup>ected in our data.

## 4.4. Procedure

This study was conducted using a one-group pre-testpost-test quasi-experimental design (Shadish et al., 2001) to examine trends in the level of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng after two interventions undertaken by superiors (reminder on June ${ 3 0 } ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ and punishment on September $3 0 ^ { \mathrm { t h } } )$ . The interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) was used to evaluate the longitudinal impact of these events (Jebb et al., 2015). Application of that method allows overcoming the limitation of many studies which measure the change caused by intervention only just after it happened and cannot asses how permanent the change is (Beer & Walton, 1987). Multiple measurements before and after intervention are analysed in the ITSA procedure, which allows examining the longitudinal trend in data. SPSS version 24.0 and SAS version 9.4 were used for the purpose of statistical analyses.

Figure 2 plots the raw time-series data for all employees. There is no visible trend change after reminder announcement on ${ 3 0 } ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ of June (the <sup>fi</sup>rst vertical line on the <sup>fi</sup>gure), whereas a clear and sudden drop can be seen after punishment on ${ 3 0 } ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ of September (the second vertical line of the <sup>fi</sup>gure). The visual analysis of post-intervention tail, covering the three months after the punishment, reveals that the decrease in average cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng time was rather steady.

## 4.5. Statistical analysis

The interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) is a statistical method that allows assessing the impact of the intervention on a time series of relevant indicators (Shadish & Cook, 2009). Time series analysis is frequently used in many disciplines (e.g., economics, engineering and natural sciences), but rarely implemented in research on individuals in organisations. This is mainly due to limited access to time-series data on individual behaviour. In such circumstances, researchers have to base their studies on few data points/measurements, which lend itself only to descriptive or casual explanatory models testing. However, thanks to increased ubiquitousness of internet-connected sensors and systems (George et al., 2014), this obstacle quickly becomes a thing of the past as time series on individual behaviour are becoming readily available. The growing availability of time series drives the popularity of time series analysis, like ITSA used in this study. This development needs to be seen as bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for the <sup>fi</sup>eld because there is a theoretical need to understand patterns of change over long periods of time (Jebb et al., 2015), since all psychological and organisational processes are timesensitive and no theory is time-independent (Zaheer et al., 1999).

Time series is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a data set containing repeated measurements of a single variable over time, where contiguous data points are dependent on each other (Box et al., 2008). The e<sup>f</sup>ect of an intervention can be estimated after adjusting for serial dependence, by using linear models to calculate the change in the mean level of the series (Jebb et al., 2015). The modelling approach is based on the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model, developed by Box and Jenkins (1976). The general ARIMA model is denoted as ARIMA (p, d, q), where p is the order of autoregressive part (AR), q represents the moving average part order (MA), and d stands for the order of di<sup>f</sup>erencing for stationary transforming (Box et al., 2008). ARIMA model enables to separate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the intervention from the general drift, trends, outliers, seasonality and serial dependencies in a series (McCleary et al., 1980). Prior to the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of best-<sup>fi</sup>tting models, the time series must be transformed into stationary, which indicates that the mean, variance and autocorrelation function (ACF) are constant. The results of the augmented Dicky-Fuller test (Said & Dickey, 1984) shows that analysed cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng time series is non-stationary. Therefore it was logtransformed to address temporal changes in variance and <sup>fi</sup>rst-order di<sup>f</sup>erenced. Moreover, the iterative outliers detection was performed (Chen & Liu, 1993).

Intervention analysis starts with identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, estimation, and diagnosis of the observations before the intervention. Subsequently, the model is re-estimated for the entire series and the results are diagnosed (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2007). Therefore, to identify the ARIMA models the autocorrelation (ACF) and partial correlation function (PACF) for the stationary series using only pre-intervention data were employed. The ACF function is a plot of the coe<sup>fi</sup>- cients of correlation between a time series and lags of itself. The PACF function is a plot of the correlation coe<sup>fi</sup>cients between the series and lags of itself that are not explained by correlation at all lower-order lags. Based on the visual inspection of the ACF and PACF, initial autoregressive (AR) and moving average (MA) terms were determined as ARIMA (0,0,1). The automatic detection of ARIMA terms available in “Expert modellers” package of IBM SPSS con<sup>fi</sup>rms the results of the visual inspection. For model <sup>fi</sup>t assessment the R squared and the Ljung-Box test (Ljung & Box, 1978) for the presence of autocorrelation was calculated. Failing to reject the null hypothesis that the errors are uncorrelated across lags means that the model has succeeded in explaining the remaining autocorrelation in data (Jebb et al., 2015).

In the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the intervention component, two dummy coded variables, “30<sup>th</sup> June” and “30<sup>th</sup> September” were used. They correspond to reminder related to incoming punishment of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng issued by the management on June 30<sup>th</sup> and punishment of employees with the highest cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng level on September 30<sup>th</sup>. Both the reminder and the punishment were treated as pulse intervention and therefore coded “1” on the date in question and “0” for the preceding and the subsequent observations. Pulse interventions refer to a one-time event that occurs on a certain date, while step interventions cause permanent change, such as the implementation of the new technology (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2007).

The e<sup>f</sup>ect of the intervention is assessed by interpreting the coe<sup>fi</sup>cients for the intervention parameters which were also tested for statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. The magnitude of the impact is measured by the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the actual observations and the predicted data pattern without the intervention (Box et al., 2008). In our case, the coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of intervention parameters express the change in the average amount of time spent daily on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng in the examined workplace after the dates of interventions.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Implications for theory

The current study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to gauge the general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ects in the context of cyberloafing on the basis of time series from an organisationwide computer monitoring system. The kind of data we use allows extending the previous research in several ways.

First, our study provides support for the General Deterrence Theory applied in the realm of curbing cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, by showing that punishment does impact the scale of misconduct (Cheng et al., 2013; D’Arcy et al., 2009). While the previous research primarily concentrated on measurement of intention to cyberloaf as a dependent variable (e.g., Cheng et al., 2014; D’Arcy & Devaraj, 2012; Henle & Blanchard, 2008; Khansa et al., 2017; Li et al., 2010), the present study measures the actual cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng and its dynamic over time after the reminder and the punishment. Thus, we believe, that the use of data from computer activity monitoring system moves the deterrence research forward by providing real-world evidence of the e<sup>f</sup>ect that previously has been studied only with the survey data.

Second, most of the literature to date, due to methodological limitations of experimental and nonexperimental surveys, has been focused on studying the responses to hypothetical scenarios rather than responses to actual punishments. The only available study based on <sup>fi</sup>eld data (Shepherd & Mejias, 2016) focused on general deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect of warnings. This means that only the general deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect has been studied thus far, but not the speci<sup>fi</sup>c one. Our study, thanks to the kind of data employed, allows to measure and compare the size of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c and the general deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect (Gibbs, 1975; Nagin & Pogarsky, 2001; Paternoster, 2010; Paternoster & Piquero, 1995). We have found that both the punished and unpunished individuals were a<sup>f</sup>ected by the intervention in the form of severe punishment. However, the e<sup>f</sup>ect for the unpunished employees was nearly two times weaker than for the punished ones (24% vs 41% decrease in the extent of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, respectively).

Third, the results indicate that Internet monitoring might be ine<sup>f</sup>ective unless employees know about cases of people being caught and punished for engagement in misconduct. Thus our study provides realworld empirical support for hypotheses formulated in the existing literature (Moody et al., 2018; Ugrin & Pearson, 2013). We maintain that the computer monitoring itself has little if any e<sup>f</sup>ect on the behaviour of the employees. The employees analysed in the present study engaged in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (in some cases devoting nearly 70% of their work time to that activity) even after they were warned that their online behaviour was watched on a constant basis. As we foreshadowed in the literature review, the reconceptualised general deterrence theory (Sta<sup>f</sup>ord & Warr, 1993) suggests that deterring e<sup>f</sup>ect is a<sup>f</sup>ected not only by the experience of punishment but also by the experience of punishment avoidance. Our results suggest that in the current study the impact of punishment avoidance could dominate the deterring e<sup>f</sup>ect of warnings. No sanctions for cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng in the past means that all of the employees avoided punishment, thus believed that sanctions are unlikely.

Fourth, our results show that employees located in close organisational proximity to the punished individuals (i.e., employed by the same organisational unit) reacted stronger than the remaining employees. This e<sup>f</sup>ect – never studied before in the literature on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng – emphasises the importance of the interpersonal dimension of the deterrence process, thus opening a new avenue for further research.

Our study also indicates – contrary to what has been hypothesised – that reminder about the possibility of punishment has no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect on the level of misconduct. This result, standing in contrast with previous research on the subject (Shepherd & Mejias, 2016), can be explained in several ways. First, Shepherd and Mejias studied the impact of technologically delivered warning (popup message), while we studied administrative solution, that is warning communicated by managers. Second, our interviews with the managers and employees of the studied organisation suggest that the most serious o<sup>f</sup>enders show certain signs of addiction, especially to gambling, computer games, and online currency trading (FOREX). This may explain why at least some of the violators of the cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng policy did not decrease their unsanctioned activity online, as addicts possess limited ability to control their addiction-related behaviour (Beard, 2005; Hormes et al., 2014). Third, the culture-related contextual factors (Davison & Martinsons, 2016; Hong et al., 2014) might di<sup>f</sup>er between these two study settings. The di<sup>f</sup>erence in the results can also be explained by the nature of the data – our study uses time series for nine months period, while Shepherd and Mejias used readings for four points in time only. Lastly, the lack of reaction to warning can be explained by the phenomenon of punishment avoidance discussed above – since no employees were punished before the warning was issued, it was not treated seriously.

Finally, the present study shows that the deterring e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment is relatively long-lasting. We believe that this <sup>fi</sup>nding – possible to obtain due to the nature of longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence analysed – moves the <sup>fi</sup>eld forward as the long-term consequences of punishment of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng have never been studied before. At the same time, we noticed no impact of reminder and punishment on the work-related use of computers, thus showing that punishment does not bring a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect that may obliterate its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Obviously, the chances remain that such punishment brings other unwanted consequences on variables not analysed in this paper, such as engagement and organisational commitment.

In order to draw causal inferences from a onegroup pre-test-post-test quasi-experiment, like the one employed in this study, three conditions have to be met (Shadish et al., 2001): cause needs to precede the e<sup>f</sup>ect, cause has to covary with e<sup>f</sup>ect, and alternative explanations for the causal relationship need to be excluded. The analysis of the quantitative data and interviews conducted in the organisation allow to claim that all three requirements were met: the interventions occurred before the change in employees’ behaviour, the punishment covary with cyberloafing levels, and no other organisational changes that could a<sup>f</sup>ect cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng levels occurred at the same time, thus other explanations are excluded. We would also like to emphasise that we have used multiple pretests and post-tests (time series before and after the intervention) which likely contribute to increased validity of the results (Shadish et al., 2001), by allowing to establishing credible pre-test baseline and precisely measure behaviour after the test.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

We believe that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant practical importance and may contribute to better organisational policies. The results suggest that the occasional administration of punishment to a small fraction (2.5%) of employees does a<sup>f</sup>ect the behaviour of the remaining organisation participants, thus providing substance to the previous theorising about this e<sup>f</sup>ect (Moody et al., 2018; Ugrin & Pearson, 2013). As foreshadowed in the Introduction, this <sup>fi</sup>nding is signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant in the context of frequent decoupling between formal organisational policies and organisational practice (Bromley & Powell, 2012; Meyer & Rowan, 1977). Punishment constitutes a strong signal to those employees that see formal regulations as irrelevant. This seems to be particularly important when dealing with small-scale misconduct, that may not be associated with long-established moral norms and thus not activating in the minds of the employees any ethical or moral valuations, that could discourage them from engaging in such behaviour. Importantly, our study shows that warnings that were not preceded by even a single case of punishment will likely bring no change in employees’ cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng behaviour. We believe that this <sup>fi</sup>nding should be considered by organisational actors responsible for drafting acceptable use policy for information infrastructure.

What is more, our study indicates that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the punishment lasts for at least three months. This result may be used for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of both the employees and the authorities of organisations when the organisational policies towards curbing cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng are designed. The relatively long-lasting e<sup>f</sup>ect means that while punishment is necessary to deter cyberloafing, it does not need to be frequently applied, thus allowing to avoid negative consequences to organisational climate and employee commitment associated with punishment.

Next, we would like to emphasise the <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the proximity to the punished individuals as a moderating variable, with employees located in the same organisational units as the punished individuals showing a stronger decrease in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. It suggests that policies and actions intended to curb cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng should consider the size of the organisation as an essential variable. When organisations grow bigger punishment selectively applied to only some departments may be ine<sup>f</sup>ective in bringing the company-wide deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect.

Finally, we need to once again emphasise the importance of the proper application of sanctions such as those studied in our paper. The managers at the studied organisation used the best available evidence to devise their sanctioning policy. That is, since short periods of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng might bring bene<sup>fi</sup>cial consequences for the employees’ well-being and performance, the intent of the exercise was to reduce rather than to eliminate cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. Sanctions were imposed only on employees whose time spent on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng exceeded two hours a day on average, thus made it impossible for them to ful<sup>fi</sup>l their work responsibilities. The punishment was preceded by a warning thus no employee could claim that they were uninformed about the company policy. What is more, the sanctions were based on an objective criterion – the average time spent on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, which was in turn measured in an objective manner by a computer-activity tracking system.

## 6.3. Limitations

While using data from computer activity monitoring software as a base for research has many advantages, it is also associated with certain challenges (Pierce & Balasubramanian, 2015). First, since the behaviours studied in this paper were not controlled by us in any way, there is a possibility that we do not have full knowledge of all the circumstances of executing the punishment. For instance, even though we went to great lengths to check if any other signi<sup>fi</sup>cant events, that could cause the decrease in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng levels occurred concurrently with the punishment, the possibility remains that they did and thus a<sup>f</sup>ected the results obtained.

Second, while we are con<sup>fi</sup>dent that the data source used in this study captures all the activity that falls within the scope of the Lim’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng (use of “companies’ internet access during o<sup>fi</sup>ce hours” (Lim, 2002, p. 677)) and that we do not make claims extending beyond the adopted de<sup>fi</sup>nition, a possibility remains that employees shifted their timewasting activity from company to private devices and avoided connecting them to company’s Wi-Fi. However, we believe that Lim’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition remains popular for a reason. That is, using company internet access for non-work related purposes exposes organisation’s IT system to threats (Chen et al., 2018; D’Arcy et al., 2009; Moody et al., 2018; Silic et al., 2017) that are not present when devices not connected to the company’s infrastructure are used. Thus, we see value in the measurement of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng that follows

Lim’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition, even if it does not cover the entire extent of time-wasting practices. Furthermore, as we said above, due to a glitch in a monitoring software (roll-out of an update) we do not have full data for some of the employees. Even though our interviews allowed us to establish that the glitch a<sup>f</sup>ected random accounts, thus likely has not introduced any systematic error to our sample, it still can be considered as a limitation.

Third, the form of sanctioning policy constitutes another boundary condition of the theory developed in this paper. The authorities at the studied organisation based their policy on the best available evidence: they recognised that moderate levels of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial, thus a total elimination of such activities would likely cause negative consequences. They also imposed the punishment on the basis of the objective criterion (time spent on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng, measured by a computer tracking system), preceded by a warning conveyed to the employees. We can easily imagine that when the managers do not follow such rules and impose sanctions haphazardly on more or less randomly chosen targets, the response of the employees may di<sup>f</sup>er. What is more, when sanctions are imposed arbitrarily the employees are not provided with cues on what constitutes the informally allowed extent of cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. As a consequence, they may stop using the internet even for job-related purposes, fearing that they will involuntarily visit a non-job-related resource and thus be sanctioned. In other words, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings apply only to similar cases where sanctioning policy is devised and applied in a fair manner. The practical implications of this study are also limited to contexts where collective bargaining agreements do not limit managers’ sanctioning options (Hayter et al., 2011; Venn, 2009). The level of unionisation and the extent of employee protection in collective bargaining agreements vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly between organisations, industries, and countries (Du Caju et al., 2008; Hayter et al., 2011; Venn, 2009), thus caution is warranted in developing sanctioning policy.

Lastly, we would like to highlight that this study was conducted in a mid-size public administration unit located in Poland. Thus caution is advised in generalising the obtained results to other organisational and national contexts. Polish society scores high on power distance (Hofstede et al., 2010), which means that members of society expect the bosses to act in a somewhat autocratic manner. At the same time, the society scores high on individualism, which means that relationships within the organisation are understood to be based on a contract that has to be bene<sup>fi</sup>cia to both parties. In such a cultural context the sanctioning policy described in the paper may be met with a higher level of acceptance than in other, less hierarchical and less individualistic societies. The external validity (Shadish et al., 2001) of our results can be tested only once similar studies are conducted in other organisational, cultural, and national contexts (Busse et al., 2017; Greenberg, 1987; McGrath & Brinberg, 1983).

## 7. Conclusions

The present study, thanks to its design based on the <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence, allows to measure the general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c deterrence e<sup>f</sup>ect of punishment on employees’ engagement in cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng. We have studied the impact of two interventions: the reminder and the actual punishment of cyberloafers. Our study shows that both the punished and unpunished individuals limited their time spent on cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng in response to the punishment, although an earlier reminder brought no change in the behaviour of the employees. We also show that the impact of punishment lasts until the end of our data set, that is for at least three months. The results of the study are of both theoretical and practical importance. They contribute to the research on the General Deterrence Theory by providing results based on real-world evidence, rather than on experimental and non-experimental surveys. They can also be employed for formulation of organisational policies related to cyberloa<sup>fi</sup>ng as the results suggest that, on the one hand, the punishment is necessary to signal to the rank and <sup>fi</sup>le that the organisational policy is binding and should be complied with, but on the other hand – the results indicate that the punishment can be imposed relatively infrequently. By measuring the impact of the proximity in the organisational structure on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of punishment we provide a novel <sup>fi</sup>nding that can be used to inform organisational policies. We also show that punishing cyberloafers brings no e<sup>f</sup>ect on work-related computer use, thus at least partially alleviating the fear that such coercive action may bring more harm than good.
