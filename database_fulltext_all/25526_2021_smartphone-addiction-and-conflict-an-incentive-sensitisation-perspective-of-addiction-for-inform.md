# Smartphone Addiction and Conflict: An Incentive-Sensitisation Perspective of Addiction for Information Systems

Jungwon Kuem <sup>a</sup>, Soumya Ray<sup>b</sup>, Pei-Fang Hsu<sup>b</sup> and Lara Khansa <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Technology Management, School of Business, State University of New York, Albany, USA; <sup>b</sup>Institute of Service Science, College of Technology Management, National Tsing Hua University, Institute of Service Science, Hsinchu, Taiwan; <sup>c</sup>Department o Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University, Blacksburg, USA

## ABSTRACT

This study seeks to develop and test a theory-driven model of technology addiction within the context of smartphone use. Drawing on incentive-sensitisation theory, we proposed a nomological network that centres on a second-order factor of smartphone addiction from a psychological perspective. We empirically evaluated the proposed model against longitudinal data collected from 441 smartphone users. Our analysis from structural equation modelling indicates that our twofactor measurement of smartphone addiction (heightened desire and mobile social interaction) is a parsimonious alternative to the four-factor model. In addition, our findings suggest that addiction and its negative consequences difer in their efects on subsequent perceptions, such as perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. We also found that personal innovativeness in information technology and a perception of loneliness explain a large amount of variation in smartphone addiction. Overall, the present study significantly contributes to the information systems literature by ofering a refined model of smartphone addiction that efectively describes pathological smartphone use while correcting critical biases in existing models.

## 1. Introduction

Part computer and part phone, early smartphones began as an afterthought to the personal digital assistant (Park & Chen, 2007). However, with dramatic improvements in their computing power, display technology, and interface mechanisms, modern smartphones’ ubiquity has made them the dominant theme of modern personal computing. Smartphone shipments have soared from a meagre 3.65 million units in 2006 (Park & Chen, 2007) to over 1.4 billion units in 2018 (IDC, 2019). Just as important, people now use their smartphones more frequently and longer each day than they do any other computing device (Oulasvirta et al., 2011). This enthusiastic adoption by users reflects, in part, the tremendous utility and pleasure of smartphones that has made them the new information technology of choice but, for many users, mobile devices have become an unhealthy obsession. A survey of North American mobile users found that up to 17% of them have some degree of addiction to their device. Over a third of these cases displayed a pathological level of addiction (Turel & Serenko, 2010). This fraction of highly addicted users is cause for concern, given that over three-quarters of all American adults own a smartphone and 94% of young adults ages 18 to 29 own and use one (Pew Research Center, 2018). With smartphone addiction increasingly linked to such harmful personal and professional outcomes as depression and employee turnover (Turel & Serenko, 2010), there is a need for a comprehensive understanding of what constitutes smartphone addiction, how it arises, and how it influences people’s perceptions.

Numerous studies have focused on various types of technology addiction, such as online gaming, gambling, auctions, and Internet use (Byun et al., 2009; Caplan, 2002; Davis, 2001; Mehroof & Grifiths, 2010; Turel, Serenko, Bontis et al., 2011; Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011; Wang & Chu, 2007). In general, this stream of research lists psychological and behavioural symptoms related to mental disorders. Such symptoms include, but are not limited to, preoccupation, tolerance, withdrawal, escape, loss of control, lying, and illegal acts. These pioneering studies have added considerably to our basic understanding of technology addiction.

Nevertheless, smartphones present unique and diverse usage experiences unlike those of other traditional technologies. Particularly, smartphones are ubiquitous devices that can be used anytime and anywhere, so addiction is not only likelier to occur but it could also afect many more people than other IT devices (Soror et al., 2015). Similarly, smartphone addiction difers from substance addiction or other IT addiction in two dimensions, namely, social and personal aspects of psychological addiction that are unique to the smartphone use context. Scholars have called for more research to further understand this new type of addiction because existing addiction studies cannot fully explain its unique characteristics (Panova & CARBONELL, 2018; Soror et al., 2015; Venkatesh et al., 2019).

Our study proposes and tests a theory-driven nomological network through the lens of incentive sensitisation theory to describe the nature, antecedents, and consequences of smartphone addiction. First, we include the social aspect of psychological smartphone addiction because smartphone use, unlike substance abuse or gambling, is strongly associated with interpersonal communication. Although researchers have recently recognised there are social aspects surrounding information technology (IT) addiction (James et al., 2017), our increasingly warped preference for online interaction with others, vs. ofline interaction, has not yet been recognised as a core component of IT addiction. Second, we take into account psychological aspects of smartphone addiction by considering individual idiosyncrasies, such as their heightened psychological desires, that bear directly on smartphone addiction. Our eforts seek to address the calls of earlier studies (Bianchi & Phillips, 2005; Hong et al., 2012) to develop a coherent theoretical framework for psychological smartphone addiction that explicitly incorporates social aspects of psychological factors.

Additionally, existing IT addiction studies focus more on examining “consequences” of such addiction (e.g., Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011), while our research targets both “antecedents” and diferent “consequences” of smartphone addiction. Examining antecedents can provide better understanding of what causes smartphone addiction, so IS scholars and practitioners can develop prevention mechanisms, rather than treatments to cure consequences. We hypothesise that two personal characteristics – personal innovativeness in IT and perceptions of loneliness – are psychological antecedents that play important roles in regulating the development of smartphone addiction. Derived directly from our theory-driven dimensions of smartphone addiction, these antecedents efectively indicate how likely a person is to develop addictive tendencies within the smartphone context.

Lastly, a unique aspect of our proposed smartphone addiction nomological network is that it distinguishes the psychological traits of addiction from their behavioural outcomes (conflict and subsequent smartphone usage) and they difer in afecting subsequent perceptions, such as perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. Most existing studies investigate only the negative consequences of smartphone addiction. Our study, on the other hand, empirically examines how these behavioural outcomes from psychological smartphone addiction can difer (positively or negatively) in afecting subsequent consequences, such as perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. To empirically test the relative eficacy of our model against alternative models, we used longitudinal data collected from smartphone users. Our findings present a refined view of the nature, antecedents, and consequences of smartphone addiction while highlighting critical biases in existing models.

## 2. Smartphone Addiction

## 2.1. Technology Addiction

Behavioural addiction that does not involve the intake of psychoactive substances is known to occur in a variety of behaviours, such as gambling, food, exercise, shopping, online game playing, and Internet use (Grifiths, 2000). Although the term addiction was previously restricted to the use of substances, recent research has revealed that “as far as the brain is concerned, a reward’s a reward, regardless of whether it comes from a chemical or an experience” (Holden, 2001, p. 980). As a type of behavioural addiction, technology addiction is widely known as a compulsive and uncontrollable need to use a technological tool (Byun et al., 2009; Kakabadse et al., 2007; Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011). In general, technology addiction is the result of repeated exposure to technological stimuli that results in harmful consequences, including reduced productivity and damaged interpersonal relationships (Turel, Serenko, Bontis et al., 2011).

Table 1 lists some representative empirical studies on technology addiction (e.g., online auctions, mobile phones, social networking sites, etc.). Our review of the extant literature indicates several gaps that motivate our study. First, little is known about the psychological factors underlying smartphone addiction. Smartphones difer from other technologies in important ways. Smartphones are quickly becoming the most popular and frequently used technological tool in our personal lives (Oulasvirta et al., 2011). Hence, the nature of addiction associated with this omnipresent technology may not be the same as that of other technologies (e.g., online auctions). Unlike some technological addictions that entail little interpersonal interaction (e.g., online shopping, online gambling), smartphones are equipped with features primarily geared towards social activities (e.g., online and ofline chat, social networking, email). However, our understanding of how this social component shapes certain technology addictions is severely limited. Therefore, researchers need a solid framework that accounts for these unique characteristics of smartphone use.

Second, existing technology addiction studies focus mostly on examining the consequences of addiction (e.g., Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011). We heed the call of recent studies that suggest researchers examine both antecedents and consequences of technology addiction (e.g., Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011, p. 1055). Our examination is guided by incentivesensitisation theory, which can better explain what causes smartphone addiction and thereby aid IS scholars in preventing the causes of smartphone addiction rather than searching for post-hoc cures.

Third, most existing studies examine the negative consequences of IT addiction as a single direct outcome. Our study uncovers two diferent routes, with diferent mediators, which help illustrate that smartphone addiction can result in both positive and negative consequences. In doing so, we respond to prior studies that suggest “future researchers should test mediators in various contexts to develop a better understanding of the addiction–perception relationship” (Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011, p. 1056).

## 2.2. A Two-Factor Model of Addiction in Smartphone Use

Originally designed to describe substance abuse, the incentive-sensitisation theory is considered one of the best explanations for addiction (TE Robinson & Berridge, 2001, 2008). In essence, this theory posits that addiction results from persistent changes in brain systems that foster psychological motivation towards cues associated with rewards. Consumers with little or no addiction perceive stimuli, such as alcohol or gambling, to be relatively neutral events; however, the brain systems of people with higher levels of addiction are sensitised to attach motivational properties to otherwise neutral cues. Addicts exhibit pathological symptoms because they attempt to approach rewardpredictive cues almost “against all odds.” At this stage of addiction, craving arises with little or no association with pleasure. Put another way, addicts simply want more, even when they no longer derive enjoyment. Incentive salience is the motivational state that associates otherwise neutral stimuli with attractive or wanted incentives (Flagel et al., 2009). The main theme of incentive-sensitisation theory is that addiction is essentially incentive salience in which “wanting” is detached from hedonic value or “liking.” In our study, an elevated state of wanting to use smartphones is referred to as heightened desire, which is defined as an abnormal level of craving to use a smartphone. Smartphones ofer numerous entertainment features, such as music, streaming videos, shopping, slot machines, and games. Some users are known to exhibit extraordinary levels of craving towards such stimuli and thereby become addicts (Holden, 2001). Thus, we expect that smartphone addiction manifests itself to some extent as heightened desire.

While heightened desire constitutes a traditional dimension of addiction, we consider mobile social interaction as another dimension that is characteristic of smartphone addiction. As discussed earlier, incentive-sensitisation theory was originally intended to explain substance abuse, which is related to an individual’s elevated desire for thrills and excitement. Unlike substance addiction, which requires little social involvement, addiction to information technologies is often accompanied by a craving for interpersonal communication (Caplan, 2002; Davis, 2001). Many researchers have shown that social interaction is one of the most important motivations underlying online use of information technologies (Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000; Yang & Tung, 2007). Similarly, research on Internet addiction indicates that people view the Internet as a communication channel that efectively addresses their craving for social comfort and mutual consolation (Davis et al., 2002). Furthermore, research in social IT contexts finds that users’ social needs to belong and interact with others is fundamental to IT addiction (James et al., 2017). Smartphones are an exemplar of social IT that makes it simple to communicate with others through email, text messaging, online chatting, and online social networking. Smartphones take the social aspect even further than other IT modalities by being always-on, always-available, and continuously notifying users of social updates throughout the day. This ubiquitous social connection allows users to interact online more frequently than face-to-face interaction or less mobile modes of social IT. Such Internet-mediated communication tools are thought to fulfill individuals basic needs for social belonging while serving as “a bufer against threatening social interactions” (Davis et al., 2002, p. 332). However, recent research demonstrates that allowing users to continuously and efortlessly fulfill their craving to belong, interact, and seek to gain acceptance from others leads to an obsessive-compulsive state of addiction (James et al., 2017). This interpersonal aspect of addiction is referred to as mobile social interaction, that is defined as the degree to which the use of information communication technology is actually preferred over face-to-face social interaction (Caplan, 2002). We propose that a person’s growing preference for mobile social interaction reflects a core dimension of smartphone addiction.

In summary, to explain smartphone addiction, we have extended incentive-sensitisation theory by incorporating recent developments in Internet addiction research. We conceptualise smartphone addiction as a second-order structure that consists of heightened desire and mobile social interaction. Whereas heightened desire shows active pursuit of thrills and excitement, mobile social interaction reflects an instinctive need for social belonging. We believe that this secondorder model is a parsimonious yet efective representation of smartphone addiction.

## 2.3. Comparison with Alternative Models

Considerable addiction research builds on Brown’s (1991) classification, which specifies six criteria for diagnosing addiction based on symptoms in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM): (1) salience – when a certain object becomes the most important part of one’s life and dominates one’s thinking; (2) mood modification – changes in mood (e.g., thrills, relief) caused by an object; (3) tolerance – the decreased efectiveness of an object in conveying the same level of pleasure; (4) withdrawal – negative emotions that arise if the consumption of an object is discontinued; (5) conflict – negative outcomes that arise from the consumption of an object, and (6) relapse – the return of prior symptoms after long periods of restraint or abstinence (Grifiths, 2000; Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011). These six factors are intended to help diagnose addiction by capturing many parts of the addiction process, ranging from the mental state of addiction to its conflicted behavioural outcomes.

As suggested by Brown (1991), addiction is believed to manifest itself in multiple ways. However, we find that the conflict component should be diferentiated from the core process of addiction because negative outcomes, which are the results of brain transformations induced by addiction, are not necessarily reflective of addiction itself (TE Robinson & Berridge, 1993). Similarly, Turel, Serenko, Giles et al. (2011, p. 1044) described relapse as being “unable to voluntarily reduce the use of the technology.” We view changes in usage level as another behavioural outcome best captured implicitly by the relationship between antecedent and subsequent use of smartphones. It is dificult to explicitly observe relapse in our context because smartphones, unlike psychoactive drugs, are regarded as an everyday necessity and adults must retain use of their device even outside of addiction. Consequently, we derive from Brown’s (1991) criteria a four-factor model of smartphone addiction that consists of salience, mood modification, tolerance, and withdrawal. Just like our two-factor incentivesalience based addiction, these four factors capture the essential mental nature of smartphone addiction that causes observable behavioural outcomes.

Scholars studying technology addiction note that “no uniform definition of this concept exists” and that there are “no accepted measurement scales for technology addiction constructs” (Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011, pp. 1044, p.1050). While we acknowledge that no single conceptualisation can currently address all manifestations of technology addiction, there are context-specific gains to be found by comparing diferent perspectives to better understand the psychological aspects of smartphone addiction. While the fourfactor approach follows the DSM’s definition of addiction that serves broader diagnostic purposes, our proposed two-factor model could be a parsimonious yet powerful alternative for causal modelling because it is rooted in established theory and contains a social factor that is specific to smartphone use. We apply the proposed two-factor model of smartphone addiction to derive arguments around its major components. Nonetheless, we also empirically examine the eficacy of the two-factor model to that of the four-factor model in the context of our study. We can then compare and contrast these diferent perspectives (twoversus four-factor) to find the best operationalisation that fits the smartphone context.

## 3.1. Antecedents of Smartphone Addiction

According to TE Robinson and Berridge (2003), individuals difer in their susceptibility to sensitisation. Some are more prone to addiction because they are more easily sensitised to certain stimuli. Consistent with this incentive-sensitisation view, studies in the literature indicate that personality plays an important role in determining the likelihood of becoming an addict (Coventry & Brown, 1993; Lin & Tsai, 2002; Mehroof & Grifiths, 2010; Nower et al., 2004). Among the personal characteristics believed to influence addiction, sensation seeking relates most closely to heightened desire. The trait of sensation seeking refers to the need for novel and stimulating experiences (Zuckerman, 1979). This trait has been shown to afect addiction in a variety of contexts, such as gambling (Coventry & Brown, 1993), online gaming (Mehroof & Grifiths, 2010), and Internet use (Lin & Tsai, 2002). Sensation seekers are dificult to satisfy and willingly take risks to get more thrills and pleasure (Horvath & Zuckerman, 1993). Because of their insatiable desire for ever-higher levels of excitement, sensation seekers are more prone to addiction. These signs of sensation seeking correspond well with the notion of incentive salience (i.e., an amplified sense of wanting) discussed previously.

To understand sensation seeking in our technological context, we sought out a more domain-specific form of sensation-seeking known as personal innovativeness in information technology, which is defined as the tendency to explore new technologies (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998). This measure of innovativeness was developed in consumer behavioural research (Goldsmith & Hofacker, 1991) in which innovativeness is seen as a preference for “new experiences that stimulate the mind and/or the senses” (Venkatraman & Price, 1990, p. 295). Thus, personal innovativeness with IT shares considerable similarities with sensation seeking. Both constructs emphasise the importance of seeking new and varied experiences, and both emphasise relishing the sensory delight of novel environments.

Using personal innovativeness with IT instead of a general sensation-seeking scale has a critical benefit: it focuses on the technological bent of sensation seekers, thus allowing us to distinguish between those who would seek thrills and new experiences through technological information channels like smartphones instead of through non-technological avenues. This distinction is critical, given that consumer behavioural research has found that generalised notions of innovativeness sufer from poor predictive validity; desire for new sensations and experiences seems to be highly domain-specific (Goldsmith & Hofacker, 1991). We also note that personal innovativeness with IT is not merely a utilitarian search for useful functions or extrinsic benefits; it strongly captures the intrinsic urge to explore that drives the hedonic nature to use absorptive information technologies (Magni et al.,

2010).<sup>1</sup> Consequently, in our information technology setting, we deem personal innovativeness with IT to be the most suitable construct to use to relate sensation seeking to addiction.

Innovative users tend to try novel technologies, a trait that typically lets them gain a competitive advantage in their jobs. However, in some cases, IT users with a bent for exploration become obsessed with experiencing stimulating sensations from IT use. Consequently, they end up exploring new ways to use IT applications, mostly for the sake of personal gratification (Lin & Tsai, 2002; Mehroof & Grifiths, 2010). For these reasons, smartphone users with a high degree of personal innovativeness tend to be vulnerable to addiction. Smartphones are increasingly becoming a source of gratification because of the number of new mobile apps available almost daily. In such a stimulating environment, innovative users can easily become preoccupied with the thrill and pleasure they derive from smartphone use. All things being equal, these users are likelier than others to become addicts. Thus, although the general trait of sensation seeking is a precursor to smartphone addiction, the IT-specific notion of personal innovativeness is regarded as even more strongly related to smartphone addiction psychologically. Therefore, we propose that personal innovativeness in IT is an important antecedent of smartphone addiction.

## H1: Personal innovativeness in IT has a positive relationship with psychological smartphone addiction.

Multiple studies suggest that people go online mainly for socially-oriented activities, such as making new friends, receiving emotional support, meeting people who share the same interests, and engaging in multiplayer games (Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000; Yang & Tung, 2007). As discussed previously, people prone to IT addiction tend to think that Internet-mediated environments help them avoid the potential embarrassment and risks of face-to-face relationships (Caplan, 2002). These findings imply that, in normal situations, addiction-prone people feel socially isolated and need social interaction. In fact, much evidence indicates that individuals who feel isolated from others are likelier than their socially engaged counterparts to become more addicted (Davis et al., 2002; Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000).

Our study uses perception of loneliness, defined as the extent to which an individual feels isolated (Hagerty & Patusky, 1995), to represent the individual characteristic related to the social aspect of smartphone use. Socially relevant personality factors, such as neuroticism and extroversion, are believed to lead to loneliness, which users then attempt to alleviate by heavily increasing their Internet use (Amichai-Hamburger & Ben-Artzi, 2003). Neurotic people seem to develop loneliness as a side-efect of their general negative bias and tendency for negative afectivity, and extroverted people appear to develop loneliness as a function of the size of their social networks (i.e., it is hard to maintain an ever-expanding mesh of relationships). At the other end of the social spectrum, recent research demonstrates that shy people also develop Internet addiction because of loneliness (Ang et al., 2018). These studies show that loneliness is a common reason why socially needful, negative, and averse personalities get more addicted to information technologies. Loneliness parsimoniously captures the social implications of diverse personalities and is the essential reason why they are drawn by the social allure of smartphones.

So, whereas personal innovativeness relates to heightened desire, we find that loneliness logically corresponds to the ubiquitous social interactions that smartphones ofer. Thus, we propose that perceptions of loneliness afect the development of smartphone addiction.

## H2: Perception of loneliness has a positive relationship with psychological smartphone addiction.

We believe the efects of personal innovativeness and loneliness on smartphone addiction largely explain the IT-specific and personal aspects of smartphone addiction. Nonetheless, we cannot discount other factors that might emerge over the course of extensive smartphone use. Turel (2015, p. 1) argues that habitual use facilitates technology addiction. If so, habitual use over time could create a spurious correlation between smartphone addiction and its antecedents. Therefore, we control for the efect of number of years of smartphone experience and volume of recent smartphone usage on smartphone addiction, to ensure that our primary antecedents are not conflated with other explanations.

## 3.2. Behavioural Outcomes of Smartphone Addiction

Brown’s (1991) broad conceptualisation of addiction, based on the diagnostic perspective of the DSM, includes both psychological and behavioural symptoms; however, our conceptualisation of smartphone addiction takes the incentive-sensitisation view that primarily focuses on psychological factors related to motivational drives (e.g., craving excitement, craving social connection). From this perspective, we posit that it is the psychological aspect of smartphone addiction that directly leads to behavioural outcomes.

Conflict is arguably one of the most significant outcomes of addiction. Conflict, defined as negative consequences, such as lower productivity and damaged relationships, is in fact a criterion included in Brown’s (1991) classification as a sign of addiction. Caplan (2002) showed in a study of problematic Internet use that compulsiveness and mobile social interaction lead to negative outcomes. In a similar vein, Caplan and High (2006) found that cognitive preoccupation with the Internet exacerbated negative outcomes caused by Internet use. In general, these findings suggest that smartphone addiction, which represents heightened desire and mobile social interaction, would also afect conflict, or negative outcomes. However, it should be noted that many previous studies of problematic Internet use are crosssectional in nature; thus, the causal efect of addiction on conflict as a behavioural consequence has not yet been empirically verified. We propose that smartphone addiction will cause conflict at a subsequent stage. By addressing the limitation of cross-sectional studies, our longitudinal study will clarify the causal precedence between addiction and conflict. The specific hypothesis is as follows:

H3: Psychological smartphone addiction will have a positive relationship with conflict.

In addition to conflict, subsequent usage is another important behavioural outcome of smartphone addiction. Addiction has long been associated with recurrent and excessive behaviour (Block, 2008; TE Robinson & Berridge, 2001). Along the same lines, the literature shows that technology addiction is strongly related to IT usage (Caplan & High, 2006; Chou & Ting, 2003; Ferraro et al., 2007; Meerkerk et al., 2010). Although several studies suggest a connection between addiction and subsequent use, the research that suggested this connection was largely conducted in cross-sectional settings. As a result, it remains uncertain whether addiction would really afect subsequent smartphone usage as the theory suggests. Specifically, it remains to be seen if the addiction-usage relationship is significant over and above the well-known efect of prior use on subsequent use that is the result of routine, nonaddictive use (SS Kim, 2009; Kim & Malhotra, 2005; Venkatesh & Morris, 2000). We explicitly postulate the significant efect of smartphone addiction on subsequent use, even when prior use is controlled:

H4: Psychological smartphone addiction will have a positive relationship with subsequent smartphone usage.

## 3.3. Perceived Usefulness and Perceived Enjoyment

Turel, Serenko, Giles et al. (2011), who were among the first to examine technology addiction in the IS discipline, argued that technology addiction afects subjective perceptions, such as perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. Although their pioneering attempt shed light on the efects of addiction on these two important IS-specific variables, their claim was inconclusive for several reasons. One of the primary reasons was that perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment are functions of not only addiction but also of other factors. In particular, several researchers have shown that prior usage tends to favourably colour perceptions of an IT application, such as perceived usefulness, attitudes, ease of use, and subjective norms (Bajaj & Nidumolu, 1998; SS Kim, 2009; Kim & Malhotra, 2005). The theory of self-perception helps explain the positive influence of past behaviour on subsequent perceptions (Bem, 1972). In essence, it posits that people formulate their judgements based on what they did earlier; if they performed the same behaviour often, they tend to convince themselves that they actually like it. Thus, as one’s prior usage increases, so does one’s favourable opinion about an IT application (Melone, 1990). In our study, perceived usefulness refers to the degree to which a person believes that using a smartphone improves personal productivity. In addition, we have adapted Davis et al.’s (1992) notion of perceived enjoyment to gauge the degree to which a person believes that using a smartphone is enjoyable. We propose that just as the self-perception theory predicts, smartphone usage will have positive impacts on perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. These so-called “feedback” loops were not examined in Turel, Serenko, Giles et al. (2011), but such relationships should be taken into account to better understand addiction. Thus, our hypotheses are formulated as follows:

H5: Subsequent smartphone usage will have a positive relationship with perceived usefulness.

H6: Subsequent smartphone usage will have a positive relationship with perceived enjoyment.

Individuals’ perceptions of their smartphones will be favourable when the outcomes of smartphone use are positive. Similarly, dissatisfaction is likely in the face of negative consequences. This line of reasoning implies that conflict significantly afects perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. We especially expect that with an increase in conflict, perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment will decrease. As Turel, Serenko, Giles et al. (2011) postulated, technology addiction is supposed to positively influence perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. Given that conflict is positively correlated to smartphone addiction, one might expect conflict to also be positively correlated with those perceptions. However, we believe that once addiction and conflict are controlled for together, the true efects of conflict on perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment will be negative. Thus, we propose that after the psychological characteristics of addiction are taken into account, conflict will have negative impacts on perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment.

H7: Conflict will have a negative relationship with perceived usefulness.

H8: Conflict will have a negative relationship with perceived enjoyment.

Drawing on the incentive-sensitisation theory, we contend that addiction is the result of changes in the brain’s systems. Such neurobiological changes alter the way addicts view and interpret their surroundings (TE Robinson & Berridge, 1993, 2003). Although miscalculation of value is a common phenomenon for many service consumers (Kahneman et al., 1991), we expect these distortions of thought to be measurably pronounced among the smartphone addicts in our study. In our context, people with a constant craving for an IT service could misinterpret this craving as proof that using their technological tool is useful and enjoyable (Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011). Similarly, people who feel safer socialising online instead of ofline could easily overestimate the merit of their smartphones (Davis, 2001). These psychological biases caused by addiction have been called “maladaptive cognitions” (Davis, 2001; Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011). Because the source of cognitive distortions resides at the neurobiological level, addicts are likely unaware that their judgements of smartphones are distorted. Nevertheless, smartphone users remain capable of recognising the negative consequences caused by smartphone use. As hypothesised earlier, such a realisation at the conscious level would help correct their cognitive biases (H7 and H8). Besides, the self-perception process is also believed to be a separate contributor to the formation of subsequent perceptions (H5 and H6). Thus, we hypothesise that, because of maladaptive cognitions, smartphone addiction will have positive impacts on perceptions of usefulness and enjoyment. Nevertheless, it is important to note that such distortions stemming from the unconscious realm difer fundamentally from self-perception (H5 and H6) and conscious evaluations (H7 and H8); thus, our final hypotheses are:

H9: Psychological smartphone addiction will have a positive relationship with perceived usefulness.

H10: Psychological smartphone addiction will have a positive relationship with perceived enjoyment.

## 3.4. Control Variables

Our model controlled for technical and demographic factors that might conceivably influence the endogenous factors of our study beyond what the exogenous factors could explain (see Controls in Figure 1). Restrictions imposed by a network provider might influence perceptions of usefulness and enjoyment (Sarker & Wells, 2003). In particular, users with a limited amount of network bandwidth might find that they could not use services that require extensive data transfer (Varshney & Vetter, 2001). Thus, our survey included binary items asking whether respondents had unlimited access to data, text, and voice services. Furthermore, age, gender, and years of smartphone experience were also recorded because these variables are known to influence how people use IT (Venkatesh et al., 2003).

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Measures

Most variables in this study were operationalised with modified versions of scales from existing studies. Table 2 shows formal definitions of the constructs. When proper scales were not available, we developed new items. First, a new one-factor global scale of addiction was created with four items. As a dimension of the proposed two-factor addiction concept, we measured heightened desire with three items, with an emphasis on one’s impulsive urge to use his or her smartphone. We used three items adapted from Caplan (2002) to measure mobile social interaction, the other dimension of the two-factor addiction. Salience, one of Grifiths (1998) dimensions of addiction, was measured with three items borrowed from Charlton (2002). We measured mood modification, another dimension of four-factor addiction, by using three items describing mood alteration from Caplan (2002). Tolerance, the third dimension of the four-factor addiction, was measured with three items from Caplan (2002) describing compulsive use. To measure withdrawal, the fourth dimension of the fourfactor addiction, we modified three items originally developed by Caplan and High (2006).

## 4.2. Data Collection

We developed a web-based survey designed to measure research variables and other information related to smartphone use. We used a nationwide online panel of American adults 18 years old or older, maintained by a market research firm, as the source of our data. At the outset, we conducted a pilot test on 51 smartphone users to check the readability and accuracy of our questionnaire items. We refined the questionnaire in response to their comments. In the next step, we conducted our main study consisting of two surveys spanning a period. The first survey (Survey 1) sought to measure individuals’ addiction to smartphone use and to ascertain the antecedents of their addiction and at the same time gather demographic information. We randomly chose 3,000 U.S.-based members from the panel pool and then in February 2013 sent each of them an email message that included a link to our web-based survey. A total of 1,202 members responded to the invitations, yielding a response rate of 40%. We found that 551 respondents, about 46% of the initial set, actually used smartphones. Only these actual users were allowed to complete the survey. The final set of respondents had a median age of 45 years, and 56% were male. We checked response bias by comparing the respondents and nonrespondents of Survey 1, but found no significant diferences in terms of age and gender. The second survey (Survey 2) was conducted four weeks after the completion of Survey 1. This follow-up survey was designed to capture the outcomes of addictive use (e.g., subsequent smartphone usage, conflict, perceived usefulness, and perceived enjoyment). We sent email messages with a link to the follow-up survey to the 551 respondents of Survey 1. From Survey 2, we collected a total of 441 responses, which represents a completion rate of 80%. Thus, throughout Survey 1 and Survey 2, the overall response rate was 15%. The data collected from Survey 1 and Survey 2 were merged by matching individual respondents. We found that the median age of the respondents was about 46, and 57% were men. No significant diferences were found between the respondents and nonrespondents to Survey 2. As a result, we used the completed responses from the 441 panel members who participated in both Survey 1 and Survey 2 for data analysis.

## 5. Data Analysis and Results

## 5.1. Measurement Model

We used LISREL 8 (Jöreskog & Sörbom, 1996) to perform a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of the measurement model. The measurement model included not only research variables but also control variables, as indicated in Figure 1. In addition, we also included other factors such as single-factor addiction, salience, mood modification, tolerance, and withdrawal; these factors were required to evaluate the eficacy of the second-order addiction factor we proposed. As a result, the measurement model consisted of 14 reflective multi-item factors and 6 single-item factors. Because the same items were used for initial smartphone use and subsequent use, these identical items were allowed to correlate in our specification. To evaluate model fit, we used several fit indices such as the comparative fit index (CFI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardised root mean square residual (SRMR), and the adjusted goodness of fit (AGFI) (Chin et al., 1997; Gefen et al., 2000). A model is considered satisfactory if $\mathrm { C F I } \geq 0 . 9 5 , \mathrm { R M S E A } \leq 0 . 0 6 , \mathrm { S R M R } \leq 0 . 0 8$ , and AGFI ≥ 0.80 (Bearden & Netemeyer, 1993; Gefen et al., 2000; Hu & Bentler, 1999). We ran CFA, and its results indicated that the fit of the model was acceptable in every aspect of the fit criteria: $\chi ^ { 2 } ~ ( 8 4 8 ) \ = ~ 1 5 0 3 . 4 6 ,$ $\mathrm { C F I } \ = \ 0 . 9 9 .$ , RMSEA = 0.042, SRMR = 0.033, $\mathrm { A G F I } ~ = ~ 0 . 8 3$ . Table 3 shows the means, standard deviations, composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), and correlations of the variables based on the measurement model.

We checked the reliability of our scales based on CR and AVE; reliability is considered adequate if $\mathrm { C R } \geq$ 0.70 and $\mathrm { A V E } \geq 0 . 5 0$ (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988; Fornell & Larcker, 1981). As shown in Table 3, the values of CR and AVE are above the cut-of, which suggests a satisfactory level of reliability. In addition, we also examined the convergent validity of the scales through item loadings from the LISREL output.

Inspection of item loadings indicated that all of the values associated with multi-item factors exceeded 0.60, which implies an adequate level of convergent validity (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988). We also examined discriminant validity by using a chi-square diference test. Specifically, we compared a model in which a pair of factors was allowed to correlate freely and an alternative model in which the correlation was restricted to unity (Segars, 1997). We found a chisquare diference was statistically significant between the two models in each pair of the factors, a diference that provides evidence of discriminant validity. Overall, the adequate levels of model fit, reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity suggested that our measurements exhibited desirable psychometric properties.<sup>3</sup>

## 5.2. The Two-Factor Model of Smartphone Addiction

We earlier proposed that the two-factor model of addiction represented by heightened desire and mobile social interaction would be a succinct yet efective alternative to a four-factor structure consisting of salience, mood modification, tolerance, and withdrawal. To compare the two competing models of addiction, we first checked concurrent validity. Concurrent validity refers to the degree to which a new scale of interest relates to other measures administered at the same time (Cronbach, 1990; Rogers, 1995). For this test, we used a one-factor scale of addiction as the standard operationalisation. We found a high correlation $( r = 0 . 9 7 )$ between the one-factor construct and the second-order two-factor construct, indicating the overall soundness of our proposed model. Meanwhile, the correlation between the one-factor construct and the second-order four-factor construct was also considerable $\left( r = 0 . 9 1 \right)$ , but we found this correlation was significantly lower than in the two-factor construct $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ . We further compared the two models from the perspective of explanatory power. We used the conflict factor to test explanatory power. As with the previous results, we found that conflict correlated more strongly with the two-factor construct $( r = 0 . 6 0 )$ than with the four-factor construct $\left( r \ \begin{array} { l } { \ = \ 0 . 5 1 } \right) \end{array}$ $\left( p < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ . Taken together, our results indicated that, despite its parsimony, no explained variance was lost by using the two-factor model rather than a fourfactor model in representing smartphone addiction.

## 5.3. Proposed and Alternative Models

We tested the proposed model and several alternatives. The first alternative model was the same as the proposed model in Figure 1, except that it did not control for the efects of addiction and subsequent smartphone usage on perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. This first model was similar to the model by Turel, Serenko, Giles et al. (2011) in that negative consequences (i.e., conflict in our study and addiction in Turel, Serenko, Giles et al. (2011)) was specified as the only antecedent of perceptions. The second alternative model built upon the first alternative model and additionally controlled for two paths from addiction to perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. This second alternative was intended to help us identify the eficacy of diferentiating between psychological addiction and behavioural conflict factors. The third alternative model was the model proposed in this study (Figure 1). This proposed model extended the second alternative model by also accounting for the efects of subsequent smartphone usage on perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. Finally, the fourth alternative model was the same as the proposed model, with the exception that the four-factor addiction structure replaced the two-factor addiction structure. This alternative model was designed to rigorously compare the two diferent types of addiction in terms of model fit and explained variance. Table 4 shows the results of these competing models.

The first alternative model showed adequate fit in general but, according to SRMR, it still had room for improvement $[ \chi ^ { 2 } ( 3 6 1 ) = 8 4 2 . 0 0 , \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 8 \ $ RMSEA = 0.055, SRMR = 0.11, AGFI = 0.85]. Interestingly, the efects of conflict on perceptions were found to be positive, which aligns with the findings of Turel, Serenko, Giles et al. (2011), but were contrary to our hypotheses. The second alternative model fit the data satisfactorily $[ \chi ^ { 2 } ~ ( 3 5 9 ) ~ = ~ 7 2 8 . 1 9 ]$ $\begin{array} { r c l } { { \mathrm { C F I } } } & { { = } } & { { 0 . 9 8 } } \end{array} .$ $\mathrm { R M S E A } \ = \ \mathrm { 0 } . 0 5 0$ $\begin{array} { l l l } { \mathrm { S R M R } } & { = } & { 0 . 0 6 5 . } \end{array}$ $\mathrm { A G F I } = 0 . 8 6 ]$ . The efects of addiction on perceptions were positive, but the efects of conflict on perceptions turned negative. These results suggest that the positive influences of conflict on perceptions shown in the first model were spurious. However, the proposed model exhibited excellent fit $[ \chi ^ { 2 } \ ( 3 5 7 ) = 7 0 4 . 1 3 , \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 8 ,$ RMSEA = 0.047, SRMR = 0.60, AGFI = 0.87], and its fit was better than that of the second alternative model ${ [ \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } ~ ( 2 ) = 2 4 . 0 6 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 ] }$ . As expected, subsequent smartphone usage had positive impacts on perceptions.

This model explained 34% of the variance in perceived usefulness and 48% in perceived enjoyment. Moreover, it explained 73% of the variance in the second-order addiction factor. In addition, this alternative model explained 32% of the variance in perceived usefulness and 46% of perceived enjoyment, but only 60% of addiction. These explanations did not compare favourably with the explanations of the proposed model. Overall, our results indicated that the proposed model represented reality better than any of the alternative models we examined.

## 6. Discussion

Our objective in this study was to develop and test a theory-driven model of technology addiction within the context of smartphone use. Drawing on the incentive-sensitisation theory, we proposed a nomological network centred on a second-order factor of smartphone addiction. The proposed model was empirically evaluated against longitudinal data collected from 441 smartphone users. Our structural equation modelling analysis indicated that, despite its parsimony, our twofactor model consisting of heightened desire and mobile social interaction adequately represents smartphone addiction. In addition, our findings suggest that identifying addiction solely by the efects of its negative consequences could be misleading. This is because, contrary to traditional views, addiction and negative consequences difer in their efect on subsequent perceptions, such as perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment. Finally, we found that personal innovativeness in IT and a perception of loneliness explain a large amount of variation in smartphone addiction. Thus, these antecedents represent noteworthy individual characteristics related to the components of smartphone addiction. Overall, the present study contributes to the information systems by ofering a refined view of technology addiction.

## 6.1. Theoretical Contributions

A major contribution from this study to the literature on technology addiction lies in showing that our twofactor model of smartphone addiction is both parsimonious and efective in representing pathological smartphone use. We took a theory-driven approach to the conceptualisation of a second-order addiction factor. Drawing on incentive-sensitisation theory, we proposed that heightened desire must be a key element of smartphone addiction. Our model explicitly accounted for this context-specific property of smartphone addiction by including mobile social interaction as the other dimension of smartphone addiction.

This is not to say, however, that the two incentivesalience based factors dismiss the four vital aspects of the DSM conceptualisation relating to mental addiction. We can observe that heightened desire captures the salience of wanting to use smartphones even when we do not have to (e.g., by telling oneself “just a few more minutes”), just as it captures the tolerance of trying to cut down on usage without success (e.g., “it’s not easy to resist the temptation”) and the loss of control associated with withdrawal (e.g., it is “dificult to overrule my impulse”). Similarly, the preference for mobile social interactions captures mood modification attempts to avoid worry and stress by resorting to one’s addiction (e.g., users “feel safer” and “more confident” interacting with others through their device). Thus, our two-factor model parsimoniously reflects many of the same issues as a four-factor model, while also paying heed to the inability to regulate behaviour.

We can now also clearly see the benefits of our proposed approach of modelling negative outcomes like conflict as a consequence of a mental addiction instead of including them in the addiction construct as prior studies have suggested. Negative consequences, such as lowered productivity, have long been recognised as a core symptom in diagnosing addiction (Brown, 1991; Grifiths, 2000). Inevitably, the conceptualisation and operationalisation of addiction in the prior literature tend to mix the behavioural outcomes of addiction with its fundamental psychological forces (Chou & Ting, 2003; Ferraro et al., 2007; Turel, Serenko, Giles et al., 2011). However, such a mixture of psychological and behavioural components is inconsistent with the tradition of IS research, which typically diferentiates between psychological and behavioural factors (Davis et al., 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). More importantly, the blend of the two types of factors prevents us from examining the subtle diferences between them. The present study indicates that the psychology-based addiction factor has positive relationships with subsequent perceptions, whereas the outcome-based conflict factor shows negative relationships. Specifically, we found that although conflict is positively correlated with addiction, its efects on subsequent perceptions of great relevance to IS, namely usefulness and enjoyment, are negative after controlling for the efects of addiction. Overall, our study is meaningful because it clearly shows that to better understand smartphone use, it is important to diferentiate the psychological force of addiction from its behavioural outcomes. Existing technology addiction studies mostly investigate the negative consequences of addiction only, whereas our study illuminates two diferent routes with two mediators that better explain how the positive and negative consequences (perception) of smartphone addiction come about. This approach addresses Turel, Serenko, Giles et al.’s (2011, p. 1056) call for future research: “a direct link between technology addiction and perceptions was proposed. However, other variables may fully or partially mediate this relationship. Future researchers should test mediators in various contexts to develop a better understanding of the addiction–perception relationship.”

Another important contribution from this study lies in our identification of two major antecedents of smartphone addiction, namely, personal innovativeness in IT and perception of loneliness. Our choice of these antecedents came as a result of the same theoretical framework that guided the development of the two-factor structure of smartphone addiction. In this study, we proposed that personal innovativeness and perception of loneliness represent individual characteristics that correspond well to heightened desire and mobile social interaction. Our results indeed indicate that these two antecedents, along with past usage, explained more than two-thirds of the variation in smartphone addiction (i.e., 69%). Even more interestingly, the efects of the antecedents were so powerful that none of the control variables other than past usage was found significant. These findings are notable against the backdrop of extensive research showing the significant efects of demographic characteristics on addiction (e.g., age, gender) (Chak & Leung, 2004; Hur, 2006; Lin & Tsai, 2002; Morahan-Martin & Schumacher, 2000; Skoric et al., 2009; Yang & Tung, 2007). We infer from these findings that personal innovativeness and perception of loneliness reflect, at least to some extent, demographic information such as age and gender.

We also note that prior studies of particular psychological antecedents to addiction, such as extroversion, neuroticism, and shyness, have separately concluded that loneliness is the major reason that their psychological factor of interest causes IT addiction (Amichai-Hamburger & Ben-Artzi, 2003; Ang et al., 2018). Our study is perhaps the first to use these findings to create an overall model of IT addiction that positions loneliness as the single major psychological antecedent to addiction. Because of the powerful efect of loneliness we observed in our results, even after factoring in other major causes, we believe that future models of addiction that focus on non-psychological issues can consider simply including loneliness as a parsimonious explanation of psychological antecedents.

Additionally, we found that using personal innovativeness as an antecedent of addiction, as successfully seen in our results, can be advantageous in future theory-building research. Innovativeness is a wellstoried construct in information systems and the way we use it may allow researchers to link related factors, such as self-eficacy, to the story of addiction. Furthermore, in the information systems literature innovativeness has been found to strongly emphasise hedonic issues (Magni et al., 2010). Consequently, we suggest that IS studies that seek to parsimoniously capture hedonic aspects of addiction can use the native IS construct of personal innovativeness with IT to tell a contextualised story of addiction.

Note that we do not argue that our model ofers an exhaustive list of the determinants of smartphone addiction. However, we believe that it parsimoniously captures the hedonic, psychological, and behavioural aspects of this addiction. Our findings convincingly point out the merit of a theory-driven, context-specific approach to the modelling of the antecedents of technology addiction.

## 6.2. Managerial Implications

Our research in this study ofers a parsimonious yet powerful measure of smartphone addiction that is rooted in theory and adapted to the specific context of smartphone use. Unlike many prior measures of technology addiction that have numerous items and dimensions, we have demonstrated that just the two factors of heightened desire and mobile social interaction, measured by a total of just six items, are a suitable alternative to the four-factor measures of addiction. This smaller but efective measurement can be easily included in market studies or in larger mental health assessments without sacrificing explanatory power.

Most service providers tend to assume that website trafic will increase if they make their website addictive. However, we found that, although the efect of addiction on subsequent usage was positive, its impact was insubstantial. Meanwhile, our findings strongly support a widely held assumption that addiction increases negative consequences. More addicted users will not be as enthusiastic about mobile technology as one might expect, and this could conceivably afect their word-of-mouth. In addition, as discussed previously, addiction was not found to have a positive impact on subsequent use, at least in the context of smartphone use. Overall, our findings suggest that smartphone providers have little incentive to promote or facilitate addiction among their customers.

As criteria for evaluating the quality of their products or services, practitioners have relied on such factors as perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and perceived enjoyment. Our study demonstrates that because of maladaptive cognitions, more addicted users regard their smartphones as helpful and fun despite the considerable harm to their daily life. Thus, we suggest that practitioners expand a set of evaluation criteria for their performance. Instead of focusing on the system criteria (e.g., perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, perceived enjoyment), practitioners are encouraged to examine the broader impacts of their services and products, which include, but are not limited to, personal consequences, word-of -mouth, and inattention to alternatives. An expanded set of metrics will help practitioners to better gauge customers’ diverse and, in many cases, conflicting reactions to their oferings.

Our study also shows that conflict is an outcome of addiction that deserves serious attention from mobile service providers. We found that addiction puts users in a conflicted state that undermines their perceptions of the utility and enjoyment of mobile devices. Consequently, users who are more addicted than others will not be as enthusiastic about mobile technology as one might expect. Although word-of-mouth and future usage increase from the incentive salience of addiction, it seems that users are also aware of the negative consequences this brings to their life and work. Thus, users with stronger addiction struggle with themselves to bring their usage and attraction to mobile devices under control. Further, Because of the Bring Your Own Device (BYOD) practices, employees become increasingly reliant on their mobiles phone to handle both personal and work-related tasks. As a result, employees are more likely to be addicted to their smartphones in such a BYOD environment where they cannot be separated from their mobile phones at any moment. Therefore, organisations need to pay more attention to these workers who are subjected to BYOD in order to prevent them from being addicted to mobile phones.

To help users avoid the conflicts that addiction brings about, mobile service providers could focus on making their services more habit-forming, but not to the point of addiction. For example, contemporary mobile phones are typically equipped with the features of showing the history of phone usage and, if necessary, warning about or blocking extensive phone use. Not only should providers adopt more features such as these, mobile phone users should also take advantage of these features to avoid the harmful outcomes of addiction.

Although our empirical study did not target young users (teenage and younger), we feel that some of the lessons of our study should nonetheless be of great interest to parents and school counsellors. Guardians and educators should take a proactive role in preventing smartphone addiction because it can interfere with children’s foundational education and socialisation opportunities. For example, parents should take advantage of software settings on modern operating systems that limit connectivity and use by children. Moreover, anyone responsible for overseeing children and youth should pay heed to the issue of loneliness, which we found is a major precursor to smartphone addiction. Previous literature has highlighted how both extroverted and shy youth are prone to addiction, albeit for diferent reasons. However, loneliness is believed to be the major mediating factor between psychological states and addiction, so we encourage parents and institutions to take advantage of this powerful mediator. For example, schools could measure and counsel students about loneliness with an eye on the addictive use of IT. Furthermore, they might help children be aware from an early age of the feeling of loneliness and of the need to self-observe whether their use of IT is an attempt to escape ofline interactions.

Our two-factor measure of addiction provides an easy way to investigate the level of IT addiction. This new scale helps practitioners distinguish between high and low levels of addiction and encourages them to further examine the diference in psychological or behavioural states between the two groups (e.g., innovativeness, loneliness, past use, etc.). Such a nuanced understanding could enable managers to keep lowlevel addicts from becoming high-level addicts by intervening to control problematic psychological or behavioural states. For example, we performed ad hoc analysis by splitting our sample into two groups (i.e., high-level addiction vs. low-level addiction) and found that higher-level addicts are associated with a higher level of past use than lower-level addicts. Thus, mobile service providers and healthcare specialists can advise users to check their past phone usage records and limit their use of a mobile device to curb their addiction, at least in part. Moreover, we found that high-level addicts are associated with a higher level of loneliness. Thus, healthcare professionals should note that mobile users who feel socially excluded are especially susceptible to smartphone addiction. In summary, our new scale provides practitioners with a useful tool to identify personal traits diferentially associated with high- and low-level addicts, which will eventually help design interventions to keep low-level addicts from becoming high-level addicts.

## 6.3. Limitations and Further Research

Our study sets up a theoretical framework for understanding how addiction occurs among smartphone users and how it afects their perceptions of their mobile technology. However, future research can refine several elements of this study. First, we relied largely on subjective behavioural data. This approach allowed us to survey numerous users across many types of devices and service providers and provided statistical power to verify our hypotheses. Yet our results are subject to the typical limitations of self-report-based research because respondents’ perceptions are not always reliable. Although research shows that subjective measures of usage are highly correlated with objective measures of usage in the SNS context (r = 0.72) (De Reuver & Bouwman, 2015), caution should be exercised in generalising our findings until they are corroborated. Besides, even though we examined social desirability and found that it was not a serious concern in this study,<sup>4</sup> respondents generally want to present socially desirable answers regarding their behaviour. Thus, a natural extension of this study will be to utilise objective data that have recently become available through functional magnetic resonance imaging<sup>5</sup> and phone usage tracker apps. Such objective data might ofer more nuanced patterns of usage that could allow us to better understand the adverse efects of smartphone addiction. In addition, we also suggest that future studies employ experiments where, for example, participants are not permitted to use smartphones and need to finish a regular or frustrating task during the experiment period. After such an experiment, participants might complete a survey and an interview about the feelings they had during the experiment (e.g., friendship, loneliness, frustration, etc.).<sup>6</sup> Our findings should be interpreted carefully until subjective behavioural data are corroborated by research with unobtrusive measurement.

Our findings may not generalise to other types of Internet users such as adolescents, elderly, minority groups, or non-Americans. For example, senior people could get benefits from extensive use of mobile phones as it can sustain social connections and help avoid isolation and loneliness (Anderson & Thayer, 2018). Thus, caution should be taken in applying our model to population groups other than the one we examined in this study.<sup>7</sup> Our findings must also be adapted to suit other technological contexts. Ample evidence suggests that addictive behaviour is prevalent in the use of social networking websites (Kirschner & Karpinski, 2010; Phillips, 2009; Tokunaga, 2011). Although addiction is an important driver in that context, other mechanisms are also likely to be involved. For example, online social networking generally involves regularly posting personal messages, pictures, and videos over time. Such personal materials are known to be involved later with switching costs, which makes it hard for people to move to an alternative service provider (Ray et al., 2012). Accordingly, it will be interesting to examine the interplay of addiction and switching costs in regulating online social networking. For example, we already understand that addiction leads to excessive use, but we are uncertain if switching costs would have the same efect. In a similar vein, switching costs are believed to make people less interested in alternatives, and it remains to be seen whether this also holds true for addiction. Such a comparative study would help us acquire a clearer understanding of the actual nature of addiction and its related factors.

Prior studies noting positive opinions towards systems by users exhibiting high usage have largely discussed voluntary contexts (e.g., Bajaj & Nidumolu, 1998; Kim & Malhotra, 2005). Similarly, we too would caution that our findings are largely related to voluntary use of smartphones and the findings might difer in the context of mandatory use. Furthermore, one of our major assumptions in this study was that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use would eventually afect future smartphone usage. We did not examine these relationships because they are already well-established in IS research. However, caution should be taken in interpreting our findings until such relationships are further validated within the theoretical framework proposed in this study.

We focused on identifying the antecedents and consequences of addiction, but our understanding is still limited as to how addiction itself develops and changes over time. Though we conducted our main study consisting of two surveys spanning a four-week period to separately collect the antecedent and outcome variables, it is still a short-term examination and we encourage future studies to collect data over a longer period to examine causality. Thus, another area for further research is to investigate the dynamics of addiction within a longitudinal context. For example, recent studies have shown that immersion and anxiety are strongly related to addiction in numerous contexts (Bian & Leung, 2015; Darcin et al., 2016; Gökçearslan et al., 2016; JH Kim, 2017; Peper & Harvey, 2018).<sup>8</sup> Thus, it would be useful to examine how immersion and anxiety afect addiction and also how addiction, in turn, influences these psychological factors. Along the same lines, it would be interesting to test whether the outcomes of addiction we identified (e.g., conflict, perceived usefulness, and perceived enjoyment) will contribute, in one way or another, to the formation of subsequent addiction.

Whereas much research on addiction has been cross-sectional, more investigation needs to be conducted in a longitudinal context to better understand the dynamics behind the evolution of technology addiction. In the same vein, we need a deeper understanding of the process of addiction formulation, meaning the steps by which a behaviour gradually develops into addiction; furthermore, what are the exact steps that allow people to get rid of it. Such research settings call for stage or process models that help us to identify the diferent stages of addiction formulation and removal processes, and what specific conditions move the process to the next stage in each case (Weinstein et al., 1998). Outlining the processes and stages of addiction formulation and removal processes is needed so that more addicted users can get optimal treatment specific to their stage of addiction.

## 6.4. Conclusions

Smartphones have become an integral part of our lives. Although these devices serve as useful sources of information and entertainment, some people become addicted to smartphones to the extent of harming their personal and professional lives. Given the potential for negative consequences resulting from smartphone addiction, it is important for researchers to have a deeper understanding of its nature, causes, and efects. We provide a second-order model of smartphone addiction that is theory-driven, context-specific, and psychological in nature. In addition to the structure of smartphone addiction, we proposed novel antecedents and outcomes of addiction. Our longitudinal empirical study demonstrates that our proposed model is more compelling and yet succinct than competing models for describing pathological smartphone use. Research on smartphone addiction is still in a nascent stage and requires further theoretical development, empirical validation, and experimental designs. We hope that our model will serve as a useful conceptual tool in future studies of this important and escalating topic.

## Appendix: Measurement Items

Heightened Desire (HD)

● I sometimes find myself saying “just a few more minutes” when using my smartphone.

● I find it dificult to overrule my impulse to use my smartphone.

● It’s not easy to resist the temptation to use my smartphone more.

Mobile Social Interaction (MSI)

● I prefer relating to others through my smartphone instead of face-to-face.

● I feel safer relating to others through my smartphone instead of face-to-face.

● I am more confident socialising online than ofline.

Salience (SAL)

● When I’m not using my smartphone, I feel I still want to use it.

● I sometimes think about using my smartphone even when I don’t need to use it.

● I often think about using my smartphone when I should not use it.

Mood Modification (MM)

● I sometimes use my smartphone to stay away from worry.
