# Ownership and governance, scope, and empowerment: how does context afect enterprise systems implementation in organisations in the Arab World?

Hillol Bala <sup>a</sup>, Mohammad Mobashar Hossain<sup>b</sup>, Akshay Bhagwatwar<sup>c</sup> and Xuan Feng <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Operations and Decision Technologies (ODT) Department, Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, USA; <sup>b</sup>TechNext, Brisbane, Queensland, Australia; <sup>c</sup>World Wide Operations, Amazon, Inc., Seattle, WA, USA; <sup>d</sup>Division of Management Information Systems, Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK, USA

## ABSTRACT

Research on enterprise systems (ES) implementation has focused on diferent aspects of implementation challenges, employee reactions, and system success. Much of this research has been conducted in developed countries and a few developing countries. This raises an obvious question about the applicability of findings to other contexts with diferent social and organisational cultures. We examine ES implementations in 12 organisations in Saudi Arabia, an important economic and political frontier and a unique sociocultural context in the Arab World. Using sociotechnical systems theory and institutional theory and the literature on contextualisation, we studied contextual aspects of three important factors related to ES implementations – i.e., ownership and governance, scope management, and employee empowerment. We found that the nature of ownership and governance plays a significant hindering role during implementation. Further, Saudi organisations implementing ES faced major challenges in managing the scope of implementation due to interferences from the top management and/or owners, and lack of justification by the consultants. Finally, we found that owners and top management were deeply concerned about losing their control over employees following ES implementations. We extend prior research and theories by ofering contextual explanations unearthed from a context that is diferent from developed and many developing countries.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise Systems (ES) such as enterprise resource planning (ERP), supply chain management (SCM), customer relationship management (CRM), and knowledge management systems have been embraced by organisations of all sizes and types due to their ability to improve operational eficiency, meet information needs of management, enhance the degree of data integration across the organisational chain and business processes and provide a strategic advantage (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Besson & Rowe, 2001; Laumer et al., 2017; Lyytinen & Newman, 2015; Marciniak et al., 2014; Staehr et al., 2012; Sykes, 2015; Sykes & Venkatesh, 2017; Tian & Xu, 2015). Gartner Research reported that the worldwide spending on such systems in 2019 was USD 457 billion and is projected to grow to USD 507 billion in 2020 with an 11% growth (Gartner, 2019). Recent industry reports have suggested that more than 40% of organisations in developed countries (i.e., North America, Europe, and Australia) have implemented one or more ES and another 40% of organisations are considering implementations (Panorama Consulting Group, 2019). A similar trend has been found in developing countries (e.g., Brazil, China, India, and Middle East countries) that are expected to have the highest market growth rate for ES through the year 2025 (KBV Research, 2019). Although much of the revenue in the ES industry still comes from developed countries, around 15% of the revenue is attributed to implementations in Asia and the Arab World that have diferent sociocultural contexts (Al-Turki, 2011; Kamhawi, 2007; Panorama Consulting Group, 2019). For example, organisations in Saudi Arabia, an important country in the Arab World and a global economic and political powerhouse, have started implementing ES in the late 2000s and investment in ES has already reached 20 billion US dollars, and is expected to grow significantly over time (Almishal & Alsaud, 2015).

There is a rich body of research evaluating the benefits of ES implementation and the challenges faced during implementation. However, much of this work has been conducted in the context of organisations in developed countries. Research and industry reports have indicated cost overruns (exceeding 25% of projected costs), failure of ES to meet management and employee expectations, and employee resistance to implementation and use of ES as the top three challenges related to ES implementation in these countries (Brown & Vessey, 2008; Deloitte, 2016; Devadoss, 2014; Markus & Tanis, 2000; Panorama Consulting Group 2019). Although a similar set of factors should be important for ES implementations in organisations in developing countries as well, prior research has found that there are contextual factors, conditions, and mechanisms in developing countries that shape the way technology implementation and difusion take place in these countries (e.g., Hayes & Westrup, 2012; Venkatesh et al., 2016, 2010; Walsham et al., 2007). For example, Venkatesh et al. (2016) suggested and found that developing countries, such as India, have a tension between a set of traditional conditions (e.g., physical, socioeconomic, cultural, and institutional) and emergent catalysts (e.g., open economic policy and economic growth, technology leapfrogging, skilled labour force) and this tension influences how the implementation process unfolds in Indian organisations. Much prior research that ofered rich contextual insights on technology implementation and use in developing countries has been conducted in a few developing countries and regions, such as India (e.g., Avgerou et al., 2019; Sahay & Walsham, 2017; Venkatesh et al., 2016), China (e.g., Avgerou & Li, 2013), Mexico (e.g., Avgerou & Bonina, 2020), Brazil (e.g., Avgerou, 2013), and a few other countries in Africa (e.g., Hayes & Westrup, 2012) and South America (e.g., Nicholson & Sahay, 2009; Silva & Hirschheim, 2007). Notably, there has been limited research that focuses on the contextual aspects of ES implementation in the Arab World (Al-Gahtani et al., 2007).

In this research, we seek to understand how ES implementations unfold in organisations in Saudi Arabia and whether challenges associated with ES implementations in organisations in developed countries generalise in Saudi organisations. Saudi Arabia is an important and unique context to study information technology (IT) implementations. Although it is considered a high-income economy by the World Bank, it is classified as a developing country by the United Nations because of various structural and sociopolitical limitations. Further, it poses various unique socio-political and cultural characteristics that are not present in other major developing countries such as India, China, Brazil, and Mexico that have been the focus of much prior research.

Prior research has identified a shortage of technical workers as a key deterrent to IT infrastructure development in Saudi organisations (Al-Gahtani et al., 2007; Idris, 2007; Rose & Straub, 1998). One of the reasons is the lack of interest among young Saudis who come from afluent families to develop technical skills and accept jobs that are considered low-status (Al-Somali et al., 2009; Bell, 2010). Further, low motivation in developing technical skills in Saudi Arabia may cause diferent perception and intention from IT employees compared to other countries (Alkraiji et al., 2016; Al-Somali et al., 2009). Apart from the cultural and language diferences from the developed world, a key challenge is the ownership structure, organisational hierarchy, and management style of Saudi organisations and similar ones from other developing countries (Almishal & Alsaud, 2015; Dey et al., 2015; Idris, 2007; Kristine, 2011). Entrenched traditional challenges and managerial issues may lead to low impetus or confronting state (i.e., concurrent presence of a high degree of traditional challenges and emergent catalysts for technology innovation and difusion) in ES implementation in Saudi firms (Venkatesh et al., 2016). The management practices in Saudi organisations are strongly influenced by tribal traditions where the owner and the senior managers are the father figure for the organisation (Al-Gahtani et al., 2007; Pillai et al., 1999). The missing voice of low-level managers and employees may give rise to process ineficiency and interpersonal conflicts in ES implementations in Saudi organisations. With such unique organisational cultures, an ES implementation that requires organisation-wide changes is likely to be hindered by challenges related to engaging and receiving support from the owners and top management to get these systems implemented successfully.

Against this backdrop, we seek to study ES implementations in Saudi Arabia, a unique sociocultural context and a sociopolitically powerful country in the world. Our research questions are 1) how does the organisational context in Saudi Arabia afect (constrain or enable) the ES implementation process in Saudi organisations? and 2) what are the important aspects of ES implementation that will be afected by unique contextual factors in Saudi Arabia? We conducted indepth case studies in 12 Saudi organisations to understand the unique contextual factors, conditions, and mechanisms that these organisations face during ES implementations. We build on two widely used theories, institutional theory and sociotechnical system (STS) theory, as our theoretical lens to highlight important organisational and managerial factors in ES implementations and extend these theories to Saudi organisational contexts. We seek to apply the notion of contextualisation to existing theories to gain insights on the functioning of Saudi organisations and how they manage the process of ES implementations (Avgerou, 2019; Hayes & Westrup, 2012). We ofer a set of propositions related to ES implementations and summarise in three specific areas: (a) ownership and governance structure, (b) scope of implementation, and (c) employee empowerment.

We find that these three aspects of ES implementations are substantially afected by the context that we studied – i.e., Saudi organisations. Our findings ofer insights on unique implementation challenges in these organisations and various contextual factors that play roles in diferent phases of ES implementations. Notably, this study is expected to contribute to the literature by highlighting the areas where ES implementations in Saudi Arabia and other similar countries in the Middle East are likely to be diferent from prior studies conducted in other contexts, including developing countries in other regions. We ofer nuances from a diferent context through which our knowledge of ES implementation will be enriched.

## 2. ES Implementation

Research on ES implementation is rich and has provided insights on various facets of implementation. As noted earlier, much of this research has been conducted in organisations in developed countries. In this section, we provide an overview of this research with a particular focus on two aspects of implementation – implementation phases and success factors. We focus on these two aspects because they provide a comprehensive representation of how ES implementation unfolds in organisations.

## 2.1. Implementation phases

Prior research has proposed four phases of an ES implementation: chartering, project, shakedown, and onward and upward (Markus & Tanis, 2000). The chartering phase involves decisions leading up to the funding of ES and related activities, such as developing a business case for an ES, creating a project team, selecting a software package, and approving a budget and schedule. The project phase involves activities needed to deploy an ES, such as changes in business processes, software configuration, integration, testing, data conversion, training, and rollout. The shakedown phase is the period from the point the system is functional and accessible by employees (going live or rollout) to the point when normal operations or routine use have been achieved (Markus & Tanis, 2000). During this phase, organisations mandate the use of the new system. Finally, the onward and upward phase continues from normal operations until an ES is replaced with an upgrade or a diferent ES. The shakedown phase is typically the first phase in which employees start using the system.

Prior research has noted numerous cases of ES implementation failure and project termination in which organisations were not able to achieve normal operation that marks the end of the shakedown phase, indicating the critical role of this phase to ensure the success of an ES implementation (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Häkkinen & Hilmola, 2008; Markus & Tanis, 2000; Sykes, 2015; Sykes & Venkatesh, 2017; Tong et al., 2015; Venkatesh et al., 2016). Although much prior research has highlighted the importance of shakedown phase and subsequent ES implementation outcomes (e.g., employee and organisational benefits), there has been limited research that ofers a comprehensive understanding of organisational undertakings in early phases of an ES implementation (e.g., the chartering and project phases). Organisations actions (or lack thereof) these early phases may pave the way for a successful (or a failed) shakedown phase. Therefore, there is a need to closely examine these early phased of implementation. Further, there has been limited research that deals with how these phases unfold in developing countries (Venkatesh et al., 2016).

## 2.2. ES Implementation success factors

Prior research has noted two major approaches to ES implementations: (a) implementation of an ES with minimal customisation (i.e., adding and/or changing system features or functionalities that require additional coding/programming) and high degree of configuration (i.e., enabling and/or disabling system features following embedded best practice-based business process blueprints) and (b) implementation with major customisation of ES and business processes to fit with existing organisational business processes (Devadoss, 2014; Holland & Light, 1999; Markus & Tanis, 2000; Van Beijsterveld & Van Groenendaal, 2016). Although the latter approach demands a longer implementation time and higher costs due to the inherent complexity of customising an ES to meet organisational requirements, the first approach is not easy either (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Glass & Vessey, 1999). For example, it is often dificult to integrate an ES with existing business processes and systems, if any (Davenport, 1998). As a result, organisations that opt for non-customised of-the-shelf ES often end up having multiple disparate systems along with the expensive ES. Overall, managing the scope of an ES implementation (e.g., the modules to implement, the level of customisation vis-à-vis configuration to be done, and the degree to integration with existing systems) is a major consideration for organisations implementing an ES (Markus & Tanis, 2000).

Given the time and personnel requirements for an ES implementation project, organisations often hire consultants to help them implement an ES (Devadoss, 2014). Consultants are expected to bring specialised skills, prior ES implementation experiences, and know-how that an organisation needs to accomplish the time-consuming and expensive task of implementing an ES (Gable, 2003). Prior studies also highlighted the role of top management. Top management’s beliefs and participation can bring essential leadership and support across ES implementation phases (Liang et al., 2007; Shao et al., 2016). While external consultants are expected to ease the task of ES implementation, prior research has shown that the conflict between consultants and top management is one of the crucial challenges faced by organisations during an ES implementation (Themistocleous et al., 2001). Often, the solution, schedule, and budget recommended by consultants are in conflict with the interests of the top management or require significant reconfiguration of an organisation’s policies, business rules, and processes (Gable, 2003; Themistocleous et al., 2001).

Research has indicated that about 57% of ES implementation projects can be categorised as being over budget, late, or under-performing (Panorama Consulting Group, 2019). ES implementation projects are more complex than any other IT initiatives because of their inherent characteristic of supporting business processes across diferent functional units (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Brown & Vessey, 2008; Krigsman, 2010). While such organisation-wide system and process integration is expected to reap strategic and operational benefits, the extent of changes often requires involving many stakeholders and resources (Gerow et al., 2015; Hong & Kim, 2002), which may bring significant complexity and challenge during the ES implementation. According to El Amrani and colleagues, “the multiplicity of people involved and the increasing interdependence between selected (ERP) modules makes the (ERP) project extremely risky, both technically and organizationally” (El Amrani et al., 2006, p. 85). Hence, how the top management of an organisation deals with managing organisation-wide changes during an ES implementation, engages in the project recognising the potential benefits of an ES, and supports the implementation through resource and other commitments are important success factors in an ES implementation project (Brown & Vessey, 2008).

To gain the intended benefits from an ES implementation, organisations need to make an efort to achieve and leverage certain critical success factors. For instance, prior studies have suggested the fit of an ES with existing business processes (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2000; Gerow et al., 2015; Hong & Kim, 2002) and organisational culture (Strong & Volkof, 2010) as a critical success factor. Strong and Volkof (2010) highlighted several other domains of fit (e.g., functionality, data, usability, role, and control) and underscored the importance of organisation-ES fit as enablers of successful implementation outcomes. Organisations even prefer to adjust the ESs to individual need and specific business processes (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Van Beijsterveld & Van Groenendaal, 2016). Also, an ES implementation might require policy changes and time commitment from key resources (Wood, 2010). Ensuring quick approval for such changes and resource commitments requires the support of top management during an ES implementation project (Liang et al., 2007; Shao et al., 2016). There are several other factors that influence the success of an ES implementation. For example, ESs often require certain technical and business process skills and employees need extensive training on the new software and business processes. With the required knowledge and skills imparted through the training programmes, employees may feel empowered and try to reap maximum benefits from a system (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Sharma & Yetton, 2007). The importance of social support in the transition of socio-technical design has been highlighted (Mumford, 2006). Sykes (2015) demonstrates the traditional support structures (e.g., training, online support, help desk support, and change management support) have a positive influence on employee outcomes after the ES implementation. With efective pre- or post-implementation training and necessary support, employees may accept an ERP solution with minimal resistance (Sharma & Yetton, 2007; Venkatesh, 1999).

Building on prior research that highlighted challenges and success factors related to ES implementation, we present a conceptual framework of ES implementation (see Figure 1). We do not present this framework as a research model. Instead, it helps us summarise prior research and structure the 12 cases of ES implementations that we studied. Drawing on prior research, we identify three high-level factors that catalyse efective completion of an ES implementation project – top management support, scope management, and employee reactions.

Prior research has noted top management support as one of the most important success factors for ES implementations (Liang et al., 2007; Sharma & Yetton, 2003). Top management support is typically associated with important ES implementation imperatives, such as change management, resource commitment, budget approval, perceived benefits, and institutional norms (Besson & Rowe, 2001; Markus & Tanis, 2000; Shao et al., 2016; Sharma & Yetton, 2003). Prior research has suggested these factors as some of the major considerations for top management as they extend support for an ES implementation project (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016).

Scope management is a major issue in ES implementation because many projects fail (i.e., cost and budget overrun, and inadequate system functionalities) because implementing organisations fail to manage the scope of implementation (El Amrani et al., 2006; Davenport, 1998; Marciniak et al., 2014). Scope management issues manifest through the trade-of between configuration (i.e., configuring an ES to present best practice business processes embedded in the system) and customisation (i.e., customising an ES to fit with existing business processes of an organisation). It also entails issues related to system integration (i.e., the extent to which an ES is integrated with other existing systems in an organisation), vendor/ consultant management (i.e., the extent of relationships with ES vendors and consultants), and project management (i.e., whether an ES implementation project is managed efectively). Prior research has suggested that these factors may lead to successful (or failed) ES implementation because they help manage the scope of an ES implementation (El Amrani et al., 2006; Marciniak et al., 2014).

Finally, there is a rich body of research that suggests that employee reactions (e.g., acceptance, rejection, use, coping, empowerment, and adaptation) are important for ultimate success of an ES implementation (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Bala & Venkatesh, 2013, 2016; Elmes et al., 2005; Kirkman & Shapiro, 1997). Various factors shape how employees react to an ES implementation (e.g., user involvement, participation, training, and facilitating conditions; Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Mumford, 2006; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Further, employees develop various technology and process perceptions that will also influence their overall disposition towards an ES (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). Organisations seek to gain favourable reactions and alleviate resistance behaviours during an ES implementation by encouraging, helping, and enabling employees to use an ES to perform their work processes (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Markus & Tanis, 2000).

## 3. Theoretical background

We draw on two theoretical perspectives – institutional theory and STS theory. These theoretical perspectives have a rich history in the IS literature as an important foundation of numerous IS implementation studies. We seek to extend these theories to the Saudi Arabia contexts and ofer theoretical insights on contextual nuances that were not underscored in prior research.

## 3.1. Institutional theory

Institutional theory explains how environmental and societal factors could play a role in influencing the organisational governance structure, norms, and overall internal functioning. Conceptualised by DiMaggio and Powell (1983), the theory focuses on the resilient and deeper societal structure that inherently influences the structure and functioning of an organisation. It suggests three specific influence mechanisms of institutional isomorphism – the process of gaining political and institutional legitimacy and market positions: coercive, mimetic, and normative. The influence mechanisms force organisations to conform to certain norms, traditions, and social expectation homogenisation (Oliver, 1991). At the inter-organisational level, these influence mechanisms expedite the process of homogenisation (Oliver, 1991). The routinised set of rules, regulations, and norms of an organisation become established as authoritative guidelines for social behaviour in that organisation (Powell & DiMaggio, 1991; Scott, 1995).

Prior studies have focussed on understanding how the governance structure and norms in an organisation emerge and get routinised due to the mechanisms suggested in the institutional theory. There are two important factors that drive coercive isomorphism: pressure from other firms on which a focal firm is dependent and pressure to conform to societal expectations (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). As a result of this mechanism, for example, Scott (1995) suggested that in order to survive and flourish, an organisation would try to conform to the belief systems and norms of the society where it is located (Meyer & Rowan, 1977). Mimetic isomorphism is a response to uncertainty wherein managers mimic a successful peer. Organisations often try to adopt practices that prevail in their industry or even those that are followed by a competitive firm, often to ensure that they can build on the successful experience of other firms (Scott, 1995). At the micro-level, employees may also behave in a similar pattern or common practices due to certain organisational culture and power distance. Normative isomorphism occurs for two reasons: (1) due to their similar professional training, managers develop a shared mental model of the institutional environment and act accordingly; and (2) managers interact with each other through professional and trade professional and trade associations and form perceptions of industry norms and expectations (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). It is likely that managers having similar backgrounds and experiences may share the same philosophy and approaches in handling their business. We view organisations in this study as natural objects and assume their organisational structures and dynamics are consistent (Davis, 2010). Davis (2010) has noted that much prior research employing institutional theory as the theoretical foundation erroneously simplified the unit of analysis (field-level vs. organisational-level), measurements, and theoretical tenets of this widely used theory. Although we recognise these limitations of the application of institutional theory, we still build on the stream of prior literature that used institutional theory and contextualise the theory for organisations in Saudi Arabia with the goal of understanding how these organisations respond to various institutional pressures and strive for institutional isomorphism.

## 3.2. Sociotechnical systems (STS) theory

STS ofers a framework and multiple perspectives for understanding the interplay between human and technological factors of modern world organisations (Leavitt, 1965; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). As noted before, the context of ES implementation in the Arab World varies from traditional ES implementation in the developed world. Consequently, we believe that taking a focused look at various perspectives of the human-technology relationship in the Arab World context is important. STS theory was developed with the primary motivation of understanding why some organisations were not able to leverage the benefits of their technology investments and implementations. To understand the human-technology relationship, STS theory suggests two interdependent sub-systems, a social system and a technical system (Bellini et al., 2016; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008; Mumford, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2010). The social sub-system comprises the structural and human elements in an IT, while the technical subsystem includes the technology and tasks that individuals perform (Avgerou et al., 2004). The focus of the technical system is to maximise task completions whereas the social system focuses on the overall well-being of the humans involved in the process. The main argument of STS theory is that for organisations to leverage maximum benefits, a joint optimisation of the two sub-systems needs to be achieved. More specifically, the joint optimisation or an alignment between the two sub-systems can be achieved only if the needs of the humans involved are considered during the various phases of a new technology implementation (Mumford, 2006; Venkatesh et al., 2010).

Organisations use technologies to accomplish tasks executed by employees. Implementation of a new technology requires that employees understand the new processes underlying the technology and requires them to learn how to eficiently perform old tasks using the technology. Since the new technology influences the fundamental business processes, it is usual for an organisation to experience a transformation in its institutional structure and potentially change existent governance and policy structure (Mumford, 2006; Venkatesh et al., 2010). Fit between technical and social sub-systems implies a process of achieving a balance between two key processes, (a) change in governance and internal organisational structure implied by new technology and (b) level of customisation required to ensure suitability of the new system. If this balance is not struck, the technology implementation would either be a failure or would not lead to significant benefits (Avgerou et al., 2004). In addition to this view of finding a balance between human needs and technical systems, there is another view that people will exhibit various adaptation and afordance behaviours based on their appraisals of a system and associated institutional structures, and gradually embrace the system or sub-system (Leonardi, 2011, 2013). In this view, people will play an instrumental role (i.e., an active role) in the implementation process and serve as an instrument (i.e., tool) to difuse a technology in the workplace through adaptation and afordance (e.g., people may alter material features of a technology during the process of implementation and subsequent use; Leonardi, 2011).

Since Saudi organisations often tend to have a well defined and a rather stringent organisational and governance structure, a conflict with the extent of change implied by the technical sub-system is not likely to be surprising. Saudi organisations, often with high power distance, are likely to have insuficient emphasis on the social sub-system. Perceptions and requests from employees may be poorly attended as they are often weakly empowered in these organisations. We posit STS theory as a fundamental theory for understanding this conflict between the two sub-systems that we observe in the Arab World, elaborated in the following sections of the paper.

## 4. Theory contextualisation

The important role of contexts in understanding organisational phenomenon, managerial, and employee behaviours has been underscored in prior research across various disciplines (Avgerou, 2001; Avgerou et al., 2019; Bamberger, 2008; Hayes & Westrup, 2012; Hong et al., 2014; Johns, 2006). Context is defined as the “situational opportunities and constraints that afect the occurrence and meaning of organizational behaviour as well as functional relationships between variables” (Johns, 2006, p. 386). Context helps link the observations to relevant facts that set the boundary conditions under which the observations can be considered valid (Bamberger, 2008). An advantage of such situational linking of observations and the boundary conditions is that it provides accuracy regarding applicability of research findings and makes interpretation of results robust (Rousseau & Fried, 2001). Contextualisation helps us understand micro-behaviours (i.e., managerial and employees) as influenced by macro conditions such as environmental, social, or organisational factors (Avgerou, 2019; Johns, 2006). Further, the process of contextualisation enriches our understanding of the tension and complementarity between distal (e.g., macrocontexts that represent a stable structural and/ or material aspect of an entity) and proximal (e.g., microcontexts that represent processual or performa tive aspects of an entity) understandings or views related to a phenomenon of interest (Hayes & Westrup, 2012). Consequently, contextualisation allows researchers to use an existing theory to a specific situation, leading to an in-depth examination of the focal phenomenon (Bamberger, 2008). Prior IS research has suggested that a way to develop a richer understanding of social and organisational phenomenon related to IS and providing actionable advice is to focus on the role of context (Avgerou, 2001; Avgerou et al., 2019; Hong et al., 2014; Weber, 2003).

George and Jones (1997) highlighted the importance of context to understand and apply to organisational and social situations. They suggest that it is important to consider the “environmental forces or organizational characteristics at a higher level of analysis that afect a focal behaviour in question” (George & Jones, 1997, p. 156). Contextualisation and contextual explanations are diferent from the theoretical underpinning of contingency theory (Woodward, 1965) that suggests that an optimal course of action (e.g., organisational or individual behaviours and decisions) should be contingent upon the internal and external situations and/or conditions that an entity (e.g., organisations, leaders, and individuals) faces and/or creates. The key theoretical diference is that contingency theory attempts to explain why and how an entity searches, finds, and undertakes an optimal course of action under certain conditions (i.e., contingencies), whereas contextualisation does not need to explain an entity’s quest for an optimal course of action under certain conditions – it ofers contextual factors, conditions, and mechanisms (at macro, micro, and meso levels) to increase explanatory power of existing theoretical assertions (i.e., helps explain a phenomenon in light of a context). The process of contextualisation and contextual explanations may help researchers augment (e.g., complement) their application of contingency theory to explain the certain phenomenon.

From the IS research perspective, context refers to the various situations that might influence the imple mentation, use, and outcome of a technology artefact (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003). The role of contexts and contextualisation in theory development has been underscored in prior IS research (e.g., Avgerou, 2001; Avgerou et al., 2019; Hayes & Westrup, 2012; Hong et al., 2014). Avgerou (2019) conducted a meticulous review of IS research and discussed various methodo logical and theoretical issues related to how contextualisation in theory development has been undertaken by IS researchers. For example, the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) has been contextualised to study user adoption and use of collaboration technologies (see Brown et al., 2010). In addition, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) is contextualised to study adoption in contexts such as e-commerce (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Li et al., 2007; Pavlou, 2003), mobile devices (Sarker & Wells, 2003), email use across genders (Gefen & Straub, 1997), and influence of cultural factors in technology adoption (Rose & Straub, 1998; Sarker & Lee, 1999). This suggests context allows researchers to use existing theories to gain a deeper understanding of the factors that influence the use of the IT artefact in specific situations (Avgerou, 2019; Hong et al., 2014). In developing countries, the contextual and environmental tension between traditional challenges and emergent catalysts can lead ES implementation projects to diferent states and outcomes (e.g., confronting, conducive, cold, and cautious; Venkatesh et al., 2016). Notably, a multitude of contextual factors can be conceptualised in macro and micro levels and understood using distal and proximal representations (George & Jones, 1997; Hayes & Westrup, 2012; Hong et al., 2014). For example, human-technology interplays in a firm are reflected by individual perceptions and behaviours (i.e., proximal views of how micro-level agents behave), which however may be influenced by the organisational context such as structure, culture, and policy (i.e., distal view of how macro-level conditions may contradict or complement micro agents’ performative actions). Hence, when investigating ES implementation in a specific environment (here, Arab organisations) involving multiple roles and groups (e.g., top management team, consultants, and employees), it is important to consider contextual factors at various levels, not only individual but also group and organisational.

We use the notion of contexts in our research to apply existing theories – i.e., institutional and STS theories – to the context of Saudi organisations. Our focus is to analyse social and organisational factors that play a role in technology implementation and use in Saudi organisations. In keeping with Avgerou (2019, p. 981, Table 1) who provided a rigorous review of theory contextualisation in IS, we focus on “Organizational/managerial” and “Institutional/cultural” distinctions in Saudi organisations as conditions of the environment, and “Behavioral" and “Powerbased” influences as mechanisms for theory contextualisation. We took a “Layered Contextualization” approach that allows us to understand the interplay and mutual influences between macro and micro-level factors, conditions, and mechanisms in Saudi Arabia (Avgerou, 2019, p. 986; Hayes & Westrup, 2012). Our goal was to unearth “factors that represent contextual conditions” in Saudi Arabia and ofer contextual explanations to enrich our current theoretical understanding of ES implementations (Avgerou, 2019, p. 981, Table 2). Using the suggested approach by Hong et al. (2014) for single-context theory contextualisation, the first step is to identify theories that are applicable to the context under focus – institutional and STS theories in our contexts. Institutional theory provides insights on the role of societal or organisational structures and norms that influence organisational work practices. STS provides insights on the interplay between the technological artefact under focus and individuals that interact with the artefact. While institutional theory considers the external factors that influence the eventual use or adoption of the IT artefact, STS focuses on the specific interaction between individuals and the IT artefact. The two theories act as a guiding framework for our case analyses.

## 5. Research methodology

We employed a qualitative approach to understand ES implementations in Saudi Arabia by using a multiple case study methodology to examine how organisations in Saudi Arabia implement ESs (Klein & Myers, 1999; Myers, 2013). We followed the guidelines by Dubé and Paré (2003) and exemplars from IS research (e.g., Bala &

Venkatesh, 2007), and undertook a positivist perspective in our data collection and analysis. Although qualitative studies in IS are predominantly interpretive in nature, we employed a positivist approach for three reasons. First, a positivist approach made it feasible for us to observe and record how ES implementation unfolded in various organisations in Saudi Arabia with “a passive, neutral role” and without intervening and/or participating in the phenomenon of interest – the ES implementation (Dubé & Paré, 2003, p. 604). Given that it was not possible for the authors to actively engage in the implementation process in diferent organisations in Saudi Arabia, a positivist approach deemed appropriate for our research setting. Second, a positivist approach helped us objectively (i.e., “value-free”) compare across multiple cases that we developed from Saudi organisations. An interpretive approach would require one or more of the authors to immerse into diferent organisational contexts during implementation making it hard for an objective comparison and contrast across the organisations. Finally, in light of the rich literature and theoretical advancements in the ES implementation literature, a positivist approach helped us build on a priori set of factors (see Figure 1) and relationships from the extant relevant literature as we attempted to extend prior research through the process of contextualisation.

## 5.2. Data collection

Data were collected during the ES implementation period at each of our case sites. We have focussed on the first three phases of ES implementation process that we discussed earlier – chartering, project, and shakedown. The onward and upward stage is likely more stable and comparatively has less challenges to organisations. We thus only observed and studied phases till shakedown, which is aligned with prior ES implementation research (e.g., Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). Over a period of five to 7 months in each organisation, we collected data using multiple approaches: semi-structured interviews, documents provided by the organisations and key informants (e.g., project schedule, functional/technical specifications, and process diagrams), and other publicly available information (e.g., press releases, financial statements, and trade press articles). Top management and employees in each organisation were interviewed (see Table 2 for respondents’ profiles). In addition to these organisational respondents, we also interviewed the consultants from the IT consulting firms that were involved in the implementations. Extensive notes were taken during the interviews.

## 5.3. Data analysis

Following the guidelines and exemplars of multiple case study methodology, we conducted both withincase and cross-case analyses. We conducted organisational level of analysis. These organisations were represented by key informants (e.g., owners, top management, employees, and consultants) who were involved in the ES implementation in their respective organisations. We iterated between within-case and cross-case to identify robust patterns in the data. Our approach was guided by the previously noted core areas (i.e., top management support, scope management, and employee reactions) that we focus on (see Figure 1). We conducted within-case analysis to understand unique patterns of ES implementation in each case. Interview notes and other documents were first reviewed by one of the authors who used a data reduction and presentation technique for analysing, triangulating, and documenting the contents of the notes and documents (Krippendorf, 2018; Miles & Huberman, 1984). We identified relevant information representing the three core focuses from the vast ES implementation research conducted in developed countries, i.e., top management support, scope management, and employee reactions. After this initial review was completed, one of the authors developed summary documents for each case that were used for the cross-case analysis.

A cross-case analysis was performed by two authors who were not involved in the within-case analysis. The objective was to understand variations and similarities across the cases and to find explanations for the findings from within-case analysis (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 1994). Individual cases were first compared to each other to find similarities and variations within each focus area (e.g., top management support, scope management, and employee reactions), allowing us to develop a general pattern of findings and plausible explanations for such findings. The patterns were then compared to understand similarities and diferences of findings, and to develop broad theoretical concepts. For example, if there were repeated quotes on the sudden change in project scope, these quotes were identified as a first-order concept. A group of similar first-order concepts were categorised as a second-order theme or theoretical categories. Finally, a group of similar second-order themes were aggregated to create a higher-level theoretical dimension.

The iteration between and integration of withincase and cross-case analyses ensured design validity of our data collection and analysis, such as descriptive validity, credibility, and transferability of our findings (Guba & Lincoln, 2005; Lee & Hubona, 2009; Lincoln & Guba, 2000; Maxwell, 1992). The comparison between within-case and cross-case findings (conducted by separate authors) helped us ensure the accuracy of our analyses across various events and behaviours that we reported in this paper – i.e., descriptive validity. The authors carefully compared their notes and codes to ensure a high degree of consistency and reliability of their interpretation of the cases. This helps with the credibility of our findings (Guba & Lincoln, 2005; Venkatesh et al., 2013). Further, cross-case analysis helped us unearth similarities (and diferences) across diferent cases with respect to how ES implementation unfolded in these organisations – thus, transferability (or lack thereof) of our findings was established across the case sites (Guba & Lincoln, 2005; Venkatesh et al., 2013). In order to establish analytical and inferential validity (i.e., whether our findings are dependable, consistent, and plausible, and can be confirmed or corroborated by others), we discussed our major findings with the participants (i.e., organisational participants and consultants) and asked for clarifications and feedback (Guba & Lincoln, 2005). This helped us resolve issues that were inconsistent and/or inconclusive in our preliminary analysis. Further, as noted earlier, the authors compared their notes and codes after they independently analysed the data that also helped establish analytical and inferential validity.

## 6. Findings

We organise our major findings into three major themes that we discussed earlier – top management support, scope management, and employee reactions. We highlight how these factors and associated considerations unfold during the chartering, project, and shakedown phases of an ES implementation. We draw on STS and institutional theories to develop theoretical insights based on our findings and highlight how contextual factors played roles in an ES implementation.

Considering that a vast majority of Saudi organisations are privately owned and the owners exert significant influence over professional management in major organisational issues, we contextualise issues related to top management support as ownership and governance issues. Similarly, issues related to employee reactions are contextualised as employee empowerment issues in keeping with our findings that corroborate important cultural aspects of Saudi organisations, such as power distance. Figure 2 illustrates these themes that emerged from the coding procedure. Table 3 summarises our major findings that we elaborate in the remainder of this section.

## 6.1. Role of ownership and governance structure

Our analyses show that the ownership and governance structure of Saudi organisations played a major hindering role in all phases of ES implementation. During the chartering phase, in some organisations (e.g., EnergyOrg, PackOrg), top management interfered with the selection of ES vendors. While it is imperative for owners and top management to provide input during vendor selection, we found that in some cases they went against the recommendation of the consultants who were appointed by the owners and/or the top management themselves. For example, owners of PackOrg decided to go against the recommendation of the consulting firm about the choice of ES vendor. We were not able to interview the owners of PackOrg to unearth the reasons for selecting a vendor that was not recommended by the consulting firm. However, it is possible that factors such as costs, prior relationships with top management and/or owners of the chosen vendor, and social influence (e.g., important others might have persuaded the owners to go with a certain vendor) resulted in the selection of ES vendors whose ES were not fully compatible with existing business processes and operating environment of PackOrg.

Regarding the issue of ownership and governance during the chartering phase at FoodOrg, one of the consultants commented, “Management at [FoodOrg] failed to provide necessary hardware that was initially agreed on and the implementation plan had to be paused for about a month before it could resume again.” Management hindrance issues were also faced at EngiOrg. “A major interruption occurred during pre-implementation when the management decided to change the entire governance structure of the organisation without informing us. This changed some fundamental assumptions of the ERP implementation plan.” said one of the consultants at EngiOrg.

This observed behaviour of top management can be tied back to the conceptualisations of institutional theory that underscores the importance of normative influence. As noted earlier, Saudi organisational norms and regulations are influenced by societal and cultural norms (Al-Gahtani et al., 2007; Al-Turki, 2011; Dirani et al., 2017; Pillai et al., 1999). We believe that due to cultural orientations such as a high degree of uncertainty avoidance in Saudi Arabia, owners and top management interfered with the vendor selection process because they were not fully sure about the implications of implementing the consultant-recommended systems. For example, in order to reduce costs, the owners of FoodOrg decided to implement only a few modules of the ES rather than the entire package, which was against the recommendation of the hired ES consultant. In addition, the owners also considered purchasing external vendor-developed software for various departments to reduce costs instead of implementing an integrated ES system. These decisions created significant delays during the pre-implementation phase as none of the recommendations of the consultants got approved. After much disputes with the consultant, the owners approved the implementation of only certain modules, which eventually led to poor work performance by certain departments and employees in the organisation.

Challenges were also faced during project phase of ES implementation. After realising that the new system would significantly modify the existent business processes, PackOrg’s management questioned various steps during the implementation. In order to ensure successful implementation of the new system, the ES consultant at PackOrg suggested hiring new IT personnel. However, these two personnel had inadequate skills and as a result were incapable of executing an ES implementation successfully. After a detailed evaluation of company’s requirement, the consultant suggested that top management hire at least five additional IT personnel to manage issues related to the new ES. Going against the recommendation of the ES consultant, the top management did not hire any additional IT personnel. This later led to significant issues related to maintenance and efective functioning of the implemented system.

This example from the project phase highlights how mimetic isomorphism plays a role in influencing organisational practices. For all 12 cases, it was observed that these organisations had similar IT infrastructures and prevailingly minimal numbers of IT personnel. In most cases, the IT personnel were located at the headquarters and managed IT support issues. Mimetic isomorphism highlights practices that are followed by organisational managers or owners only because similar practices are prevalent among peer organisations. Consequently, practices such as overruling the decisions made by other employees and taking the sole responsibility for all decisions made in the company are not considered a hindrance but rather a norm by top management in Saudi organisations. Top management perceives such activities to be imperative and an integral part of organisational culture. In all the 12 cases that we analysed, it was found that such decision-making by the owners and/or top management resulted in choices that were later realised to be a problem in the efective functioning of the newly implemented ES.

Similar issues were encountered during the project phase at IndyOrg. “The top management forced the vendor to customise and implement the Business Intelligence (BI) Module in the middle of the project, while the ideal is to implement BI at the end.” said one the consultants working at IndyOrg. This led to significant technical issues that stalled the progress of the implementation many times after the BI module was implemented. Even then, the top management remained uncertain as to whether their decision to ask for the implementation for BI module first was appropriate. Saudi organisations follow a stringent work culture and set of norms. Influenced by the Saudi societal norms and belief systems, the top management does not hesitate to overrule the decisions made by other employees and the ES consultants. This governance structure has been institutionalised into the working culture of many Saudi organisations. This was evident in all phases of ES implementation.

Finally, during the shakedown phase, owners and top management refused to delegate authority to employees as required by the newly implemented ES and associated business processes. For example, in PackOrg, the top management was reluctant to delegate specific authorities (e.g., approval) to middle- and low-level employees. As a result, the consultants and vendors had to perform extensive reconfiguration of the system in the shakedown phase, resulting in a departure from industry best practices. Typically, system customisation and configuration activities take place in the project phase of an ES implementation based on organisational requirements that are gathered early in that phase. We observed that the owners and top management did not ask for delegation-related configuration during the project phase in PackOrg. However, as employees started using the new ES in the shakedown phase, the owners and top management realised that the new ES would eventually empower the employees and they (the owners and top management) demanded to add additional approval layers to the ES to reduce the level of potential employee empowerment. A similar situation was faced during the shakedown phase at EnergyOrg. “The top management did not agree to delegate appropriate authority to middle management and therefore, the workflow had to be customised to meet top management requirements.” said one of the consultants working at EnergyOrg.

We believe that in order to maintain a high power distance and to avoid uncertainty in the organisation, owners and top management were reluctant to make these changes in existing practices and accept the reality that an ES might empower employees by giving them access to information and making various steps of a business process visible and transparent. Given the collective structure of the society in Saudi Arabia, the owners and their family manage their company and make all decisions without much transparency and/or accountability (Al-Gahtani et al., 2007; Al-Turki, 2011; Idris, 2007). Consequently, top management considers it imperative to exercise control over all aspects of their business, avoiding delegation of any authority to lower level employees. This specific context brings opportunity of simplistic governance structure and eficient decision-making and execution. In addition, opposing the decision of the owners or top management is not a common practice among employees and ES consultants. This also brings situational constraints such as hindering implementation process by going against consultants’ recommendation (e.g., vendor selection, IT personnel hiring), delay in necessary approval, and decision overruling. In developed countries, organisational leaders normally interfere minimally once a technology project is approved and underway. Having a more complex organisational structure (versus simplistic and flat style in Saudi organisations), organisations in developed countries spend more efort in initial decisionmaking and overall strategic governance of an implementation, rather interfering in mundane project tasks.

Applying the notion of contextual moves (i.e., moving between distal and proximal accounts to develop a holistic view of a phenomenon; Hayes & Westrup, 2012) and using the power-based influence mechanisms (Avgerou, 2019), we are able to understand how societal, cultural norms, and institutional environment (i.e., distal or macro views) shape managerial action to interfere with various aspects of ES implementation in diferent phases (i.e., proximal or micro views). The macro views characterised by an authoritarian culture and high power distance help us understand that it is a normative practice in Saudi organisations for managers and/or owners to overrule various recommendations by consultants and vendors during an ES implementation. Building on institutional theory, it can be noted that mimetic isomorphism plays a critical role in the development of a common organisational culture of high power distance in Saudi organisations (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). The societal norms form influential mechanisms that define organisational norms (Oliver, 1991). Due to the influence of common professional training, top management in Saudi organisations develops a common mental model about the organisational culture and work environment (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Scott, 1995). The same is true for the employees who consider it important to keep a distance from their management and consider it imperative to approach management for even minor decisions. Consequently, practices cultivated over the years have become part of the organisational culture in most family-owned Saudi organisations impacting ES implementation process.

Proposition 1: Top management and/or owners of Saudi organisations are likely to interfere and/or overrule the decisions made by consultants and/or implementation team during the first three phases of ES implementation (i.e., chartering, project, and shakedown), which may result in a selection of ESs not compatible with the organisation as well as significant challenges for the ES consultants and/or implementation team throughout the process.

## 6.2. Scope management during implementation

We found that the Saudi organisations struggled significantly in managing the scope of implementation. In some cases, owners and top management increased the scope of system during the project phase of implementation. In other cases, the scope was reduced. “The implementation scope was unexpectedly changed in the middle of the implementation. The top management of ServiceOrg decided to include a module which was not a part of the initial agreed scope of implementation. This interrupted the implementation and caused significant delay in project completion.” said one of the consultants.

Given their limited understanding of IT, it is evident that management (including the owners) was unaware of the issues related to scope management in ES implementations (Almishal & Alsaud, 2015). The owners and top management may be unaware about what ES modules would be required to run their business processes efectively. This resulted in an uncertain project scope during the chartering phase and the later expansion or reduction in project scope, which is often fatal to the project. Research has noted that ES projects that sufer scope creep end up facing time delays and cost overruns (Davenport, 1998). At FoodOrg, the top management made a decision to add additional modules (i.e., human resources and sales modules) in the middle of the project phase. After a thorough analysis, consultants had suggested that FoodOrg implement Finance, HR and Payroll, Production Management, Warehouse Management, Distribution Management, and Sales Module. Two members of the top management,

Executive Director and General Manager, did not subscribe to HR and Sales modules, since they did not consider expenditures-related to managing human resources as crucial. However, after the implementation was initiated, the Executive Director and General Manager had a meeting with the Chairman of the company where they decided that the Sales and HR modules should be implemented as well. Due to this sudden scope creep, consultants had to stop the ERP configuration process and go back to the initial analysis and redesign many business process flows.

We observed similar issues in the case of PackOrg and IndyOrg where sudden decisions about project scope change were made by top management at whim. At IndyOrg, the top management asked the consultants to include the Business Intelligence module while the implementation was underway. This caused a temporary halt in the implementation process to accommodate the new module. At PackOrg, the implementation process was interrupted by the top management. The top management asked to revise the project scope to include the CRM module and agreed to pay for the increased project cost. “Companies should budget as per business needs and not as per the management’s wish . . . suddenly adding a new module doesn’t make any implementation easy.” commented one of the consultants on the challenge faced at PackOrg. In some other cases, the top management decided to reduce the scope of the system by eliminating modules during implementation. For example, in EngiOrg, the top management decided not to implement the HR module during the project phase. “I would never drop the HR module in the middle. If I knew the hassles, I would have waited to integrate this change after payroll implementation is done.” commented a consultant on this issue.

We found that there were two reasons for the scope management challenges that were faced in the Saudi organisations. First, drawing from the normative isomorphism aspect of institutional theory, it is clear that the top management (including the owners) came from a similar family and educational background. Consequently, they had similar professional experience in handling the functioning of the organisation. The top management and/or owners did not have adequate computer skills and knowledge of ESs and therefore, they were not able to understand the implications of implementing (or not implementing) certain modules (Al-Gahtani et al., 2007). As a result, when they consulted each other about certain modules or aspects of the ES, they consented on decisions that led to scope management issues. Consequently, the top management and/or owners could not restrain their changing mind in scope management during the implementation and cannot keep the implementation procedure on the rational track. From the STS theory perspective, the management tried to interfere with the aspects of the technical system, which they did not have enough knowledge about. However, they had an immense control and authority over the social system due to their position and power in their respective organisations. This resulted into an imbalance between the social and technical systems related to the ES implementation and hindrances in the plans of the ES consultants.

Second, in some cases, the consultants were not able to provide reasonable justifications to owners and top management about the importance of diferent modules. For example, in the case of EnergyOrg, the consultants suggested an ES implementation with the full range of modules, which required substantial investment in technological infrastructure. Given the high costs associated with this plan and the lack of justification from the consultants on how all the modules could be of use to the organisation, the top management having normative cultures and isomorphism decided to reject the recommended proposal of the consultants. Consultants tried to follow industry best practices in suggesting about the implementation of full range of ES modules. However, since EnergyOrg had the minimal technological infrastructure and limited automation of processes, implementation of all ES modules seemed like a big leap and an unnecessary investment to the management. At the end, the top management of EnergyOrg accepted about 40% of the recommendations of the consultants.

In light of these findings, we suggest that Saudi organisations are likely to have a situational opportunity to access mature technologies and following best practices in the field, if the top management can minimise their interference especially in the middle of the implementation process. This is consistent with the observations of Venkatesh et al. (2016) who found that India has similar emerging catalysts. However, unlike the case reported by Venkatesh et al. (2016), we found that in Saudi organisations the scope of ES modules was frequently dictated and/or revised during implementation by the top management and/or owners. Moreover, once a wrong decision in scope management was made, it was dificult to correct and/or reverse it as the communication with the top management and/or owners was inefective due to high power distance and authoritarian culture. In developed country organisations, scope management in ES implementation is equally challenging, especially if it involves multiple stakeholders. However, defining the scope at an initial stage together with efective coordination across relevant parties through proper governance typically help organisations in developed countries efectively control scope creep (Häkkinen & Hilmola, 2008; Markus & Tanis, 2000). This was not the case in Saudi organisations that we studied.

Proposition 2: Saudi organisations are likely to face challenges with scope management during ES implementations (a) due to interference by owners and/or top management in the selection of ES modules, and (b) due to the lack of justification by the ES consultants on the modules selected for implementation.

## 6.3. Employee empowerment

We found that top management in our case sites did not feel the need to involve employees in the ES implementation process during the chartering and project phases. Besides, the amount and quality of training received by the employees during the project phase were not adequate for them to execute business processes efectively. “We could not involve end users in the implementation process and also did not conduct adequate hands-on workshops except a few formal training sessions at the end of the implementation.” commented one of the consultants regarding this issue at MediaOrg. The consequences of inadequate user involvement and training were evident postimplementation. The utilisation of the ES at MediaOrg during the first 6 months was only 30%. MediaOrg had to appoint several experienced ES consultants and trainers for a certain period to train the employees on the system. Even after 1-year postimplementation, MediaOrg was still using the old system in some departments. It took more than 2 years for MediaOrg to reach a full utilisation of the new ES across all departments.

Similar experiences were reported by employees at PackOrg, MetalOrg, ConstOrg, AgriOrg, AutoOrg, and ServiceOrg. The employees complained that they did not receive enough training to understand and use the ES. Consequently, they were reluctant to use the system to perform their tasks. In these organisations, the system utilisation was lower than 30% and in some cases as low as 15% during the first 6 months. These observations in Saudi organisations show a lack of balance between the social and technical systems – an important mechanism described in STS theory (Bala, 2013; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008; Venkatesh et al., 2010). We found that the top management at the Saudi organisations focused more on the technical systems, that is, on completion of the tasks related to implementation rather than user training. The management did not seem concerned if the employees received adequate and efective training. A balanced STS demands timely completion of tasks in a process along with a focus on the human aspects (Bala, 2013). The top management in Saudi organisations did not focus on the social system, that is, on the aspect of overall well-being of the employees during and post ES implementation. Further, we found that during the shakedown phase when employees started using the system, owners and top management were not willing to give employees authority to access information and execute business processes without their approval.

Such a lack of willingness to delegate authority defeated the purpose of implementing ESs in Saudi organisations. “The top management didn’t agree to industry standard authority delegation procedures in fear of losing their power and the vendor did not involve end users in hands on workshops as agreed initially.” said one of the consultants regarding this issue.

In the context of Saudi organisations, the lack of employee empowerment following an ES implementation represents situational constraints, similar to the notion of traditional challenges suggested by Venkatesh et al. (2016). Employees do not receive suficient support and encouragement from the management team regarding the use and adaptation of an ES. Meanwhile, managers were reluctant to listen to employees’ feedback that worsens the poor communication and employees’ perceptions after an ES implementation. Developed country organisations typically adopt established training systems and ofer rich support structures (Sykes, 2015). At the same time, their employees may also exhibit more resistance and complaints as they have higher organisational power compared to employees in Saudi companies.

The issue of employee empowerment cannot be blamed on top management completely. Research has shown that employees in Saudi organisations expect to be guided and directed on every aspect of their job responsibilities (Iqbal, 2010; Yavas, 1997). Employees in Saudi organisations are not proactive in pursuing opportunities related to professional advancement and personal development (Elamin, 2012; Iqbal, 2010). Employees have become used to executing functions that they are responsible for, neglecting the need to pursue any opportunities to advance their skills or improve their work practices (Dinger et al., 2015; Elamin, 2012; McGrath, 2006). Given the high power distance, employees also hesitate to approach the management to discuss any issues and instead wait for their directions (Almishal & Alsaud, 2015). Employees lack leadership qualities and as a result only execute the work tasks that the management asks them to execute (Al-Turki, 2011; Idris, 2007). The influence of macrolevel mimetic isomorphism was evident at the micro-level as middle- and low-level employees try to imitate the work behaviour of their colleagues and employees in other peer organisations. Consequently, common work practices have developed among these middle- and low-level employees in Saudi organisations.

Proposition 3: Saudi organisations are likely to face the issue of employee empowerment during ES implementations along two lines: (a) employees will not take lead in any decision-making activities and are expected to be directed about all aspects of work activities, and (b) employees will hesitate to express concerns about the new ES, as a result of which the organisations are likely to face many issues with acceptance of new ES postimplementation.

In sum, ownership and governance, the scope of implementation, and employee empowerment were major issues in Saudi organisations during ES implementations. Table 4 provides a summary of our key findings. Briefly, interference and reluctance to change from the top management and/or owners endured across all three phases we observed (i.e., chartering, project, and shakedown). They did not show enough understanding of ES modules; however, they scoped them up and down during the project phase and in some cases towards the end of the project phase making it dificult for the implementation team continue with the implementation. Employees were not well engaged in decision-making and execution activities with inadequate support and training. In some cases, the top management attempted to limit their access to ES to reduce their potential empowering opportunities enabled by the new ES resulting in lack of employee interest in ES during the shakedown phase.

We note that the three factors that we focussed – top management (i.e., ownership and governance), scope of implementation, and employee empowerment – are also important in family-owned small and medium enterprise (SME) contexts in general and it is possible to argue that the findings that we observed are primary due to the fact that several of our case sites were indeed SMEs (organisations 1 through 6). As noted earlier, we had six organisations (organisations 7 through 12) that are not SMEs in light of the definitions of SME by the European Union (i.e., SMEs are organisations with less than 250 employees) and the US Small Business Administration (i.e., SMEs are organisations with less than 500 employees). These six organisations had a few thousands employees each and their total assets were in billions with significantly larger than 100 million US dollars in annual revenue during the time of our data collection. Our findings were consistent across both SMEs and large organisations in Saudi Arabia highlighting the importance of contextual conditions and mechanisms that we observed in our cases. The layered contextualisation approach that we took ofers explanation that it is not the size of the organisation that explained how they went through the ES implementation process (Avgerou, 2019). The proximal view (i.e., micro contexts) may suggest that owners and top management of an SME would behave in the way that we observed in our cases. However, building on the contextualisation principles suggested by Hayes and Westrup (2012), if we make contextualisation moves between the distal and proximal views, we can observe an influence mechanism (e.g., power distance and authoritarian culture) that explains how the sociocultural environment and traditional values and beliefs (macro contexts that represent a tribal authoritarian culture, value system, and political closeness with absolute monarchy in Saudi Arabia) encapsulated the three aspects of ES implementation making them enacted, reinforced, and manifested the way we observed them (Hayes & Westrup, 2012).

Given that Saudi organisations operate in a unique sociocultural environment, even large organisations may exhibit behavioural patterns that may seem similar to an SME in a developed country. For example, owners and top management interfering late into an implementation process to change the scope of an implementation is not a behavioural pattern that we expect to see routinely in large organisations in a developed country (unless there is an extraordinary situation) because such an interference so late in a process would be considered unprofessional and a sign of managerial incompetence in a developed country organisation. However, in Saudi organisations, owners and top management might not hesitate to make such interference without any fear of being perceived unprofessional and incompetent. These are culturally, socially, and institutionally acceptable behaviours, and external stakeholders and employees are used to such behaviours that have little or no nega tive institutional and professional consequences for the owners and top management.

Further, we compared the Saudi and developed country organisations in terms of two contextual dimensions – i.e., situational opportunities and constraints – in Table 5. Briefly, the family-owned Saudi organisations broadly have a high power distance with a flat structure, leading to inefective communication and low project eficiency. Even if they perceive the necessity of implementing ESs to keep up with the global demand, technological advancements, and digitalisation, they may still face traditional challenges and reach incompatible outcomes (Venkatesh et al., 2016). Comparatively, developed country organisations have better-defined structure and scope control mechanism and provide better employee support.

## 7. Discussion

Our objective was to study ES implementation in a developing country that has unique social and cultural orientations. We examined contextual factors that afected three aspects – i.e., ownership and governance, scope of implementation, and employee empowerment – of ES implementations in organisational in Saudi Arabia. We discuss theoretical and practical implications in this section.

## 7.1. Theoretical contributions

We contribute to the ES implementation literature in at least three ways. First, we conducted multiple case studies in a context that is diferent from developed countries (e.g., North America, Europe, and Australasia). As noted in prior research, the IT implementation process may significantly vary in diferent parts of the world and it is important to conduct IT implementation research in diferent contexts to enrich our theoretical understanding of IT implementations (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2016, 2010). Building on the concept of contextualisation, we used existing theories to gain deeper insights on the functioning of Saudi organisations. Building on the literature on contextualisation in IS (e.g., Avgerou, 2001, 2019; Hayes & Westrup, 2012; Hong et al., 2014), we employed a process of layered contextualisation with multiple views (i.e., distal and proximal) to contextualise two theories – institutional theory and STS – to Saudi organisational contexts. One of our contributions is to strengthen theoretical explanations from institutional theory and STS with the help of contextualisation. This approach helped interpret our findings that extend current understanding of ES implementation by ofering a set of unique challenges that organisations in the Arab World may experience. Drawing on institutional and STS theories, we noted that the organisational culture and work practices in Saudi organisations are influenced by the resilient societal variables based on traditional cultural values. Consequently, issues such as top management support and employee empowerment play a key role in influencing the ES implementation process in Saudi organisations. That is, lack of both could lead to problematic and deviant implementation outcomes. Our findings extend current understanding of ES implementations by highlighting the importance of factors that result from cultural and societal variables inherent to a specific region. Essentially, our findings contribute to the issue of lack of generalisability of findings of well-established theories related to IT and

ES implementations that are based on developed country organisational contexts (Avgerou, 2001, 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2016, 2010).

Research has questioned the generalisability of wellestablished theories and models developed and tested in developed nations to developing countries because of the unique socioeconomic, cultural, and regulatory conditions in the latter (Ali et al., 2015; Dey et al., 2015; Kirkman & Shapiro, 1997; Lachman et al., 1994; Rosenzweig, 1994; Venkatesh et al., 2016, 2010). It has been suggested that the notion of contextualisation can help understand the nuances of IT implementation in diferent cultural contexts (Avgerou, 2019; Hong et al., 2014; Johns, 2006). From an IS perspective, contextualising known relationships and/or constructs to a novel situation can help elucidate the nuances of how IT implementation, use, and impacts might difer for that situation (Avgerou, 2019; Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Hong et al., 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2010, 2016). Our findings and associated theoretical insights related to factors influencing ES implementation in Saudi Arabia, an important country in the Arab World, will enrich our current understanding of such factors in other diferent contexts.

Second, we contribute to the literature related to ES success by providing an in-depth understanding of three aspects of implementation – ownership and governance, scope of implementation, and employee empowerment. As noted in the case analyses, organisational culture, management and ownership structure, and work atmosphere of Saudi organisational are remarkably diferent than that in developed country organisations. Due to a stringent ownership structure, the top management and owners of the Saud organisations are reluctant to delegate tasks to subordinates. In fact, the owners and top management are responsible for most decision-making. ES implementation process impacts governance practices in involved functional departments (Teltumbde, 2000; Wang & Chen, 2006), since it demands substantial reengineering or a complete reconfiguration of existing business processes. While such changes are expected to be beneficial to an organisation, convincing the top management to approve such changes is challenging. In developed countries where ES implementation is a common phenomenon, these changes get approved by top management because they perceive that consultants and vendors are aware of the organisational practices and can efectively embed them into an ES (Davison, 2002). In countries like Saudi Arabia, this task becomes complex since management is concerned about losing control over their organisational practices due to the changes in the systems (Srivastava & Teo, 2012). The entire ES planning and implementation operate on the whims and wishes of management. This finding extends the traditional understanding of role of management in ES implementation by introducing the aspect of ownership of organisation, which is perhaps unique to context of Saudi organisations.

Besides, organisational culture in the Arab World is known for high power distance index (Hofstede, 1983). Power distance index is a measure to capture the extent of inequality among levels in the organisation or society (ITIM International, 2007). Saudi Arabia has a power distance index of 80 (out of 120) and is among the top 15 nations in the world on this index (Hofstede, 1983). Given such high power distance, the management and owners of the organisations want to avoid any practices that would empower their employees. This implies that employees typically have little say about how an organisation is governed and how the policies are framed (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Bochner & Hesketh, 1994). Using the notion of contextualisation for the institutional theory, it can be further noted that organisational and governance structure in these organisations is influenced by environmental and societal climates in Saudi Arabia. The societal and cultural norms are reflected through organisational functioning, which result in an organisational environment that difers from developed countries. Although may not be important to developed countries, the three factors that we noted are critical to the functioning of Saudi organisations. We expect them to be critical in developing countries that have similar cultural characteristics, such as India (Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015) and Bangladesh (Dey et al., 2011). Moreover, given no major societal and cultural change in many of these developing countries, we believe our findings remain insightful even for nowadays.

Finally, we contribute to the literature by highlighting factors that are important in diferent phases of implementation – i.e., chartering, project, and shakedown. Specifically, we break down the importance of various cultural and organisational factors during the three phases of implementation. Our findings indicate that ownership and governance structure played the most hindering role during the chartering and project phases. Scope management was a critical issue during the chartering and project phases. Employee empowerment was a major issue during the shakedown phase. By categorising the focal factors for each phase of implementation, we provide perspectives that can help researchers and practitioners alike understand what factors would play a critical role during a particular ES implementation phase. Thus, our findings contribute by ofering insights on factors that are critical at diferent phases of implementation in developing countries.

## 7.2. Practical implications

We ofer three major practical implications. First, managers from organisations that develop and implement ESs (e.g., Oracle, SAP, and Microsoft) and consultants who help implement these systems in developing countries need to be mindful about the unique cultural orientations of the Arab World and other countries with a similar cultural profile. Given that ESs are primarily developed in the developed countries for their organisational settings, it is possible that the traditional implementation processes will not be appropriate in other cultural contexts. Furthermore, ES implementation likely transforms the incumbent structure, tasks, people, and their relationships inside an organisation (Leavitt, 1965), which can be contradictory to certain cultures and managerial styles in countries like Saudi Arabia. Our findings suggest that while owners and top management in the Arab World recognise the need for implementing ESs, they may not necessarily create an environment conducive to needed change during the implementation process. Particularly, they may need the systems to be customised in certain ways and implementation process to be managed in certain ways (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). It is important that managers, consultants, and vendors manage the implementation process in such a way that owners and top management feel that the ESs will provide long-term benefits for their organisations.

Second, owners and top management in Saudi organisations and other developing countries need to play a supportive role during ES implementation processes. Prior research has found top management support is a critical success factor for engendering the operational and strategic benefits from ESs (Liang et al., 2007). In addition, it could be beneficial to the ES implementation if the involvement of management during the planning or actual implementation of ERP is directed towards facilitating the process. By delegating decision-making activities to reliable subordinates and IT experts, managers or owners in Saudi organisations can ensure timely completion of ES projects. While this move might be in contrast to the current organisational structure in Saudi organisations, the top management and owners in these organisations can try to start acclimating themselves to more delegated work environments. Actions by owners and top management geared towards supporting ES implementations such as involving employees in the implementation process, allocation organisational resources to support the ES planning and implementation process, ofering adequate training, and delegating proper authorities so that employees can utilise these systems efectively can help Saudi organisations achieve success in their ES implementation eforts.

Third, we believe that our analyses of the factors afecting diferent phases of ES implementation could be used as a guideline or framework by ES experts and managers involved in designing ES implementation plans in Saudi organisations. Our findings highlight that not all three factors – ownership and governance structure, scope management, and employee empowerment are critical during all ES implementation phases. Ownership and governance structure were found to be a roadblock during the chartering and project phases. Scope management was found to be a key issue during the chartering and project phases, often leading to increased project execution duration or increased cost. Employee empowerment was a critical issue during the shakedown phase. With limited IT and personnel resources, it could be crucial for Saudi organisation to plan for the allocation of resources during the three ES implementation phases. For implementation efort across multiple locations (Sheu et al., 2003; Van Fenema et al., 2007; Wagner & Antonucci, 2009), the lack of human resources and coordination challenge could be even more problematic. ERP experts and personnel involved in the ES implementation process could use the results of this study as a guideline to better allocate organisational resources in managing factors that are critical during the specific ES implementation phase. In addition, by breaking down the implementation process into discrete phases and the factors afecting these phases, Saudi organisations can also better analyse what factors afected or are expected to afect the ERP project’s budget and schedule the most. Such insights can help Saudi organisations better manage their future projects related to organisation-wide systems.

## 7.3. Limitations and future research

Our findings should be interpreted in light of the limitations of our research. First, we collected data only from small- and medium-sized organisations in Saudi Arabia. It is possible that our findings will be diferent in other organisational contexts. However, we suggest that these small- and medium-sized organisations are an important part of the Arab World. In fact, it is customary in these countries to have familyowned businesses that are large in terms of total assets and revenues. Future research should incorporate organisations that are larger in terms of their employee base, number of departments or ofice, or the scale of their operations to provide additional insights on ES implementations in the Arab World.

Second, we collected data from 12 organisations through an IT consultant. It is possible that other consultants have diferent experiences related to ES implementations. In addition, future research can focus on understanding insights from a variety of stakeholders in ES projects such as line managers, customers, and top management (Bellini et al., 2016). Especially, the low-status perception regarding technical positions in the Arab World is worthy of further investigation (Al-Somali et al., 2009; Bell, 2010). Inclusion of perspectives from a wide set of stakeholders can provide further insights on some of the issues that we noted in our research. While we selected 12 cases to maximise variability, we believe that future research should focus on other organisations and possibly include organisations from other important or relevant Arab countries, such as UAE, Qatar, and Kuwait, to test the generalisability of our findings. Finally, we only collected data for the first three phases of ES implementation. Future research could focus on collecting data beyond the shakedown phase to understand the long-term impacts of ES implementation in the Arab World.

## 8. Conclusion

ES provides important operational and strategic benefits to organisations. While much is known about ES implementation in developed countries, there has been limited research on ES implementation in other parts of the world, particularly in the Arab World. Given the importance of the Arab World in the global economy, it is imperative to understand the challenges associated with ES implementation in these countries. We gathered data from 12 organisations in Saudi Arabia to understand the contextual factors that afected ES implementations in these organisations. Our qualitative analysis unearthed three aspects of ES implementation that was salient in these organisations and afected ES implementations – ownership and governance, scope of implementation, and employee empowerment. We found that Saudi organisations face several unique challenges during ES implementation with respect to these three aspects. These challenges represent various sociocultural aspects of Saudi Arabia that are diferent from many developed and developing countries. We expect that our work will ofer insights on IT implementations in diferent cultural contexts and engender interests in conducting IT implementation research in diferent parts of the world.

## Appendix

We collected data from 12 organisations in Saudi Arabia. This section provides some details of their background and IT status before the ES implementation.

Case 1: EnergyOrg is a petrochemical company established in 1995 and owned by a family. The company has close to 200 petrol stations around Saudi Arabia and has its ofices located in four cities in the country. The main services of the company include distribution of petrol, diesel, and automotive products. Prior to the ES implementation, the IT infrastructure of the company consisted of 15 desktop computers, 5 printers, and Microsoft Ofice Suite as the installed software. The company had an IT administrator whose primary job was to manage IT infrastructure at the headquarters.

Case 2: PackOrg is a leading packaging and distribution company employing more than 300 employees. PackOrg was established in 1975 and is owned by a family in Saudi Arabia. PackOrg has six small ofices in the country and a central warehouse located in Dammam. Main products include paints, mental containers for milk products, and aerosol cans. Prior to the ES implementation, the IT infrastructure included 35 desktop computers, 15 printers, and a LAN at the headquarters. PackOrg had an IT manager and an IT support personnel at the headquarters and at each of the branch ofices.

Case 3: FoodOrg is one the leading producers of food products and distributors of dry foods in Saudi Arabia and employs 170 people. FoodOrg is owned by an individual who is the Chairman of the company. Due to a busy schedule, the involvement of the Chairman is limited to major decision-making like major investments, expansions, major cut downs, and so on. The Chairman depends on an Executive Director and a General Manager for all other decisions. The company has a factory and a warehouse located in a major industrial area and three ofices located elsewhere in the country. Prior to the ES implementation, the IT infrastructure included 12 desktop computers, 3 printers, and a LAN at the headquarters. It had one IT administrator taking care of the infrastructure at the headquarters.

Case 4: MetalOrg is an aluminium-based product manufacturing company employing 180 people. MetalOrg was established in 2005 by two families and has ofices in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia. MetalOrg’s main products include aluminium sheets, window and door frames, and aluminium cladding. MetalOrg has headquarters in Dammam and has three ofices in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia. Prior to ES implementation, the IT infrastructure included 26 desktop computers, 15 printers, 5 plotters, and a LAN at the headquarters. MetalOrg had an IT administrator at headquarters.

Case 5: ServiceOrg provides industrial services and maintenance services for roads, highways, and bridges. ServiceOrg was established in 1970 by a family in Saudi Arabia and has ofices located in United Arab Emirates (UAE), Bahrain, and Saudi Arabia. The company employs 152 employees. The headquarters is located near Dammam in Saudi Arabia. Prior to ES implementation, the IT infrastructure included 28 desktop computers, 25 printers, and a LAN at the headquarters. The company also had an IT administrator and an IT support personnel at the headquarters.

Case 6: EngiOrg is a civil and mechanical engineering firm employing 262 employees. The company was established in 1970 and is owned by a Saudi family. The company is headquartered in a major industrial area and does not have any branch ofices. The main services include pipeline construction, civil works, electrical works, and fabrications. Prior to the ES implementation, the IT infrastructure of the company included 25 desktop computers, 15 printers, and a 100 Mbps Ethernet LAN at the headquarters. IT had an IT administrator and an IT support personnel at the headquarters.

Case 7: LandOrg is a public-limited transportation and logistics company established in 1984. The company employs 865 employees. Being a public company, LandOrg is primarily owned by Saudi citizens and also partially by other public investment agencies in Saudi Arabia. The company has around 260 flat-bed trucks, 150 chemical tankers, and approximately 200 other heavy transportation vehicles. The company has three ofices located in Riyadh, Khobar, and Jeddah. Prior to the ES implementation, the IT infrastructure of the company included more than 100 desktop computers, various software packages like Microsoft Ofice and Project, a file-sharing server, more than 40 printers, and high speed 100 Mbps LAN connection in all ofices. The company also has an IT manager and other IT personnel, who manage the IT infrastructure.

Case 8: Established in 1967, AutoOrg is a motor sales and maintenance service company with about 1,800 employees. The company is primarily family-owned but also has 49% of its total share distributed across various local shareholders. The company is in the business of selling new and used cars, vehicle parts, and provides for after-sales maintenance services. The company has its head ofice in Riyadh and has three other branch ofices.
