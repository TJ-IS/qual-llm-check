# Exposing patterns of adult solicitor behaviour: towards a theory of control within the cybersexual abuse of youth

Connie S Barber<sup>a,</sup> and Silvia Cristina Bettez<sup>b,</sup>

<sup>a,</sup>Computer Management & Information Systems, Southern Illinois University Edwardsville, Edwardsville, United States; <sup>b,</sup>Department of Educational Leadership & Cultural Foundations, The University of North Carolina Greensboro, Greensboro, United States

## ABSTRACT

The online solicitation of youth has been established as an unintended consequence of the connectedness aforded individuals through computer-mediated conversation. Information systems research focused on the behavioural patterns of online solicitors of youth is scant and lacks a strong theoretical foundation. We address this gap through an evolved grounded theory analysis of 90 Perverted Justice transcripts to extract the behavioural patterns that emerge when online solicitors use instant messaging to enact behaviours of grooming, predation, ofending, and control. The aims of the research are: (1) delineate between grooming behaviour, predatory behaviour, and ofending behaviour; (2) diferentiate between passive and controlling solicitor language; (3) disentangle these behaviours from what the technology permits; (4) extend the current understanding of cybersexual abuse to show grooming as only one aspect of a phenomenon that also includes predation and ofence; and (5) identify how these results are important for academics and software developers.

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 14 August 2020 Accepted 21 August 2020

KEYWORDS

Social media; social media theory; grounded theory; online sexual predation; child sexual abuse

## 1. Introduction

The field of information systems (IS) provides a unique platform for theorising because it includes the technology, the people, and the interaction between the two (Lee, 2001). One technology that provides ample opportunity for IS theorising is dark social media. In an interesting turn of events, this new version of social media is a move away from the open, public online forums like Facebook to more personal, one-on-one communications like Facebook Messenger, Kik, and Whatsapp (Read, 2016). In fact,

A quarter of all downloaded apps are abandoned after a single use. Only instant messaging bucks the trend. Over 2.5 billion people have at least one messaging app installed, with Facebook Messenger and WhatsApp, which is also owned by Facebook, leading the pack. Within a couple of years that will reach 3.6 billion, about half of humanity. Many teenagers now spend more time on smartphones sending instant messages than perusing social networks. WhatsApp users average nearly 200 minutes each week using the service. (“Bots, the next frontier”, 2016)

Facebook Messenger and Whatsapp include end-toend encryption in an attempt to secure conversations from outside detection. Kik is a temporary messenger service that allows for messages and pictures to be sent without keeping a record of the exchange. This shift back to instant messaging type applications, ones that allow for dyadic communication, makes theorising about the technology and behaviours even more important because the confidentiality inherent to many of these applications increases youth risks of unwanted solicitation. The “constant innovations of social media platforms and applications have transformed ways of interacting” (Urquhart and Vaast, 2012, p. 1). Through IS research, academicians have an opportunity to understand the ways that youth and online solicitors interact within these applications. One way to do this is through theorising as there is growing opportunity for IS researchers to move beyond describing and towards theorizing these new contexts and associated practices” (Urquhart and Vaast, 2012, p. 2). IS researchers have a legitimate claim for theorising about social media as the issues are salient for information systems, businesses and society; the research keeps the field informed about those issues, and such theorising can aid in producing strong results (Lyytinen and King 2004). Researchers building theories to improve the understanding of social-media-related phenomena should consider both the technology and social actors (Urquhart and Vaast 2012). This can assist us in understanding both intended and unintended consequences of the increasing pervasiveness of social media.

One such unintended consequence of the connectedness provided individuals through dyadic communication in social media is the online solicitation of youth (Whittle et al. 2013a; Williams and Hudson, 2013; Wolak et al., 2008). People have come to fear the “[i]nternet child molester who uses trickery and violence to assault children” (Wolak et al., 2008, p. 112). However, these are not as common as the adult solicitor seeking to engage in sexual fantasies through the solicitation of youth, a subset of which never manifest in face-to-face meetings. The nature of these online solicitations is both illegal and psychologically harmful to the youth.

Individuals who most often solicit youth online are adult men who target underage youth by encouraging them to engage in sex acts with promises of love and romance and even though the youth know that the plan is to engage in some kind of sex act with the adult, they still often agree to meet face-to-face (Briggs et al., 2011; Craven et al., 2007; Wolak et al., 2008). The implication is that online solicitors, rather than using violence, may utilise persuasion, bribery, blackmail, loving comments, etc. as enticements to draw youth into an ofline meeting. In essence, they develop online relationships with youth and build trust. David Finkelhor, director of the Crimes against Children Research Centre, points out that 60% of children who experience sexual abuse perpetrated ofline do so at the hands of someone in their social network (McLanahan et. al, 2009). However, in a survey of 412 adolescents ranging from 12 to 18 years of age, Peter et al. (2006) found that younger adolescents are more likely to talk with strangers online and that those conversations were more likely to be intensive. The authors note that this “may reflect the developmental changes in early adolescence, particularly the identity crises typical of early adolescence that make adolescents experiment with various identities” (Peter et al., 2006, p. 529). In essence, social media bridges a gap for online solicitors in that it provides a venue where they can transition from being a stranger to an adolescent’s social media friend, a transition which can play a role in online solicitation. A theory of how this transition occurs has yet to be developed.

We aim to be a part of the larger theory-building eforts in IS, through the study of the behaviours of online solicitors of youth. Researchers across a variety of disciplines have investigated online solicitation, including sexology (Craven et al., 2006; Elliott and Beech, 2009; Williams and Hudson, 2013), social sciences (Singer et al., 1992; Davidson & Martellozzo, 2008a), criminology (Whittle et al., 2013a; Gillespie, 2008; Shannon, 2008), criminal law (Craven et al., 2007; Gillespie, 2006), family law (Jones and Parkinson, 1995; Akdeniz, 2016), computer law (Kierkegaard, 2008), electronic commerce (McGhee et al., 2011), and computer science (Gupta et al., 2012; Thom et al., 2011).

To further protect youth from online solicitations we must understand the behaviour patterns of the online solicitors. Instant messaging technologies are useful for studying this phenomenon as they allow for the examination of dyadic computer-mediated textual conversations between online solicitors and youth.

The current study is part of the body of research aimed at protecting youth from online solicitations by answering the research question: What are the computer-mediated behavioural patterns of adults who solicit youth within social media? To answer this question, we accessed a publicly available data set of instant messenger transcripts from the Perverted Justice website. Perverted Justice was a non-profit organisation stafed by volunteers, the goal of which was to expose online solicitors and have them tried in a court of law for their ofences. Conversations that led to an adult solicitor being arrested, found guilty, and convicted of online sexual crimes with minors in the United States are the only transcripts available on the website. Because the individuals have been convicted, all identities are known and thus the age, gender, etc. of each adult solicitor represented in the transcripts is accurately recorded by Perverted Justice. This precision makes the data set appealing because it removes the speculation of the identity of the adult solicitor.

We apply an evolved grounded theory approach to 90 of the Perverted Justice instant messenger transcripts (IM). Due to the nature of IM technology, these are all dyadic conversations between Perverted Justice volunteers who posed as youth and adult solicitors. As such, our study generalises to those technologies that allow two individuals to engage in dyadic exchanges. The contributions of this research are significant and fivefold: (1) we delineate between grooming behaviour, predatory behaviour, and ofending behaviour; (2) we diferentiate between passive and controlling solicitor language; (3) we disentangle these behaviours from what the technology permits; (4) we extend the current understanding of cybersexual abuse to show grooming as only one aspect of a phenomenon that also includes predation and ofence; and (5) we identify how these results are important for academics and software developers. The outcomes result in a theory of control within the cybersexual abuse of youth.

We proceed by first presenting the extant literature regarding online grooming of youth. Next, we ofer the applied methodology and results. A discussion of the findings, implications and limitations follows, wrapping up with a conclusion.

## 2. Literature review

The act of adults soliciting youth for sexual purposes is not a new phenomenon. It has roots in societies long before the advent of the Internet. Statistics show that in most sexual assault cases the perpetrator is someone the youth knows like a family member or friend. However, social media technologies have opened up the world for adult solicitors and provide a medium through which they can transition from strangers of youth to trusted people and advisors, to solicitors. Unfortunately, we do not know exactly how they are using social media to make this transition. In order to sensitise ourselves to the concepts within the literature, we conducted a non-committal review of the extant literature (Hekkala & Urquhart, 2013; Urquhart & Fernández, 2013). “A noncommittal literature review allows the researcher to look at concepts in the area without imposing those concepts on the data” (Hekkala & Urquhart, 2013, p. 77). From this review, we identified three themes in the literature regarding the online solicitation of youth: solicitor and child interaction within social media, solicitor-victim discourse, and the behaviours and processes of online adult solicitors. We present the findings for these themes in the following sections.

## 2.1. Solicitor and child interaction in social media

Individuals who perpetrate sex crimes have been operating within society for generations. In 1937, J. Edgar Hoover’s War on the Sex Criminal was published in the New York Herald-Tribune. He was quoted as saying, “[t]he sex fiend, most loathsome of all the vast army of crime, has become a sinister threat to the safety of American childhood and womanhood” (Frosch & Bromberg, 1939, p. 761). This statement opened the door for expanded research into sex crimes and the individuals who perpetuate them. Sex ofences are both criminal and public health problems and sexual victimisation can have both long-term and traumatic efects (Robertiello & Terry, 2007). Within the online environment, sexual predators are typically adult sex ofenders, their predatory behaviours revolve around seduction, and the victims are underage teenagers (Wolak et al., 2008).

The Internet “provides a new context where curious and rebellious minors can be seduced and manipulated” (Berson, 2003, p. 13) through the solicitors’ skills at establishing trust with a child and then deceiving him/her “with charm and feigned afection” (Berson, 2003, p. 12). This view of the Internet paints a picture of a virtual world ripe with the opportunities for solicitors to groom children. Berson (2003) refers to grooming as the reduction of a child’s inhibitions “through active engagement, desensitization, power and control” (p. 11). Shannon (2008), in his research to understand the specific methods used by adults to groom children both online and ofline, examined police reports. The analysis assists in understanding what is included in online victimisation, which is important for the current study. Elements of online contact such as persuading the “victim to send sexual pictures” or using “blackmail against the victim” supports the notion that some type of coercion exists within these types of interactions (Shannon, 2008, p. 170).

While Berson (2003) and Shannon (2008) discussed the acts of individuals, Quayle and Taylor (2011) emphasised the importance of examining the environment in which the crimes are taking place. They presented the social elements of online social networking that afect sexual predators. Through online social interaction with other predators, sex ofenders are able to justify their ideology through “involvement in deviance through a rejection of larger social norms” (Quayle & Taylor, 2011, p. 47). So, while predators reject the social norm of boundaries between children and adults, children are posting personal information online, talking with strangers and adding those strangers to their buddy lists (J Wolak & Ybarra, 2008), making themselves potential targets for predators.

## 2.2. Solicitor-victim discourse

Due to the open context of the Internet, children can be sexually exploited and victimised. “Without much efort, a child may inadvertently or deliberately be exposed to online content that is obscene, pornographic, violent, racist, or otherwise ofensive” (Berson, 2003). Online sexual predators take advantage of the mechanism of social media and the vulnerability of children. Online sexual predators commonly use grooming, “developing a bond as a precursor to abuse” (Quayle & Taylor, 2011, p. 46). This sets the stage for a definition of an online groomer as “someone who has initiated online contact with a child with the intention of establishing a sexual relationship involving cybersex or sex with physical contact” (Quayle & Taylor, 2011, p. 46).

Shannon (2008), in a study of 315 Swedish police reports of “sexual ofences against persons under 18 years of age where the perpetrator and the victim had been in contact with one another online” (p. 164), identified four categories of cases: (a) predator/victim online contact only, (b) predator/victim online contact with proof that an ofline illegal sex act was committed, (c) predator/victim online and ofline contact with no proof of an illegal sex act, (d) instances when the predator/victim knew each other ofline and the Internet was used to exploit the child for sex. These categories give insight into the types of interactions that occur between online sexual predators and potential child victims. For the cases when an illegal sex act was committed, some predators made promises to get the child modelling work or ofered to pay for sexual services (Shannon, 2008). Elements of online contact such as persuading a “victim to send sexual pictures” (Shannon, 2008, p. 170) or using “blackmail against the victim” (Shannon, 2008, p. 170) demonstrates that some type of coercion exists within these types of interactions.

Researchers have also investigated the identification and understanding of online predator characteristics. Elliott, Beech, Mandeville-Norden, and Hayes’s (2009) study afirmed diferences between online and ofline (contact) predators. They revealed that Internet ofenders are less likely to be repeat ofenders or escalate to ofline sexual abuse. Also, ofenders scored higher on empathic concerns than contact sex ofenders and are able to relate to fictional characters, which leads to potential success in psychotherapy. Another aspect of online predator behaviour manifests in the types of websites predators frequent. K. J. Mitchell et al. (2008) and Wolak et al. (2008), in their studies, found that instant messaging services, dyadic and dialogic by design, were the choice online medium for predator use in solicitations of children rather than online journals, blogs and social networking sites. In our current study, we expand upon this finding through examining dyadic instant message conversations between online sexual predators and potential victims.

Research shows how children are vulnerable to the threat of online predators. Studies revealed that children are sending personal information to people they meet online (K. J. Mitchell et al., 2007), posting personal information in blogs (K. J. Mitchell et al., 2008) and meeting adult ofenders in chat rooms (Wolak et al., 2004). This research helps us understand how children’s online behaviours may make them susceptible to online solicitation.

While the extant literature presents insights regarding predator and potential victim online behaviours, Quayle and Taylor (2011) noted that empirical research in regarding online grooming and/or solicitation is sparse and most existing research has focused on the behaviours of youth, not on the adult sex ofenders. Thankfully, since that statement, there has been an increase in computer discipline studies on online grooming (al-Khateeb & Epiphaniou, 2016; Dorasamy et al., 2018; Quayle et al., 2014). However, the research IS behavioural research is still relatively scant. Our study addresses this gap through a focus on the behaviours of online adult solicitors within the predator-victim discourse. We examine how solicitors are able to dominate and manipulate youth through interpersonal relationships within social media, and we develop a theory of control within the cybersexual abuse of youth. The next section outlines the methodology used in this study.

## 3. Research methodology

In this study, we applied an evolved grounded theory methodology based on Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998). This is the appropriate methodology for use in answering the research question as it addresses “the questions of who, when, where, why, how, and with what consequences, analysts are able to relate structure with process” (Strauss et al., 1998, p. 127). The aim of grounded theory research is to derive theory from actual data rather than force-fitting data to a priori theory and hypotheses (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Corbin & Strauss, 2008). “Researchers who claim to follow the evolved approach of Strauss & Corbin (1990, 1998) tend to be more flexible on the use of a priori theory and initial literature review in the substantive area of study” (Matavire & Brown, 2013, p. 122). We chose an evolved grounded theory methodology because it provides a mechanism to study the elements of the phenomenon via the interpretation of text.

We applied interpretive coding techniques to the data, which revealed the constructs that then answered the research question. By formulating theory within this approach, the theory is so intimately tied to the data, the resultant theory is likely consistent with empirical observation (Eisenhardt, 1989). Following Strauss and Corbin’s (1990) recommendations, we paid attention to theoretical relevance, purpose, similarities, and diferences across data sources with regard to appropriateness of the data sources. Through the application of open, axial, and selective coding phases (Strauss & Corbin 1990, 1998; Orlikowski, 1993) we aligned with a Straussian method of grounded theory and attempted to “let the data speak before imposing a conceptual framework upon it, and to let the theory emerge from the data and become gradually refined through constant comparison of the data among themselves and with the emergent coding and existing theories” (Vaast & Walsham, 2013, p. 11). The goal of this research was to further open the black box of online solicitations of youth. We sought to “advance theory by building conceptual explanations of the phenomena and processes” (Vaast & Walsham, 2013, p. 21) in which online solicitors engage. We analysed all 90 transcripts even if theoretical saturation was reached, in order to also discuss pervasiveness of observed behaviours (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Corbin & Strauss, 2008). We combined emergent concepts into categories and constructs that were integrated through coding-for-process (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Corbin & Strauss, 2008) to build the proposed theory to address how adults can use instant messaging to solicit youth. In the remainder of this section, we first discuss the data set, including the contentious opinions about it. We then provide details of the data collection, and the coding process used in the analysis.

## 3.1. Data set background

The data set is composed of conversations by online solicitors in the United States (US) who are therefore bound by the laws there. As described previously, Perverted Justice captured these online conversations via an instant messaging application. According to the US Department of Justice, Internet crimes against children and youth include both “[e]nticing them through online contact for the purpose of engaging them in sexual acts” and “[u]sing the Internet to expose youth to child pornography and encourage them to exchange pornography” (Gillis, 2001, p. 2). Perverted Justice took two specific steps to ensure that the evidence they provided stood up in court: (a) all volunteers went through training to learn how to efectively interact in chat-rooms to identify potential online ofenders, and (b) the organisation created their own software to capture solid evidence against ofenders. The general process of a volunteer was to (1) enter a social media chat-room and present themselves as a 13–14 year old, (2) wait for a potential online ofender to make one-on-one contact using an instant messenger type software like AOL messenger, (3) engage in online conversations with the potential ofender, (4) conduct a verification phone call with the potential ofender, (5) continue dyadic conversations with the individual until which time the potential ofender engaged in an online solicitation crime or was no longer interested in talking, and (6) turn alleged online sex ofenders over to law enforcement (Perverted Justice, 2016). Because the crime takes place in textual communication between one online solicitor and one Perverted Justice volunteer, in the current study we draw a boundary, in our theorising, around the technological artefact of social media to include only those types that provide a mechanism for dyadic conversations via instant messaging. As previously noted, these types of software are now considered to be commonplace applications in the lives of teenagers and their social media use (Read, 2016).

## 3.2. Critical discussion of the data set

We acknowledge that the data set is not without controversy. There are two main critiques. The first is that Perverted Justice’s focus on stranger attacks shrouds the realities that most sex ofenders victimise people they know. Some have argued that groups like Perverted Justice display an unrealistic side of the crime, stating that they “are not representative of ‘typical’ child sexual abuse cases in which children are victimized by someone they know and trust” (Fox, 2006, para. 22). Although we understand this perspective, and believe investigations are needed into how social media may be used by sexual abusers with children they know, we do not believe this precludes the importance of conducting research that might prevent stranger assaults. As we have argued, the pervasive use of anonymous, dyadic communication tools has dramatically altered the nature and prevalence of this type of threat to youth.

The second critique is that Perverted Justice volunteers engaged, wrongly, in entrapment. The controversy focuses on the methods employed; adults posed as children in online venues in eforts to catch online sex ofenders. Brad Russ, Northern New England’s former commander of the Internet Crimes Against Children Task Force (ICAC) was noted as saying “[i] t’s a mistake for law enforcement to abdicate its responsibility to citizens” (Cook, 2006, para. 34). He believed that law enforcement should take care of the problem without the help of plain citizens. As noted below, Perverted Justice members dispute this entrapment accusation.

Addressing their methods, Perverted Justice members responded directly to critics’ complaint of entrapment. According to the Ofice of the United States Attorneys, “[a] valid entrapment defense has two related elements: (1) government inducement of the crime, and (2) the defendant’s lack of predisposition to engage in the criminal conduct” (Ofice of the United States Attorneys, 2016). Perverted Justice volunteers were not government oficials. They did not say or do anything to entice ofenders; they waited in online chat rooms to see if individuals would contact them: and, they reveal their (fake) age early on in discussions. The transcripts we reviewed confirm this. Additionally, the individuals convicted of these online solicitation crimes all gave their phone numbers to Perverted Justice volunteers for verification, and willingly went to a house thinking they were going to meet an underage individual (Frequently Asked Questions, 2008). The Perverted Justice group members are not the only ones to come to the defence of their actions. Detective Mike Burns from Ohio stated plainly, “None of us hesitate to draw information from a druggie out on the street or a convicted felon. But everyone wants to turn their heads when it comes to an agency that’s really trying to help” (Garrett, 2007, para. 51). Florida Assistant State Attorney Stacey Honowitz clearly stated that these individuals ultimately could be arrested and charged because “[t] hey’re coming to the house to engage in a sexual act. So, it’s solicitation. It’s enticing, encouraging a minor to commit sex via the Internet” (Scarborough, 2006).

The transcripts used in this project were publicly available on the Internet. No interaction occurred between the researchers and the individuals in the transcripts. However, this still leaves the question of engaging individuals’ expectations of privacy within the exchanges exposed in the transcripts. As Palen and Dourish (2003) noted “[a]s a dynamic process, privacy is understood to be under continuous negotiation and management, with the boundary that distinguishes privacy and publicity refined according to circumstance” (p. 129). The circumstances that created the examined transcripts demonstrate how these dynamics change the state of privacy within an online relationship. Dynamical privacy isn’t new to online social networking research, though. In their study of users of Facebook and MySpace, Dwyer et al. (2007) found that “online relationships can develop in sites where perceived trust is low and protection of privacy is minimal” (p. 7). In the case of online sex ofenders, it could be argued that their strong desire for privacy is rooted in not wanting to be caught soliciting minors for sexual interaction. When interacting with anyone in a social setting, whether online or in person, the expectation of privacy is tenuous. One can hope that the information shared with another person is kept private, but it is almost impossible to ethically control the actions of others. As such, we believe that it was unreasonable for the individuals engaged in the instant messenger conversations studied to expect absolute privacy. In fact, in several of the transcripts, individuals conversing with Perverted Justice workers named concerns about being caught by either police or parents; they were aware of the potential of losing privacy.

## 3.3. Data set selection

After reviewing the controversy surrounding the data set and potential privacy concerns, we grounded the decision to use it on three facts. First, individuals identified through the actions of Perverted Justice were brought to trial and, as evidenced in the National Sex Ofender Registry, the charges were upheld in a court of law. The US Court acknowledged the unlawfulness of the context of these transcripts and actions of the offenders, which provides additional validation for their use within the current research. While the ofenders were, in fact, conversing with Perverted Justice volunteers, it was legitimised that their words and actions proved that they believed they were talking to youth. Second, as previously noted, other research on the subject of conversations between online adult solicitors and children/youth has been successfully conducted utilising this data set (Kontostathis et al., 2010; Laorden et al., 2013; Thom et al., 2011). These studies provide a sample of the viability of the data set to predict online adult solicitor behaviour. Lastly, due to the public availability of the data and our use of aliases for the adult solicitors involved in the instant messenger conversations, the research protocol was designated exempt from further IRB review according to the US. federal regulations on human subjects research as allowed in 45 CFR 46.101 (b) (4) by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the lead author’s university. Thus, we believe the use of this data is appropriate both in terms of its collection, veracity and relevance to the objectives of the research.

## 3.4. Data collection

To obtain this subset we downloaded all of the transcripts from the Perverted Justice website. At the time of the initial data capture, the downloaded number was approximately 500 transcripts. These transcripts were initially downloaded as pdf files for storage in a non-editable format to prevent data corruption of the original files. Each transcript was then transformed into a text file and imported into its own spreadsheet for further analysis. The screen name of each adult solicitor was typed into an Excel spreadsheet along with the identified real-life name of the individual. We then randomised the list of screen names and chose the top 90 as a starting point with the decision to code more transcripts if we did not reach theoretical saturation within the initial data set. We conducted this method of common random sampling for “two reasons: first, it enables maximum ‘fit’ of the theory by keeping it up-to-date with changing circumstances; and second, it facilitates the extension of the theory’s substantive limits” (Urquhart et al., 2010, p. 372). If a real-life name appeared in the randomised list more than one time, we chose only the first screen name that appeared for that name and to ensure that for each adult we examined only one transcript for each adult solicitor. The purpose of this was to minimise the risk that we would be observing the same behaviour executed by an individual in diferent conversations. We wanted to ensure that we observed a variety of adult solicitor behaviours.

A single researcher conducted initial coding. Both researchers conducted explication and refinement of identified categorical elements. The unit of analysis in this study is a single-typed transcript composed of the dyadic conversations between each online adult solicitor and a Perverted Justice volunteer. Each transcript encompasses all of the online conversations between the participants from the first dyadic contact to the final online contact prior to the adult solicitor’s arrest. The conversations within each transcript can be viewed in days, hours, and number of lines of text. The number of contact hours ranged from a quarter of an hour to 34.25 hours with an average transcript duration of 8 hours. Contact days (the number of diferent days during which they engaged in a dyadic conversation) ranged from 1 to 28 with an average of 7 days. As an additional note, 81% of the 90 adult solicitors studied made contact with the Perverted Justice volunteer on more than one day. We reduced each transcript to the granularity level of lines of text (Birks et al., 2013). Overall, the number of lines of text within the individual transcripts averaged 1,133 with a range of 80 to 4,426.

Examining the individuals who engaged in the transcripts, the average age of the adult solicitors was 33 years old, demonstrating that these individuals were old enough to understand that sexual contact with a minor is illegal. The Perverted Justice volunteers presented as an average age of 13 years old, situating themselves within the target age range of youth. Additionally, it is important to note that all 90 online adult solicitors were male, of which only 7 were seeking sexual contact with a male youth. Over 90% of the adult solicitors were in search of a female victim. As such, throughout the remainder of this article, we use male pronouns to refer to the adult solicitors and female pronouns to refer to the potential victims. ## 3.5. Data analysis

In this section, we describe how the data was analysed and provide precursors to the results of the analysis described in section four. First, given that we are situating this work within the IS field, we discuss the technological aspects. Then, we detail how we engaged in evolved grounded theory through the open, axial, selective, and coding for process, explaining how we scaled up (Urquhart et al., 2010) to our final theory.

## 3.5.1. About the technology

Adult solicitors met the Perverted Justice volunteers in open chat rooms. They then sent a private message to the volunteer to engage in a dyadic conversation. As such, instant messaging was the most commonly used technological communication tool in the studied transcripts. This type of technology allows for conversations that may or may not be saved/logged depending on the settings chosen by the user on his/her local computer. Trust in an online environment can be defined as “the willingness of the trustor to rely on a trustee to do what is promised in a given context, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control the trustee, and even though negative consequences may occur” (Aljazzaf et al., 2010). As such, not being able to prevent the other party from logging the messages adds another layer to the idea of trust in the conversational partner. While the children, ofenders, and the technological artefact are separate entities, “possibilities for action emerge from the reciprocal interaction between actor and artifact” (Faraj et al., 2011, p. 1233). The language structures people use within instant messages add a level of dificulty to the analysis. Chiluwa (2012) explains that a real-time synchronous platform of interaction “tends to be more unpredictable, due to the fact that it more frequently illustrates features of oral language” (p. 226). ## 3.5.3. Axial coding

In this step, we arranged all codes into categories based upon their relation to each other, leading to the discovery of themes (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Once we identified initial codes during open coding, we met in person to conduct axial coding and discuss the codes and memos that resulted from the open coding step. During this process, we merged the data into axial codes (categorical groupings), and identified themes based upon common elements of the codes. We further compared sample data from each open code to those of other codes to determine similarities and diferences. We grouped similar codes together into broader axial codes. For example, we determined that when an adult solicitor was asking about a child’s location, availability to meet, and willingness to meet, these could collectively be referred to as assess meeting potential. In total, eleven axial codes emerged from the original 42 open codes. Those codes included: assess meeting potential, assess potential victim (PV) relationships, attractiveness assessment of PV, domination, enticement, sexual fantasising, random negativity, negative increased predator attractiveness, positive increased predator attractiveness, substance use assessment of PV, and sexuality assessment of PV. We present the original 42 codes mapped to these eleven codes in Appendix B. During this step, we began to see how adult solicitors utilise text to seduce. Revealed within the text was the story of complex relationships. Ofender personalities and patterns difered, such as those who focused on being friends with the youth versus those who remained aloof and pushed the youth to meet face-to-face.

## 3.5.4. Selective coding

After determining axial codes, we met in person to revisit the data and determine the selective codes. We further refined the categories in the selective coding process. In this step of the process, we looked for gaps in the logic of the categories and relationships (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). These gaps emerged in the data itself, and in comparison, to the categories of extant literature. We strengthened weak categories and reconstructed excessive categories. This process served to validate the scheme (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Although these categories had been a concern in extant literature, our data did not support this finding. Ultimately, through the coding process, we revealed four main categories and related subcategories of online solicitor actions that are presented in the results section. These categories and subcategories provided the outline for the results narrative and allowed us to compare the findings to extant literature regarding online sexual grooming.

## 3.5.5. Theoretical coding

The next sections explain our findings resulting from our coding for process, also referred to as theoretical coding. According to Strauss & Corbin (1990), this is “the investigation of causes, contexts, contingencies, consequences, covariances, and conditions” (p. 153). Though we considered this during axial and selective coding, creating related memos, we did not code explicitly for these categories. Rather, the original open codes emerged from the data itself, were arranged into categories during axial coding and refined in selective coding. The final act of an evolved grounded theory method, theoretical coding, involves engaging in a deeper comparison of the selective codes to the extant literature and scaling up the constructs to a more formal theory, referred to by Gregor (2006) as a theory for explaining. This type of theory is substantive and generalisable explaining “how and why things happened in some particular real-world situation” (Gregor, 2006, p. 624). Specifically, this type of theory is an appropriate outcome for behavioural research conducted using the evolved grounded theory methodology (Urquhart et al., 2010). We generalise to those technologies that allow dyadic communication between individuals.

In order to scale up (Urquhart et al., 2010) from a typology to a theory, we must be cognisant of the relationships between our constructs and explain “how, when, where, and why events occur” (Gregor, 2006, p. 624). The aspects of when, where, and why have been answered by the data in the open, axial, and selective coding steps: when the events occur are random days and times that can vary in the number of days and times of contact; where begins in a chat room and moves to a dyadic conversational technology such as instant messengers, and why is for the gratification of the adult solicitor. Our resultant theoretical model best represents the phenomenon exemplified within the data and explains the “how” of control within the cybersexual abuse of youth. We share the outcomes of our theoretical coding in the discussion section.

## 4.3. Control

We begin with control in order to establish a baseline for understanding the communication nuances of “how” behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofending occur. We found it challenging to draw finite boundaries around behaviours that can be considered control. However, the text revealed concrete references to identify control. For the purpose of this study, we determined that control occurs when there is an attempt to “induce or pressure people to do things they would not freely do” (Deci & Ryan, 1987, p. 1026). Thus, the behaviour expressed through the text within the dyadic conversation between the online sex ofender and the potential victim becomes an “external event that restricts self-determination” (Deci & Ryan, 1987, p. 1026) of the potential victim. We uncovered “structural forms that compel obedience” in the actions and words within the conversations (Lehmann et al., 2012, p. 915).

In this study control is not a stand-alone behaviour. Rather, aspects of control are weaved through the three behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofending. Our results reveal that online solicitors exert control over youth through two types of language within the behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofending within the social media text: passive language and controlling language. Passive language appears to give control to the potential victim. For example, the solicitor may ask the child what they would like to do, when they would like to do it, etc. In contrast, controlling language removes the youth’s options. The solicitor may tell the potential victim that he is going to drive by the house, when the potential victim needs to be online to chat, or what the potential victim will wear when they meet. The nuances between the text of passive versus controlling language helps distinguish between a casual conversation, say between teenagers, and the potentially cybersexual advances of an online solicitor.

In the following sections, we explain the categories of actions by the solicitors that form the behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofending. Within each behaviour, we distinguish between passive language and controlling language. The importance of these nuanced diferences in wording are rooted in the ability to distinguish between an innocuous conversation and one that is moving towards cybersexual abuse of youth. Additionally, we discuss how solicitors move between the behaviours, and technology’s role in the phenomenon.

## 4.4.1. Assessment

The act of assessment involves the scanning of one’s situation to draw conclusions regarding a point of interest. We identified two subcategories of assessment emerged from the data: environment and personal attributes. We found that online adult solicitors assessed three aspects of a potential victims’ environment: where they lived, whom they lived with and whom they were close to within their circle. Additionally, they were concerned with three personal attributes: what they looked like, if they were willing to meet face-to-face and if they were available to meet face-to-face. We provide examples of each of these assessments in Table 5 and discussed them further following the table.

As noted, the three aspects of a youth’s environment important to the online adult solicitor were location, family and relationships. We identified that when assessing the youth’s environment, the online solicitors were not only curious about where the youth lived, but also who they lived with, if they were ever allowed out on their own, if their parents/grandparents were strict, etc. These actions are all considered passive because they do not attempt to force the potential victim into any action/reaction. Solicitors used these questions to gather information to determine vulnerability level of the potential victim.

Additionally, we found that online sex ofenders inquiries regarding youths’ personal attributes ranged from a discussion of height and weight to eye and hair colour as well as breast size and the amount and location of pubic hair on the youth. Availability and willingness refer to the youth’s inclination to meet in person an individual who she initially met online. On one level, the online solicitor is curious as to whether the youth is even willing to meet. If it is established that yes, in fact, she would meet in person then the ofender questions her about her availability. It should be noted that we coded this as a non-sexual inquiry. In these cases, the online solicitors suggested meeting to grab a bite to eat, go to a movie and/or just hang out. We identified the language of personal attribute assessments as both passive and controlling. For physical appearance, passive language attempts to determine what the potential victim looks like. Controlling physical appearance includes language attempting to control the potential victim’s appearance. The passive language of willingness asks the potential victim about meeting, giving them an option. The controlling language of willingness attempts to force a meeting. Similarly, the passive language of availability questions when the potential victim can meet or chat whereas the controlling language asserts when contact will be made. The age diference actions refer to the solicitor using the age diference as a means of pressure to get a reaction from the potential victim. These reactions are responses like “I’m not a baby” or “I’m not too young”.

## 4.4.2. Enticements

The objects of enticement online adult solicitors use refer to the inducements put forth to compel the youth into a sexual relationship. In the transcripts, we found that the online solicitors used force minimally in the observed cases. There were only 38 combined nota tions of bargaining, peer pressure, challenging, daring, threatening, and/or using insensitivity and only 68 mentions of alcohol, cigarettes or drugs occurred. Considering we analysed over one hundred thousand lines of text, these numbers are very low. Instead, we found that solicitors employed six diferent methods of enticement. Three of these methods focused on the youth and included compliments or making the youth feel that they had power and choice. Compliments and illusion of power are passive language as they are positive towards the potential victim or allow them to think they have some control in the situation. Some solicitors enticed their potential victims by focusing on them. They gave the potential victims illusions of power through compliments – flirting with them and making statements about them being smart, pretty, or mature for their age. Additionally, this illusion of power included the idea of illusion of control – pre senting a false security for the youth through indication that they were in charge. Solicitors portrayed themselves as being willing to be controlled by the youth when they met, only engaging in behaviours of which she approved. They also ofered a willingness to stop if asked to do so by the youth. These behaviours could serve to draw the youth deeper into an online relationship. Adult solicitors used the language of positive reinforcement and being trusted to build up a youth’s self-esteem, figuratively bringing her up to the adult level of the online solicitor. She could then feel a stronger sense of belonging with that individual and be more inclined to follow his lead. Feigned afection takes on a more controlling language because it attempts to impose a relationship or emotion onto the potential victim. It is the nuanced diference between asking someone if they love you and declaring love for them with an unspoken expectation of getting something in return.

In contrast, online solicitors pointed out their own strengths or exposed their own vulnerabilities as tactics to entice youth. Non-sexual vulnerabilities were both passive and controlling as solicitors attempted to gain the sympathy of the potential victims or once again tried to impose a weakness of emotional need. Some enacted self-deprecating, self-pitying and insecure behaviours. One interpretation of these behaviours is that they were meant to play on her emotions, get her to feel sorry for him and encourage nurturing feelings within her to arise. These solicitors positioned themselves in need of care, creating the space for youth to fill the role of a caretaker. Another method used by online solicitors to entice children was to highlight their own perceived strengths. Non-sexual strengths were passive as they were aimed at making the solicitor appealing to the potential victim. Part of the strategy for some solicitors was to make themselves desirable to youth. Ideas of how and which strengths to highlight varied. For some solicitors, this was accomplished through showing kindness and generosity as strengths. One example is the solicitor ofering relationship advice with boyfriends or parents. These techniques positioned the solicitors as desirable through a positive lens. They became people the youth could turn to, look up to and trust. Other ofenders attempted to entice youth through bragging behaviours that adults would likely identify as being egotistical. Some solicitors talked about how great they were at their job, that no one was better than them. Although adults would likely classify such behaviour as obnoxious, it was obvious that the ofenders thought such overt arrogance would be appealing to youth.

The last category, relationship claim, spans both the solicitor and potential victim perspectives and can be both passive and controlling. In passive language, the solicitor attempts to get the potential victim to ofer the idea of a relationships between them. He might do this through asking the potential victim if she would like to be boyfriend/girlfriend, or if she would like to get married. Conversely, in controlling language the solicitor possessively declares a relationship between the two saying such things as “you’re my girlfriend now” or “we will get married”. ## 4.5. Predation

Predation is enacted through self-preservation. Online solicitors engage in acts of self-preservation through three methods: expressing concern for the youth, expressing concern for themselves, and secrecy. Concern for the youth included expressions of protection, of not wanting her to get caught and/or get in trouble. In contrast, concern for self-included instructions for the youth to delete online messages, directly asking them if they are with law enforcement, and explicitly stating how much trouble he could get into if law enforcement found out he was soliciting a youth. Online solicitors used these techniques to determine their level of safety. The language used in these actions is passive as they attempt to elicit compliance rather than impose it.

Secrecy actions involve the solicitor telling the potential victim to turn of chat logs, delete messages, throw away phone numbers etc. so that no one finds out they are communicating, what they are communicating or that they have plans to meet. Some solicitors even walk the potential through manipulating the software on their computer so that it does not save any of their conversations. They communicate all of these actions with controlling language. Table 7 includes examples of the subcategories of behaviours identified within the category of self-preservation.

## 4.6. Ofending

Ofending behaviours are where the adult solicitors behaviour becomes illegal. Within our studied phenomenon, this occurs when the dyadic conversation becomes sexualised. Sexual solicitations are defined as “online interactions with minors for sexual purposes, including plans to meet ofline” (Seto, 2012, p. 1). It is important to note that law enforcement recognises two types of adult solicitors: contact-driven solicitors who have the goal of a face-to-face meeting with the youth, and fantasy-driven solicitors who experience sexual gratification through the online contact only. Both groups engage in illegal acts and, if caught, will be prosecuted for Internet sex crimes (Seto, 2012). Eighty-eight out of the 90 online adult solicitors whose transcripts we reviewed engaged in cybersexploitation. We add to the extant literature the important category of live webcam. This subcategory brings a distinct behavioural pattern to the understanding of mechanisms used by online sex ofenders within social media.

## 5. Discussion

As previously stated, in order to understand the phenomenon of study, we gathered literature from a variety of fields. We executed searches in Google Scholar, PsychINFO, Ebsco, Academic Search Complete, and the AIS Digital Library. Search terms included “online grooming”, “online sexual predator”, “online sex ofender”, “online solicitation”, “online child solicitation”, and a general search for “grooming”. Only those articles within the realm of the phenomenon were studied. Any that talked of grooming positively, such as grooming athletes, mentees, employees, etc. were excluded. Additionally, we referenced articles that focused on the face-to-face grooming/predation of children only to gain an understanding of the psychological impact these behaviours have on children. We identified similarities and diferences between our results and the extant literature both regarding the content of our findings, and problems within the extant literature that have impeded the development of theory of the cybersexual abuse of youth. We discuss these in the following sections, along with the aspects of technology that allow dyadic communication.

## 5.1. Findings compared to extant literature

Regarding the content characteristic of assessment we found that, in alignment with O’Connell (2003),

Williams et al. (2013), and Lorenzo-Dus et al. (2016) online solicitors do assess the youths’ environment, seeking to identify where they live, who they live with and how strong their relationships are with those around them. O’Connell (2003) identified six patterns of adult solicitor behaviour, of which the first two are like the assessment stage of our results. O’Connell’s first two stages are: (1) the friendship forming stage: non-threatening conversations with a “get to know you” message and, (2) the relationship forming stage: solicitor works to become a child’s best friend, learn about her family life, school, etc. For Williams et al. (2013) assessment includes the sub-themes of assessment of the child and assessment of the environment. Assessment of the child encompasses analysis of the child’s trust level, vulnerability and receptiveness to interaction. Assessment of the environment involves analysis of obstacles, opportunities and information that could impact the grooming process and hinder secrecy (Williams et al., 2013). Lorenzo-Dus et al. (2016) included the exchange of personal information in a category named deceptive trust development. However, this is misleading even by the authors’ own definitions. They define deceptive trust as hiding the true goal of the communication, but in the subcategory of exchange of personal information note that the solicitors are sharing real names, addresses, phone numbers, etc. They also have a category called compliance testing in which the solicitors attempt to ensure they are talking to a minor and how likely the minor is to engage in sexual activity (Lorenzo-Dus et al., 2016). Through our analysis, we were able to dissect assessment further than what has been done in the extant literature and determined that online soli citors assess youths’ physical attributes of appearance, willingness and availability to meet face-to-face in a non-sexual encounter.

Concerning the content characteristic of enticements our findings relate to O’Connell’s (2003) discussion of the friendship forming, and relationship forming stages. Many online solicitors present the façade of desire to be a friend to their potential victims and draw them in with compliments and the guise of the illusion of power. The results of our analysis build upon O’Connell’s (2003) findings. They also align with Williams et al.’s (2013) findings of positivity and negativity, and Lorenzo-Dus et al. (2016) subcategories of strategic withdrawal, sociability, praise, activities, and relationship. Williams et al. (2013) describe this negativity as “negative and impatient traits” (p. 143) that would sometimes show up as “a passive-aggressive approach to achieve a response from the child” (p. 143). For Lorenzo-Dus et al. (2016) these behaviours were part of deceptive trust development (relationship, activities, praise, and sociability) as well as compliance testing (strategic withdrawal). They all revolved around drawing the youth into a belief of a relationship in which they were in control. We extend the extant literature by further breaking down the negative and positive actions to examine each as specifically enacted by youth and solicitor. Engaging in this decomposition allows the detection of more specific patterns of behaviour within the text. Also to note, we refrain from presenting them as “stages” due to the fact that the conversations we analysed did not demonstrate a linear progression of topics or behaviours.

As to the content characteristic of ofending, we found that adult solicitors conducted a sexuality assessment of the youth. This was in contrast to O’Connell (2003), Williams et al. (2013), and Lorenzo-Dus et al. (2016). As far as cybersexploitation, what we found aligns with what O’Connell (2003) referred to in her fifth and six stages: (5) sexual stage: amplifying the intensity of the conversation and bringing sexual content into the conversation, and (6) cybersexploitation or fantasy enactment stage, in which the ultimate goal is sexual gratification. She outlines three variations of cybersexploitation: fantasy enactment based on perception of mutuality, fantasy enactment using overt coercion counterbalanced with intimacy and a cyberrape fantasy enactment involving overt coercion, control and aggression (O’Connell, 2003). O’Connell (2003) refers to this cybersexploitation or fantasy enactment as the acts of solicitors who “fluctuate between inviting and emotionally blackmailing a child into engaging in cyber sex” (O’Connell, 2003, p. 9). Williams et al. (2103) refer to this as the “sexual content” of the conversation. In their discussions of both the introduction and maintenance/escalation of sexual content, they mention force as a finding. The findings from our data set did not support solicitors use of blackmail or force when attempting to persuade a youth to engage in online sexual activities. If the solicitor pushed a youth who did not want to engage, often the solicitor would just leave the conversation.

It is important to note that our delineation of grooming, predation, and ofending is not an implication that the danger of the phenomenon resides only in the illegality of certain behaviours. Youth who have experienced grooming, predation, and/or ofending are susceptible to many negative psychological and relational outcomes. Those outcomes can include shame, embarrassment, dificult relationships with family, loss of confidence, and even self-harm (Whittle et al., 2013b). Additionally, the illegality of ofending is not limited to ofender attempting to meet the youth face-to-face. Online actions that may also lead to legal action against an ofender include exploitation of youth through the request of sexual photos, sending pornography to the youth (as noted in the ofending results), or even blackmailing the youth through threats to expose the relationship to their parents/guardians (J. Davidson & Gottschalk, 2011;

J. Davidson & Martellozzo, 2008a). Our findings support the fact that the cybersexual abuse of youth is a complex phenomenon that requires continued study and collaboration between researchers, practitioners, and law enforcement.

However, our comparison to extant literature revealed four problems within the literature that hindered efective development of a theory of cybersexual abuse of youth prior to our study. There was a lack of agreement regarding the definition of grooming. Adult solicitors were not labelled consistently within the literature. The line between the behaviours of the solicitors and the aspects of the technology are blurry. Lastly, the significance of control is not emphasised. Each of these hindrances is discussed further in the next section.

## 5.2. Problems hindering theory development

First, researchers lacked agreement on what constitutes grooming behaviour. By a strict dictionary definition, to groom is to prepare an individual for a position (dictionary.com). So, thinking in terms of sexual grooming, this would include behaviours leading up to, but not encompassing actual sexual solicitation. As such, studies that include the sexual aspects of the phenomenon in the definition of grooming (Eneman, 2010; Eneman et al., 2010; Wachs et al., 2012) may be blurring the lines between online conversations and sexual crime. Our findings bound grooming to those actions by solicitors that attempt to prepare youth for cybersexual abuse.

Second, researchers inconsistently labelled adults who solicit youth. Some studies refer to them as sexual predators (Jones et al., 2013; Livingstone & Smith, 2014; Lorenzo-Dus et al., 2016; McGhee et al., 2011; Nair, 2006) and others as sexual ofenders (Craven et al., 2007; Pranoto et al., 2015; Wachs et al., 2012; Williams et al., 2013) – both of whom are said to engage in grooming behaviours. Again, by dictionary definition, a predatory person is one who is “acting with or possessed by overbearing, rapacious, or selfish motives” (dictionary.com). Thus, a sexual predator would engage in solicitations for their own gratification. This still leaves the idea of a sexual ofender. To ofend is “to err in conduct; commit a sin, crime, or fault” (dictionary.com). The last includes the execution of a crime, which incorporates the sexual aspects of the phenomenon. The lack of clear definitions of the adults engaging in solicitations makes it dificult to determine where the behaviours of one group stop and another begins. Our bounding of actions by solicitors, predators, and ofenders as executed online provides a means to identify their diferences and potentially stop the interaction while it is still grooming, before it reaches predation or ofending.

Third, distinctions between the aspects of the technology that are used for the solicitation of youth and the actual behaviours of the adult solicitor were lacking. While it is important to understand what a technology permits a person to do, the actual behaviour in which the solicitor engages using the technology is what detection mechanisms attempt to identify. Thus, it is important to distinguish between the two. Our findings identified aspects of the technology that enable online solicitors to engage in four cybersexual abuse behaviours: grooming, predation, ofending, and control.

Lastly, and of great importance, in the extant literature, researchers do not parse out the significance of control within the cybersexual abuse phenomenon. Control is an important aspect of the phenomenon of cybersexual abuse of youth that we feel has not received the attention it deserves in the extant literature. Studies of domestic and sexual abuse have been conducted for decades and included the identification of control as an underlying motivation and action for most perpetrators (Johnson & Ferraro, 2000; Kelly & Westmarland, 2016; Yawn et al., 1992). In cases of domestic abuse perpetrated by men, they typically employ a range of tactics to control their partner and prevent them from ever leaving the relationship (Johnson, 2006; Johnson & Ferraro, 2000). In their work with domestically violent men, Kelly and Westmarland (2016) noted that one man said “There’s so many things in controlling behaviour, I mean there’s physical control, there’s mental control . . . I wanted things to be and to suit me (Gregory, Time 2)” (p. 23). Regarding sexual abuse, researchers have come to understand that perpetrators want to control an individual, often a weaker person (Ward et al., 1997). This holds across genders and same-sex rape where the perpetrator sees the potential victim as someone they “want to control, punish, and/or destroy, something they want to conquer and defeat” (Scarce, 1997, p. 172).

The importance of control in the phenomenon of cybersexual abuse of youth is twofold: (1) it aids in understanding how the adult solicitors move between friendship and solicitation within the behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofending, and (2) it aids in understanding the dyadic conversation applications that are part of dark social media. First, the importance of understanding how solicitors exert control allows us to identify what is really happening between adult solicitors and youth through the dyadic conversations within instant messenger solicitations – cybersexual abuse. The World Health Organisation (WHO) defines child sexual abuse as:

the involvement of a child in sexual activity that he or she does not fully comprehend, is unable to give informed consent to, or for which the child is not developmentally prepared and cannot give consent, or that violates the laws or social taboos of society. Child sexual abuse is evidenced by this activity between a child and an adult or another child who by age or development is in a relationship of responsibility, trust or power, the activity being intended to gratify or satisfy the needs of the other person. This may include but is not limited to:

\- the inducement or coercion of a child to engage in any unlawful sexual activity;

\- the exploitative use of a child in prostitution or other unlawful sexual practices;

\- the exploitative use of children in pornographic performance and materials.

(Chiesa & Goldson, 2017)

The behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofending executed by an adult with an individual whom they think is a minor exemplifies this definition provided by the WHO. The solicitors gain the trust of the youth, involve them in sexual content/activity that they are not developmentally prepared for and that violate laws and social taboos of the United States. The Centre for Disease Control (CDC) includes the phrasing of noncontact sexual activity in their definition and includes behaviours “such as exposing a child to sexual activity” (Murray et al., 2014, p. 321).

Understanding how adults solicit youth online requires an analysis of online behaviours that can identify the nuances of communication allowed by the technology through which control can be exerted. Extrapolating these controlling behaviours from chat logs and linguistically determining the diference of these behaviours can bring technology one step closer to protecting youth online. Our results show that not only can the enactment of control within the text be parsed out, but we are able to distinguish between controlling language and passive language. To our knowledge, no other study has made this contribution to the cybersexual abuse literature.

## 5.3. Aspects of technology

In this study, aspects of technology are aligned with “immediate concrete outcomes the actors experienced or expected to experience” (Volkof & Strong, 2013, p. 823) by understanding what the technology enabled individuals to do, what it made more dificult to do, and what it was used to do (Volkof & Strong, 2013). Within our study, the technology-enabled online solicitors to: identify vulnerable individuals in online social spaces, move from online social spaces to dyadic conversations, express themselves through emoticons, share pictures, share hyperlinks, and stream live video. Online solicitors used the technology to engage in cybersexual abuse of youth and as such made it more dificult to identify the truth within the behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofending.

As previously discussed, we analysed four specific aspects of the social media technology: access, anonymity, information sharing capabilities, and inap propriate use. Upon analysis, we found that access is dependent upon variables outside of the technology itself. An individual’s house situation, parental/guardian monitoring, work situation/schedule, school situation/schedule and type of technology available are variables that can afect an individual’s access to instant messenger. Attempts at anonymity within the instant messenger conversations existed on a continuum. Some individuals shared a lot of personal demographic information without hesitation while others actively worked to share as little as possible with varying degrees of sharing between these two extremes. Information sharing capabilities were not restricted to words within the technology. Individuals also used emoticons, hyperlinks, live webcam, and voice to share information. In some instances, text construction facilitated the use of other sharing mechanisms. For example, an ofender might ask if the potential victim wants to see his webcam or a link to a sexually inappropriate website. Other times he might just share without using text as a precursor. Like the continuum of anonymity, the degree of inappropriate use also ranged and is closely linked to information sharing. Some solicitors spent a majority of the conversation in seemingly innocuous conversation with the potential victim covering topics like school, friends, parents, interests, etc. They worked to develop a friendship before introducing the sexual content, thus putting of the blatantly inappropriate use of the technology as long as possible in order to heavily groom the potential victim. Others started with sexual content, using the technology inappropriately from the beginning.

## 6. Implications and future research

The theory of control within the cybersexual abuse of youth can be extended to other types of deviant activity within social media. If we take the definitions of grooming, predation, and ofending we can see that they are applicable to other cybercrimes in which the perpetrator needs to develop some level of relationship with the intended victim. In the cybersexual abuse of youth, grooming was composed of assessment and enticement, predation of self-preservation, and cybersexploitation made up ofending. Cybercrimes like sex traficking and cyberbullying would have difering subcategories for each of the constructs of grooming, predation, and ofending, filling the constructs shown in Figure 1. The aspects of control would potentially weave through the constructs. For example, cyberbullying, defined as “an aggressive, intentional act carried out by a group or individual, using electronic forms of contact, repeatedly and over time against a victim who cannot easily defend him or herself” (Smith et al., 2008, p. 376) may also involve aspects of these behaviours. Researchers have found that many youth victims of cyberbullying are known to the perpetrator (Dooley et al., 2009; Smith et al., 2008; Tokunaga, 2010), which could indicate grooming behaviours. Unlike traditional face-to-face bullying, the technology allows cyberbullies to access victims anytime from anywhere and “the persistence of the bullying behaviors may result in even stronger negative outcomes than traditional bullying” (Tokunaga, 2010, p. 279). The persistence refers to the repetition that typically occurs in bullying and is accompanied by a power imbalance which may be strengthened by the anonymity of the technology (Dooley et al., 2009). This power imbalance afords the perpetrator control and the anonymity is a way to protect one’s self, which may indicate behaviours of predation. Lastly, the contents of the messages sent during the act of cyberbullying are negative, which may indicate ofending behaviours.

Another example of deviant behaviours within social media is the Internet-facilitated commercial sexual exploitation of children (CSEC), also known as sex traficking. This phenomenon is defined as “the recruitment, harboring, transportation, provision, or obtaining of a person for the purpose of a commercial sex act” (Kotrla, 2010, p. 181). This is another potential phenomenon that is both serious and disturbing because “in addition to being sexually abused and assaulted, victims are treated as commodities and used for economic gain” (K. J. Mitchell et al., 2011, p. 44). A significant amount of control is used in sex traficking and tactics can include “threats, physical and sexual assault, taking away of legitimate travel and immigration documents, and threats against family members back in the source country” (Schauer & Wheaton, 2006, p. 157). K. J. Mitchell et al. (2011) noted “information about how the Internet is being used by CSEC ofenders is purely anecdotal. Systematic research is lacking” (p. 47). Our theory of cybersexual abuse of youth, focused on control, may provide a mechanism to investigate the role of the Internet in the phenomenon of sex traficking as the aspects of technology are integral to the theory.

What is evident based upon the data we examined is that while all three behaviours were present, the amount of time spent in each could vary. This could be similar to other phenomenon like sex traficking and cyberbullying. Additionally, there is a ribbon of control that runs through phenomena like sex traficking and cyberbullying and our theory of control within the cybersexual abuse of youth indicates the importance of this aspect by separating it into its own investigative construct as shown in Figure 1. Lastly, because these crimes are initiated and some fully perpetrated online, it is necessary to study the IT artefact as indicated within the theory. This newly developed theory provides a foundation for future research into the deviant behaviours enacted within social media.

Additionally, future research could build upon this study in two important directions. First, researchers could build upon our findings to improve pattern recognition programming for automated detection software. Studies using the Perverted Justice data set have been conducted to determine the most efective means by which to identify acts of predation in an online setting, with the goal of preventing the occurrence of ofline acts of victimisation. Kontostathis et al. (2009) applied the theory of luring communication (Olson et al., 2007) to improve the software called ChatCoder, which integrates theories of communication with computer science algorithms. Using this theory, researchers improved the systems detection capabilities by 13%. While similar to the current research, Kontostathis et al.’s (2009) applied a theory based on an ofline phenomenon to an online phenomenon. In similar research, Thom et al. (2011) developed an accessory for the open-source software called Pidgin, an instant messaging tool. Their plugin, called SafeChat keeps track of user interactions, detects age, and categorises words and phrases as potentially predacious based on established system rules. They achieved a 68% accuracy rate (Thom et al., 2011). Taking a diferent approach, Laorden, Galan-Garcia, Santos, Sanz, Maria, Hidalgo and Bringas (2013) applied a game theory methodology to the detection of predation online. They developed a system called Negobot that applies Natural Language Processing methods, chatter-box technologies and game theory to create a strategic decision-making situation. The goal of the system is to collect the maximum amount of information possible from the conversation for post-conversation analysis (Laorden et al., 2013).

However, these previous studies do not have the benefit of our results to demarcate grooming, predation, and ofending. Nor do they have the passive/controlling language diferentiation. The identification and understanding of the structural and content characteristics of this corpus could be the building blocks for the next step towards this goal. Verification of these characteristics using qualitative analysis software would serve to improve upon the previously identified categories. Additional and more refined categories may then emerge. Tightening the definitions of these characteristics would then provide the terminology dictionary for programming pattern recognition algorithms. We would not only have knowledge of the form and structure of the conversations, but also the function and purpose of the discourse used by adult solicitors. From this, it could be possible to develop software that would detect the acts of control within the grooming, predation, and ofence behaviours that occur during the cybersexual abuse of youth. This, in turn, could prevent a face-to-face meeting of a sex ofender and potential victim. Moreover, access to potential victims by adult solicitors is not the only unintended consequence of social media. As previously noted, cyberbullying and sex traficking are other phenomenon to which the theory of control within the cybersexual abuse of youth might be applied.

Second, this work could be the foundation for improving educational tools for youth, parents, guardians, educators, law enforcement, etc. Although the number of studies that examine this phenomenon at the level of lines of text is increasing no other study that we are aware of breaks the behaviours into grooming, predation, and ofence and delineates between the passive and controlling language used by the online solicitors. Therefore, the patterns we identified in our research regarding these distinct behaviours and the manner in which adult solicitors use words, hyperlinks, and live web cam streaming could provide information to parents/guardians and educators that can be used in conversations with youth about cybersexual abuse with the aim of youth empowering themselves with knowledge, increasing their agency to disengage and alert an adult when suspicious behaviour arises. Additionally, law enforcement oficials who patrol the online environments could be aforded an increased understanding of the techniques utilised by online ofenders that may or may not appear as the traditional methods of grooming previously addressed in the literature. Also, mental health professionals’ knowledge of the manipulative techniques used by online ofenders would be expanded. Understanding patterns of behaviour could provide improved research-based services to children who have been victims of cybersexual abuse.

## 7. Limitations

In the current study, we use a Perverted Justice dataset that is comprised of social media conversations between adult solicitors and potential victims. The potential victims are Perverted Justice volunteers trained in communicating as decoys representing youth. This limitation inhibits theorising about the communicative responses of children. Researchers should obtain anonymised conversations between adult solicitors and actual youth to conduct further research. Additionally, as previously mentioned, we coded the transcripts manually. The inclusion of additional coders could add to the knowledge identified in the transcripts. Also, while the manual coding provided an in-depth review of the conversations, the findings could be used to enhance text-mining techniques being used to detect the cybersexual abuse of youth. Examples of techniques that have been used to detect online sexual predators include support vector machines (Bogdanova et al., 2014; Cano et al., 2014; Ebrahimi, Suen, Ormandjieva et al., 2016; Morris, 2013), chain classifiers (Escalante et al., 2013), multilayer perceptron (Cheong et al., 2015), and convolutional neural networks (Ebrahimi, Suen, Ormandjieva et al., 2016). Applying the results of the current study to improve may yield more accurate results as they would allow a deviation from the binary classification of negative versus positive datasets (Ebrahimi, Suen, Ormandjieva et al., 2016) and provide the nuanced passive versus controlling language. It is also important to acknowledge that adult solicitors, victims, and the interactions between them are often unique and varied (Whittle et al., 2013a). Thus, the categories will need some flexibility and the algorithm terminology dictionary will need to be continuously updated to adapt to changing form and structure of the text utilised in this phenomenon.

Additionally, during reiterative coding, we observed that control was weaved through the three behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofence. To attend to this discovery, we redistributed the content coded as control into the appropriate categories. It is possible that, if all of the transcripts were recoded from this perspective, more content within the three categories of behaviours may actually be identified as control. This complete recording could alter the results and possibly improve the understanding of the role control plays in the solicitation of youth.

## 8. Conclusion

The focus of this study was to address the question: What are the computer-mediated behavioural patterns of adults who solicit youth within social media? To this end, we endeavoured to identify patterns and/or communicative techniques employed by adult solicitors during the solicitations of youth. We applied an evolved grounded theory analysis to the data, from which concepts emerged. Our analysis aligned with previous findings in the extant literature, expanded upon others, and revealed additional expressed behavioural patterns not previously identified. The contributions of this research are significant and fivefold: (1) we delineate between grooming behaviour, predatory behaviour, and ofending behaviour; (2) we diferentiate between passive and controlling solicitor language; (3) we disentangle these behaviours from what the technology permits; (4) we extend the current understanding of cybersexual abuse to show grooming as only one aspect of a phenomenon that also includes predation and ofence; and (5) we identify how these results are important for academics and software developers. The outcomes result in a theory of control within the cybersexual abuse of youth. Delineating between grooming, predation, and ofending also allowed us to (a) map the categories of action (assessment, enticement, self-preservation, and cybersexploitation) to the behaviours of grooming, predation, and ofending, (b) delimit the labels for the adult solicitors to distinguish between groomers, predators, and ofenders, and (c) extrapolate how control is exerted within those categories of actions.

As previously noted, Quayle and Taylor (2011) found that, “the empirical research in relation to grooming or online solicitation is still sparse and has largely focused on the behaviour of the young person as opposed to the ofending adult” (p. 46). This study helps to fill that gap and is the first study within the information systems literature to apply an evolved grounded theory approach to the discourse between adult solicitors and potential victims within social media. The application of grounded theory to transcripts resulted in an advanced understanding of how adult solicitors perpetrate cybersexual abuse through social media. The analysis and the resulting theoretical framework demonstrate the value in exploring solutions to the unintended consequences of social media usage.

Due to the pervasiveness of social media in the everyday lives of society’s children (Brown, 2011), and a move from the community-type social media to the dyadic applications (Read, 2016) increases the need to focus on the unintended consequences of its use. Unfortunately, few theories of social media exist that can be applied to these types of phenomenon. Urquhart and Vaast (2012) spoke of the urgency with which the IS community needs “to develop ways of building theory for social media because many IS researchers have embraced these environments as contexts for their research (e.g., Ransbotham & Kane 2011; Wattal et al., 2010), and many more have been thinking about doing so” (p. 2). The current study presents the evolved grounded theory approach as one method for building theories for social media.

Recent IS research points to “individuals, groups, and organizations that have been transformed – in intended and unintended ways – by technology” (Dang and Brown, 2010, p. 2). The cybersexual abuse of youth is one of these unintended consequences. It is executed by adult solicitors upon youth who are “vulnerable to seduction by online molesters . . . [due to complex factors] related to immaturity, inexperience, and impulsiveness” (Wolak et al., 2008, p. 116). Identifying expressed behavioural patterns utilised by adult solicitors within social media shines a light of hope on the ability of computer programs to improve detection mechanisms in information systems. Researchers have found that an adult solicitor “is a kind of traveller, in that he uses the Internet to gain access to a child whom he coerces into meeting him for sexual purposes” (J. Davidson & Gottschalk, 2011, p. 43). Therefore, improving information systems to identify adult solicitors could potentially prevent both face-to-face and cybersexual abuse of youth. Thus, it is critical for information systems researchers to investigate and understand how adult solicitors victimise children online. The knowledge gained could help interested individuals and groups – such as IS researchers, law enforcement, parents, educators, and youth themselves – to develop interventions to better protect youth online from adult solicitors. The current study is a necessary brick in the construction of a better world for our children through information systems.
