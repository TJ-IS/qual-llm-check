# Exploring firm strategy using financial reports: performance impact of inward and outward relatedness with digitisation

Wael Jabr <sup>a</sup> and Zhiqiang (Eric) Zheng<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Supply Chain and Information Systems, Pennsylvania State University, University Park, PA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Dallas, TX, USA

## ABSTRACT

A firm’s success critically hinges on its strategies in selecting its portfolio of products and services. In this paper, we study how diferentiation and market alignment at the ofering level impact firm performance. To that end, we mine firms’ 10-K filings to characterise the portfolio of oferings through the lens of outward relatedness, inward relatedness, and digitisation. We define outward relatedness as a measure of alignment of firm oferings within its market space, inward relatedness as a measure of diferentiation of firm oferings with its own past oferings, and digitisation as a measure of the firm’s focus on IT. We find that markets react positively to firms that operate with high levels of outward relatedness, low levels of inward relatedness and high levels of digitisation. However, we find that highly digitised firms do not have to conform to peers’ oferings. Digitisation enables these firms to diferentiate by internally diversifying their oferings. Interestingly, our results show that only firms already highly digitised benefit from further digitisation.

## 1. Introduction and problem statement

A core complexity at the interface of the information systems, management, and organisation literatures is the identification of how firms source their knowledge, improve their performance, and ensure their survival. The focus has been on strategically managing competing tensions to be both “like” and “diferent from” their peers in the market space (Deephouse, 1999; Zhao et al., 2017). On the one hand, the information systems and management scholars use the lens of the resource-based view of the firm to emphasise how such firms strive to be diferent to gain competitive advantage (A. Bharadwaj et al., 2013). On the other hand, organisation literature asserts that firms aim to be similar to peers to avoid performance deterioration related to deviating from existing expectations and practices (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). A variety of paradigms have been used to refer to this tension: the exploration versus exploitation (Lee et al., 2015; Nwankpa & Datta, 2017), innovation versus imitation (Lieberman & Asaba, 2006), related versus unrelated diversification (Palepu, 1985), internal versus external capabilities (Schryen, 2013), and conformity versus diferentiation paradigms (Zhang et al., 2020), among others. This long stream of research has unfortunately resulted in fragmented theorisation as to what a firm is conforming to and diferentiating from, or whether these attempts are inward facing (within the firm) or outward facing (outside the firm). Recognising that this tension does not result in the firm making a binary choice for sourcing knowledge and improving performance but rather choosing from a continuum that taps both inward and outward resources, the literature has subsequently shifted to studying the complementarity efects resulted from internal and external knowledge creation by showing that returns from one strategy increases as the intensity of the other strategy increases (Cassiman & Veugelers, 2006; Kogut & Zander, 1992). In fact, studies have shown that many firms use both inward and outward strategies to leverage the corresponding eficiencies (Cassiman & Veugelers, 2006; Katila, 2002). The one common denominator across this literature is however the emphasis on the moderating role of Information Technology through bringing new assets, creating products and services, modifying existing processes or connecting the firm with its environment (Nwankpa & Datta, 2017).

In this paper, we propose a framework to examine how firms access knowledge, update their capabilities and innovate in the pursuit of improved performance. This framework builds on firm’s reliance on both inward-facing and outward-facing knowledge, capabilities, and innovation, as well as digitisation and explores the role of this intricate balance on firm performance over time. Our research therefore investigates the all-too-common decisions firm make to improve their market performance: 1) should a firm conform with the industries’ trends or ensure they’re diferentiated?; 2) should a firm expand into areas closely related to its core business or expand in newer directions?; and 3) what role does the level of digitisation of the firm play? We characterise and investigate those three decisions using three firm proxies, outward relatedness, inward relatedness, and digitisation. We derive the first two proxies based on an extensive body of literature in IS and related disciplines that investigate firms’ strategies through the lens of relatedness (see Gao and Hitt (2012), Zschocke et al. (2014), and Lüthge (2018)). Outward relatedness refers to the degree of similarity of a firm’s oferings vis-à-vis its peers’ in the same industry and thus captures a firm’s external strategy in terms of aligning with competitors in the same product/services space. Inward Relatedness refers to the degree of similarity of a firm’s oferings over time vis-à-vis its own past oferings and thus captures a firm’s internal strategy with regard to how the firm chooses to diferentiate on the basis of its past oferings. Digitisation on the other hand generally refers to the level of a firm’s strategic focus on IT products, services, and processes.<sup>1</sup> Together, these three proxies provide a comprehensive view of a firm and the corresponding impact on performance, thus answering the research call to study firm product strategy in a multidimensional manner.

On a theoretical level, our framework contributes to filling an existing gap in research by proposing a unifying lens. In fact, the extant literature has studied the phenomenon of outward relatedness mostly in isolation, also with less focus on inward relatedness and little regard to the role of digitisation on relatedness. However, in line with a more recent stream of research (Adner and Zemsky, 2016; Zschocke et al., 2014), we contend that these three constructs need to be studied in tandem to accurately characterise a firm’s access to knowledge, capabilities updates, and innovation in pursuit of improved performance. Additionally, we contend that the viability of a firm’s efort is contingent on the level of internal and external sources as well as the related boosting efect from IT. Our research therefore investigates the return that firms derive from adopting various levels of outward relatedness, inward relatedness, and digitisation in their oferings. Additionally, our approach complements and enriches the large swath of extant literature examining firm imitation. While imitation has been traditionally considered as a binary choice thus labelling firms as either imitator or innovator, our approach allows imitation to vary on a continuum with one dimension describing the internal level of imitation and the other describing the external level of imitation.

On a methodological level, our implementation of relatedness overcomes prevalent shortcomings in the literature. The common approach to defining relatedness has mostly been based on firm membership in coarse industry sectors (Cottrell & Nault, 2004;

Tanriverdi, 2005), or limited to a set of industries where product listings are readily available. In contrast, we consider the broad set of firm oferings to include both products and services and we do not preimpose a segment or pre-define how oferings are related. Specifically, our approach utilises legal firm filings in the U.S., namely 10-K’s, to assess a firm’s strategy and operationalise the construction of relatedness (which we further diferentiate as outward relatedness and inward relatedness) and digitisation to study their simultaneous efect on firm performance. As such, our approach builds on a large corpus of textual data assorted from firms’ 10-K filings, and then uses text analytics to algorithmically quantify firm similarities. This approach provides a finer level of granularity to derive metrics that compare a firm’s portfolio of products and services to that of its industry competitors (outward relatedness) and with itself over time (inward relatedness). Similarly, the traditional approach to defining a firm’s digitisation has mostly relied on IT investments (e.g., Dewan and Chung-ki (1997) and Mittal and Nault (2009)). Rather, our approach explores firms’ 10-K filings, and uses text analytics to algorithmically quantify digitisation. With this approach we are able to expand the analysis beyond the traditionally narrow set of firms and industries.

Using data from a large sample of firms over time, we apply a panel vector autoregressive statistical (PVAR) model estimated by the generalised method of moments to investigate the dynamic relationship among firms’ outward relatedness, inward relatedness, digitisation, and firm performance over time across a large panel of public firms. Without making restrictive assumptions, the PVAR model considers each of the main time series variables to be endogenous and interdependent. We further supplement the analysis with an instrumental variable approach to further address endogeneity. Our results show that a firm benefits from expanding away from its core line of products and services (low level of inward relatedness) into aligning itself with its industry (high level of outward relatedness) and maintaining a focus on technology (high level of digitisation). On the other hand, when firms increasingly digitise themselves, they do not have to rely on the industry knowhow for diferentiation but rather they are better of expanding in areas related to their existing core oferings. Our findings derived from the three proposed metrics help inform firms on how to formulate their portfolio strategies and how to exploit technological capabilities to create new or improved products or services.

## 2. Research Background

The questions we address become especially pressing given the high pace of change and the ensuing disruptions introduced by information technology. We start by reviewing this context.

## 2.1. Approach to firm oferings

A well-established stream of research in management treats firms as entities consisting of bundles of attributes (e.g., products, resources) (Lenox et al., 2006) with firms continuously searching for a better configuration of such attributes. Given the disruptive environments they operate in, firms face the challenge of determining the appropriate mix of products and services to keep in their portfolio in order to make efective resource allocation decisions (Zschocke et al., 2014). A key strategic question arises: what approaches provide the greatest reward when deciding on oferings mix: remain in tandem with the industry while staying inwardly focused? To deal with these challenges, capturing the complexities is best achieved through a battery of linked metrics (Bendoly et al., 2007). In this paper, we explore these complexities using metrics derived from the firm’s own financial statements.

## 2.2. Similarity of firm oferings

Central to our outward relatedness and inward relatedness metrics is how to measure similarity between firms’ oferings. Similarity has been defined as the extent to which a firm’s diferent lines of business are linked to the extent to which the firm’s activities draw on similar input (resources and technologies) or output (products and services). Attempts to measure such similarity attracted a large body of research resulting in a variety of measures (Lüthge, 2018). In an extensive review of the literature, Tanriverdi (2005) identify well-established similarity constructs: product, manufacturing, technology, and R&D similarity. The derivation of these variations has been based on the relative similarity of firm inputs or outputs compared with other members’ inputs or outputs within specific SIC (or NAICS) codes. Yet, empirically characterising a firm’s oferings and their similarities have proven to be rather complex. The first challenge lies in the identification of the extended lists of products and services ofered by the firm. The second is to use more comprehensive approaches besides simply surveying a small set of managers. The third is to overcome the restrictions imposed by tying the similarities to membership in SIC codes. Our approach overcomes these challenges.

## 2.3. Ten-K filings for mining firm oferings

Financial filings are becoming a well-established data source to identify firm oferings (Hoberg, Phillips, et al., 2014; Li et al., 2013). Among those filings, firms

10-Ks provide a useful window to view the extent of imitation the firm is engaging in. Ten-K filings are formal public releases of firms’ detailed financial statements mandated by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and include disclosures such as management discussions of current status of the firm as well as research and development disclosures. Ten-K filings are one of the major sources of information for investors and analysts to evaluate the current status of the firm. As such, 10-K filings contain unique information complementary to quantifiable accounting numbers such as historical cash flow (Hoberg, Phillips, et al., 2014).

With improved techniques to mine textual content, 10-K filings have gained an increased interest among researchers, with Li et al. (2013) developing a measure of competition based on management disclosures in 10-K filings, Hoberg and Phillips (2010) identifying firm-pairs that have similar product descriptions based on the textual descriptions in their 10-K filings, and Wang et al. (2013) extracting information security risk factors from annual financial reports. We follow this stream of machine learning-based approach.

## 2.4. Disruptive role of digitisation<sup>2</sup>

Digitisation, enabled by information technology, has intensified competition and accelerated the pace of transformation requiring firms to adjust by exploiting their existing competencies as well as exploring new ones (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006). Digitisation has shortened both technology and oferings lifecycles, and engendered hypercompetition (Brynjolfsson et al., 2009). Consequently, the rate at which firms introduced oferings into the market becomes crucial for firm success (Harter et al., 2000). The push for newer technology, products and services also comes from the consumer side. As a result, the whole cycle from R&D to product conceptualisation, manufacturing has fundamentally changed, requiring increased levels of agility and flexibility (Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012). To thrive under such conditions, firms need to be artful in balancing their oferings and their levels of imitation. In this paper, we explore the role that IT plays in this artful timing and balancing.

## 3. Hypotheses development

In this section, we present the relevant literature that motivates our hypotheses. Given our focus on the implications of digitisation, and for each of our hypotheses, we include two specifications, one for all the firms in our dataset and another specifically for highly digitised firms.

In a competitive environment, firms face the dilemma of whether to maintain a focus on a core set of oferings and whether to remain aligned with their market. On one hand, it is hard for a firm to succeed with a limited and static pool of oferings (products and services). On the other hand, firms always on the cusp of transformation typically increase their exposure to failure (Kleindorfer, 2010). Thus, firms need to artfully manage their oferings to improve market performance, e.g., by making strategic diversification choices in response to external market competition (Fernhaber & Patel, 2012); or balancing exploration and exploitation for better product and services development (Lee et al., 2015; Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012) and deciding whether to respond to competition through imitation and incrementalism (Zschocke et al., 2014). The profitability of firm oferings is there fore greatly afected by the extent to which firms are meaningfully diferentiated from their own (internal) as well as competing alternatives (external). However, determining the appropriate mix of oferings remains an open question (Klingebiel & Rammer, 2014). On one hand, high level of internal and external similarity among oferings benefits from synergies and shared learning within and outside the firm. On the other hand, an undiversified portfolio is susceptible to turbulences in a volatile market (Fernhaber & Patel, 2012). Furthermore, the direction of the impact of diferentiation on performance is still a subject of debate. Cottrell and Nault (2004) for example, argue that changes in firms’ oferings in the software industry enable them to improve market performance. Stern and Henderson (2004) nevertheless hold that the degree of within-industry diferentiation and the introduction rate of new oferings both lead to decreased firm failures in the personal computer industry. To address those challenges, we characterise a firm in terms of three dimensions: outward relatedness, inward relatedness, and digitisation.

## 3.1. Firm outward relatedness and performance

Outward Relatedness refers to the similarity of a firm’s oferings (products and services) compared to its peers in the same industry. In this sense, it captures the diferentiation in tandem with the industry. The notion of an outward relatedness can be traced to the seminal work by DiMaggio and Powell (1983) on institutional pressures (Huigang et al., 2007). Being aligned with the capabilities of one’s rivalling group helps reduce the uncertainty around outcomes (Ruckman et al., 2015). Studying the personal computer industry, Stern and Henderson (2004) show that within-industry diferentiation reduces firm failures when the external conditions remain unchanged. Furthermore, diferentiation, while cultivating new oferings and sometimes new markets, are often unaligned with the industry and not without perils. Based on the earlier discussion, we develop the following hypothesis:

H1a: Outward Relatedness in firm oferings is positively associated with market performance.

With the advent and substantial proliferation of digital technologies, firms are presented with considerable opportunities to employ IT to further diferentiate themselves (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004; Youngjin et al., 2012). The success of those firms which rely heavily on IT has been shown to depend on their understanding of the advances in technology and what they make possible (Fichman et al., 2014). Additionally, IT also enables firms to align themselves with their peers much quicker. In their exploratory work, Lichtenthaler et al. (2011) find that Sponges – those technological firms that mimic their competitors by actively absorbing external technology – achieved a quicker and higher return on sales than those which are sources of the technological knowledge. This finding further highlights the role that outward relatedness plays among highly digitised firms: outward relatedness reduces the risks from developing new oferings and speeds up acceptance by the market. We accordingly postulate:

H1b: For highly digitised firms, digitisation is positively associated with outward relatedness.

## 3.2. Firm inward relatedness and performance

We next use inward relatedness to refer to the extent to which a firm’s various lines of business are linked by a common skill, resource, market, or purpose (Lüthge, 2018). In this sense, inward relatedness captures the within-firm and over time diferentiation (Oraiopoulos & Kavadias, 2014). One central theme in IS and strategy literatures that investigates the existence and nature of the relationship between firm diferentiation and its performance is whether firms should operate only in related businesses (Nocker et al., 2016). The influential argument of Teece and Rumelt (1994) for the “coherence” of a firm’s portfolio of products and services assumes that the lines of business of a firm have common technological and market characteristics. This coherence, or inward relatedness in our case, has been shown to result in an improvement in market performance. Yet, this improvement has not been conclusively established especially that higher levels of inward relatedness have also been shown to exacerbate resource overstretching and coordination problems thus resulting in lower profitability (Gary, 2005). As a result, another view is that there is substantial variation in performance among diversifiers where some outperform while others underperform (Lyandres, 2007). However, a closer examination of this literature shows that diferentiation has been mostly analysed as an exogenous decision, not accounting for the firm’s peers (Adner and Zemsky, 2016) with only few exceptions that have begun to explicitly link diferentiation decisions, competition, industry relatedness, and market structure. Our paper attempts to complement this literature by providing comprehensive empirical findings accounting for all these factors. Just like Adner and Zemsky (2016) who show that the relationship between firm related ness and profit is unambiguously negative and is moderated by the industry wide relatedness, we expect that, with market competition, increasing reliance on ofering relatedness year over year leads to negative firm performance. Additionally, in the long run, firms that maintain a high level of inward relatedness tend to lack the ability to transcend themselves and are expected to experience lower market performance. We accordingly propose the following hypothesis:

H2a: Inward Relatedness in firm oferings is associated with a deteriorated firm performance.

As discussed earlier, IT has been shown to accelerate the pace of transformations and their spread amongst peers. As such, IT provides firms with opportunities to diferentiate themselves (Swanson & Ramiller, 2004; Youngjin et al., 2012). IT has been crucial for firms to monitor and react to market changes swiftly, decide what products and services to produce, which ones to keep or retire (A. Bharadwaj et al., 2013). Therefore, IT can play a mitigating role to inward relatedness negative impact thus reversing the impact and improving the firm performance at higher digitisation levels. We therefore conjecture:

H2b: For highly digitised firms, digitisation is associated with lower levels of inward relatedness.

## 3.3. Digitisation and firm performance

We use digitisation to refer to the extent to which the firm owns IT resources and uses them in various aspects of its oferings and business processes throughout the firm. As such, our definition of digitisation accounts for IT as both a resource and a capability building (Makadok, 2001) which then exerts influence on the firm through complementary relationships with the other firm’s assets and capabilities. Such digitisation has changed every aspect of firm portfolio development, from R&D to production and marketing in today’s world (Kohli & Grover, 2008). IT has enabled firms to become more diversified in terms of their products, services, and intellectual properties, and to reduce the risk in a hypercompetitive market (Gao & Hitt, 2012).

Furthermore, with digital technologies playing an increasingly central role in competition, the level of digitisation has become a key barometer for firm success. Mittal and Nault (2009) demonstrate that the value from digitisation is accrued not only directly through changes in IT inputs, but also indirectly through IT-enabled augmentation of non-IT inputs. Bardhan et al. (2013) provide evidence of the moderating role of IT on the efectiveness of firm R&D through providing the infrastructure that amplifies the capabilities of developing and launching complex products. Digitisation, for example, has been found to have a greater efect on firm profitability than advertising or R&D investments (Mithas et al., 2012). Research has also recently shown that while IT improves firm performance, the efect is reciprocal in that firms with greater market valuation and expectations of growth are more likely to leverage IT into their products, services, and even business models (Nandkumar et al., 2018). Consequently, how firms should best leverage IT in their portfolio of products and services has become a major challenge. These decisions exert direct implications on firms’ financial and market valuation. We expect that digitisation is an essential driver for improved market performance. We therefore build on this literature to develop the following hypothesis:

H3: Digitisation of firm oferings is associated with an improved market performance.

## 4. Metrics for characterising firms’ oferings

## 4.1. Extracting firm Portfolio of oferings from 10- K filings

As it has been customary to rely on item 1 (Business) and item 1A (Risk Factors) in the 10-K filings to identify a firm’s current line of businesses (Hoberg & Phillips, 2016), we develop web crawlers in Python to collect 10-K filings from the SEC website for all the firms in our dataset. We search for filings and their amendments listed as 10-K, 10-K405, 10-KSB, or 10- KSB40 (which we refer to as 10-K filings hereafter). For each filing, we parse out among others, the firm’s name, filing year, and information under headings item 1 and item 1A. We supplement this data using MorningStar’s proprietary database (Document Research for 10-K filings) to ensure consistency and accuracy of all the filings we collect.

## 4.1.1. Constructing the Similarity Metric from 10-K Filings

For all the firms over all the years in our dataset, our approach is to first vectorise the text of 10-K filings and then derive the between-filings similarities using Word2Vec. This text analysis implementation is developed by researchers at Google (Mikolov et al.,

2013) and is based on reconstructing the linguistic contexts of words. It is known to perform better than traditional word-based implementations such as bagof-words. Next, we use the “Word Mover’s Distance” to derive word vector embeddings for document similarity (Kusner et al., 2015). We note here that existing statistical methods generally regard actual text as bag of words thus ignoring the information from the text structure and losing information on the text’s semantic. More recently, methods such as Word2Vec which are based on deep learning overcome such shortcomings by vectorising the text while allowing for those semantics. The approach we use thus accounts for word embedding approaches and improves the derivation of similarity thus overcoming the inability to detect the use of diferent terms that refer to identical concepts across firms. As such, the metric will be less impacted by diferent peer firms using non-matching though related terms. This process is then repeated for the same firm comparing its filings year over year (for inward relatedness), and for the same year comparing firms’ filings (for outward relatedness). A lower distance means a higher similarity between filings. We include a diagrammatic illustration in Figure 1. A sample topic modelling analysis is provided in the Appendix.

## 4.2. Constructing digitisation metric from 10-K Filings

To construct digitisation, we also rely on the textual content of 10-K filings to identify the extent to which firms focus on digital technologies. To that end, we compile a dictionary of IT keywords based on listings by three authoritative sources on information. These sources are a) NAICS identification of products and services (North American Product Classification System) for the various IT-related industries, b) keywords provided in a glossary compiled by TechTarget and identified as being IT-focused, and c) keywords provided in a glossary compiled by Gartner and also identified as being IT-focused.<sup>4</sup> After deduplication, we end up with a dictionary of 3,025 IT-related keywords which we then run against each of the extracted 10-K filings to identify the frequency of IT keywords mentioning in each of those 10-K filings after normalising for the text length. We use a publicly available tool (Meaning Extraction Helper) to calculate those frequencies (Boyd & (Producer), 2017). The digitisation metric for firm f in year t is then derived as follows: $\begin{array} { r } { D i g i t i z a t i o n _ { f t } = \frac { ( \# I i c t i o n a r y W o r d s i n 1 0 K f i l i n g ) _ { f t } } { ( \# W o r d s i n 1 0 K f i l i n g ) _ { f t } } } \end{array}$ with $( \# D i c t i o n a r y W o r d s i n 1 0 K f i l i n g ) _ { f t }$ as the total count of words in the dictionary that are mentioned in the filing of firm f in year t, and ð#Wordsin10KfilingÞ as the total word count in that filing. Derived this way, the digitisation metric measures the firm’s focus on IT as reflected by its 10-K filing.

## 4.3. Construct validity

An essential question to address is whether the metrics we propose are valid conceptualisations that serve as good proxies for inter and intra firm similarity. We acknowledge that full validation is quite dificult to establish and this has been a prevalent concern in the literature (Sohl & Vroom, 2014). That said, we attempt to provide partial validation. Our similarity metric is closely related to the well-accepted metric proposed by Hoberg and Phillips (2016) which was also constructed using textual analyses based on firms’ current product descriptions.<sup>5</sup> Additionally, we conduct a comparative analysis to compare our metrics with the publicly available firm similarity scores from Hoberg and Phillips (2016). We aggregate all the scores per firm-pair under our approach and contrast them with theirs. In line with Dou et al. (2020), we run a regression to test the closeness between our similarity measure and the aggregation of their measures over time. The results, included in the Appendix, show a significant and positive correlation suggesting that our measure is comparable to that of Hoberg and Phillips (2016). Lastly, we provide in the Appendix anecdotal evidence where we report the close correlation between our firm inward relatedness measure and the firm’s 10-K filings as well as their public announcements. Our approach is also in line with a growing number of studies that rely on a set of keywords to develop economic measures (Bao & Datta, 2014; Hoberg & Phillips, 2016; Loughran & McDonald, 2013).

As for our digitisation measure, Kim and Lee (2018) empirically show that firms identified as active in software-related arena do discuss software-related activities in their 10-K more frequently. They do so by measuring the frequency of the term “software” in 10-K filings and then regressing the frequency of “software” on their measure of firm-level software intensity. They find that firms with higher software intensity use “software” more frequently in their business descriptions in the subsequent year and that this relationship is robust to the inclusion of several control variables.

## 5. Methodology

In this section, we introduce the data and variables, then present our econometrics specification.

## 5.1. Data

Our data covers a large panel of 1,620 publicly traded firms over the period 1995–2015. This set of firms is retrieved from the Compustat database and meets two conditions: 1) each firm has at least two consecutive yearly observations to ensure a meaningful panel data analysis; and 2) each firm comes from industries that report IT investments. The NAICS industries represented in our dataset are provided in the Appendix. To derive our sample of firms, we follow the extant literature on sample selection (Lu & Shang, 2017; Saunders & Tambe, 2015). To that end, we drop firms with negative, zero, or missing observations for any of the following parameters: market values, Tobin’s Q, balance sheet assets, sales, or employment, negative or missing values of capital expenditures, inventories, or cost of goods sold, and drop firms with missing values of operating profits. We also eliminate outlier firms with extreme Tobin’s Q values greater than 10. We lastly drop firms with missing NAICS code.

## 5.2. Variables

Besides the three main variables identified in section 4, we derive the following variables.

Firm Performance: We adopt Tobin’s Q as the measure of firm performance which captures the value of a firm based on its future earnings relative to the current book value. Tobin’s Q represents a forward-looking measure of firm value that is less susceptible to changes in accounting practices (Bardhan et al.,

2013). Tobin’s Q has been widely used in the IS literature to measure the business value of IT (Bardhan et al., 2013; A. Bharadwaj et al., 2013).

Tobin’s Q is constructed as the ratio of the market cap for a firm over the firm’s total assets in a given fiscal year in our study period, with both market cap and total asset value extracted from Compustat and cross examined with S&P’s Capital IQ database. Specifically, Tobin’s Q is calculated as the sum of market value of common equity (CSHO×PRCC\_F from Compustat), liquidating value of preferred stock (PSTKL or PSTKRV if PSTKL is missing in Compustat) and book value of debt scaled by total assets (AT from Compustat) measured at the end of fiscal year t. The book value of debt is computed as the diference between current liabilities (LCT from Compustat) and current assets (ACT from Compustat) plus inventory (INVT from Compustat) and long-term debt (DLTT from Compustat), all in year t (Bardhan et al., 2013).

Control Variables: Following the extant literature (Bardhan et al. (2013) among others), we include three commonly used firm level control variables that have been found to afect firm valuation.

● Emp: This item (EMP from Compustat) represents the number of employees in the firm and its consolidated subsidiaries in thousands for firm i in year t. This is a widely used measure to control for firm size.

● R&D: This item (XRD from Compustat) represents all spending incurred by the firm for the research and development of products or services in millions of dollars for firm i in year t. The R&D measure has been widely used to control for firm’s research propensity.

● Liquidity: This is the ratio of total liabilities (LT from Compustat) to total assets (AT from Compustat) both in millions of dollars for firm i in year t, a ratio widely used to control for firm’s financial health.

● Firm age: This is a log transformed count of years since establishment. The values are derived based on the year of first posting on the SEC website.

● Market location: This is a dummy that takes the value of 1 if the firm’s zip code is located in a high-tech US metropolitan area (MA) and 0 otherwise. To identify those MAs, we use the report by Milken Institute (DeVol et al., 2013) which lists the top 200 cities based on high-tech GDP growth. We first derive the ±s of each of those cities. Next, and for each firm in our dataset, we derive its zip code from its 10-K filing. Then, we check whether the firm’s zip code is located within the top 200 cities (i.e., same zip code as those cities or within a range of ± 250).

## 5.3. Econometric analysis

We implement a panel vector autoregression (PVAR) model to study the dynamic relationship between firm portfolio and its performance. Without making restrictive assumptions, PVAR models consider each of the main time series variables to be endogenous and interdependent (Holtz-Eakin et al., 1988; Sakhartov, 2017). In the IS literature, a growing number of empirical studies have adopted PVAR models in their analyses (e.g., Dewan and Ramaprasad (2014). PVAR explicitly models endogenous variables as a function of other endogenous variables and other exogenous variables; it accounts for the cross-section as well as the temporal dynamics; and it allows for correlated disturbances via the systems of equations in a PVAR model. Such a model is adequate for analysing our setup where there exists simultaneity between digitisation, outward relatedness, inward relatedness, and firm performance, as well as their own past values; in fact Nandkumar et al. (2018) emphasise that while digital technologies improve firm performance, the efect is also reciprocal, making firms with greater market valuation and expectations of growth more likely to engage in digital innovation and focus less on imitation. Additionally, the generalised method of moments (GMM) implementation which we adopt (see, for example, Hansen (1982)) uses lagged dependent variables and lagged diferences of dependent variables as instruments and overcomes potential bias when estimating dynamic panel models. As such, PVAR allows for bidirectional inferences between endogenous variables and over time.

In PVAR, each dependent variable is assumed to be endogenous and is a linear function of its own lag values, lag values of all other dependent variables, a set of exogenous variables, and an error term. In our specification, this approach endogenizes the time series of all variables for outward relatedness, inward relatedness, and digitisation as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} y _ {i, t} = \left( \begin{array}{c} T o b i n ^ {\prime} s Q \\ O u t w a r d \\ I n w a r d \\ D i g i t i z a t i o n \end{array} \right) _ {i, t} \\ = \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {L} \beta_ {l} \left( \begin{array}{c} T o b i n ^ {\prime} s Q _ {i, t - l} \\ O u t w a r d _ {i, t - l} \\ I n w a r d _ {i, t - l} \\ D i g i t i z a t i o n _ {i, t - l} \end{array} \right) + \Phi * C o n t r o l s _ {i, t - 1} \\ + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

where $\bf { < i , t > }$ is the firm-year panel; $y _ { i , t } = \left( T o b i n ^ { \prime } s Q _ { i , t } . \right.$ Outward $_ { i , t } , I n w a r d _ { i , t } , D i g i t i z a t i o n _ { i , t } ) ^ { ' }$ is a four-element column vector of variables for firm i at time t; $\beta _ { l } ^ { \mathbf { \Upsilon s } }$ are the $4 \times 4$ matrices of coeficients for the endogenous variables; l is the number of lags which will be empirically determined from the data; Φare coeficients for the control variables; $C o n t r o l s _ { i , t - 1 }$ include: $E m p _ { i , t - 1 }$ – the yearly stafing level of firm i at time t-1, R& $D _ { i , t - 1 }$ – the yearly research and development expenditure of firm i at time t-1, and $L i q u i d _ { i , t - 1 } -$ the yearly liquidity (ratio of liabilities to assets) of firm i at time $t \mathrm { - } I ; \varepsilon _ { i t }$ is a four-element vector of serially uncorrelated error terms.

We start by conducting unit root tests to check the stationarity of variables using an augmented Dicky-Fuller test (Maddala & Wu, 1999), since the standard GMM estimator has been shown to break down if the model contains unit roots. Based on the p-values of individual unit root tests, the Fisher’s test assumes that all series are non-stationary under the null hypothesis against the alternative that at least one series in the panel is stationary. The testing results in Table 2 reject the null hypothesis of the presence of unit root.

We then determine the number of lags in our PVAR specification. Following Abrigo and Love (2015) and the three moment model selection criteria (MMSC) proposed by Andrews and Lu (2001), namely MMSC-Bayesian Information Criterion (MBIC), MMSC-Akaike Information Criterion (MAIC), and MMSC-Hannan-Quinn Information Criterion (MQIC), we find that the first-order PVAR is the preferred model as it results in the smallest MBIC, MAIC and MQIC.<sup>6</sup> Based on the selection criteria and the results in Table 3, we fit a first-order PVAR model.

Hypothesis H1b is not supported $( \beta _ { D i g i t i z a t i o n } ^ { E Q 2 h i g h } = \ 0 . 0 0 3$ not significant in Table 5) indicating that for firms on the higher spectrum in Digitisation, there is no correlation between outward relatedness and digitisation. In other terms, digitisation helps free firms from the cycle of imitation.

Hypothesis H2b is supported $( \beta _ { \mathrm { { D i g i t i z a t i o n } } } ^ { E Q 3 h i g h } = - 0 . 0 0 9$ significant and negative at p <0.001) suggesting that for firms on the higher spectrum of digitisation, increasing levels of digitisation subsequently enable a lower inward relatedness and thus subsequently more diverse oferings. Combined, the lack of support for H1b and the support for H2b indicate that for highly digitised firms the market does not value imitation, more so for the industry-level imitation, and not just incremental reduction in relying on imitation of a firm’s own past.

## 7. Discussion and conclusion

Worldwide spending on digitisation grew 6.5% over the last decade according to Gartner Inc (2013). Concurrently, however, the downward spiralling of numerous firms due to the mismanagement of technology and the misallocation of production capabilities across ofering portfolios made firm failure a common topic in the mainstream news (Barker, 2007). It becomes pressing for the academic community to strengthen our understanding of a firm’s sustainable portfolios of products and services.

This research aimed at informing firms on how to best operationalise their portfolio of oferings to achieve optimal performance. To that end, we propose the use of 10-K filings which are widely available and readily accessible. We characterise the portfolio in terms of outward relatedness, inward relatedness and digitisation, three metrics that quantify the firm’s similarity to itself, its peers, as well as its focus on technology. While these three concepts are wellgrounded in the literature, we expand innovative methods to operationalise and quantify them by making use of the 10-K filings as well as the advances in text analytics. Accordingly, this provides a new lens for shareholders to determine the value of a firm and for managers to assess management strategies. Our results show that outward relatedness, inward related ness, and digitisation all are significantly associated with firm performance, yet each metric has its own unique influence. These findings suggest that the metrics we propose to quantify firm’s portfolio allow us to adequately assess a firm’s market performance. These findings also suggest that firms should not become too complacent, dwelling on their past achievements (represented by inward relatedness); rather, a successful firm is earmarked with low imitation over time (low inward relatedness) and a high focus on IT (high digitisation) while being in-sync with the market (i.e., high outward relatedness). Firms can leverage these three metrics to devise their portfolio strategy and then allocate production capabilities.

This study makes three salient contributions. First, this paper applies a text analytics-based approach for the purpose of measuring a firm’s portfolio of oferings anchored in its own 10-K filings and constructs three metrics to that end – outward relatedness, inward relatedness, and digitisation. This approach is easy to implement while at the same time much more scalable than traditional approaches. The reliance on the publicly available 10-K filings overcomes the data limitation for both the measurements of firm digitisation as well as firm relatedness that had inflicted extant literature. Second, this paper represents one of the early attempts at quantifying the impact in tandem of firm behaviour metrics on performance. Our econometrics analysis using PVAR allows us to endogenize the cross impact of outward relatedness, inward relatedness, digitisation and Tobin’s $\mathrm { Q } . ^ { 8 }$ As such, it accounts for both the cross-section efects and the temporal dynamics while at the same time allowing for correlated disturbances via a system of equations. This helps uncover the dynamic relationships among firms’ outward relatedness, inward relatedness, digitisation. Third, our findings shed light on how a firm can benefit from its portfolio along the three dimensions to achieve desirable market performance. Specifically, we find that markets react positively to firms that operate with high levels of outward relatedness, low levels of inward relatedness and high levels of digitisation.

Our study also has some limitations. We do not observe the contribution of each specific product or service in a firm’s portfolio on its performance, an impact which might be quite dificult to disentangle given the lack of comprehensive reporting and publishing of firm products and services. We therefore had to rely on the aggregated performance at the portfolio level. Future research using big data may be able to compute the three metrics at the ofering leve and examine the firm performance accordingly. Furthermore, in this study we focus on measuring the performance (return) of a portfolio of oferings in the form of Tobin’s Q. In the future, we plan to factor in the risk-return trade-of (Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004). This will be quite essential given that firms at diferent stages in their life might choose diferent risk levels to achieve the desirable long-term return. We do not distinguish firms on the basis of being IT-producing and IT-consuming. While this is an essential dimension for an interesting future research, we opt to focus on the impact of digitisation irrespective of what is driving it. Lastly, our dataset spans a long tie period (from 1995 to 2015) which unavoidably includes several time periods during which firms witnessed abnormal performance as in recessions such as the dotcom bust in 2001 and the global financial crisis in 2008. Our method does not specifically account for such events as we are more interested in long term average efects. Isolating the efects of short or long catastrophic market events is a valuable future research topic. Typically, during such turbulent times the market behaves more conservatively resulting in lower Tobin’s Q values. Furthermore, the market would typically value firms more on the basis of their stock value (i.e., more cash flow) rather than stock growth potential (i.e., more innovative and highly digitised firms) – an assessment in line with the Fama-French factor models. This also means that the market would de-value activities with respect to innovation and digitisation. As a result, it would be expected that the coeficients of our three metrics go down during those turbulent times. Additionally, it would be expected that outward innovation would be devalued more than inward innovation. Such findings have been echoed by prior studies as with Dos Santos et al. (2012) who find that digitisation did not pay of in the period right after the dot com bust yet starting in 2004 the efect was reversed. While the assessment we provide is rather a tentative and speculative one, a full investigation on the impact of such catastrophic events warrants future exploration.
