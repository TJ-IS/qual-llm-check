# Productive and connected while working from home: what client-facing remote workers can learn from telenurses about ‘belonging through technology

Ella Hafermalz <sup>a</sup> and Kai Riemer <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Business and Economics, KIN Center for Digital Innovation, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, Amsterdam, Netherlands; <sup>b</sup>Business Information Systems, The University of Sydney, Sydney, Australia

## ABSTRACT

Client-facing work is challenging at the best of times, but the sudden shift to remote work during the COVID-19 pandemic has created additional dificulties. Concerns about how employee productivity and wellbeing are negatively impacted by the unfamiliar remote work context abound. We draw on a study of skilled remote workers that was conducted before the pandemic to theorise how client-facing work can be conducted efectively. In particular we examine how client-facing employees can foster a sense of belonging when they are not colocated. We use an abductive research approach, combining empirical material from interviews with tele-nurses with theoretical material from Gofman’s categories of “communication out of character”. We thereby theorise what we call “belonging through technology”, as the outcome of four types of team communication that contribute to the performance of efective clientfacing work in a remote context. We distinguish and conceptualise functions of such team communication as coping, learning, plotting, and positioning, which together create belonging. We argue that “belonging” relates to both wellbeing and productivity, because belonging to one’s team (wellbeing) and belonging to one’s work role (productivity) are inherently connected in the performance of client-facing work. We ofer insights to help practitioners in such remote work contexts.

## 1. Introduction

Work has moved online during the COVID-19 pandemic. This widespread shift to remote work has raised concerns – both about continued productivity, and risks to wellbeing (Rubin et al., 2020). On the one hand, it is dificult to maintain a high standard of work in this newly distributed context. On the other hand, workers can experience isolation and lose their sense of belonging to each other and to the organisation when working from home (Bartel et al., 2007; Dery & Hafermalz, 2016; Whittle & Mueller, 2009). These concerns are particularly acute in client-facing work contexts such as in healthcare, education, sales, consulting, and professional service work (Whittle & Mueller, 2009). Client-facing work is stressful and taxing. Doing it efectively has traditionally relied upon intensive face to face interaction both with the client and with colleagues – yet this has now become impossible in many cases.

How can client-facing work be performed remotely and efectively? How will employees cope while apart? To build useful knowledge on these issues that can help others in times of the COVID-19 crisis (Ågerfalk et al., 2020), we utilise a case study on tele-health nurses. These healthcare professionals already worked remotely from their homes before the pandemic. We show how these telenurses use technology skilfully to create a sense of belonging – both to their work and role as nurses (productivity) and to each other (wellbeing). We theorise what we call the “work of belonging through technology” by drawing on Gofman’s (1959) theatre metaphor, in particular, his four types of “communication out of character”. We use this theoretical framework to analyse our interview data and derive a process model that shows how technology can be used to execute a successful “performance” as a work team, in relation to the client as “audience”, even while working remotely. We further highlight skills that experienced remote workers have developed in order to maintain the necessary team support structures for successfully coping with the challenges of remote client-facing work.

## 2. Home alone: where is the “social” in remote work?

Remote work is a key source of economic and organisational resilience in times of crisis (Colbert, 2011). Employee benefits of regular remote work include autonomy and temporal and spatial flexibility in individual work processes (Bernstein, 2014). Yet there is also an acknowledgement that feelings of isolation can be a problem when working from home, and maintaining a sense of social and organisational connection is a significant challenge in remote working arrangements (Fried & Heinemeier Hansson, 2013; Fritz et al., 1997; Orlikowski, 1996; Staples, 2001). Relatedly, conflict in virtual teams is a well-established research topic (Bartel et al., 2007; Grifith & Neale, 2001; Hinds & Bailey, 2003). Consequently, how building and maintaining a social connection can help to remedy barriers to collaboration and coordination in distributed work is of interest to Information Systems scholars (Bordetsky & Mark, 2000; A. R. A. R. Dennis et al., 2008; Leonardi, 2014; Ravishankar, 2015).

Such issues are usually approached from the perspective of how technologies can assist in repairing deficits in trust and control (Adams et al., 1999; Ang et al., 1993; Aten et al., 2014; Bentley et al., 2016). A social connection is considered desirable because it improves commitment and trust and therefore minimises conflict while increasing loyalty and pro-social behaviours (O’Reilly & Chatman, 1986; Strohmeier, 2013; Walton, 1985). Such an instrumental approach to leveraging social connection for increased productivity presents a top-down view of the role of the social in remote work. When the social side of work is seen as separable from work, it can be deemed possible to treat workers as discreet units who collaborate only in the service of task accomplishment. Remote work is often based upon this understanding of a modular workforce, where tasks are disconnected from the social side of work (Bailey et al., 2012).

In this paper, we join more worker-oriented research that problematises (Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011) the above assumptions, by highlighting how distributed workers support one another and build and maintain social relationships with the help of ICTs (Bartel et al., 2012; Goodall & Roberts, 2003; Kelly & Noonan, 2008; Riemer et al., 2007). Belonging emerges here as an important aspect of remote work performance, specifically in the context of client-facing work. We argue that the issue of belonging is twofold: there is both the matter of 1) how remote workers build a sense of occupational or professional belonging, of maintaining a distinction between themselves and their clients, and 2) the issue of how they come to understand themselves as members of the same team, when their teammates are as geographically distant as their clients. We show how the social and the task aspects of client-facing work are inseparable, and ofer advice on how belonging can be achieved efectively in remote work, using technology.

## 3. Methodology: an abductive study of remote telenursing

We investigate a case of remote work practices in a telenursing context utilising in-depth interviews in a health services firm in Australia, which we refer to as HealthOrg (pseudonyms are used throughout and identifying details have been changed to protect anonymity).

## 3.1. Case context: triaging via phone

One of HealthOrg’s divisions is contracted by the Australian federal government to provide a phonebased nurse triage service, which was built to reduce pressure on emergency services and is free to the public. Callers go through a triage process over the phone with a registered nurse who will advise the patient on whether they should remain at home, see a doctor, or go to the emergency department. Approximately 400 registered nurses with clinical experience work for the service. The work is conducted solely remotely, from each nurse’s home ofice. After initial co-located training, all contact with the organisation takes place via phone, email, and instant messaging. Nurses receive a setup comprised of computer, headset and phone system; video conferencing is rarely used except by managers.

Access to the nurses’ work context was facilitated by the organisation’s senior managers, who were interested in nurses’ views on working remotely and their use of a new software system guiding the triage process. Overall, 10 nurses and three managers were interviewed for this study. Given the focused nature of our study and the homogenous nature of the work tasks among the nurses this number was deemed sufficient. We conducted in-depth semi-structured interviews with these workers over the phone. This arrangement suited our participants, as they were accustomed to discussing complex matters via the phone. Interviews were recorded and later transcribed. A total of 16 hours of material were collected. Additional discussions with managers and a demonstration of the triaging software informed our understanding of the case context, which was instrumental in facilitating and making sense of conversations with nurses. The interviews were very candid, with participants ofering rich accounts of and reflections on their activities, feelings, and practice.

Through this process we learned that triaging over the phone requires considerably diferent skills to those needed in hospital wards. This is corroborated by prior literature (see Ernesäter et al., 2009; Hafermalz & Riemer, 2020; Snooks et al., 2008; Wahlberg & Wredling, 2001). The work that went into figuring out this new way of nursing was significant, as was the challenge of dealing with setbacks and dificult calls. Nurses not only emphasised the stresses and challenges of their tasks but also the use of technology in connecting with one another during shifts. Most would log on to an instant messaging group chat at the start of their shift to make “small talk” with their colleagues. While they would rarely meet in person, fixed rosters allowed nurses to get to know each other online.

## 3.2. Methods and data analysis: an abductive research approach

Initial data screening revealed the importance of the instant messaging tool Ofice Communications Server (OCS) in providing the nurses with a dedicated channel for connecting and chatting with each other during their work shifts. OCS is an instant messaging system that integrates into Microsoft-based software applications. Its features enable instant messaging, file exchanges, voice or video calls and web-based conferences via a browser interface (Seeling, 2011). The nurses reported using primarily the instant messaging and screen sharing functionality. This enabled them to have conversations during their shifts and made it possible to see what other nurses were doing.

It became apparent that OCS was being used as a “back channel”. Shared chat windows in which it was possible to exchange dialogue, pictures, and emoticons, were used to connect on a personal level, but on initial analysis of the case, we found that this OCS chat also played an important yet subtle and complex role in organising the triaging work itself.

The notion of a “back channel” (Krauss et al., 1977) has been employed in prior IS research discussing communications in terms of Gofman’s (1959) notions of front stage and back stage (A. A. Dennis et al., 2010; Orlikowski, 1996), whereby technology provides a “safe enclave” (Niall Hayes & Walsham, 2000; N., 2001) in which groups can discuss matters privately when away from their “audience” (Gofman, 1959).

Yet, through reflexive (Alvesson & Sköldberg, 2009) iteration between our case material and close reading of Gofman, we noticed that the “front stage” and “back stage” dichotomy did not capture certain types of OCS use as described in our interviews. For example, the messaging system was also used while nurses were on a call (so, were engaged in a “frontstage” performance while using “backstage” communication). We, therefore, engaged further with Gofman’s text and found that his more nuanced work on communication out of character appeared to capture in more depth how nurses made use of ICTs, particularly OCS.

Guided by these insights, we subsequently employed theoretical coding and an abductive data analysis process, as outlined by Kevin G Corley and Gioia (2013). Our emphasis in this analysis was thus on deriving useful concepts that captured “qualities that describe or explain a phenomenon of theoretical interest” (Kevin G Corley & Gioia, 2013, p. 16), in our case communication out of character in the skilful performance of telenurse triaging. Our coding and analysis integrated empirical and theoretical material to develop these concepts. Abductive research involves the “systematic combining” (Dubois & Gadde, 2002, 2014) of inductive and deductive approaches, and is therefore diferent from grounded theory (K. G. Corley, 2015), which aims to find truth “in” the data itself without any particular theory guiding the analysis.

The assumptions that underpin an abductive research process are that 1) research participants are “knowledgeable agents” who are able to reflect on their life-worlds and 2) that as researchers we can usefully combine understandings that emerge from the field with our own interpretations including the use of theoretical material and frameworks to further and develop new concepts that help us to achieve a fuller understanding of the research phenomenon (Kevin G Corley & Gioia, 2013; Dubois & Gadde, 2002). We would further like to note that this research does not depend on large-scale cross-sectional data, but on theoretical generalising from in-depth analysis of data relevant to the phenomenon. In other words, we take interest in how nurses “go about constructing their experience and less on the number or frequency of measurable occurrences” (Kevin G Corley & Gioia, 2013, p. 16).

Consequently, in drawing on existing literature the research process transitioned from inductive, where we considered the case as a whole, to an abductive (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007), iterative process as we alternated between our observations and what we learned from Gofman. Through this iterative process of working with the data and with theory, we gained further insight into our case while at the same time extending Gofman’s categories to reflect what we observed in our data. We translate the voice of the research participants into an abstracted level of theorising that combines our interpretations with prior scholarship (Kevin G Corley & Gioia, 2013). True to our abductive approach, in the following section, we introduce Gofman’s work and iterate between his ideas and our analysis of the empirical material in deriving our own framework, which we present in full thereafter.

## 4. Analysis: communication out of character in remote work

Gofman (1959) uses the theatre stage as a metaphor for interaction in everyday life. Any interaction requires an audience to whom we perform, and teammates who help us to sustain our character in front of the audience. Team-mates are important also because we need someone with whom we can discuss the shared experience of the performance. Gofman’s (1959) work thus provides a foundational framework for studying the social aspects of interpersonal interactions in general, and the interdependent nature of teamwork in front of an audience (the client) more specifically.

A major conceptual contribution that has found traction in IS scholarship is Gofman’s (1959, p. 66) notion of “Regions and Region Behaviour”. In this work, Gofman extends an analysis of theatre to everyday life. In general, the front region or front stage is any space where the performance takes place in relation to the audience. On the “back stage” the “supressed facts” of the front stage can be aired (Gofman, 1959, p. 69).

The back stage region is where the performance is “constructed, rehearsed and contradicted” (Orlikowski, 1996, p. 77). Gofman (1959, p. 70, emphasis added) ofers the following description of how the back stage area is important to performance:

Here the team can run through its performance, checking for ofending expressions when no one is present to be afronted by them; here poor members of the team, who are expressively inept, can be schooled or dropped from the performance. Here the performer can relax; he can drop his front, forgo speaking his lines, and step out of character.

A key element of the back stage region then is that performers are able to “step out of character” and, as a team, relax, and analyse and improve on their performance, which is particularly pertinent in client-facing teamwork.

In our case, the nurse’s use of OCS, screen sharing, and telephone calls was not easily explained by just the temporal and spatial separation signified by the front stage/back stage dichotomy. Their interactions are much more clearly understood through Gofman’s wider framework of “communication out of character”, as the nurses are alternating between acting “in character” (as a nurse towards a client) and “out of character” (as a colleague among colleagues). While IS scholarship considers how technologies create and serve as separate front stage and back stage spaces (A. A. Dennis et al., 2010; Jackson, 1999; Kelly & Noonan, 2008; Pauleen & Yoong, 2001), the role of the back stage as a place to be “out of character” and the wider implications of the in/out of character distinction, have not yet found wide application. We, therefore, extended our analysis to consider how attention to types of communication out of character might provide an understanding of the seeming fluidity of social and professional interaction in clientfacing work, as exemplified by the nurses.

Gofman (1959, p. 108) identifies four specific types of communication out of character. These are 1) treatment of the absent, 2) staging talk, 3) team collusion and 4) realigning actions. Each of these are types of communication “in which the performer engages and which convey information incompatible with the impression oficially maintained during interaction” (Gofman, 1959, p. 108). Each type, therefore, involves some form of covert activity. They all refer to the communication of information that would spoil the “oficial” performance if the audience were to become aware of it, and yet is important for successful execution of a performance to “clients” as audience.

Gofman repeatedly acknowledges that communication out of character is important for collegiality however he does not go into detail as to the specific role such collegiality plays. Our abductive process allows us to build on his initial ideas to develop theory. Through combining Gofman’s ideas with our own insights we develop a framework that links each type of communication out of character to a function important in clientfacing remote work; these are 1) coping 2) learning 3) plotting and 4) positioning. We now introduce and illustrate the four types and show how technology is used to support each in a case of client-facing remote work.

## 4.1. Treatment of the absent: coping with dificult work situations

Treatment of the absent refers to how team members treat their absent audience. This communication occurs out of character before or after a performance, away from the audience. Gofman explains that when the audience leaves the performance space, or when a team of performers retreats back stage, the performing group “very regularly derogate the audience in a way that is inconsistent with the face-to-face treatment” that is given to them (Gofman, 1959, p. 168). Examples are sales staf imitating absent customers, or consultants after a meeting referring to clients by uncomplimentary nicknames.

Gofman reasons that derogation is more common back stage because it “serves to maintain the solidarity of the team, demonstrating mutual regard at the expense of those absent and compensating, perhaps, for the loss of self-respect that may occur when the audience must be accorded accommodative face-toface treatment” (Gofman, 1959, p. 169). Interestingly, such treatment of the absent has little to do with “the ‘actual’ feelings of the performers” towards the audience (Gofman, 1959, p. 173). Rather, sharing an impression of the audience that is contrary to how one is expected to act in front of them helps the performers save face and brings the team closer: “solidarity” is built at the expense of the audience.

In our case the nurses reported using OCS in this way, to share stories about or coin uncomplimentary nicknames for their clients, in particular “dificult” patients such as men who made sexually explicit phone calls. By derogating this absent audience in OCS, the nurses were able to restore a sense of normalcy and thereby better cope with these troublesome clients. Blair, a senior triage nurse, provides an example:

There’s one we call . . . [nickname] . . . We know him. He used to ring up and if he hadn’t spoken to you before he’d talk about his male appendage, shall we say, but once he got the message from you that you weren’t going to put up with that nonsense when he hears your name or voice he hangs up immediately. Never says a word. So if any of us start getting those we’re [on OCS] “oh, he’s back.” So we don’t mind because it helps our call times . . . we don’t mind it. We sometimes get up to a dozen a night.

The statement that “we don’t mind it” would be unremarkable in a co-located ofice; however, in the context of remote work it is a noteworthy response to a dificult situation, because it implies that there is a sense of group cohesion and a shared understanding of the challenge, followed by a mockery of it. At base, this reflects Gofman’s analysis of building “solidarity”, a form of belonging. In addition, we argue that treatment of the absent is important for what we term coping. This interpretation is supported by Gofman’s statement that such talk helps restore “self-respect” that is lost in the course of meeting the audience’s needs at the expense of one’s own.

The nurses explained that such “water cooler conversations” were important to helping them to get on with their next call and in the task of conducting themselves professionally once more. As one nurse put it: “the OCS is equivalent to bitching in the corner over a cuppa or a smoke [laughs]”. In their remote working environment, the nurses have come to replace the lunchroom or water cooler with OCS chat windows, as an important “back stage” space for communicating out of character away from the audience. Importantly, while seemingly peripheral to the work task, such communication directly contributed to that task’s competent performance through the function of coping with the performance.

## 4.2. Staging talk: learning the craft

“Staging talk” concerns the mechanics of a performance (Gofman, 1959, p. 173). It takes place primarily after a performance, in anticipation of the next one, and also contributes to “collegial solidarity”. Staging talk involves discussing how the performance was received, how it compares with past performances, what needs to change, how disruptions were dealt with and how they can be managed in the future, as well as an evaluation of the efectiveness of the stage setting and use of props (Gofman, 1959, p. 173).

In our case we found evidence of staging talk used to discuss the mechanics of the triaging process; to evaluate calls and to ofer each other tips, particularly on what phrasing would help them keep the caller happy while satisfying the requirements of the algorithmic triaging system’s prompts. Several nurses explained that such “shop talk” might seem unimportant but it was actually associated with crucial learning: “You learn a lot in chatting about what happened to people. It might seem like idle chatter but it’s not actually.” We found that such “idle chatter” took place over both OCS and, less often, the phone. This finding challenges a common assumption that remote workers may sufer isolation because they miss out on ofice “gossip” (D. E. Bailey & Kurland, 2002).

Staging talk also took place in training situations that involved the use of multiple technologies. Such interactions involved OCS, dual-line phones, and screen sharing software, highlighted here:

We’ll send them a message on the OCS “hi, this is Blair, let me know when you’re ready to log on, can you put yourself into coaching”, and then we connect and we chat on the phone for a while. [Trainees] listen to three of our calls, they have the ability to come in and listen sitting at their phone at their home. They can listen – they can also watch on their computer screen, because we share screens – to how we process the call. Then we’ll come together again on the phone and we’ll say “okay what did you think of that, what do you think of this,” that sort of thing and “do you understand why we did this, and do you understand why we did that”. We’ll talk about policy procedure.

The nurses thus used a range of technologies to first construct a shared experience of a situation, so that the mechanics of this performance could later be dissected, examined, and learned from. The conversational focus in these coaching sessions was on how the performance was received, what a skilful performance looked like, and how a recent performance could be improved.

While Gofman again links staging talk to the more general function of “solidarity”, we add a more nuanced understanding in proposing that out of character talk about the performance brings the team closer to each other by helping each performer in learning their character. In this case, what was being learned was how one acts as a triage telenurse. While each call is expected to take place in relative isolation, the nurses found ways to share their performances so that they can dissect them and refine what it means to be a telenurse. We thus propose that staging talk is strongly linked to learning one’s character, which in turn helps the team to maintain the credibility of their collective and individual professional identities.

## 4.3. Team collusion: plotting progression of a work performance

The previous two types of communication out of character relate to team discussions that happen before or after a performance, away from the front stage performance space. As such they are similar to “back stage” communications already found in IS literature. However, Gofman (1959) emphasises that communication out of character also happens during performances through what he terms team collusion, that is, “any collusive communication which is carefully conveyed in such a way as to cause no threat to the illusion that is being fostered by the audience” (Gofman, 1959, p. 175).

Consider how, in any situation, there is more than one “channel” of communication in play. For example, a prompter whispers forgotten lines to an actor; two teachers concur with a shared glance that the class activity should end; parents spell out a word to conceal its consideration from a child. These are examples of how, in co-located performances, one actor subtly colludes with a team mate in deciding the course of action while on stage.

We find team collusion is particularly challenging to perform in remote work contexts that are entirely mediated by ICTs, because of the requirement for subtle, nearly invisible and instantaneous interaction, while being engaged in a performance. While unreflective and fluid in co-located performances (a glance; a hand signal), many communication technologies instead require intentional actions such as dialling or typing, which takes efort and attention, and can even be physically challenging. It is therefore not easy to subtly communicate with one’s own team members while engaged in a professional performance. We find that such a barrier to collusive communication has potentially negative consequences for the efective performance of client-facing remote work.

The telenursing case gives a helpful illustration. In a co-located nursing scenario in a hospital, glances between colleagues may serve to silently call over assistance or perceive the need for assistance. We were told by a telenurse that in an emergency hospital situation, if a triage nurse is not correctly tending to a patient, other nurses would subtly intervene in helping to direct the “performance” without breaking the impression that the nurses are, as a team, professionally competent. For nurses working remotely, this assistance is harder to both give and receive, because in a remote context collusive communication requires: 1) skilful manipulation of several communication channels at once 2) the time required to either include another nurse in the call or to provide context without interrupting the call, 3) a way of dealing with an influx of assistance from team members, and 4) a mutual understanding of the meaning of coded signals. These challenges are particularly dificult to manage under time pressure and in life-threatening situations.

The dificulty in receiving real-time assistance while on calls manifested in anticipatory anxiety amongst newer telenurses. Eleanor for instance, had almost resigned shortly after starting in the role because of the “dread” she began to feel before taking a call. Much of this anxiety stemmed from a traumatic experience early in her telenurse employment that difered from her previous experiences in a co-located ofice:

When I’d worked in an ofice environment with a call centre before, you hold up this little sign which says “help” – if you need help. So you need someone to come, and then they’re there and they can help do things like call the police . . . but when you’re remote, you need to do that online. So I had to start an OCS chat, while I’m talking to the caller trying to get details . . . there was something about this call which was – it just threw me. So I was trying to work the chat and I was having a lot of problems with it. I didn’t know what was going on and I couldn’t seem to get help and I needed to keep the guy on the phone and down the bottom of my screen – because so many people were trying to talk to me to say “do you need help? Tell me the details” – their little chat boxes were just all lighting up down the bottom of the screen and everything was just going crazy on the screen. I felt really flustered.

We see that it was dificult both for Eleanor’s colleagues to know what assistance was required and whether or not someone had already stepped in to help. It was even more dificult for Eleanor to use surreptitious communication to secretly explain the situation and receive useful input on to how to proceed. While there were plenty of avenues available to her for assistance after the performance, it was harder to collude with team mates while she was “on stage”.

We did find examples of smoother execution, mostly amongst more experienced nurses who had learned to manipulate various channels in seeking assistance from the clinical manager on duty. On the whole however, this form of collusive communication and assistance, quite naturally done in a co-located environment, seemed to pose a challenge that nurses found dificult to deal with in an ICT-mediated context. This challenge could leave nurses feeling helpless or lost at a time when they were most in need of assistance; it could even impact a nurse’s ability to do her job.

We conclude that the role of team collusion is to agree upon and plan actions in situ, without the “audience” realising that assistance has been sought or that what they are witnessing is anything other than a competent performance. We, therefore, propose that team collusion serves the purpose of surreptitious, “on-stage” plotting, which we describe as a form of collective planning about what course of action should be taken by the performing team, while the performance is in play. Plotting is particularly important, yet also challenging, in stressful or high-stake situations, where advice and assistance are needed to sustain and execute a successful performance without the audience realising that something is askew. If a remote worker is unable to successfully engage in plotting, they may feel isolated and anxious about their ability to perform their work efectively in future.

## 4.4. Realigning actions: positioning oneself within the work context

The fourth type of communication out of character Gofman calls “realigning actions”. The three types of communication out of character we have introduced thus far are all hidden from the audience, either by taking the communication to another space or by disguising it. Realigning actions however are deliberately ambiguous in this respect (Gofman, 1959, p. 189). A feature of this type of communication is “double-talk”, which “involves the kind of innuendo that can be conveyed by both sides and carried on for a sustained period of time” (Gofman, 1959, p. 191). Double-talk is a layered communication that can have more than one meaning. For this reason, it is useful in situations where the social roles of those involved and their relationship to one another are ambiguous or complex.

Examples include a subordinate and their superior speaking to one another or acting in such a way that reveals an intimacy incongruent to their supposed social distance, but in such a way that this intimacy can be easily denied, even to themselves. For example, in a study of nurses in an operating theatre, a disgruntled nurse was observed “slapping an instrument into a surgeon’s hand with a resounding crack” (Riley & Manias, 2005, p. 6). While the nurse was acting as if she could publicly deride the surgeon, the gesture could easily be denied by both parties as misconstrued, with the usual social positioning of the two parties preserved (Gofman, 1959). This communication can be directed at both the audience and the team at the same time. Significant in such realigning actions are the issues of power and loyalty.

In our case, we found “double talk” in dealing with the ambiguity of who triaging calls were being performed to. This is because the nurses were mindful that calls were always being recorded and would potentially later be reviewed by a supervisor. A random sample of three calls for each nurse was reviewed monthly using audio recordings and a summary of how the algorithmic prompts were responded to. Each nurse was assessed on whether and how every question prompted by the computer system (the “guidelines”) had been addressed. The issue that arises is that, according to the nurses, the questions that the system would prompt were often inappropriate and would lead to the caller getting upset, yet policy demanded to follow its procedures. For example, questions relating to pregnancy would sometimes appear repeatedly even when not relevant. Hence, in these situations the nurse has to at once speak both to the primary audience (the client) and her own team (the call reviewer).

Blair described how she dealt with satisfying both demands at once through what can be considered a form of “double talk”:

If you asked a question about a kid’s temperature at the beginning of the whole process and suddenly a question popped up in what we call the guideline triage, about temperature, one way of satisfying the caller, which is not to make them feel stupid by asking that question again, and the reviewer, was to say “okay, now we’ve already discussed temperature, let’s move on.” That would be a way of addressing that question without acting an idiot and just getting that answer “I already told you he didn’t have a temperature”, that sort of reaction. It gave us the wiggle room . . . to deal with the guidelines.

This describes communication out of character for realigning actions because the nurse is talking at once to her call reviewers and is therefore in “employee” mode, while also maintaining the impression of being a caring nurse in “client-facing” mode. We add to Gofman by theorising that realigning actions serve the function of positioning oneself within the work context vis-à-vis diferent audiences simultaneously. It is thus a skilful form of communication that allows the nurse to perform to her main client audience in a caring way, while at the same time conveying out-of-character a kind of skilful performance in signalling to her nursing team mates that “I am one of us”, by using her tools correctly while also upholding the performance vis-à-vis the main audience.

We note that the recording of calls added a further facet to this positioning work because it allowed one intended audience of the realigning actions to be temporally and spatially distant from the performance itself. This observation raises opportunities for further remote work research, for example, into how an understanding of technological surveillance influences positioning work in a performance that ostensibly is directed at one audience, with knowledge of the anticipated involvement of a second audience at a later time.

## 5. Discussion

We have shown how telenurses engage in surreptitious communications about their audience, their role, and the performances they are involved in. We utilised Gofman’s (1959) four categories of “communication out of character” to make sense of how this talk about and alongside client-facing work plays out in a remote and distributed work environment. We now generalise these findings into two conceptual models with implications for client-facing distributed work beyond the nursing context.

Firstly, in Figure 1 we summarise the four activities of communication out character and their spatiotemporal dimensions (where and when they occur), and their focus of communication (the main orientation of the topic of conversation). We further argue that the outcome of this work of communication out of character is a sense of belonging that team members create, to both the team and their work. As shown in Figure 1, the activities associated with “coping” with dificult work situations and the joint “learning” from past performances are both done away from the performance, of stage. These activities contribute to a sense of belonging to each other and stronger team bonds that build the basis for better work performance, as experiences are shared and learned from and a sense of support is derived. In addition, both “plotting” and “positioning”, done on stage during the work performance, allow for better work outcomes and thus a stronger sense of belonging to the work itself, as team members derive a stronger sense of professional identity, or character.

We contribute to Gofman’s work by theorising that this process whereby one’s character is sustained through collective performance creates a sense of belonging to the group and its work performance.

Figure 2 shows a process model of what we term the Work of Belonging. We map the “virtuous cycle” (Chesbrough, 2006) of the work that goes into belonging to the team and supporting a performance. We show how 1) Work “on” the performance is associated with coping and learning. These activities serve to create a sense of collective team identity by diferentiating “us” from “them” (the audience). This diferentiation contributes to a sense of 2) Belonging to the team. Belonging to the team then acts as a resource for 3) Work “in” the performance, where plotting and positioning assist the appropriate performance of the task in relation to the audience. These activities serve to create a sense of shared professional identity as telenurses, further strengthening the sense of belonging. Once a performance has taken place, the capacity to talk about it arises, therefore creating the opportunity for a continuation of the cycle that when taken as a whole, we argue, captures the work of belonging. Overall, the framework shows that performing belonging and performing work are interconnected; they are “two sides of the same coin”.

With examples, we have illustrated how technology facilitates the work of belonging, through channels that enable communication out of character. We particularly draw attention to how belonging requires demarcating and positioning vis-à-vis external stakeholders. It is therefore important for the team to have technologically facilitated channels of communication available to them that the audience is excluded from – both for the sake of belonging and for the sake of work performance.

ICT-enabled remote work presents both challenges and opportunities here. Instant messaging, for example, is a useful “back channel” for communication out of character, but juggling several channels simultaneously for the sake of plotting during a performance can be overwhelming for inexperienced workers. We, therefore, highlight that new skills and even explicit strategies and rehearsal of collusive tactics are required to cope with the complexity of managing surreptitious communication in client-facing remote work.

## 6. Conclusion

The Covid-19 pandemic has pushed traditional work online, and previously co-located teams now work remotely and in a distributed way. This is particularly challenging for teams who need to perform vis-à-vis client audiences yet struggle to maintain a sense of being a team and find ways to support each other. We have highlighted how communication out of character, that typically happens efortlessly in co-located work settings, becomes troublesome and requires explicit attention in distributed ICT-mediated work contexts. As a result, new skills have to be gained to maintain or develop the kinds of supporting communication away from the audience that create a sense of belonging to both team and work. Our case study, carried out before the pandemic, is thus useful as it derives insights from telenurses, who are highly skilled remote workers, that can gainfully inform newly emerging remote work practices in other contexts.

Our findings have practical and theoretical implications. For the IS literature on remote work, we have theorised the role of back stage communication in a new way, using Gofman’s framework of communication out of character. Our final conceptual model (Figure 2) theorises how various types of communication out of character contribute to both the belonging of workers to their team (identity as a group) and to their work (professional identity) with implications for successful team performance.

We further suggest that for IS scholars researching remote work and virtual teams, the framework of the work of belonging can serve as a useful sensitising device. We suggest that a future methodological contribution is made, where in looking for the dynamics captured in Figure 2 – The Work of Belonging, researchers are encouraged to consider multiple channels of communication and to investigate the function and meaning attributed by the research participants to the use of these channels. Rather than focus on one platform (e.g., video call platform) as “front stage” and one (e.g., OCS) as “back stage”, it may be more productive to investigate how these categories are materialised in action and what purpose such enactments serve for those performing work in front of a client audience.

We argue that management intervention in remote teams should be considered cautiously. It has already been shown that “back stage” spaces, in particular, are candid spaces where workers share views necessarily contrary to their “oficial” work persona (Niall Hayes & Walsham, 2000). From a practical perspective, we argue that surreptitious communications play an important part in building the collective resource of belonging and this, we argue, is important for clientfacing work performance. Therefore while provision of guidelines, tools, and training in executing collusive communication is likely increasingly important in remote contexts, managerial intrusion in these channels could be counterproductive. We further counter rationalistic, task-based managerial views which may seek to separate the notion of belonging, as it is understood in terms of wellbeing, from eficiency initiatives relating to productivity. We emphasise that following the argument presented here, the social and task aspects of work, and therefore belonging and productivity, are strongly related, forming two sides of the same coin.
