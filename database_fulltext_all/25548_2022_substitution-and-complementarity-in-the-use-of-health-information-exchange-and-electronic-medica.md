# Substitution and complementarity in the use of health information exchange and electronic medical records

Liran Politi <sup>a</sup>, Shlomi Codish<sup>b</sup>, Iftach Sagy<sup>b</sup> and Lior Fink <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Industrial Engineering & Management, Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Beer Sheva, Israel; <sup>b</sup>Clinical Research Center, Soroka University Medical Center, Beer Sheva, Israe

## ABSTRACT

While considerable literature has addressed the separate use of health information systems, limited research has examined how the use of one system changes following the implementation of another. We address this gap in the literature by developing and testing hypotheses about substitution and complementarity in physicians’ use of health information exchange (HIE), which merges patient data from multiple sources, following the implementation of a local electronic medical record (EMR) system. The hypotheses are anchored in theories foundational to understanding the motivation for information seeking behaviour. The hypotheses are tested with unique data, recorded in system log files, that describe HIE use by physicians in an emergency department before and after EMR implementation. The findings confirm that system use is characterised by substitution when it is defined dichotomously as whether the system is accessed or not (lower HIE access rates after EMR implementation) and by complementarity when it is defined as the extent of system use (higher volume and duration of HIE use after EMR implementation). Our findings suggest that the information seeking literature has underestimated the explanatory value of the cost-benefit approach and the occurrence of complementarity in system use because of its overreliance on a narrow definition of use.

## KEYWORDS

System use; information seeking behaviour; health information exchange; electronic medical record; substitution and complementarity; log file analysis

## 1. Introduction

Motivated by expected clinical and financial benefits, governments and private sector organisations have been directing substantial resources in recent decades towards the digitisation and standardisation of health data by implementing health information systems that encompass, integrate, and exchange patient data (Blumenthal, 2009). A prominent example of these eforts is the US’s 2009 Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) act, which allocates over 27 USD billion to incentivise healthcare facilities to practice “meaningful use” of health information systems (Blumenthal & Tavenner, 2010). These eforts have been partially fruitful as reflected by the adoption of at least a “basic” health information system by 80% of US hospitals (Adler-Milstein et al., 2017).

These constructive eforts, however, have also increased the complexity of the clinical information ecosystem that surrounds a caregiver at the point of care, which is often distributed and consists of sources that are electronic, paper-based, and human. Moreover, despite attempts to form a single integrated interface for all medical data, the medical environment often includes multiple systems, which cater to diferent needs of storing and accessing patient data. This distributed nature of health data produces information gaps that potentially endanger patients and increase healthcare costs (Hillestad et al., 2005; Kaelber & Bates, 2007).

This evolution calls for a deeper understanding of how system use by physicians changes as additional systems are implemented at the point of care. Research in this domain, however, mainly examines the use of a single new system of interest, while the use of existing information sources and systems remains unexplored (e.g., Vest et al., 2011). Such an approach implicitly assumes that the efects of the introduction of a new system on existing systems are negligible, thereby misrepresenting the oftendisruptive consequences of system implementations on work processes (Cresswell & Sheikh, 2013). While this approach may be suitable to describe system use in absolute terms, it disregards possible changes in the use of existing systems following the introduction of a new system. Such changes may reflect adjustments in the perceived value and relative advantage of systems in addressing information needs.

Against this background, this study aims at advancing the understanding of how system use by physicians is afected by the introduction of a new system alongside an existing system. Specifically, we focus on two health information systems that have become dominant in contemporary medical environments: health information exchange (HIE) and electronic medical record (EMR) systems (Adler-Milstein et al., 2015). Whereas an HIE merges patient data from multiple sources within and across organisational boundaries (Hersh, 2009; Vest & Gamm, 2010), an EMR focuses on within-facility information and supports the entry of clinician notes and orders. The research question addressed in this study is: How is HIE use by physicians afected by the transition from having an HIE as the only system to having access to both an HIE and an EMR?

From a theoretical standpoint, this research question is addressed by drawing on the vast literature about information seeking (e.g., Case & Given, 2016; Ingwer sen & Järvelin, 2005), in particular about the motivation at the basis of such behaviour and whether it is driven by the principle of least efort (Poole, 1985) or by costbenefit considerations (Atkin, 1973; Hardy, 1982). Whereas research on information seeking behaviour has generally employed narrow definitions of use, where an information source is either used or not used, this study employs a more elaborate conceptualisation of system use as a two-stage decision process, in which the user first makes a dichotomous-use decision of whether or not to use the system and, if this decision is positive, decides on the extent-of-use. The theories foundational to understanding the motivation for information seeking behaviour serve as the conceptual basis for hypothesising that dichotomous-use of the HIE is suppressed by the introduction of the EMR, implying system substitution, while extent-of-use of the HIE is facilitated by the introduction of the EMR, implying system complementarity.

From an empirical standpoint, the research question is addressed by focusing on a medical environment that accentuates the conditions that have been shown to be consequential for information seeking behaviour. Specifically, we focus on treatments of critically-ill patients in the emergency department (ED), where forming an efective care plan is contingent on obtaining longitudinal data in a timely manner (Hripcsak et al., 2007). Approximately one in every three ED treatments is estimated to occur under critical information deficits (Stiell et al., 2003), whose resolution requires comprehensive knowledge of patient history that can primarily be acquired by utilising health information systems. The ED setting is most suitable for addressing the above research question because it is also characterised by considerable information seeking costs, requiring physicians to be eficient in expending their limited cognitive resources. We leverage a unique dataset that describes treatments of critically-ill patients that were conducted prior to and after the introduction of an EMR in an ED in which an HIE was available throughout the research period. The dataset describes the use of an HIE by physicians, as documented in the system log files. It also includes an expansive set of control variables, which describe the treated patients, situation, the availability of information in the HIE, the physicians using the HIE, and the dynamics of HIE use.

The present study makes significant contributions to both research and practice. First, this study advanc es the understanding of a situation in the use of information systems that, despite its prevalence, has received little attention in the literature, primarily by observing self-reported perceptions and intentions (Strauss et al., 2015; Thorn et al., 2014). Although this study focuses on medical environments, the situa tion where a new system is implemented alongside an existing system is not unique to such environments and can be observed across various work and home environments. As the theoretical foundations of this study are derived from the literature on information seeking behaviour, its conceptualisations and findings can straightforwardly inform other investigations into system use in multi-system environments. Second, this study makes a theoretical contribution to the literature on information seeking behaviour by ofer ing an avenue to reconcile existing disagreements and contradictions about whether such behaviour is best explained by the principle of least efort or by cost benefit considerations and whether anticipated bene fits play a role in use decisions. This work suggests that the importance of benefits, relative to that of costs, has been underestimated in the literature because of the common definition of use as a single dichotomous decision. It proposes that both the principle of least efort and the cost-benefit approach are important in explaining system use when it is viewed as a process of multiple use decisions. Third, from a practical stand point, this study can inform the design and implemen tation of HIE and EMR systems to better support the information needs of physicians when both systems are available simultaneously. Furthermore, as available or lacking information can significantly afect the suc cess of emergency treatments, the way systems co serve the physician in addressing information needs may influence treatment outcomes. With considerable resources invested in recent years in the broader and deeper implementation of health information systems, it is important to facilitate the understanding of the efects of system implementation on the use of systems already in place. The present study represents an important step in this direction.

This paper is organised as follows: Section 2 provides the background for this work by introducing the research setting of EMR implementation when an HIE is available and by anchoring the study in the literature on information seeking behaviour. Section 3 is dedicated to hypothesis development following the conceptualisation of system use as a two-stage process and the definitions of substitution and complementarity in information seeking behaviour. Section 4 provides a detailed account of the clinical setting, the examined HIE and EMR systems, as well as the unique dataset that is utilised to test hypotheses. Section 5 presents the results of our analyses and of tests conducted to confirm their robustness. In Section $^ { 6 , }$ we interpret our findings and their implications, while discussing limitations and avenues for future research.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Health information systems: HIE and EMR

HIEs merge patient data from multiple sources within and across organisational boundaries to display integrated patient files (Hersh, 2009; Vest & Gamm, 2010). The expected benefits of HIEs are vast in terms of improved patient safety and financial eficiency (Frisse et al., 2012; Shapiro et al., 2016), particularly when a longitudinal view of patient history is essential, as is commonly the case in the ED (Hripcsak et al., 2007). However, the realisation of these expected benefits has been a matter of controversy: while some studies present improvements in care delivery (e.g., Bailey et al., 2013; Menachemi et al., 2018), others report mixed findings (Shapiro et al., 2016). This controversy partially hinges on a lack of agreement on the way the efectiveness of such implementation projects should be measured (Vest & Gamm, 2010). Nevertheless, HIE implementations have become increasingly widespread since the electronic exchange of health information was defined as an important qualifier of the “meaningful use” criterion (Adler-Milstein et al., 2014).

This work pertains to a stream of studies that explore health information systems by means of examining their actual use patterns, particularly at the user level. This standpoint is essential for several reasons, at the basis of which is the observation that system usage is not guaranteed by system availability (Rudin et al., 2014). The first reason is that the compatibility of health information systems with user workflow is one among many organisa tional and technical factors required for their successful implementation (Ortiz de Guinea & Webster, 2013; Shapiro et al., 2007). Understanding actual use is, hence, an imperative step towards designing systems that return their investment by fulfiling their potential (Agarwal et al., 2010; Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). Second, measuring use can also reveal whether successful implementation (Johnson & Gadd, 2007) and meaningful use are obtained by focusing on factors that are meaningful in the caregiving process, such as information needs, time spent on information search, and time to decision (Karsh et al., 2010). Last, studies demonstrate a positive association between encounter-level use and improved clinical outcomes (Ben-Assuli et al., 2013), which may be, to some extent, attributed to the voluntariness of use (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). Therefore, understanding how health information systems are used is crucial to understanding whether and how their implementation can lead to clinical and financial benefits.

However, studying the use of a specific system in isolation may fail to reflect the reality of clinical environments. HIEs are often used in tandem with local systems,

EMRs in particular (The National Alliance for Health Information Technology, 2008). While HIEs are typically read-only systems with the added value of access to information that originates outside the facility, EMRs focus on internal information and support the entry of clinician notes and orders. As the use of HIEs and EMRs for forming care plans is often voluntary, a physician can utilise either, both, or none of these systems to address information needs.

Due to the increase in the deployment of EMR and HIE functionalities in hospitals (Adler-Milstein et al., 2015), understanding their combined use has become highly important but has received insuficient attention in the literature. A few studies have explored the use of an HIE and an EMR when both are available, yet they have mostly relied on self-reported perceptions, intentions, and information needs (Strauss et al., 2015; Thorn et al., 2014), which may be biased due to their subjective nature. There is, therefore, a persistent need to empirically explo re actual system usage and the factors that motivate it. Objective empirical evidence is also valuable as it can reduce uncertainty among healthcare providers about the implications of health information systems, as such uncertainty has been identified as an inhibitor of their successful adoption and implementation (Fichman et al., 2011).

## 2.2. Information seeking behaviour: least efort and cost-benefit considerations

Whereas research on information seeking, at least on the use of such sources as books or newspapers, dates back about a century, much of this older literature was initially source- and later system-oriented, as well as focused on how information sources and formal information systems served users (Case & Given, 2016). It was not until the 1970s that emphasis began to shift away from formal systems towards people as finders and users of information. This shift resulted in the emergence of two disparate research camps: information retrieval, describing system (i.e., algorithmic) behaviour, and information seeking, describing user behaviour (Ingwersen & Järvelin, 2005). Wilson (1981) suggested that “information-seeking behavior results from the recognition of some need, perceived by the user” (p. 2). Dervin (1998) considered information seeking behaviour as a bridge – a means of closing the gap between situation and outcome. These two researchers, as well as others (e.g., Ellis, 1989; Kuhlthau, 1991; Leckie et al., 1996), laid the theoretical foundations for research on information seeking behaviour in the last decades.

An important theme in research on information seeking behaviour has been to understand why a user decides to seek additional information. Two, somewhat contradicting theoretical approaches have been promoted to explain information seeking decisions. The first is the principle of least efort, which suggests that behaviour is guided by the desire to minimise efort. This principle, proposed by Zipf (1949), was later shown to describe information seeking behaviour, as evident across information seeking studies (Poole, 1985). In the context of information seeking, this principle suggests that users seek information in the most economical way until minimally acceptable results are found. However, economists have suggested that users may be better of by continuing to seek information beyond the point of minimally acceptable results, because the benefits associated with the additional information may outweigh the costs of acquiring it. This argument is the basis for the second theoretical approach employed to explain information seeking behaviour – the cost-benefit approach. According to this approach, optimal decisions are the consequence of maximising benefits over costs (Mishan, 1976; Prest & Turvey, 1966). Whereas cost-benefit considerations have been frequently discussed in a broader decisionmaking context (e.g., policy making, project investment decisions), our focus here is on such considerations in understanding the behaviour of individuals. The costbenefit approach has been shown to explain human behaviour in various contexts, such as prosocial behaviour (Piliavin et al., 1969), and it has been shown to be valuable in explaining information search by consumers. In a seminal paper, Stigler (1961) analytically showed that buyers should search for information about sellers and their prices as long as the cost of search is lower than the expected marginal benefit of the search.

These two theoretical approaches yield contradicting predictions of information seeking behaviour. Whereas the principle of least efort suggests that only the costs of information seeking determine the likelihood of such behaviour (Allen, 1977; Curley et al., 1990; Rosenberg, 1967), the cost-benefit approach suggests that both costs and benefits are consequential (Atkin, 1973). Trying to solve this theoretical contradiction, as well as explain the accumulated evidence in support of both approaches, Hardy (1982) surveyed the use of information sources (e.g., public libraries, professional manuals, and colleagues) by forest service professionals. His findings suggested that both costs and benefits determine information seeking behaviour, although costs are about twice as important as benefits in choosing information sources.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Given our research setting (Section 2.1), and drawing on the theoretical foundations of information seeking behaviour (Section 2.2), we hypothesise about how the information seeking behaviour of physicians in a constrained setting (the ED) changes when a new system is introduced alongside an existing system. Specifically, we hypothesise about substitution and complementarity in the use of an HIE when an EMR is implemented alongside the HIE. Because empirical research on information seeking behaviour has generally employed limited, unidimensional definitions of use, commonly measured via structured questionnaires (e.g., Allen, 1977; Curley et al., 1990; Hardy, 1982), we employ a more elaborate conceptualisation of system use as a two-stage decision process. According to this conceptualisation, the user makes two sequential decisions when an information system is available to address an existing information need: a dichotomous-use decision of whether to use the system, and an extent-of-use decision. Naturally, the second decision is irrelevant unless the first decision is positive. This conceptualisation of system use provides a basis for hypothesising about the existence of both substitution and complementarity in system use, where system substitution is founded on the importance of cost considerations and system complementarity is founded on the importance of benefit considerations.

## 3.1. Dichotomous-use and extent-of-use

Whereas empirical studies on the use of multiple information sources have typically measured dichotomoususe of systems (e.g., Kujath, 2011; Wu et al., 2006), a dichotomous account of system use is often insuficient when examining user engagement (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Observing both dichotomous-use and extent-of-use can yield multidimensional descriptions of system use. The extent of system use is commonly measured by observing the two key dimensions of information search: the volume of consumed information and the time required to consume it (Jackson & Farzaneh, 2012; J. W. Payne & Bettman, 2004). Similar approaches were adopted in studies that described the extent of HIE use (Politi et al., 2014; Vest & Jasperson, 2012).

The volume of consumed information is often measured as the number of information items that are accessed by the user (Kock, 2000; Politi et al., 2014; J. W. Payne & Bettman, 2004). This measure is associated with the utilisation of memory and attention (J. W. Payne & Bettman, 2004). However, it implicitly assumes that various information items are similarly informative to the user, an assumption that may not be accurate, particularly in the context of health information systems (Johnson et al., 2011).

Measuring the duration of information consumption, in addition to its volume, alleviates this dificulty by better reflecting user interaction, interest, and engagement with information sources. Moreover, the dimension of time is often considered an inherent component of eficient decision making (Karsh et al., 2010; Xue & Harker, 2002) and as a key cognitive resource that governs information search (Davies, 2007). In the medical context, information search may consume a large part of the time devoted to the diagnostic efort (Lin et al., 2010). The duration of use is often measured as the time devoted to an information source or to specific information items (Politi et al., 2014; De Waal et al., 2005).

Importantly, many of the studies cited above relied on self-reported behaviour, potentially being exposed to the biases associated with self-reporting. Such biases have been shown to exist in physicians’ accounts of their information needs (Davies, 2007). By contrast, the current study quantifies use behaviour with unmediated measures of actual system use.

## 3.2. Substitution and complementarity in information seeking behaviour

The theory of consumer behaviour in microeconomics provides loose definitions of substitution and complementarity in describing the consumption of commodities. Specifically, “two commodities are substitutes if both can satisfy the same need of the consumer; they are complements if they are consumed jointly in order to satisfy some particular need” (Henderson & Quandt, 1958, p. 29). In the setting of this study, the commodities being consumed by a consumer to satisfy a need are the systems, namely HIE and EMR, being used by a physician to satisfy an information need. Consistent with the definitions above, the two systems are considered substitutes if any can be used to satisfy the same information need of the physician, and they are considered complements if they are used jointly in order to satisfy some particular information need.

Empirical research on the substitution and complementarity of information sources has been driven both by the uniqueness of information goods (Varian, 1998) and by the pervasiveness of settings in which multiple sources are available (e.g., Bei et al., 2004; Fodness & Murray, 1998; Wu et al., 2006). A study by De Waal et al. (2005) described the associations of substitution and complementarity in using online newspapers and other information sources (e.g., printed newspapers, television, and other online sources) with the type of desired information and consumer attributes. Male respondents demonstrated substitutional patterns of use both by restricting information consumption to fewer sources and by limiting the duration of use of other sources when they use online newspapers. The study found that users who expressed a preference for online newspapers usually refrained from using the printed edition altogether (i.e., substitution). Interestingly, the duration of use of printed newspapers did not change when they were accessed together with online ones. Complementary use was demonstrated by female respondents, whose duration of use of online newspapers and other information sources were positively correlated. These findings suggest that substitution and complementarity in the use of information sources can both be observed in the same research setting contingent on the definition of use.

## 3.3. Efect of EMR availability on HIE dichotomous-use

The principle of least efort and the cost-benefit approach to information seeking both suggest that users aim at minimising the resources expended in information seeking. In the setting of this study, resources are cognitive capacities and intangible means that can be allocated by a physician towards seeking information for the treatment of a patient. Time capacity is arguably the most important resource at the physician’s disposal and is considered a key resource of information search (Davies, 2007; Jackson & Farzaneh, 2012). In the context of emergency care, time constraints are often imposed by task-related factors, such as the medical condition of the patient and situational workload (Vankipuram et al., 2013). An additional cognitive resource is short-term working memory, which is known to be severely limited (Sweller, 1988). Working memory is afected by problem complexity (e.g., making a diagnosis on the basis of observed symptoms and patient history) and utilising it for a highly detailed and complex task may lead to cognitive overload, specifically, the exhaustion of cognitive resources (Mayer & Moreno, 2003; Sweller, 1988). The last cognitive resource we consider is attention, which is fundamental to the information processing approach and is often deemed the most important scarce resource for decision makers (Simon, 1978). Attention is the ability to allocate processing resources to a source of information while disregarding others (Fernandez-Duque & Johnson, 1999) – an ability that is damaged when attention is split between multiple sources (Chandler & Sweller, 1991).

The medical setting has been the focus of multiple studies on information retrieval and processing under limited cognitive resources (Davies, 2007; Dawes & Sampson, 2003; Hall & Walton, 2004). The mere multiplicity of information sources (e.g., the Internet and libraries) and the sheer volume of available information often lead physicians to experience overload of cognitive resources (Hall & Walton, 2004). When these sources present ambiguous or redundant information, increased utilisation of cognitive resources is often observed (Eppler & Mengis, 2004; Zwaanswijk et al., 2011). Various characteristics of emergency care are associated with further exhaustion of cognitive resources, among which are increased time constraints (V. L. Payne & Patel, 2013), case complexity, task interruptions, and collaborative caregiving (Eppler & Mengis, 2004; Hall & Walton, 2004).

Lack of cognitive resources often leads to changes in information retrieval, information processing, and decision making (Eppler & Mengis, 2004), as well as to limited knowledge acquisition (Chandler & Sweller, 1991). Individuals with little available cognitive resources often adapt their behaviour by applying cognitive simplification mechanisms, such as shortcuts and rules-ofthumb (J. W. Payne & Bettman, 2004). Aimed at coping with overload, these mechanisms are often efective but sometimes result in sub-optimal decisions (J. W. Payne & Bettman, 2004). When multiple information inputs are available, they are commonly accessed for shorter periods, implicitly prioritised, and low-priority inputs are often disregarded (Eppler & Mengis, 2004). Physicians experiencing lack of cognitive resources have been shown to adopt information collection and processing patterns that may be biased (Croskerry, 2003; Dawes & Sampson, 2003; Kostagiolas et al., 2014; V. L. Payne & Patel, 2013).

We study the constrained setting of an ED in which an EMR is introduced alongside an HIE, implying that physicians gain the ability to search for information in two diferent systems instead of one. As the EMR is typically used for visit documentation purposes, its access rates are commonly high. When both an HIE and an EMR are available, physicians frequently prefer not to access the HIE, as they often estimate the expected benefit of utilising an HIE to be low (Yaraghi et al., 2015). Studies report that less than 50% of encounters include access to an HIE in addition to an EMR (Rudin et al., 2014). The HIE is sometimes accessed only if no relevant information is first found in the EMR (Thorn et al., 2014), because redundant or outdated information in the HIE may consume cognitive resources, such as time and memory, while addressing too few information needs (Strauss et al., 2015; Zwaanswijk et al., 2011).

Physicians with EMR access state that they use an HIE mainly when treating critically-ill patients and when they are in need of older medical information or a wider perspective of the patient (e.g., longitudinal views of past tests) (Strauss et al., 2015). Accordingly, the introduction of an EMR to an environment with an HIE may incur the reallocation of information seeking eforts from the HIE to the EMR. The introduction of an additional information source implies that some information needs can be addressed by the new system instead of the older one. This shift is expected to occur because information search is often done “strategically”: information sources are employed according to their likelihood of satisfying a specific information need (Katz et al., 1974). Indeed, earlier research showed that the use of “traditional media” changed due to the introduction of the Internet (Althaus & Tewksbury, 2000; De Waal et al., 2005). In the medical context, a study by Kannampallil, Franklin, et al. (2013) examined critical care delivery where an EMR and paper charts were the physician’s primary sources of information. The findings showed that physicians exhibited substitutional patterns of use by employing the source that better satisfied their information need.

Given this research setting, we hypothesise that dichotomous-use of the HIE is suppressed by the introduction of the EMR, implying system substitution. All else being equal, the principle of least efort clearly implies that a user is unlikely to use an older system after the introduction of a new system if the user did not use the older system when it was the only one available. Aiming at the minimisation of efort, the user is more likely to substitute use of the older system with use of the new system. Furthermore, the information seeking literature suggests that costs are significantly more consequential than benefits for information seeking decisions (Hardy,

1982). On the costs side, the ED setting is characterised by limited personnel, long working hours, distributed work, high workload, in addition to complicated and lifethreatening medical conditions, which imply that information seeking costs are high for physicians. On the benefits side, physicians often predict they will gain little added value from utilising an HIE when an EMR is also available (Yaraghi et al., 2015). Consequently, it is unlikely that the benefits of HIE use outweigh the costs of use, especially if costs are weighted more heavily than benefits. Together, both the principle of least efort and the costbenefit approach support a hypothesis that the likelihood that an HIE is used decreases when an EMR becomes available.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). The efect of EMR availability on HIE dichotomous-use reflects system substitution: the likelihood of HIE use is lower when an EMR is available compared to when it is not.

## 3.4. Efect of EMR availability on HIE extent-ofuse

H1 predicts that the likelihood of HIE use declines following EMR implementation. However, H1 does not suggest that the likelihood of HIE use reaches zero, as some ED encounters are likely to include HIE use even when an EMR is available. Put diferently, the dichotomous-use decision for an HIE may be positive under certain circumstances. The costbenefit approach to information seeking suggests that these circumstances occur when the benefits of using an additional information source outweigh the costs of use. The previous section suggested that the ED setting is characterised by high costs and that physicians often estimate the expected benefits of HIE use, when an EMR is available, to be low. In such a setting, especially if costs are weighted more heavily than benefits (Hardy, 1982), benefits are expected to outweigh costs only if benefits are unusually high. Under such circumstances, it is predicted that the HIE is used despite the availability of an EMR.

The literature considers the benefits of information seeking as the ability to address information needs and bridge information gaps. Dervin (1983) defined an information gap as a discrepancy or lack of “sense”, which arises within an individual who, in turn, strives to fill it with information. Such gaps and the uncertainty associated with them have been identified as fundamental inducers of information seeking and use (Case & Given, 2016; Dervin, 1983), in particular among physicians (Fox, 1959). An individual seeking to bridge information gaps and minimise uncertainty often does so by utilising various sources of information (Case & Given, 2016). Information gaps drive individuals to engage in the search for information to bridge those gaps, although such eforts may not always decrease uncertainty; relevant information is not always found and additional information may give rise to additional gaps (Case & Given, 2016).

Therefore, while a significant need to bridge information gaps may drive physicians to search for information in additional sources, the use of multiple sources, by itself, may induce additional information gaps and further facilitate information seeking. The complexity of an environment is a function of the components whose comprehension is necessary for understanding the environment as a whole (Simon, 1973). This complexity increases with the number of components and their interrelationships (Kannampallil, Cohen, et al., 2013), as these components also represent possible sources of uncertainty and information gaps. The components of the medical environment (e.g., patient, caregiver, and information sources) are interrelated and are the source of many gaps related to the patient’s medical condition, the caregiver’s knowledge, and conflicting or inaccurate information. Additional systems are, hence, additional interrelated components, which potentially enhance information gaps.

The decision to use a system reflects a belief that the expected benefit, in terms of resolved information gaps, is worth the expected cost, in terms of invested cognitive resources (Strauss et al., 2015; Thorn et al., 2014). However, the use of multiple information sources often requires not only the understanding of the information in each source separately, but also the integration of the information to obtain coherence across sources (Stadtler et al., 2013). As a result of the use of multiple sources, information gaps may emerge and induce system complementarity. Such gaps may arise due to discrepancies or contradictions between information found in the two systems. This account is supported by a study on the information seeking patterns of physicians in paper charts and EMRs (Kannampallil, Franklin, et al., 2013). The study showed that in cases in which both sources were used, they were used to complete missing details, thereby exploiting the sources in a complementary fashion.

Furthermore, a physician who decides to use a system but fails to find relevant information in it may be susceptible to the sunk-cost fallacy, which increases an individual’s desire to commit additional resources in the case of failure, when unrecoverable expenditures (e.g., invested cognitive resources) have been made (Arkes & Blumer,

1985). This fallacy exists when alternative information sources are considered (Oulasvirta et al., 2009). It may induce extensive system use particularly when an alternative system is available, because the expected benefits and costs of system use are more salient when an alterna tive is available. This fallacy, therefore, serves as another explanation of complementarity in system use.

In conclusion, a positive HIE dichotomous-use decision is less likely after EMR implementation (H1), but when it occurs, we hypothesise that HIE extent-of-use is higher than before EMR implementation. Such system complementarity is theoretically explained by considerable benefits of HIE use, deduced from the fact that the physician chose to use the HIE despite the principle of least efort and the higher weight of costs. Such system complementarity is also explained by the likelihood of additional information gaps that arise from the actual use of multiple information sources. These benefits of HIE use, whether pre-existing or emerging, should be reflected in higher levels of the two key dimensions that are commonly observed to measure extent-of-use: volume and duration of use (Section 3.1).

Hypothesis 2 (H2). The efect of EMR availability on HIE extent-of-use, when it is used, reflects system complementarity: the volume and duration of HIE use are higher when an EMR is available compared to when it is not.

Table 1 summarises the theoretical arguments at the basis of the two hypotheses, which are founded on the conceptualisation of HIE use as a two-stage decision process. The first user decision is whether or not to use the HIE. The principle of least efort suggests that the minimisation of costs (cognitive resources) is the dominant consideration in this decision, resulting in system substitution, observed as a decrease in the likelihood of HIE use following EMR implementation. Upon deciding to use the HIE, the second user decision is the extent (volume and duration) of HIE use. The cost-benefit approach suggests that the earlier decision to use the HIE indicates that the expected benefits (bridging information gaps) of system use outweigh the costs, resulting in system complementarity, observed as an increase in the extent of HIE use following EMR implementation. Together, the two hypotheses suggest that following EMR implementation, the HIE is expected to be used less frequently, but more extensively when actually used.

## 4. Methods

The hypotheses are tested with data on 1493 ED encounters of physicians with critically-ill adult patients in Soroka University Medical Centre (SUMC), a large tertiary Israeli hospital. The dataset includes all encounters that took place during 2011–2012 (888 encounters), before the implementation of an EMR in an ED in which an HIE was available, and during 2015 (605 encounters), after EMR implementation. The HIE was available throughout the research period, and the EMR implementation period (2013–2014) was excluded. The dataset used to test the hypotheses consists of HIE use indicators (dependent variables), computed based on HIE log files, and a comprehensive set of variables that describe the patient, situation, available information in the HIE, physician, and HIE use dynamics. The focus on critically-ill patients in a single ED aims to control for the confounding efects of organisational factors (e.g., Speedie et al., 2014).

## 4.1. Setting

The setting of this study is a busy medical ED, which treats over 150,000 patients annually, in SUMC. The catchment area of SUMC includes over 1 million people, and it is operated by Clalit Health Services, the largest healthcare provider in Israel that provides coverage for over 50% of the Israeli population and 67% of the population in the hospital’s vicinity. Some of the patients treated in the medical ED are defined by the medical staf as critically-ill and are treated at the resuscitation room (RR), a sub-unit that operates solely when such treatment is required. The RR, equipped with advanced medical equipment, is adjacent to the ED, although physically separated from it. Treatments given in this RR are the focus of the current study.

When a patient arrives at the RR, either directly by emergency services or transferred from the ED based on vital signs, a designated senior physician on duty in the ED moves to the RR until treatment is completed. Survi ving patients are admitted either to an intensive care unit or to an appropriate ward. Critically-ill patients in this setting are typically in a non-communicative state, rendering secondary medical information sources as the primary source of historical clinical data. These sources include health information systems (as described in the next section), medical paper charts (that document vital signs and treatment given by paramedics and in the RR), and family members.

## 4.2. Health information systems

An HIE implementation project was carried out in 2005 throughout facilities operated by Clalit Health Services, including SUMC. This HIE, called OFEK HIE (dbMotion), grants access to patient-level data from all facilities operated by Clalit Health Services, although only from these facilities. This HIE provides read-only access to the following internal (originating in SUMC) and external (originating outside SUMC) historical data: discharge summaries from hospital wards and ED admissions; laboratory and imaging test results; outpatient and community clinic visits; diagnoses; medication history; as well as surgeries and procedures. Upon user request, the HIE gathers information on a specific patient and displays an integrative patient record, which is discarded when the record is closed. Until 2014, the HIE was the sole health information system in the ED. No change was made in the scope of the HIE during the research period.

In 2012, an EMR called Chameleon EMR (Elad Systems) was implemented in the internal wards of SUMC. In 2014, this EMR was implemented in the medical ED and RR, alongside the HIE. The medical data that can be examined via the EMR is restricted only to data created since 2012 within SUMC. However, the EMR provides read-and-write abilities for care management and is used by physicians to document treatments and view clinician notes, test results, diagnoses, medication history, in addition to surgeries and procedures. The HIE and EMR overlap in data created within SUMC (i.e., internally) since 2012. The two systems difer by the inclusion of external or pre-2012 information items only in the HIE and by the ability of only the EMR to ofer editing capabilities.

Patients are included in the HIE and EMR automatically and their consent is not required. No fees are incurred per system use, access to data is subject to user permissions, and data are available at all times. An automatic log-out is enforced when the HIE is idle for 30 minutes. Both systems are accessible from all computers available at the RR computer station. Throughout the research period, no change was made in the RR in terms of work protocols, layout (i.e., computer stations or treatment equipment), or the use of paper charts for the documentation of vital signs.

## 4.3. Data

We analyse data related to all RR treatments given in SUMC to adult (i.e., age 18 and above) patients during 2011–2012, in which only an HIE was available, and during 2015, in which an EMR was additionally available. Data were collected following the approval of SUMC’s institutional review board. Drawing on previous research (Politi et al., 2014), the hypotheses are tested at two levels of analysis: the encounter level and the physician-encounter level.

An observation at the encounter level is an encounter with a specific patient by one or more physicians. Therefore, HIE use is defined as use of the system at the aggregate level by all physicians who treated the specific patient. The encounter dataset is employed to test H1, about the efect of EMR availability on HIE dichotomoususe (i.e., use versus no-use), because no-use can be attributed to a specific patient but not to a specific physician. Consequently, physician-related variables are unavailable when observing dichotomous-use. The encounter dataset includes 1493 observations (888 in 2011–2012 and 605 in 2015) of all encounters during the research period that either did or did not include HIE use.

An encounter level observation consists of one or more physician-encounter observations. An observation at the physician-encounter level is the intersection of a specific physician during an encounter with a specific patient. Therefore, HIE use is defined as use of the system by the specific physician who treated the specific patient. The physician-encounter dataset is employed to test H2, about the efect of EMR availability on HIE extent-of-use (i.e., volume and duration). This dataset includes 1942 observations (1262 in 2011–2012 and 680 in 2015) of HIE use. This dataset includes only encounters with HIE use (i.e., at least one HIE screen was accessed by a physician), in which 1.6 physicians, on average, accessed the system per encounter.

4.3.1. Dependent variables: measures of HIE use HIE use is measured in this study by manipulating HIE log files as a means of overcoming methodological issues that stem from self-reporting and measurement unreliability, which are prevalent in healthcare settings (Zheng et al., 2015). Multiple studies recommend and employ log file analysis in the exploration of HIE use (Johnson & Gadd, 2007; Vest & Jasperson, 2010). Each record in the HIE log files represents a screen display and contains the name of the displayed screen, a timestamp that indicates the time of access, as well as identifiers of the patient and physician.

HIE dichotomous-use is set to 1 for encounters in which at least one HIE screen was displayed and to 0 otherwise, implying no HIE access during the encounter. HIE extent-of-use is measured as the volume and duration of HIE use in each physician-encounter session (hereinafter referred to as session). The volume of HIE use is measured as the total number of screen displays per session. Any screen display is counted, even if that screen was viewed more than once during the session. To measure the duration of HIE use, it is necessary to first calculate the duration of display for each screen as the diference, in seconds, between the timestamps of two screens that were displayed consecutively. A common practice in log file analysis is the truncation of display times using a threshold, which represents the duration above which the user has likely left the system (Cooley, 2003). The application of a threshold aims at mitigating the efects of overly long and unreliable time measurements. Interviews we conducted with ED physicians indicated that a single screen is seldom viewed in the RR for longer than 30 seconds. Consequently, we apply a more conservative threshold of 60 seconds and truncate any display time that is above 60 seconds to this value. Once display duration is calculated for each screen, it is possible to measure display duration for each session. We use the median duration rather than the total duration (sum of display durations) because the latter is strongly correlated with the volume of use (Pearson correlation is 0.934, p< 0.001). The mean duration is not used as the summary statistic because it is more afected by truncation, extreme values, and the volume dimension. Given use of the median as the summary statistic for duration, truncation afects only 3.3% of session duration values.

## 4.3.2. Control variables

Control variables are divided into groups that characterise the (I) patient, (II) situation, (III) information available in the HIE, (IV) physician (HIE user), and (V) HIE use dynamics during an encounter. These variables are provided by databases maintained by the Ministry of Health, Ministry of Interior, and Clalit Health Services, excluding the last group, which is computed from the HIE log files. As physician data are relevant only for encounters with HIE use, H1 is tested with encounter data that include only the first three groups of control variables, whereas H2 is tested with physician-encounter data that include all five groups of control variables, described next.

4.3.2.1. Patient. Patient-related variables include demographic and clinical characteristics, generally associated with task complexity and time pressure. Demographic characteristics include gender, an indicator of whether the patient was born abroad, and age. Clinical characteristics include the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), computed based on the International Classificati on of Diseases, ninth revision – Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) (Quan et al., 2005). In addition, to better reflect the patient’s condition, we include two binary indicators of mortality within 24 hours and 7 days from the time of ED admission. Two additional patient characteristics, which also reflect time constraints, are includ ed. The first characteristic is a binary variable of whether the patient arrived by emergency services, which increases time pressure (Burt et al., 2006). The second characteristic is ED length of stay (LOS), defined as the elapsed hours from ED admission until RR discharge, which decreases time pressure given the lower urgency. Finally, a binary indicator of whether the patient is insured by Clalit Health Services is included. This patient characteristic is important because the systems are expected to include more information on such patients.

4.3.2.2. Situation. Situational variables reflect time and work pressures that originate from the treatment environment. These variables include the ED workload, measured as the number of patients that arrived at the ED between 30 minutes before and after the focal encounter. ED stafing and workload are also captured by including two binary variables of whether the encounter started during a morning shift and during a weekday, which are typically busier. As patient handofs create miscommunications and information gaps (Kaelber & Bates, 2007), a binary variable is included to indicate that a physician shift change occurred during the encounter. Finally, a variable is included to measure the number of hours elapsed from shift start until HIE access for the physician. Longer work hours, which afect fatigue, are associated with a decreased ability to manage cognitive resources eficiently (Papp et al., 2004).

4.3.2.3. Information available in the HIE. The availability of information in the HIE is indicative of the potential volume of information a physician may consume, while also reflecting patient condition (e.g., the availability of an ED visit summary indicates a past ED visit). The availability of excessive amounts of information in the HIE increases the likelihood of redundancy and contradictions with the EMR. To reflect the information available in the HIE, binary variables are included to indicate the availability of information from the year preceding the encounter or session, depending on the level of analysis, in six information categories: hospitalisations; ED visits; visits in community and outpatient clinics; laboratory tests; imaging scans; as well as surgeries and procedures. Two additional binary variables are included to indicate whether information on new laboratory tests and imaging scans was made available in the HIE during the encounter or session, depending on the level of analysis. Such new information can safely be assumed to originate within SUMC and, therefore, to also be available in the EMR.

4.3.2.4. Physician. Physician-related variables include three characteristics of the physician using the HIE, namely, the physician’s experience, expertise, and gender. Physician experience is measured as elapsed years from licensure, reflecting overall experience in practicing medicine. Physician expertise is measured as a binary variable of whether the physician is a board-certified emergency medicine (EM) expert. Finally, physician gender is included as a binary variable.

4.3.2.5. Use dynamics during an encounter. This group of variables captures efects associated with the utilisation of the HIE by multiple physicians during the treatment of a single patient. Three binary variables are included to indicate whether the HIE was used by a single physician during the encounter, whether the physician was the first to use it, or whether the physician was the last to use it. Additionally, online consultation is a binary variable, which is set to 1 when the HIE is used concurrently with another physician in the ED for the same patient, and to 0 when there is no access by another physician for the same patient during the session.

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

To test H1, a logistic regression analysis is employed to estimate the efects of EMR availability on HIE dichotomous-use at the encounter level. This analysis includes all relevant control variables (patient, situation, and information availability variables) and their interactions with EMR availability. The inclusion of main and interaction efects of the same variables introduces multicollinearity, which is addressed in the analyses by mean-centring all explanatory variables (Jaccard & Turrisi, 2003). Variables that are associated with variance inflation factors or bivariate correlation coeficients that are above 5 or 0.7, respectively, are excluded from the analyses (Hair et al., 2005). The results, presented in Table 4, show that EMR availability is associated with a decrease in the odds of HIE use $( O R \ : = \ : 0 . 2 2 7 , \ : p < \ : 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , implying a substitution efect, which is also evident in the descriptive statistics (Table 2). These results, therefore, provide support for H1.

## 5.3. Matching techniques and the robustness of findings

Given the observational nature of this study, encounters are not randomly assigned to treatment (EMR available) and control (EMR unavailable) conditions. Findings may, therefore, be susceptible to selection bias due to the inability to control for all unobserved variables that drive HIE use contingent on EMR availability. We address this potential bias by applying matching techniques based on propensity scores (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983), as a means of producing unbiased estimates of the efects of EMR availability (Dehejia & Wahba, 2002).

We use the encounter as the matched unit, obtain propensity scores (i.e., the likelihood of EMR availability for an encounter), and test the robustness of our findings against several matching techniques. Propensity scores are estimated using a logistic regression of EMR availability on the control variables for patient, situation, and information available in the HIE (groups I–III), while excluding variables that chronologically come after HIE use, specifically, 24-hour and 7-day mortality as well as information made available in the HIE during the encounter. Matching techniques are applied to couple encounters with and without an EMR, and unmatched encounters are discarded. To ensure the robustness of findings, we apply several techniques that represent different trade-ofs between accuracy and bias of estimates (Dehejia & Wahba, 2002). These techniques vary in the following parameters: the number of matched no-EMR encounters per a with-EMR encounter (one, two, or three); whether a no-EMR encounter can be matched with more than one with-EMR encounter (matching with or without replacement); a maximum distance limit (i.e., caliper) between matched encounters (no limit or 0.25 of the standard deviation of all propensity scores); and the matching procedure (“greedy” nearest neighbour or optimal matching (Hansen, 2004) that minimises the average absolute distance across all matched pairs (Ho et al., 2011)). For the data produced by each matching technique, we re-run all regression models employed for hypothesis testing.

For the prediction of HIE dichotomous-use, we find that the efect of EMR availability is even more negative than that reported in Table 4, providing further support for H1. Generally, the efects of control variables retain their significance and direction. For the prediction of HIE extent-of-use, only sessions from matched encounters are retained. We find that the efects of EMR availability in both models for volume and duration are even more positive than those reported in Table 5, further supporting H2. Again, most efects of control variables are unafected. Overall, we conclude that our findings are robust to the self-selection bias and to the application of a specific matching technique.

## 6. Discussion

Studies on information seeking behaviour have frequently investigated the motivations at the basis of decisions to search for information in various sources by observing whether sources are used or not (e.g., Allen, 1977; Curley et al., 1990; Hardy, 1982). When we adopt a similar dichotomous definition of use, our findings of system substitution lead to similar conclusions that use decisions are guided by the principle of least efort and that they are highly influenced by information seeking costs. The analysis for HIE dichotomous-use is indicative of the importance of lacking cognitive resources in the research setting under investigation, driven by the need to provide emergency care in a constrained setting. We consider this combination of system substitution for dichotomous-use and complementarity for extent-ofuse to be an important finding. In our view, it directly reflects the tension between the costs (i.e., cognitive resources) and benefits (i.e., bridging information gaps) of information seeking. Cognitive resources are typically in high demand in an ED setting, although situational variables may increase or decrease cognitive load. For instance, ED workload and the number of hours from shift start may enhance or reduce the cognitive burden on the physician when they are high or low, respectively.

Given the high costs in this setting, the finding that the HIE is used less frequently after EMR implementation is consistent with existing evidence. However, when observing cases in which the HIE is used, we find more extensive use of the HIE in terms of volume and duration after EMR implementation. Supposedly, EMR implementation causes low levels of HIE use to turn into no HIE use while increasing more extensive HIE use. Cases in which information gaps are small and the HIE is used in a limited manner are more susceptible to substitution by the EMR, leading to a reduction in the frequency of HIE use. After EMR implementation, remaining cases of HIE use are likely to be driven by significant information gaps, implying that system complementarity is observed. Therefore, when use is conceptualised in a more elaborate manner, both system substitution and complementarity can coexist in a single setting, highlighting the role of both costs and benefits in information seeking decisions.

This study contributes to the literature on the use of information systems in a medical context by describing substitution and complementarity in the use of multiple systems, specifically HIE and EMR. The hypotheses about system substitution and complementarity are tested with a unique, integrated dataset that includes both log file records of actual user (i.e., physician)

behaviour and a comprehensive set of control variables. The study provides an account that is critically absent in the literature on system use, which tends to look at system use independent of the existence of other, potentiallyoverlapping systems. While studies often examine the use of a single new system of interest in isolation (e.g., Vest et al., 2011), such an approach may fail to reflect the reality of clinical environments, which have been characterised by frequent system implementations in recent years, in particular of HIE and EMR systems (Adler-Milstein et al., 2015). We demonstrate the value of a broader approach that highlights system use dependencies following changes in the information ecosystem, thereby representing the consequences of new system implementation in a more comprehensive manner. Altho ugh this study examines medical environments, its conceptualisations and findings can usefully inform other investigations into system use in multi-system environ ments.

The theoretical contribution of this study lies in reconciling incongruities about whether information seeking behaviour, in particular a decision to use a specific information source, is best explained by the principle of least efort or by cost-benefit considerations. In general, empirical studies have shown that the principle of least efort has high explanatory value and that costs are highly predictive of use while benefits are not (Allen, 1977; Curley et al., 1990; Rosenberg, 1967), implying that substitution should dominate information seeking behaviour. However, these findings have been obtained by employing narrow definitions of use as dichotomous. By employing a broader conceptualisation that considers use as a two-stage process, this study shows that although system substitution dominates dichotomous-use, system complementarity dominates the extent-of-use. In so doing, this study suggests that both costs and benefits have value in explaining system use, although this value may manifest at diferent stages of information seeking and may require a more dynamic view of information seeking behaviour. Consequently, this study suggests that the cost-benefit approach is a valuable platform for theorising about the considerations that motivate users to seek information, in particular when multiple information systems are available.

The significant drop in HIE use rates following EMR implementation is interesting from a practical standpoint. A possible risk when both an EMR and an HIE are available is overlooking the latter when it can be beneficial. While there are claims that an integrated EMR with HIE capabilities is a better configuration than two separate systems (Vest et al., 2011), this solution is not always possible due to financial, organisational, or technological reasons. A possible way to mitigate this risk is to alert EMR users when the HIE contains relevant information. Such alerts, however, come with a caveat: their success depends on their accuracy and specificity to information categories, as excessive alerts may backfire and decrease HIE use rates even further (Davies, 2007; Strauss et al., 2015). Therefore, an interesting avenue for future research is to study how complementarity in system use can be facilitated by signalling in one system about the information available in other systems. However, complementarity (i.e., increased system use) is not necessarily positive, especially if it arises from cross-system information discrepancies. Such complementarity can be targeted as a source of insight into necessary improvements in system design.

This study is not without limitations. The generalisability of our findings to other settings and systems is limited as our data describe specific patients, physicians, and systems in a specific clinical environment. Although we control for an expansive set of situational variables, the specific organisational characteristics, system specifications, and sequence of system implementation (Angst et al., 2011) may afect system use. Another limitation stems from the reliance on log files to observe system use. While this methodological approach provides accounts of actual system use that do not depend on self-reporting, it can only reveal information needs that are followed by information search. Furthermore, we rely on the log files to infer which physician used the system. As we are unable to identify treating physicians unless they utilise the system, we cannot include variables that describe the physician in our investigation of dichotomous-use. Finally, the costs and benefits of information seeking, which together serve as the theoretical mechanism for explaining the existence of both substitution and complementarity, are not directly observed in this study. Instead, their relative importance is inferred from observing dichotomous-use and extent-of-use, respectively. This limitation is another “cost” of employing a methodology that does not involve self-reporting. We believe that the “benefits” of employing this methodology outweigh the abovementioned costs.

As data on EMR use in the relevant period is unavailable, we cannot fully capture all information seeking eforts involved in the medical treatment. We assume that the EMR is typically accessed because physicians are obligated to document ED visits in the EMR. While this study examines how the use of an HIE is afected by the availability of an EMR, interesting insights may be revealed by observing use of both systems. Future research may continue in the path of examining use at the system level or it may define use at the multi-system level by observing the information consumed from all available systems. Such research eforts can benefit from drawing on the definition of use profiles as common configurations of use-related variables, which has been shown to be valuable in describing use behaviour (e.g., Politi et al., 2014).

Additional avenues for future research emerge from the findings of significant interaction efects of contextual variables with EMR availability on HIE use. For instance, the significantly positive interaction efect of physician experience with EMR availability on the volume of HIE use suggests that the availability of an additional system may require more experienced physicians to invest more time in information search. Given the advantages generally attributed to experience, this efect should be further explored to determine whether it reflects an advantage of having access to richer information or an adverse implication of having access to multiple systems. Another example is the significantly negative interaction efect of online consultation with EMR availability on the volume of HIE use, which suggests that such consultation can replace system use in a multi-system environment. This finding highlights the importance of considering the interactions among physicians, who also serve as a source of information, in research on system use, especially when the information ecosystem changes. Whereas these interactions are controlled in the present study to enhance internal validity, they ofer a promising direction for future work on system substitution and complementarity in the use of health information systems.

Future research may also investigate how substitution and complementarity in system use evolve over time. For example, changes that occur over time in workflows, medical knowledge, and user roles can lead to system “decay” (Wetter, 2007). Monitoring indicators of use, such as volume, duration, and use rate, at multiple levels of analysis may assist in combating this phenomenon. Finally, a point of particular interest may be the association of substitution and complementarity in system use with patient-level clinical decisions, which is expected to provide important empirical evidence about the efects of the information ecosystem.

## 7. Conclusion

Against a dearth of research on changes in system use following the implementation of a new system to address the same information needs, we investigate changes in HIE use following the implementation of an EMR. The study focuses on a demanding ED setting and highlights the role of costs (i.e., cognitive resources) and benefits (i.e., bridging information gaps) in driving either substitution or complementarity in system use. We hypothesise and find that both substitution and complementarity are evident in HIE use following EMR implementation, depending on whether we observe dichotomous-use or extent-of-use, respectively. We demonstrate these efects with a unique, integrated dataset that describes both actual use behaviour, recorded in system log files, and a comprehensive set of situational variables. The findings represent a significant advancement of the literature on the use of information systems in a medical setting by highlighting cross-system dependencies in the information seeking behaviour of physicians, particularly when their information ecosystem changes. From a theoretical standpoint, the findings suggest that the information seeking literature underestimates the importance of benefits relative to that of costs in motivating information seeking behaviour, and that this importance can be more evident once the focus is shifted from dichotomous-use to dimensions that reflect extent-of-use. Consequently, the findings suggest that the cost-benefit approach to information seeking deserves more attention than it has received in the relevant literature. Despite the focus on a medical setting, this study provides the theoretical foundation and methodological approach with which to study substitution and complementarity in system use in other settings.
