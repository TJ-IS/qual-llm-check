# Complementary Technology Traces in Software Practice: A Retrospective Reflection over Sixteen Years of Evolution at Ericsson

Lars Mathiassen<sup>a</sup> and Anna Sandberg<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Computer Information Systems Department, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Volvo Personvagnar AB, Gothenburg, Sweden

## ABSTRACT

To advance knowledge about evolution of practices within a software organisation, we ofer a retrospective reflection based on several years of research into a software unit at Ericsson AB. Covering 16 years of evolution, we developed multiple publications from continuously expanding sources of software technology implementation data. While these publications report from specific junctures of software practice evolution at the unit, our retrospective reflection allows us to consider the impacts of the unit’s investments into two technology paradigms over the sixteen-year period: first into CMM-RUP with a focus on discipline and structured practices and years later into Agile-Lean with a focus on flexibility and responsive practices. Hence, we adopt retrospective reflection to investigate how imprints from the two paradigms, despite considerable timespan between the related technology implementations, eventually complemented each other in the unit’s software practices. Because one paradigm focuses on discipline and structured practices and the other on flexibility and responsive practices, our retrospective reflections consider how the two paradigms together impacted the software unit’s ambidextrous capability. As a result, we advance the idea that as software organisations over time invest in complementary technology paradigms they leave important technology traces with sustainable impacts on their software practices.

## KEYWORDS

Software technology paradigm; technology trace; software practice evolution; ambidextrous software organisation

## 1. Introduction

As a means to stay competitive, software organisations continuously invest in new methods, processes and systems. However, although implementation of such technologies across shifting software development paradigms (Iivari et al., 1998) has been widely studied (Coleman & O’Connor, 2007; Conboy et al., 2011; Fitzgerald, 1996; Persson et al., 2012; Vidgen & Wang, 2009), most research focus on how a technology, or combination of technologies, from one paradigm is implemented at a particular juncture of an organisation’s evolution with little concern for imprints from preceding technology paradigms. To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies of how traces from a previous, dominating technology and from a new, dominating technology may complement each other in an organisation’s software practices. This is unfortunate as such insights can shed light on how organisations can benefit from their previous investments into established paradigms as they consider investing in new technology paradigms that are emerging within the industry.

Against that backdrop, our research engagement with a software unit at Ericsson AB covering a sixteenyear period ofers unique opportunities to consider this gap in knowledge. During our engagement, the unit first implemented Capability Maturity Model (CMM) and Rational Unified Process (RUP)

technologies with a focus on discipline and structured practices and later it renewed its practices based on Agile and Lean technologies with a focus on flexibility and responsive practices. Based on collaborative practice research (Mathiassen, 2002; Mathiassen & Sandberg, 2013; Sandberg et al., 2011) and unlimited access to continuously expanding sources of technology implementation data, we covered this period of evolution at the Ericsson unit through multiple publications (Table 3). Reflecting back on this experience and the insights it provided, we started out from two related assumptions. First, although all our publications focus on implementation of software technologies from one of the paradigms at a particular juncture of practice evolution at Ericsson, we expected that both paradigms had resulted in sustainable improvements of the unit’s software practices. Second, as one technology paradigm focuses on discipline and structured practices and the other on flexibility and responsive practices, we expected the unit’s software practices had become increasingly ambidextrous (Boehm & Turner, 2004; Napier et al., 2011; Vinekar et al., 2006).

Accordingly, we adopted retrospective reflection to address the following research question: How can software organisations invest in complementary technology paradigms over time to sustainably improve their development practices? While previous research (Mathiassen, 1998; Mathiassen & Purao, 2002;

Werner, 2001) has emphasised the important role of reflection in software practice and research, this is the first software practice study that adopts retrospective reflection inspired by studies into professional practices such as project management (Hodgson & Cicmil, 2016), nursing (Alteen et al., 2009), coaching (Gilbert & Trudel, 2001), and teaching (McAlpine & Weston, 2002). The advantage of this approach is that it afords opportunity to systematically consider and draw insights from multiple years of engagement within a professional practice.

As a result, we advance the notion of technology traces to capture the sustainable impacts of a software organisation’s investments into methods, processes and systems over time. The concept of technology traces ofers new insights into software technologies and software practices based on the assumption that practices based on new technology paradigms and practices based on previous paradigms may eventually complement each other. As such, we contribute a novel perspective on paradigmatic impacts to the literature on the complex relations between software technologies and software practices (Fitzgerald et al., 2002; Introna & Whitley, 1997; Karlsson, 2013; Truex et al., 2000).

Our argument proceeds as follows. Section 2 positions our research in relation to the literature on software technologies and software practices. Section 3 introduces technology traces and organisational ambidexterity as analytical lenses. In Section 4, we describe our approach to retrospective reflection and the context at Ericsson. Section 5 presents our retrospective reflection over practices at the Ericsson unit. We conclude in Section 6 with a discussion of the contributions to theory and practice.

## 2. Theoretical background

Software organisations regularly invest in new methods, processes and systems to increase eficiency and responsiveness to customers and markets. Methods refer to sets of guidelines and techniques that teams use to support software development (Fitzgerald, 1996). To stay competitive, organisations need to continuously revisit available methods to improve their practices. For example, many organisations have recently adopted agile methods because their focus on flexibility enables fast delivery of software. Processes refer to coherent sets of activities for specific software development tasks (Aaen et al., 2001; Börjesson & Mathiassen, 2005; Hansen et al., 2004). For example, in one of the most popular agile methods, Scrum, software development teams use daily stand-up meetings as a process to continuously evaluate team and task progress. Systems refer to software platforms that help teams coordinate and collaborate on specific development tasks (Augustsson et al., 2019;

Dube & Pare, 2001; Herbsleb & Moitra, 2001; Persson et al., 2012). As examples, version control systems and defect management systems facilitate inter-team development and help teams align their work. Over the past decades, significant research has been conducted around methods, processes and systems and provided important insights into how software technologies are implicated in shaping software practices. Below, we highlight insights from the three streams of research with exemplary studies as summarised in Table 1. The exemplary studies were selected to be representative of and to illustrate the richness of each stream of research.

As methods are implemented, they become closely intertwined with organisational culture, informal ways-of-working and the employees’ overall perception of their workplace (Conboy et al., 2011; Kautz & Nielsen, 2004; Kozar, 1993). Method improvement initiatives are therefore described as especially challenging. Earlier research has investigated implementation initiatives based on a variety of software methods, including structured methods and object-oriented methods (Bansler & Bødker, 1993; Fitzgerald, 1996; Wieringa, 1998). More recent literature documents how software organisations across domains have adopted agile methods (Abrahamsson et al., 2009; Fitzgerald et al., 2006; Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001; Wang et al., 2012). These developments have had major impacts as agile methods are distinctly diferent from traditional methods. By emphasising short iterations and development of smaller increments of functionality, agile methods have transformed traditional-structured development with long project lead times. In agile methods, development teams are cross-functional, they work closely with customers and seek to continuously deliver software to shorten time-to-market and enable frequent feedback from users. For most organisations, these shifts afect not only the development teams and the methods they apply, but also the roles and functions at all levels in the organisation (Tripp & Armstrong, 2018). Today, agile practices have become the de facto way-of -working in many industries, ofering a successful example of how new methods can help organisations better accommodate changing customer and market needs.

Software Process Improvement (SPI) initiatives are concerned with the capability of a software organisation to continuously improve its development processes in order to produce high-quality products in a consistent and predictable manner (Dybå, 2000; Pino et al., 2008: Unterkalmsteiner et al., 2012). These initiatives imply that elements of an efective software process can be defined, that existing practices can be assessed against those definitions, and that an improvement strategy can be deployed to transform existing practices into practices that are more focused, more repeatable and more reliable in terms of quality and timeliness of delivery to customers. Hansen et al. (2004) reviewed and categorised the literature into prescriptive, descriptive, and reflective contributions; Aaen et al. (2001) provided an in-depth examination of the SPI literature and identified the fundamental ideas underlying SPI approaches and practices; and, Müller et al. (2010) revealed the organisational metaphors underlying the SPI literature. In addition, there are numerous studies on factors, models and conditions for successful SPI (Afzal et al., 2016; Coleman & O’Connor, 2007; Dybå, 2000; Rainer & Hall, 2001; Sulayman et al., 2012; Syed et al., 2019). As reported in this literature, new technology, changing customer needs, laws and regulations and competition are important drivers of improved software quality, but SPI initiatives are complex, they involve technical as well as social transformations, and their success is dificult to assess as they often target long-term strategies rather than short-term tactics.

As argued by Qin et al. (2007), successful method and process improvement require that changes are deployed and supported by appropriate software. As a supporting infrastructure, such systems should ideally leave developers with a sense of freedom in making choices rather than being told to follow explicit instructions. Achieving this is, however, far from trivial and previous research suggests that the adoption and use of new supporting software – such as CASE tools, verification and validation tools, configuration tools, project management tools and more – are challenging organisational change eforts (Mathiassen &

Sørensen, 1996; Persson et al., 2012). Software support systems change not only productivity, software quality and development practices in an organisation, but also the intentions and actions of key players, the way in which software methods and processes are adopted and used, and the organisational context within which they are introduced (Augustsson et al., 2019; Majchrzak et al., 2005; Massey et al., 2003; Orlikowski, 1993; Sarker & Sahay, 2004). In this way, software support systems afect the technical as well as organisational aspects of software practices. Fredriksen and Rose (2003) recognised this in their investigation of how new support systems are sometimes chosen and implemented in a way that reinforces rather than alters existing practices. Hence, existing patterns of thought and values are easily reproduced in the selection and use of software support systems (Fredriksen & Rose, 2003). As in most change eforts, organisational culture can either support adoption of a new software system and foster new work habits or it can hinder change initiatives by reinforcing already existing ways-of-working (Rainer & Hall, 2001).

Based on this review, extant literature presents the implementation of new software technologies as complex organisational change eforts, in which managers and engineers seek to reinforce or transform existing practices. While extant research emphasises the important role of existing practices, dominant beliefs and cultural values in shaping change outcomes, the software industry has gone through significant shifts in the technology paradigms that have shaped software practices over the past several decades (Fitzgerald et al., 2006; Iivari et al., 1998; Müller et al., 2010). Each paradigm represents a set of co-existing technologies with similar assumptions about software, how software is developed, and the actors involved in the process (Hassan & Mathiassen, 2018; Iivari, 1991; Iivari et al., 1998). In that light, it is noteworthy that the dominant focus of existing studies is on implementation of methods, processes or systems that represent one paradigm without explicitly taking imprinting from previous paradigms into account. One possible explanation for this shortcoming is that moving to a complementarity perspective on technology paradigms requires access to experiences and data that spans two paradigms, such as the domination of structured technologies up until the early 2000s and the following shift to agile technologies.

Against that backdrop, we seek contribution to the discourse on the complex relations between software technologies and software practices by shedding light on how organisations deliberately or emergently (Mintzberg & Waters, 1985) sustain well-functioning practices grounded in a previously dominating paradigm as they move towards practices grounded in a contemporary paradigm. In the method engineering literature, researchers have convincingly argued that technologies cannot be used as-is; they need to be tailored to the specific values, goals and contextual characteristics of the adopting organisation (Brinkkemper, 1996; Karlsson, 2013). Researchers have also emphasised the important diferences between technologies as embodying general knowledge and technologies in action representing the specific ways developers act inspired by and adapt technologies as embodying highly ideosynchratic knowledge (Bansler & Bødker, 1993; Fitzgerald et al., 2002). Similarly, the reflective systems development discourse argues that it is not only the repertoire of technologies that determine the eficiency and quality of software development; of equal importance is the way in which developers reflect on the complex and uncertain situations they face to selectively apply, adapt and complement the available repertoire of technologies (Mathiassen, 1998; Mathiassen & Purao, 2002; Werner, 2001). Finally, researchers have promoted the idea of a methodological systems development in which the highly emergent and idiosyncratic nature of software development requires developers to respond to the challenges they face over following technological prescriptions (Introna & Whitley, 1997; Truex et al., 2000). While each of these perspectives contributes to our knowledge of the complex ways in which technologies are implicated in software practices, none of them have investigated how technologies from diferent paradigms that were implemented during seperate time periods may eventually complement each other to shape an organisation’s software practices.

## 3. Theoretical framing

We combine the notion of technology traces with concepts about contextual ambidexterity to investigate the software practices that resulted from Ericsson’s investments in two technology paradigms over a sixteen-year period, first emphasising technologies focused on discipline and structured practices and later technologies focused on flexibility and responsive practices.

## 3.1. Technology traces

Drawing on Singh et al. (2015), we define a technology path as the progression over time of the use of technologies from a specific paradigm for software development (Iivari, 1991; Iivari et al., 1998; Hassan & Mathiassen, 2018), embedded into a software organisation’s practices. A technology path may be initiated based on a single method, process or system, but over time it will typically result from using a combination of methods, processes and systems from the same paradigm. Either way, it represents certain assumptions about software development, such as giving priority to discipline and structured practices or to flexibility and responsive practices.

As a software organisation starts implementing a technology paradigm, it will constitute a new path that impacts some or most of its practices. As other methods, processes and systems are added, the impact will increase and sustain to varying degrees over time. Eventually, the organisation may initiate another technology path based on a competing paradigm with the intention that the new paradigm will positively impact its practices. We define a technology trace as the impacts a technology path have on the organisation’s practices at a given stage of evolution. As such, the trace of a technology path and the underlying paradigm will typically be weak initially, grow stronger as the path constitutes over time, and then start weakening as the organisation engages a diferent technology paradigm.

Technology traces are in this way expressions of imprinting in organisational behaviour. According to Marquis and Tilcsik (2013), imprinting originated in studies of animal behaviour more than a century ago as a description of how animals may develop in response to environmental conditions during one period and how the developed characteristics can sustain despite significant environmental changes in subsequent periods. In organisational contexts, such imprinting can establish powerful behavioural patterns with long-lasting impacts on an organisation’s practices and strategic choices (Kriauciunas & Kale, 2006).

By investigating the trace of an organisation’s investments in a given technology paradigm, we will be able to understand how the impacts of that paradigm on software practices sustain over time. Moreover, such analyses allow us to understand how traces of diferent technology paradigms, initiated at diferent stages of an organisation’s evolution, may eventually complement each other in the organisation’s software practices.

## 3.2. Contextual ambidexterity

Organisational ambidexterity, an organisation’s ability to be eficient in its management of business and adaptable in coping with changing demands (Raisch et al., 2009; Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996), has become a valuable way to understand the complexity of today’s dynamic business landscape (Boehm & Turner, 2004; Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Vinekar et al., 2006). A common definition of ambidexterity is the ability of an organisation to compete by continuously aligning its processes and systems while simultaneously adapting to changes in its environment (Raisch et al., 2009; Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996). The most common approach, structural ambidexterity, refers to a situation where organisations divide alignment and adaptability eforts between diferent business units. In contrast, contextual ambidexterity refers to a situation where contextual characteristics empower individual employees to make decisions regarding alignment and adaptability as part of their everyday work.

Researchers have investigated how software organisations can benefit from applying ambidextrous principles to address contradictory and challenging strategic choices in software development and management (Boehm & Turner, 2004; Napier et al., 2011; Vinekar et al., 2006). Software organisations need to continuously improve eficiency and reduce development costs, and they need to exploit the value of existing products in relation to their existing customers, hence emphasising alignment. At the same time, they need to stay flexible and respond to evolving customer needs, and they need to explore and capture new technological advances and market opportunities, hence emphasising adaptability.

Notably, Venkatraman et al. (2007) found that a software organisation’s capacity to develop ambidextrous capabilities significantly influences its sales growth. Similarly, Lee et al. (2006) and Ramesh et al. (2012) found that ambidexterity can help software organisations develop strategies for globally distributed development and improve adoption and use of agile methods in distributed development projects. In software management, discipline is needed to fulfil customer requirements and deliver according to deadlines and within budget. At the same time, successful software management requires strong social support to build requisite trust between developers and managers and between the organisation and its customers. Practices such as retrospectives, participatory design and peer review suggest that the very essence of software management is to create an environment in which software development teams become empowered and are given responsibility for the products and services they develop (Weinberg, 1986). It is therefore not surprising that contextual ambidexterity has been studied in relation to software organisations based on mixture of the formal and informal control mechanisms that are typically used to govern software projects. This research concludes that competing perspectives in software development are not contradictory but rather mutually compatible as they can simultaneously be complements and substitutes (Napier et al., 2011; Tiwana, 2014).

To support our retrospective reflections on the sustainable impacts of Ericsson’s initial investments in CMM-RUP technolgies focused on discipline and structured practices and its later investments in Agile-Lean technologies focused on flexibity and responsive practices, we, therefore, adopt concepts from contextual ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Napier et al., 2011). This framing integrates the organisational context, i.e. the systems, processes and beliefs that shape individual behaviours within an organisation with the organisation’s capability to combine its diferent skill-sets to perform its activities and develop new directions. It does so by defining performance management and social support as critical for understanding the organisational context, and alignment and adaptability as critical for understanding the organisation’s capability. ## 4. Research design

We adopted an exploratory case study design as particularly well suited to advance theory in response to how questions (Yin, 1994). Moreover, we relied on purposeful sampling (Suri, 2011) to ensure access to the type of deep engagement and rich data that are required to retrospectively reflect on how a software organisation improved its software practices by investing in diferent technology paradigms over a considerable time period.

## 4.1. Organisational context

Our research was carried out in close collaboration with Ericsson, a world-leading provider of telecommunication systems, services and equipment for mobile and fixed network operators. During the CMM-RUP era, the CMM was used in a yearly cadence where the organisation was formally assessed by external auditors and internally by CMM trained employees. When Rational presented RUP, Ericsson adopted these methodologies which also came with tools like Clear Case, Requisite Pro and Rational Rose. In the early phases of the Agile-Lean era, there were few standardised methods and most processes were home-made. Inspired by methods like SAFe, Ericsson invented how to scale agile, but as time passed, they also started to use tools like GIT and JIRA. While the agile methodologies focused on responsiveness, the lean methodologies focused on continuously improving flows. As such, the agile and lean practices generally complemented each other well.

In this study, we focus on a software unit responsible for developing network nodes for packaged data, with internal customers across Ericsson as well as external customers worldwide. At the start of the considered period, the unit had about 30 cross-functional teams located at two diferent sites in diferent time zones. Each cross-functional team consisted of 7–8 members and they worked closely with program management, feature integration management and release management of new features to customers. The unit is responsible for the ongoing development of one of the nodes in next-generation networks, including customer-requested features as well as general support for mobility management. The product has been continuously developed over the considered sixteen-year period and contains about 10 million lines of code. The product is built on one of Ericsson’s platforms and the majority of code changes need to be verified in relation to this platform. Hence, regression suits run at all time and on diferent test levels, allowing teams to continuously integrate their code. To further improve, the unit gives high priority to automated test technologies.

## 4.2. Retrospective reflection

Our retrospective reflection builds on research conducted in close collaboration between Ericsson and Gothenburg University during a sixteen-year period (2001–2016), from when the second author initiated her industrial PhD study with the first author as an advisor while working full time at Ericsson (Mathiassen & Sandberg, 2013) and until the second author ended her tenure at Ericsson. Using continuously expanding data sources from year 2001 onwards, we have worked closely with other researchers and practitioners to develop multiple publications on technology implementations at specific junctures of software practice evolution at the unit (Table 3). The data sources cover the unit’s implementation and use of CMM-RUP and Agile-Lean technologies over the considered period and the publications ofer focused analyses of technology implementations from both paradigms. As such, researchers and practitioners have established a well-functioning relationship in which insiders and outsiders have worked as a close collaborative team (Mathiassen, 2002; Mathiassen & Sandberg, 2013; Sandberg et al., 2011) with both parties bringing expertise to facilitate ongoing data collection, analysis and subsequent publication (Bartunek & Louis, 1996). Our reliance on multiple data sources throughout the research collaboration (Table 4) has supported triangulation (Patton, 1987; Yin, 1994) and helped minimise bias and achieve higher reliability of the findings. While scrutinising presentations, minutes of meetings, other textual documents and informal technology feedback interviews have been important in our ongoing research collaboration, the most important data sources have been direct involvement, participatory observations and formal assessment and research interviews.

To ground the reflections, we relied on our previous publications (Table 3) and the established data sources (Table 4). To focus and guide the reflections, we used our theoretical framing of technology traces and contextual ambidexterity through several iterative steps.

First, we adopted thematic and temporal bracketing (Langley, 1999) to reveal performance management, social support, alignment and adaptability impacts (thematic bracketing, Table 2) from both the RUP-CMM era and the Agile-Lean era (temporal bracketing). In this process, the authors relied on insider knowledge in addition to previous research and available data. Importantly, the second author managed a team responsible for all method, process and system implementations and support across the unit over the entire sixteen-year period. In that capacity, she had spearheaded the continuous collection and analysis of data and co-authored all publications about the unit. The other author had been engaged with the unit as participant observer and as co-author of several of the publications while serving as full-time professor at academic institutions. As such, he could leverage insights into and independence of the unit to help navigate the available sources to reveal technology impacts.

Second, we worked closely together to develop an account of the trace of each of the two technology paradigms with a focus on how the respective technologies impacted performance management, social support, alignment and adaptability in the unit’s software practices. Recalling that a technology trace captures the impacts a technology or collection of technologies from a particular paradigm has on software practices at a given stage of a software organisation’s evolution, we used retrospective reflection to account for how the many investments in CMM-RUP had left a trace in 2006 before the investments in Agile-Lean technologies became dominating. Similarly, we used retrospective reflection to account for how investments in the Agile-Lean paradigm had left a trace in the unit’s software practices in 2011 when the major technologies had been implemented. During these activities we iterated between writing, critiquing and revisiting the data until the authors found both accounts acceptable.

Third, we drew on these insights to develop an account of how the technology traces from CMM-RUP technologies and from Agile-Lean technologies had complimented each other to impact the unit’s performance management, social support, alignment and adaptability in 2016. In doing so, we considered which parts of the 2006 trace of CMM-RUP technologies and the 2011 trace of the Agile-Lean technologies had sustained and complemented each other in 2016. As a final step, we shared our three accounts with select managers from inside and outside the software unit and with other researchers involved in the collaboration to elicit their feedback and perceptions, which led to revisiting the sources and further developing the three accounts.

Although we designed our analyses to provide as strong validity as possible, retrospective analyses provide several threats to validity (Altheide & Johnson, 1994; Maxwell, 1992, 1996; McLeod et al., 2011). Hence, we strived to increase internal validity through insider-outsider roles, data triangulation, iteration and practitioner feedback. For example, the insider second author worked closely with another outsider researcher involved in the collaboration to initially articulate the three accounts based on multiple sources. The accounts were then questioned and critiqued by the outsider first author, which led to four iterations of revisiting the publications and data and triangulating between sources. Similarly, we strived to increase external validity by describing the software unit and its context and by avoiding generalising beyond the two technology traces and how they eventually complemented each other.

## 5. Retrospective reflection

In the following, we provide our retrospective reflections on how technologies from the CMM-RUP era and from the Agile-Lean era each had impacted performance management, social support, alignment and adaptability within the Ericsson software unit in 2006 and 2011, respectively. Subsequently, we consider how the two traces complemented each other in the unit’s software practices in 2016. As a result, the unit developed capabilities that helped them manoeuvre the opposing forces they faced within the rapidly changing software industry. In these retrospective reflections, we provide references to our previous research listed in Table 3.

## 5.1. Technology trace from CMM-RUP paradigm (2006)

This first trace accounts for the impacts of Ericsson’s investments in the CMM-RUP paradigm on the unit’s performance management, social support, alignment and adaptability in 2006.

Performance Management: In 2006, Ericsson had established an engineering tradition with a culture characterised by strong expertise. For decades, the unit had centred on core functions with system management, system design and system test being among the most important. The CMM-RUP paradigm had reinforced and strengthened these characteristics with a strong focus on individual expertise and development practices based on a rigorous iterative process with predefined milestones and deliverables. Said one manager: “It is of highest importance that we focus on having deep skills among our engineers in each function to secure development eficiency.” Hence, a lot of efort was put into the requirement engineering phase in order to detail and specify the system as accurately as possible (Börjesson & Mathiassen, 2005). For each of the functions, the CMM-RUP technologies played a major role in defining development activities, milestones and deliverables and in continuously assuring a high level of software quality (Börjesson & Mathiassen, 2005). Typically, customer involvement was limited, and system testing was done when development was completed and before shipping the solution to customers (Holmberg et al., 2008). As a result, and with CMM-RUP as the foundation, the unit developed structured and systematic ways-of-working with clear roles and responsibilities. Said one developer: “I have to talk to our product manager to understand if customer X would benefit more if we develop solution Y instead of solution $Z . ^ { \ n }$ Moreover, CMM-RUP was implemented with few system releases and incremental delivery allowing designers to focus their eforts and spend time on one task at the time. As a result, managers and engineers grew rapidly in competence and each function could continuously strengthen its expertise. In relation to encouraging people to set challenging goals, CMM-RUP was implemented with long development cycles and available time to focus on each task, which made it easier for people to practice this ideal. In addition, with performance metrics and reward systems closely related to individual’s expertise the unit could efectively measure achievements and reward people accordingly. From a monitoring perspective, it was easy to hold people accountable for their specific tasks as these were well defined and the roles to which they were assigned were clear. It was, however, dificult to hold people accountable for the end result as there were several handovers between functions before releasing to customers.

Social Support: Based on the CMM-RUP paradigm, the unit had a well-established post-mortem project evaluation process to help teams assess their results and any challenges they had experienced during a development project. As one manager said: “Now it is time to close this two-year project. So, let us all come together in a big workshop to summarize lessons for upcoming projects.” To assist in these eforts, there were clear business and team metrics in place and the post-mortem evaluations followed a strict template with predefined areas for evaluation. The process focused primarily on internal objectives rather than external customer goals and it was designed to provide a basis for teams to assess to what extent their tasks aligned with overall business goals, how they handled documentation, and how eficient they were in handovers between functions. There were defined metrics that teams used to measure progress. In relation to the unit’s ability to treat failure as a learning opportunity, the post-mortem project evaluations were important as they encouraged people to share experiences and challenges and to discuss failures. Also, the structure of the unit, with clear roles and responsibilities, made it easy for people to share information through welldefined channels. In relation to risk-taking, the lack of external control encouraged people to take prudent risks as the unit defined the scope of releases, not the customer.

However, the unit experienced challenges in relation to learning, risk-taking and sharing of information. First, the post-mortem project evaluations did not provide lessons that were immediately useful, and the identified improvements could not be implemented as the project had finished. Also, the slow feedback cycles and the infrequent releases to customers made any error or failure expensive. One project manager said: “There is little for me to learn from previous projects. We started our work long time ago and we have already set our ways of working.” Second, and in relation to risk-taking, the lack of external control from customers made it dificult for teams to understand overall risks. In addition, risks could stay latent due to long and complex projects and not become salient before it was too late and expensive to address them. Finally, the information sharing between teams was sometimes limited due to very strong functional boundaries. Typically, the diferent functions operated in silos with little interaction and sharing of expertise and experience.

Alignment: In 2006, when CMM-RUP technologies dominated the unit, the organisational design followed a traditional line structure with diferent functions in separate departments. Each project had a project manager with responsibility for the project, the project members and the overall deliverables. One project manager said: “We must make sure we are thorough when deciding on deliverables upfront, so project members can focus on the right tasks.” This rigid structure with strong alignment in the methods, processes and systems used across projects, ofered clear boundaries with handovers between functions and a reward system that encouraged people to spend time on the tasks they were assigned to and that aligned with their competence profile (Aaen et al., 2007). Also, as mentioned earlier, the relatively long development cycles allowed for a long-term, and in-depth focus that made people and teams highly productive. Based on post-mortem evaluations of projects, the unit had the opportunity to continuously learn and improve and to have future projects apply best practices from previous projects.

However, despite the many benefits of a clear and well-defined structure, the line organisation sufered from weaknesses that challenged the overall productivity and coherence of development practices. Most importantly, the function-oriented structure with expertise separated across silos caused sub-optimal coordination between individuals and projects. Decision-making was non-transparent with complex dependencies between teams and functions. Also, the long development cycles provided limited opportunities for evaluation and feedback on whether what was produced was appreciated by customers and generated appropriate revenue to the unit. As a result, resource optimisation and productivity measures could easily rely on “we are doing well because everyone is busy” rather than on documentation that time was spent on value-adding tasks.

Adaptability: Based on the CMM-RUP paradigm, long development cycles allowed for time to dive into and learn new practices. As lead times were relatively slow and deliveries infrequent, the unit could aford to have experts spend time on advancing their individual skills. In 2006, a major contribution to this was the function-oriented organisation in which individual performance was rewarded and in which methods, processes and systems were coherently adopted across teams. This arrangement aforded amble opportunity to learn new practices and to have them efectively adopted across the unit. Furthermore, CMM-RUP technologies were embedded into a top-down management structure that, despite other weaknesses, supported fast implementation of new practices in response to shifting business and market needs. One executive said: “We need to spend more eforts on methods and tools and a good way forward is to invest in research and industrial PhD studies in this area.” 2

However, in 2006 it required vast amounts of resources to introduce, train and continuously support new methods, processes and tools. Although adoption decisions were made easily and often quickly, it was tiresome and expensive to implement a new technology consistently across functions and projects. In addition, the development approach with long cycles and infrequent deliveries made it dificult to respond efectively to user feedback and demands. Typically, individual performance was rewarded in relation to milestones and comprehensive deliveries and this made it dificult to introduce more flexible approaches. The emphasis on requirements specification and resource allocation in the early phases also made it dificult to respond to changes in business and market needs (Börjesson & Mathiassen, 2005). One project manager said: “We can’t limit the time spent on quality. We must never let go of our high-quality ambitions. If we have to prioritize, we must let go of functionality.” As such, there was little support and few mechanisms in place to help re-prioritise software features and scope. Although large projects and long cycles had quality advantages, they also made it difi cult to respond to rapid changes and evolving needs (Sandberg & Mathiassen 2008).

Summary: Our retrospective reflection suggests that the many investments in CMM-RUP technologies up to 2006 had reinforced the established engineering traditions at the unit with strong capabilities in performance management and alignment. Although the paradigm had also positively impacted social support through post-morten project evaluation and adaptability through individual skill development, these capabilities were underdeveloped and posed challenges for the unit at the time.

## 5.2. Technology trace from Agile-Lean paradigm (2011)

The second trace accounts for the impacts of Ericsson’s investments in the Agile-Lean paradigm on the unit’s performance management, social support, alignment and adaptability in 2011 when the major technologies had been implemented.

Performance Management: In 2011, the units investments in Agile-Lean technologies had shortened its time-to-market, improved its responsiveness to customers and increased the frequency of releases to leverage customer feedback. With these technologies, the unit had shifted from focusing on expert performance to focusing on team performance (Sandberg et al., 2011). The silo-oriented functions with an emphasis on individual responsibility were replaced by cross-functional teams with an end-to-end shared responsibility for feature development. Rather than having strong functions separated from each other, and with complex handovers in between each other, the unit was organised around small cross-functional teams that included the necessary competences for developing and delivering customer value. Said one manager: “We must mitigate, or if possible, remove the many hand-overs between our engineers to improve our development eficiency.” As such, the new technologies encouraged people to set aggressive goals by increasing team responsibility with more frequent feedback from customers. Especially, the cross-functional teams increased collaboration and knowledge sharing to leverage synergies between diferent skills. Although it took a while for teams to navigate in the new structure, most teams reported that they appreciated the increased focus on teams and team responsibility. Also, people felt that the end-to-end responsibility of teams made it easier to understand and relate to highlevel business goals, further contributing to making teams more accountable not only for their tasks, but for the actual customer value created.

Despite the many advantages of performance management based on the CMM-RUP paradigm, these practices had drawbacks that afected the unit. The low frequency of releases made customer feedback limited, meaning that requirements became obsolete even before reaching the customers and that it was dificult for designers to adapt to changing customer needs. In addition, the rigorous process made people focus their attention on internal goals rather than on external customer goals and the overall feeling was that the unit was far from, and not aligned with, the overall business. In 2011, the Agile-Lean paradigm had made business goals more visible to teams and helped address these drawbacks in the CMM-RUP paradigm. However, the Agile-Lean paradigm was not without disadvantages. In particular, development teams felt lost because the end-to-end team responsibility removed the traditional hierarchy and the functional roles managers and engineers were used to. Without the traditional line organisation with project managers monitoring teams, the increased “freedom” was sometimes experienced as unclear and without defined responsibilities. One manager said: “I am responsible for requirements management and I need to secure that requirements are thoroughly understood by developers. It is not my responsibility to work with test and quality assurance.” Also, the shortened release cycles to customers caused people to adopt a shortterm focus with less time for technical innovation and refactoring of the overall system.

Social Support: With the introduction of Agile-Lean technologies and to address the challenges mentioned above, the unit adopted a new approach to learning. Instead of post-mortem evaluations, teams had daily stand-up meetings, frequent retrospectives and they worked in shorter iterations with more frequent customer feedback (Baaz et al., 2009). Instead of project managers, the agile teams had a product owner that acted as a proxy to customers and made sure that feature development was informed by external rather than internal needs. As a result, teams got feedback on feature content and potential failures and mistakes could be identified early in the process and before they became too costly to address. Said one manager: “We emphasize the importance of continuous learning in every team and make sure we act on our findings or escalate when they are impacting more than the team can address.” In contrast to CMM-RUP technologies, in 2011 the Agile-Lean technologies supported a coaching culture with facilitating leadership. This involved a transition from a highly centralised structure with a top-down approach to decision-making to a decentralised structure with a strong focus on empowered teams. The goal was to have teams take more responsibility, to hold them collectively accountable for larger tasks and to increase inter-team communication and coordination. In these practices, the Agile-Lean paradigm ofered guidelines with an emphasis on individual engagement and voluntary commitment rather than the command and control culture that had previously been dominating.

Still, in 2011 the Agile-Lean practices were not without social support challenges. Although transparency and fast customer feedback had improved the quality and value of development, it made failures visible and individuals feel insecure. Also, the frequent customer contact resulted in continuously changing requirements for which there was no management process. The teams appreciated frequent customer feedback, but there were no mechanisms in place to make efective use of it. From a risk-taking perspective, the short development cycles limited explorations of new ideas and approaches beyond the actual feature under development. Said one developer: “There is no time for us to work on our innovative ideas. We are always forced to prioritize new features defined by other stakeholders.” Teams also struggled with staying focused on a specific feature while at the same time keeping track of how the overall system evolved. Finally, the shift in leadership style was not without challenges in a unit that traditionally had relied on hierarchies and plan-driven ways-of-working. Especially, people found roles and responsibilities unclear and although they appreciated the increased responsibility, they found team interdependencies complex and information sharing dificult.

Alignment: In 2011, the Agile-Lean paradigm focused on specific software features, they made teams prioritise based on a feature backlog, they involved daily stand-up meetings were teams coordinated and they made use of frequent retrospectives that helped teams identify which tasks were productive and added value to customers. In addition, these technologies ofered a transparent communication structure and a flat organisation in which teams took increased responsibility for the features they developed. In this way, the Agile-Lean paradigm supported value flows and allowed teams to take an end-to-end responsibility. One team leader said: “We must make sure customers are involved when deciding upon and evaluating deliverables so that team members know in detail what to do.” These practices increased productivity and the teams experienced a situation in which they could more easily assess whether what they spent time on, i.e., the features they developed, did indeed add value to customers. Also, the focus on features and sub-system functionality helped teams narrow their scope and increase the accuracy of resource allocation for specific tasks.

At the same time, Agile-Lean technologies made the reward system less clear and people were unsure on what tasks they were assigned to and rewarded for. This caused uncertainty and could at times result in a less productive work environment in which similar features were developed in parallel without clear coordination. In addition, methods, processes and systems were implemented and used diferently across individuals and teams as Agile-Lean technologies tend to focus on “what” rather than “how”. Said one architect: “To be successful today and let teams decide, a strong architecture with well-defined interface between components is even more important.” Further, the feature focus that was regarded positive from a productivity perspective caused dificulties for teams to get an overall understanding of the software system. As a result, there was the risk of sub-optimisation with teams aiming for diferent outcomes without a common understanding of the overall system design.

Adaptability: Based on the Agile-Lean paradigm, in 2011 adoption of new ways-of-working had become a team decision rather than a top-down directive. This was a significant change in comparison to earlier practices, afording amble opportunity to explore and evaluate new ways-of-working in short cycles without afecting many other activities. Also, the narrow scope of features enabled teams to adopt new practices with limited impact on the software which meant that potential mistakes could be corrected early. In a similar fashion, the Agile-Lean paradigm was conducive to evolving needs and helped the unit face fast changing market and business needs. With the small cross-functional teams having end-to-end responsibility for features, changes could be quickly addressed and teams could be more easily reallocated. The feature backlogs allowed for continuous prioritisation of deliverables and for a more conscious scoping of new functionality. Also, and as a major contribution to the ability to act and respond fast, the close collaboration with customers allowed for frequent feedback on features as well as input on what to do next. One team leader said: “We must increase speed if we want quality. We can’t get quality with the long time it takes from we do something to verification of what we did.” In comparison to earlier practices, in 2011 the close customer collaboration was the most significant benefit of the Agile-Lean paradigm and the entire organisation experienced a significant shift in how they could reprioritise, shift focus and avoid investing resources in non-value adding functionality.

Despite the increased responsiveness of the Agile-Lean paradigm, the stronger team autonomy in relation to decision-making and adoption of new methods, processes and systems proved challenging (Olsson et al., 2013). Ericsson had traditionally emphasised coherence and alignment across individuals and projects, and the new approach caused teams to move in diferent directions without mechanisms that could replace the centralised management structure that had been in place for decades. To move from top-down directives to more informal structures turned out to be more dificult than expected and despite frequent feedback from customers, teams found it dificult to agree on common directions. One change leader commented: “We lack the transparency and ways of working that allow teams to understand what other teams prioritize.”

Summary: Our retrospective reflection suggests that the many investments in Agile-Lean technologies up until 2011 had strengthened the unit’s social support and adaptability capabilities with a focus on empowerment and learning in small cross-functional teams and an emphasis on frequent feedback cycles. Although the paradigm had also emphasised performance management and alignment capabilities, the focus had shifted from individual to team performance and from vertical control of deliverables to horizontal flows in and between teams with end-toend feature responsibility. These shifts had, at the time, created individual uncertainties about roles and responsibilities in cross-team coordination.

## 5.3. Complementary technology traces in software practices (2016)

Based on the separate accounts of the traces from the CMM-RUP paradigm and the Agile-Lean paradign in 2006 and 2011, respectively, we consider how the two traces sustained and eventually complemented each other in the unit’s software practices in 2016.

Performance Management: In 2016, the focus on setting challenging goals remained strong as practices based on the CMM-RUP paradigm and new practices based on the Agile-Lean paradigm complemented each other. While the focus on customer needs is important in both paradigms, in CMM-RUP most customer-related activities were carried out in the early phases of the projects to specify requirements and lay the foundation for securing quality. These practices made it dificult to hold people accountable for the end result and this shortcoming was further exacerbated because there were several handovers between functions before releasing to customers. The new Agile-Lean practices addressed these issues by continuously conducting customer-related activities to better manage and respond to changing customer needs. At the same time, the unit continued to measure goals throughout, albeit with a shift in focus from the individual towards the team. To support this shift, mechanisms eventually came into place and new structures were created to support teams decision-making (Sandberg et al., 2015). While these resembled the traditional CMM-RUP mechanisms in that they were grounded in centralised management goals, they difered by emphasising team autonomy and allowing for increased flexibility in the introduction and adoption of new ways-of-working. This led to a situation in which teams could tailor generic practices to better suit individual customers, development of specific features and the competence profile of the team (Mathiassen & Sandberg, 2014). One team member said: “Today, I can support our customer specific needs rapidly, while staying attentive to the strategic goals set for my team.” The emphasis on accountability in the CMM-RUP practices was also reinforced together with Agile-Lean practices that moved the business context closer to the development context.

Social Support: Practices were eventually characterised by a strong interest to continuously improve and to help employees develop through social support. Based on the experience from the CMM-RUP period, there was still a strong tradition for assessment and evaluation of results (Vard et al., 2017). However, these practices had evolved and together with practices from the Agile-Lean paradigm they further encouraged employees to take risks and to emphasise failures as opportunities for learning. While transparency and sharing of information had always been important, the mechanisms for how to do this changed. During the CMM-RUP period, clearly defined roles and responsibilities were the basis for information sharing supported by the hierarchical reporting structure. With the introduction of Agile-Lean technologies, reporting structures and formal information sharing were replaced by a coaching culture and coordination mechanisms among teams. The frequency of retrospectives increased and their scope decreased, making it possible for teams to engage in and act on their own findings. One manager said: “Today, our teams learn quicker than ever as the retrospectives are conducted with such a high frequency.” Overall, this allowed managers to facilitate broad and engaging employee development beyond what was feasible during the CMM-RUP era when functions operated separately.

Alignment: Although the Agile-Lean paradigm initially sufered due to unclear roles and responsibilities, the unit eventually moved away from a silo-oriented way of working towards crossfunctional teams that shared tasks and resources. The practice of maintaining a feature backlog from which teams pick a feature to work on replaced previous topdown allocation of tasks (Sandberg et al., 2015). Likewise, the culture of empowered teams led to a situation in which teams were responsible for allocating resources as well as for defining the scope of development. However, the overall system goals were set centrally and managed consistent with the CMM-RUP paradigm, while specific tasks to achieve the goals were mutually agreed upon by the teams as advocated in the Agile-Lean paradigm. One manager said: “When I have ideas of how to improve our business, I can more easily lead the teams in that direction with help of the measures we have in place now.” As such, the unit had combined its use of Agile-Lean technologies with practices from the CMM-RUP paradigm to aford an appropriate level of alignment between software goals and development practices and across development practices of diferent teams.

Adaptability: In 2016, the rapid feedback cycles, the increase in team autonomy and the opportunity for teams to tailor use of methods, processes and systems to their needs, had allowed the unit to simplify its topdown structure and combine it with a bottom-up structure in which teams evolved more rapidly (Mathiassen & Sandberg, 2014). While having the same willingness and understanding to adapt to change during both the CMM-RUP and the Agile-Lean era, the approaches to do so had changed radically. When cycles got shorter, the ability to adapt to changes increased. One change leader said: “The processes helps us understand how we should do things systematically in cadence to always improve and become better.” Although this shift towards a more responsive organisational culture was challenging, and not well understood due to the many years spent in a CMM-RUP paradigm with rigid project structures and long development cycles, the unit managed the transition based on its extensive experience with method, process and system implementation.

Summary: Our retrospective reflection suggests that CMM-RUP practices in 2016 had combined with Agile-Lean practices based on the complementary strengths of the two paradigms. As a result, the software unit’s practices had become increasingly ambidextrous through strengthened performance management, social support, alignment and adaptability, as compared to each of the two paradigm-specific traces in 2006 and 2011.

## 6. Discussion

Our collaborative practise research (Mathiassen, 2002; Mathiassen & Sandberg, 2013; Sandberg et al., 2011) with a software development unit at Ericsson over a sixteen-year period aforded us unique opportunities to investigate how CMM-RUP and Agile-Lean initiatives – despite being implemented during distinct time periods and representing competing technology paradigms – together impacted software practices. Drawing on this engagement with multiple publications and a rich sources of technology implementation data, we combined the concept of technology traces with concepts of contextual ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Napier et al., 2011; Tiwana, 2014) to retrospectively reflect on software practice evolution at the Ericsson unit. As a result, we suggest that the unit had become increasingly ambidextrous as traces from CMM-RUP’s emphasis on discipline and structure and from Agile-Lean’s emphasis on flexibility and responsiveness complemented each other in the unit’s software practices. These theoretical and empirical advances ofer important contributions to the literature on the complex relations between software technologies and software practices.

A technology trace represents the sustained impacts of an organisation’s investments in a particular technology paradigm. As software technology paradigms include co-existing technologies with similar assumptions about software, how software is developed, and the actors involved in the process (Hassan & Mathiassen, 2018; Iivari, 1991; Iivari et al., 1998), technology traces aford opportunities for advancing research into the relations between software technologies and software practices. By moving from specific technologies to sets of co-existing technologies, researchers can investigate how investments in methods, processes and systems that mutually reinforce each other may have comprehensive and sustaining impacts on an organisation’s software practices. As our review of the literature illustrates (Table 1), research on software technology implementation has predominantly contributed to three literature streams focused on implementation of methods, processes and systems, respectively. While each of these streams of research provides important insights and guidance for managing technology implementations, they are not well integrated and they ofer limited insights into how multiple technologies together may contribute to shaping software practices over time. Investigations of technology traces can reveal how an organisation’s investments in sets of methods, processes and systems from the same paradigm can contribute to creating a context that supports the enactment of software development (Fitzgerald et al., 2002; Mathiassen, 1998). This is illustrated well by how the 2006 trace of the CMM-RUP paradigm at Ericsson emphasised individual expertise within functional roles and rigorous iterative development with predefined milestones and deliverables, and how the 2011 trace of the Agile-Lean paradigm emphasised learning based on frequent customer feedback within a decentralised structure of empowered cross-functional teams.

A related idea has earlier been promoted within the method-engineering literature, arguing that software development does not necessarily follow a specific method; instead, developers select and assemble a portfolio of diferent method fragments to improve performance and deliver software (Brinkkemper, 1996; Karlsson, 2013). While the notions of method fragments and technology traces share the idea of combining diferent technologies rather than relying on a single technology, there are significant diferences between them. Method engineering focuses primarily on methods, whereas technology traces focus broadly on methods, processes and systems for software development; method fragments conceptualise how organisations can create tailored methods as assemblages of parts of diferent methods, whereas technology traces conceptualise how investments in technologies from the same paradigm can have sustainable impacts on software practices; and, the method engineering literature is largely normative with prescriptions for how to develop and apply methods, whereas technology traces supports empirical research aimed at understanding and explaining the evolution of software practices.

Accordingly, technology traces emphasise enactment of technology in software organisations as a process that “enables a deeper understanding of the constitutive role of social practices in the ongoing use and change of technologies in the workplace” (Orlikowski, 2000, p. 404). Specifically, technology traces capture the practice impacts of investments in a technology paradigm at a particular stage of a software organisation’s evolution. A technology trace perspective is in this way distinctly diferent from a technical rationality perspective (Mathiassen, 1998; Mathiassen & Purao, 2002), in which organisations choose and tailor technologies or parts of technologies to address their objectives and perform required tasks (Brinkkemper, 1996; Karlsson, 2013). Although software technologies can help organisations structure practices, ofer useful modelling techniques and tools, and provide a common language for communicating about software development across activities and projects, the technology trace perspective assumes that the role technologies play in shaping software practices is limited (Introna & Whitley, 1997; Truex et al., 2000). Technologies are typically used selectively and pragmatically (Bansler & Bødker, 1993) and in combination with other non-canonical elements of practice (Mathiassen & Purao, 2002), such as developer knowledge and highly idiosyncratic mechanisms and traditions from the particular software organisation in question (Fitzgerald et al., 2002).

Importantly, while extant literature emphasises how implementation of technologies impact software practices without paying attention to imprinting from previous technologies, our research advances concepts and empirical insights that shed light on how investments in technology paradigms over time constitute as paths in software organisations (Singh et al., 2015) and how each path leaves traces that sustain as part of software practices, even as organisations transition to investments in a new technology paradigm (Hassan & Mathiassen, 2018; Iivari, 1991; Iivari et al., 1998). As our retrospective reflections over software practices at the Ericsson unit suggest, such traces may eventually complement each other in the organisation’s software practices, despite representing investments from distinct time periods and paradigms. Although the CMM-RUP paradigm was in focus before 2006 and was followed by a focus on the Agile-Lean paradigm, in 2016 traces from both paradigms complemented each other into strengthened performance management, social support, alignment and adaptability, as compared to each of the two paradigm-specific traces in 2006 and 2011. As such, our study suggests that the Ericsson unit, over time, cultivated contextual ambidexterity capability by combining established practices based on a discipline and structure-focused paradigm (CMM-RUP) with new practices based on a flexible and response-focused paradigm (Agile-Lean). This finding is corroborated by research that suggests it is necessary to add structure to agile development practices based on selective use of plan-based approaches (Baskerville et al., 2011; Port & Bui, 2009).

These theoretical advances and empirical findings suggest that software managers should be careful not to throw the baby out with the bathwater when investing in technology. That is, as software organisations start investing in a new technology paradigm, managers should carefully consider the advances ofered in relation to the strengths of its established practices. Following methodological prescriptions and technology trends rigorously is ill-advised against preserving and reinforcing practices that serve the organisation well and that can efectively be combined with new principles and technological advances. As demonstrated by the experiences from Ericsson, such pragmatic approaches to develop and maintain efective software practices will not necessarily result from deliberate strategizing, but may, instead, result from emergent strategizing that gives priority to the efectiveness of an organisation’s software practices over simplistically following software development fashions (Abrahamson, 1996; Mintzberg & Waters, 1985).

## 7. Conclusions

Based on retrospective reflection into 16 years of collaborative practice research within a software unit at Ericsson in which we developed multiple publications and maintained rich sources of technology implementation data, this study advances technology traces as a contribution to our understanding of the complex relations between software technologies and software practices. As a key insight, our study reveals how imprints from two software technology paradigms, despite considerable timespan between the related technology implementations, eventually complemented each other in the unit’s software practices.

Considering that our research is based on retrospective reflection, we encourage additional studies into technology traces based on dedicated longitudinal data collection and analyses. In addition, as our research is based on a single case we invite further studies to critically adapt and develop our theorising and findings in other contexts that involve diferent technology paradigms and other types of software organisations. Specifically, we encourage more research into how software organisations, given knowledge about technology traces, can deliberately manage both the positive as well the negative imprinting from previous technology paradigms as they invest in and start implementing technologies from new paradigms.
