# The show must go on - virtualisation of sport events during the COVID-19 pandemic

Daniel Westmattelmann , Jan-Gerrit Grotenhermen , Marius Sprenger and Gerhard Schewe

Center for Management, University of Muenster, Muenster, Germany

## ABSTRACT

Due to the high risk of COVID-19 transmission, many sport events, including organised cycling competitions, have been cancelled. Nevertheless, staying active is important for individual well-being and professional athletes need to compete to earn their living. We propose that mixed-reality applications like ZWIFT allow to conduct virtually mediated sports competitions, which resemble traditional sport realistically and could serve as a remedy for this contradiction. To test this proposition, we compare professional cyclists’ performance data from virtual competitions with data obtained from traditional cycling races and find that the physical activities are comparable. Thus, we contribute to the discussion on the distinctiveness of sports and eSport. Moreover, we interview 11 professional cyclists, who participated in virtual race series regarding their evaluation of the virtualisation of cycling events. Accordingly, we contribute to the literature on mixed-reality applications by focusing on user perceptions of a novel and physical dimension of virtually mediated interaction. Thereby, we apply the sports digitalisation framework proposed by Xiao et al. (2017). Professional athletes mainly perceive the virtualisation of sport as positive, but also mention some specific risks. Consequently, we derive implications for practice that include the supportive role of virtual sport to help master pandemics such as COVID-19.

## 1. Introduction

In a short time span, the COVID-19 pandemic has drastically changed the way we go about our daily lives. Social distancing, travel restrictions, temporarily closed schools, many jobs lost and an increased need for medical supplies are only some of the implications caused by the virus (Nicola et al., 2020). Within this construct of a “new normal”, the role of sports remains a double-edged sword. While the importance of staying active to prevent mental and physical illness during COVID-19 is frequently emphasised (Jiménez-Pavón et al., 2020; Simpson & Katsanis, 2020), the potential virus spread is also proven to be more severe during physical exercise, such as running and cycling (Blocken et al., 2020), fitness classes (Jang et al., 2020), or contact sports (Lippi et al., 2020). Massattended sport events pose an even higher threat as they are described to regularly be the source of infectious diseases spreading globally (McCloskey et al., 2020). As a result, major sporting events like the Olympic Games in Tokyo, UEFA EURO 2020, or the Tour de France have been postponed, while events like the prestigious tennis tournament Wimbledon or cycling’s cobblestone classic Paris-Roubaix 2020 have been cancelled due to COVID-19 (Independent, 2020; The New York Times, 2020a, 2020b, 2020c, 2020d, 2020e). Financial losses for sport organisations as well as the individual athlete are immense and, in many cases, threaten their financial survival (Horky, 2020; Timpka, 2020).

While for work and diferent parts of private life, digital solutions already exist and have been studied (e. g., Carillo et al., 2020; Waizenegger et al., 2020), the COVID-19 pandemic shows that there is substantial lack of digital sporting alternatives (e.g., Hacker et al., 2020), even though sporting activity, in many cases as part of a group, represents an integral part in the lives of billions of people (Mehrsafar et al., 2020; Yeo, 2020). However, a novel type of application enables users to engage in physical activity and to interact virtually mediated, while being embedded in a virtual world. The “online cycling game” ZWIFT (Delaney & Bromley, 2020) ofers its users the possibility to connect their bike to the ZWIFT application using a so-called smart trainer. Through the use of multiple sensors, the user performance – as measured by the provided relative power output (w/kg; watts per kg body weight) – is transferred to an avatar within a virtual world, which allows to virtually exercise with friends or compete in regularly held races while at the same being physically active (Borrill, 2020; Delaney & Bromley, 2020). Besides a steadily increasing user base, ZWIFT has also found its way into the highest level of sports, with the first “Virtual Tour de France” held in July 2020 (The New York Times, 2020d). Therefore, in this study, we focus on the exemplary application of ZWIFT, because it is currently the most commonly used platform for organising competitive virtual cycling events with an extensive range of features that diferentiate it from applications like Peloton. Other providers focus on virtual alpine sports, running, rowing, or golfing, but their virtual solutions have not yet reached the top-level in the respective sport (Golf Digest, 2020; SkyTechSport, 2020; The Guardian, 2018; World Rowing, 2019).

Since COVID-19 might have long-term efects on practicing exercise and related sport events, novel applications like ZWIFT that allow to virtually mediate interactions represent a chance to digitally participate in sports. Thus, ZWIFT and similar applications might play an important role in decreasing the spread of the current and future pandemics and additionally could help users to better cope with the dificult circumstances by ofering a digital exercising alternative. However, even though actual physical activity is frequently cited as one of the main features of ZWIFT (Delaney & Bromley, 2020), it remains unclear whether sport transferred into a virtual sphere and mediated by a mixed-reality (MR) technology has the potential to actually complement traditional sports in times like these and beyond. The aim of this paper is therefore to systematically compare virtual and traditional sports in the time of COVID-19 and beyond. Our research questions read as follows:

RQ 1: Can virtual sports resemble traditional realworld sports in terms of objectively measurable performance parameters?

RQ 2: Which diferences and similarities between traditional real-world sports and virtual sports do professional athletes perceive and how are these diferences evaluated by them?

To address these research questions and therefore the wide-ranging digitalisation of sport through MR technologies, we refer to the framework by Xiao et al. (2017). A comparison of performance data in virtual professional races with data of professional road races reveals that the delivered physical performances are to a large degree comparable. Besides that, building on semi-structured interviews with eleven professional cyclists, we show how sport in the virtual world difers from real-world sport based on their perceptions and evaluations. In particular, the athletes perceive the flexibility and coverage of virtual sports as very positive, while potential manipulation due to incorrect performance or weight data represents a major challenge.

We contribute to the information system and (e) sports literature by investigating this new form of virtual sports competitions for the first time and demonstrating that the performances provided on MR sports platforms like ZWIFT resemble those from the real-world, a perception that is also shared by athletes. Our findings can be applied to overcome barriers to the participation and acceptance of virtual sports and thus help, particularly in times of pandemics, to enable professional athletes to pursue their passion and profession without increasing the risk of infection.

## 2. Background and literature review

## 2.1. Mixed-reality technologies

In order to systematically position virtual sport and corresponding applications in research on virtualisation, we refer to the reality-virtuality continuum (Milgram & Kishino, 1994), which describes MR technologies (further divided into “augmented reality” (AR) and “augmented virtuality” (AV)) as located in between the poles of the real and a purely virtual environment. The closer a technology is to the “virtual environment”-pole of the continuum, the higher is the degree of computer-generated stimuli (Flavián et al., 2019). Table 1 provides definitions for all four categories and includes studies focusing on diferent application examples.

Related work – also focusing on the role of technology to help better cope with the COVID-19 pandemic (Carillo et al., 2020; Hacker et al., 2020; Keesara et al., 2020; Waizenegger et al., 2020) – emphasises the acceptance, usage, and implications of video conferencing technology. Research on virtual worlds often relates to virtual video gaming worlds, but also highlights use cases in professional athletes’ training, education, and healthcare (Billieux et al., 2013; Flavián et al., 2019; Vignais et al., 2015). Regarding AR, literature reviews (Bacca et al., 2014; Dey et al., 2018) identify interactional, perceptual, medical, and educational application areas as commonly studied ones. In contrast, the field of AV is less frequently addressed (Flavián et al., 2019; Regenbrecht et al., 2004), while design-oriented research focuses on enabling simple real-world tasks and perceptions while being immersed in a virtual environment (Von Willich et al., 2019).

Most research focuses on audio-visual stimuli and audio-visual “interaction”, which is due to the fact that the reality-virtuality continuum is limited to audiovisual stimuli and their output on displays (Speicher et al., 2019). However, we assume audio-visual interaction to be only a small component within a much bigger construct that allows for increasingly complex interactions caused by recent technological advancements.

Therefore, we refer to the framework of Speicher et al. (2019). Building onto the reality-virtuality continuum and incorporating related research, the framework establishes seven dimensions to describe MR technologies, whereby (1) the level of virtualisation (resembling the reality-virtuality continuum) describes one of those. Further dimensions consider (2) the number of necessary (real and virtual) environments, (3) the minimum number of required users, (4) the level of perceived user immersion, (5) the degree of (implicit or explicit) interaction with virtual objects as well as (6) user input and (7) output. Thereby, input resembles anything that can be tracked by sensors, including motion and consequently physical activity, while output considers anything that users can perceive and may go beyond audio-visual stimuli by, e.g., including haptic feedback (Speicher et al., 2019).

## 2.2. The impact of digitalisation on sport

Platforms like ZWIFT and those of other providers have the potential to sustainably change the sport industry. In order to systematically evaluate these possible changes, we refer to Xiao et al. (2017), who illuminate the possible impact of digitalisation along four components, initially proposed by Loy (1968) in the context of traditional sports.

Regarding the first aspect, the organisational component, digitalisation considers changing roles and responsibilities of involved stakeholders (athletes, coaches, sponsors, staf, or governing bodies; Loy, 1968; Xiao et al., 2017). These changes might lead to tensions due to potentially conflicting interests, as, for example, the administrative overhead of sportive competition could substantially be reduced (Junghagen, 2018; Xiao et al., 2017). Moreover, the digitalisation might attract new stakeholders like data service providers or broadcasters, who exhibit novel customer touchpoints (Hoye, 2015). In this context, it becomes clear that the digitalisation also transforms existing interactions and processes and additionally creates new ones.

The second aspect, the technological component relates to (physical) equipment, physical skills, and knowledge that stakeholders possess, whereby digitalisation here is mainly concerned with continuous improvement of the equipment through novel technological possibilities, such as MR applications (Loy, 1968; Xiao et al., 2017). Beyond that, digitalisation increases the amount of available (performance) data and their visibility, which enables a more extensive analysis and more data-driven scouting (Davenport, 2014; Xiao et al., 2017). We acknowledge that innovative approaches regarding the technological component of sport may also enable changes of specific other components, like symbolic or educational aspects.

The symbolic component, the third aspect, is concerned with the “secrecy, display and rituals” (Loy, 1968) of sports. Secrecy – resembling the concealment of individual tactics and skills (Xiao et al., 2017) – might be reduced due to data availability and visibility. Novel digital communication channels could transform public display, defined as the degree of public attention created, as they might allow for innovative forms of involving the crucial stakeholder group of sport consumers, e.g., through enabling immersive forms of feedback (Gofman, 1981; Xiao et al., 2017). Moreover, rituals – well-established gestures and routines, which contribute to the character of a sport, such as shaking hands or fan chants – can be transferred into the virtual sphere, further blurring the line towards the real world (Serazio, 2013; Xiao et al., 2017).

Lastly, the educational component of sports digitalisation concerns how it enables and requires the acquisition of specific skills, i.e., training and learning, not only for athletes but also related stakeholders, such as coaches (Loy, 1968). Digital technologies allow for innovative training methods, which might improve training possibilities and efectiveness. At the same time, they require the acquisition of specific skills to lever the athletes’ potentials. For example, data analysis can provide valuable insights on the athletes’ performance, but also requires an understanding of the applied methods and applications (Davenport, 2014; Xiao et al., 2017). Beyond that, the availability of data leads to increased transparency and contributes to the “democratisation” of sports by reducing information asymmetries (Xiao et al., 2017).

By relating the status quo of digitalisation in professionalised sports to the reality-virtuality continuum (Milgram & Kishino, 1994), we find that digital technologies are predominantly applied to measure, analyse, broadcast, or improve athletes’ performances in context of real-world sports (Davenport, 2014; Xiao et al., 2017). MR applications support these purposes. As an example, virtual overlays augment videos or broadcasts of real-world football games and other sports and add information to the display that allow a more indepth analysis of the events on the pitch (Fischer et al., 2019). Beyond that, MR applications can create immersive virtual worlds and help contribute to more individual and efective types of training, like training of specific, uncommon, or hard-to-replicate scenarios (Vignais et al., 2015; Xiao et al., 2017). Studies with professional athletes have applied graphical simulations (i.e., a purely virtual world) in context of training handball goalkeepers and soccer professionals’ reactions to virtual opponents and balls (Craig et al., 2009; Vignais et al., 2015).

In the sphere of purely virtual environments (Milgram & Kishino, 1994), professional competition in video games (e.g., sports simulations, such as FIFA) has emerged at the intersection of gaming and sports, called eSport (Funk et al., 2018; Seo & Jung, 2016; Xiao et al., 2017). Following the common definition of sport, an activity must include voluntary play, be governed by rules, be competitive, require certain skills, include physical skills, have a wide following beyond local attraction and be institutionalised by social entities, which set up regulating rules as a framework (Guttmann, 1978; Jenny et al., 2017; Suits, 2007). While eSports fulfils most of these criteria, the focal issues in the ongoing discussion on whether eSport is to be understood as sport in the traditional sense considers the degree of physical activity eSports requires and whether it matches the existing definitions of sport that specifically emphasise this aspect (Cunningham et al., 2018; Jenny et al., 2017; Loy, 1968).

We conclude that MR technologies have up until now mostly been considered as support and augmentation for real-world sports, but not as a means for conducting serious, traditional sports competitions. On the other pole of the continuum (Milgram &

Kishino, 1994), competitive video gaming is (at least) closely related to sport but takes place in purely virtual environments with very limited physical input and haptic feedback and additionally requires skills that difer from those of real-world sports. We propose that, specifically through innovative interaction, input and output design (Speicher et al., 2019) the novel technology allows to transfer a traditional, real-world sport into a virtual environment in the most realistic way possible, and thereby enables serious competitions that fill the gap between “traditional” sport and eSport.

## 2.3. Virtual sports platforms: what they are and how they work

We analyse the impact of sport digitalisation regard ing the four components derived by Xiao et al. (2017) along the specific example of ZWIFT. The sport platform ZWIFT allows users to ride their bike in a virtual world (Delaney & Bromley, 2020). Using a so-called smart trainer, on which the user’s road or mountain bike is mounted, ZWIFT allows to physically simulate diferent topographical character istics in the virtual world as well as other environ mental conditions such as the interaction with other users through a virtually mediated slipstream efect. This technology enables athletes to participate in realistic races, join group rides, or perform diferent types of workouts and training sessions (Delaney & Bromley, 2020). The physical component integrated in ZWIFT combined with the additional features provided by the interactive smart trainer create an experience that goes beyond the one of traditional home trainers or video games. In times of COVID-19, where diverse renowned competitions have been cancelled, it is not surprising that many of those have been conducted via ZWIFT instead, e.g., the Tour de France or the German Cycling Bundesliga (German Cyclists Federation, 2020; The New York Times, 2020b, 2020d, 2020e). Besides the possibility of conducting races, ZWIFT also ofers social elements like chatting and leisure rides with other users In addition to the aforementioned athletecentred functionalities, ZWIFT and comparable plat forms are attractive for other stakeholders like teams, trainers, sponsors, broadcasters, or scouts. By mediating their interaction in the virtual sphere, they fulfil the definition of a multi-sided platform (Evans, 2003). Further, customising features and socalled powerups (e.g., speed-boost or invisibility for some seconds) allow for individualisation of the avatar and add gamification aspects to the platform (Borrill, 2020; Hamari & Koivisto, 2015; Schöbel et al., 2020).

Referring to the reality-virtuality continuum (Milgram & Kishino, 1994), and therefore considering only the audio-visual dimension of virtuality (Speicher et al., 2019), we consider ZWIFT and similar platforms to be categorised as virtual reality/environment technologies, as they visually present a virtual world. For accurately classifying this type of application, we refer to the additional dimensions introduced by Speicher et al. (2019). Regarding the number of environments, ZWIFT provides a joint virtual environment, while users are present (at diferent locations) in the real environment. Competitive use of ZWIFT requires at least two athletes, which means that many users are involved in using the technology at once. We assume the perceived user immersion to be rather high due to the high degrees of attention and activity required to compete professionally. Regarding the degree of interaction, users can virtually interact with other users (e.g., slipstream efects, chatting) as well as with the environment (e.g., powerups, changing slope on mountains). Riding the bike, which is mounted to the adjusting smart trainer, generates motion (i.e., physical input), which is an explicit way of interaction. Further, the interaction in the virtual world is intentionally controlled.

Combining the aforementioned considerations, in case of ZWIFT (and related applications), MR is applied as a type of collaboration (Speicher et al., 2019). These applications augment a visually presented virtual world with the athletes’ real-world physical performance (i.e., input), which is accurately measured and authentically transferred into the virtual sphere and provide audiovisual as well as physical feedback (i.e., output) resembling interactions taking place in the virtual world through the same hardware. Consequently, beyond the visual aspects, which are purely virtual, ZWIFT can be categorised as an MR application.

Applications like ZWIFT add a novel aspect to the discussion on whether eSports can be considered as sports, as competitive success is based on the same metric (relative power output for a specific time) as in the original sport (Seo & Jung, 2016) and requires comparable physical skills and actions. Thus, a comparison between performance of virtual and “traditional” cycling is reasonable, which would imply that virtual sport can be considered as sport. In the following, we will illuminate whether platforms like ZWIFT enable the virtual performance of cycling, which requires a comparable physical performance to the traditional sport of cycling.

## 3. Study I: comparison of performance data

## 3.1. Methodology

Addressing RQ1, we assess whether the performance provided in virtual world competitions are comparable to those in road races. To do so, we compare available data of professional virtual races with performance data of professional road races from publications in sports science covering races on World-Tour level like the Tour de France. Performance data from virtual races were scraped from ZWIFT Power, a community-driven website that allows to track specific race results as well as extensive performance data from the respective race participants (Ostanek, 2020). In order to measure athletic performance on ZWIFT and compare it to “real-life” performance data, we chose three racing series (both women and men racing) that were exclusively held on ZWIFT due to the COVID-19 pandemic. First, we included the German Cycling Bundesliga that was held from April 18 2020 until May 16 2020. Second, we scraped data from the international five-stage race Pro Tour for All held between May 4 and May 8 2020. Finally, we included the Virtual Tour de France series, the virtual version of the most prestigious bike race in the world held each Saturday and Sunday between July 4 and July 19 2020. In all virtual events, only holders of a professional licence from the International Cycling Union (UCI) were eligible to compete, meaning that most of the participants in the virtual events have been competing in equivalent events in the real world in recent years. The scraped data includes general facts about the riders like sex, rank and time, performance data like average watts per kilogram as well as health data, such as average heart rate in beats per minute (bpm).

While the real-world road races are on average 466% longer for men and 239% longer for women than the virtual equivalent, the relative power output is 38% and 32% lower (comparing data points 1 and 4, respectively, 8 and 11 in Figure 1).

## 3.3. Discussion

Comparing the performance data of virtual and realworld races reveals that the performance in virtual races for both men and women does not match the performance profile of entire road races. This is particularly due to enormous diferences in the race durations. However, when comparing the virtual races with realworld races of comparable duration (time trials) or decisive sections of real-world races (climbs or mountain top finish), it becomes evident that the performances provided in the two modes of competition match. Answering RQ1, we can state that virtual activities, as they are performed on platforms such as ZWIFT, can be considered as sports, since all criteria are fulfilled to be defined as sports (Guttmann, 1978; Jenny et al., 2017; Suits, 2007) and that the major counter-argument in the eSport debate considering physicality does not apply here (Cunningham et al., 2018). Regarding the objective performance data, platforms like ZWIFT enable athletes to pursue their passion and profession in the form of sporting competitions in times of COVID-19 under social-distancing conditions. Nevertheless, the virtual transformation of sport apparently bears new potential for manipulation in the form of incorrect body weight data, which would increase the relative power output on whose basis the speed of the avatar is calculated. The comparison of the weight data in virtual and real-world races reveals remarkable diferences, while the body height barely difers.

## 4. Study II: professional athletes’ perception of virtual sport events

## 4.1. Methodology

We follow a qualitative approach to investigate how participants of virtual sport competitions perceive and evaluate diferences between the virtual and real world (RQ 2). In order to help guide the interview process while simultaneously preserving flexibility, we designed a semi-structured guideline (see Appendix A) based on the recommendations of Schultze and Avital (2011) as well as Myers and Newman (2007), which we pre-tested and carefully adjusted afterwards. As demanded by Schultze and Avital (2011), the interview guideline is structured by an explicit framework (Xiao et al., 2017) to enable more thematically relevant responses by the interview participants. Beyond that, we tried to foster an in-depth elaboration on potential advantages of virtual cycling competitions from a general perspective and in relation to COVID-19 and therefore encouraged an open discussion by following up on specific thoughts mentioned by the interview participants.

To ensure suficient qualification of the interview partners regarding their evaluation of diferences between cycling races in the virtual and real world, male and female athletes were contacted who 1) hold a valid professional licence from the UCI, 2) have participated in the Virtual Cycling Bundesliga 2020 and 3) have participated in the real-world Bundesliga in the previous years. Fourteen athletes who met these three criteria were contacted. Eleven athletes responded to our request and were interviewed in June and July 2020. Three participants were female, eight were male. All female participants competed in the Virtual Cycling Bundesliga, the Virtual Tour de France and the Pro Tour for All also covered by study I. Among the men, two interview partners participated in all three races. The interview partners include Olympic participants and winners of internationally recognised one- and multi-day races. Regarding the virtual races, a stage winner of the Virtual Tour de France is among the participants. The issue of social dissonance between interviewer and respondent (Myers & Newman, 2007) can largely be ruled out, as the interviewer is a former professional cyclist.

The interviews lasted between 22 and 74 minutes, were recorded after gaining permission from the interview partners (Myers & Newman, 2007), transcribed and then coded afterwards. The coding was performed by two authors (Coder A and B) in the form of a qualitative content analysis (Krippendorf, 2009; Mayring, 2014) utilising the software MAXQDA. Coder A coded first and thereby developed a coding system that is structured into three levels. Level 1 distinguishes between the four components “organisational”, “technological”, “symbolic” and “educational”, which we derived from the framework proposed by Xiao et al. (2017). On level 2, the coding system of level 1 was specified according to the context of (virtual) cycling events based on the interviews, as the framework of Xiao et al. (2017) is generically applicable to diverse types of sport. Therefore, Coder A derived a codebook for each level-2-category and applied the coding system to all relevant text segments in the eleven interviews (Mayring, 2014). During this coding process, Coder A developed definitions (Appendix B) that summarise the coded text segments of each category and discussed them with Coder B to create a consistent understanding of how the codes should be interpreted (Velsberg et al., 2020). The final category system on level 2 consists of 27 categories structured into the four aspects (level 1 categories) proposed by Xiao et al. (2017). On level 3, a sentiment analysis was added to the coding system (Chen & Xie, 2008; Gordon et al., 2020; Liu, 2010). Thereby, two subordinate codes per level-2-category were established to identify positive (e.g., optimism) or negative (e.g., anxiety) sentiment of the respondents towards possi ble diferences between real-world and virtual cycling (Gordon et al., 2020; Liu, 2010). This leads to 54 categories (2 sentiments x 27 level-2-categories) on level 3.

To assure coding objectivity, Coder B replicated the coding procedure. In the first step, Coder B coded the eleven interviews based on the co-created level-2 codebook provided by Coder A (Appendix B), without knowing which text segments were identified by Coder A. Based on this coding, Coder B also carried out a manual sentiment analysis in a subsequent step, labelling positive and negative connotations on text segment level. We measured coding reliability on text segment level by applying Cohen’s Kappa, which accounts for corresponding coding by chance as well as for diferent value distributions in diferent categories with a desired threshold value of 0.7 or higher (Brennan & Prediger, 1981; Cohen, 1960; Lombard et al., 2002). Results support reliability with a Kappa of

0.86, which is deemed as substantial, while a value of 0.0 would depict agreement by chance (Cohen, 1960; Shrout, 1998). To make sure that RQ 2 is answered with reliable results on respondent level, we additionally calculated the percentage agreement (Lombard et al., 2002) on interview level. The overall percentage agreement, across all level 3 codes on respondent level, accounts for 95.9%. According to this, Coder A and B identified expressed sentiments regarding each category (coding level 3) at least once per interview transcript with a 95.9% agreement.

## 4.2. Results

In order to present the results depicted in Table 3 comprehensively, we report the number of respondents mentions for each level 2 category regardless of the level 3 sentiment coding. Regarding the latter, we aggregated the count of positive, respectively, negative sentiments per category for each of the 11 respondents on interview level, so that up to one positive and one negative sentiment towards each category is potentially counted per respondent. By this means, we rule out distortions due to single respondents focusing specifically on one issue with multiple text segments being coded. At the same time, respondents could express negative and positive opinions towards a certain level 2 category, leading to a maximum count of 22 mentions (11 positively opinionated respondents + 11 negatively opinionated respondents) per category.

The impact of virtualisation on the organisational aspects of cycling events is visible through multiple aspects. First, various administrative processes are omitted. This includes coordination with authorities as well as organisation of road closures, barriers, support staf, travel, police and ambulance. From the athletes’ perspective, travelling, hotel stays and interactions with team and staf are reduced. Many of these aspects might induce a reduction of costs associated with organising races. The respondents perceive the reduction of financial and time-related eforts as a positive aspect of virtual competition, e.g., stating “Nobody needs to drive 6 hours through the country every weekend to meet for the Bundesliga race. [.] I think, compared to an organisation of a real race, made much easier and faster. So especially with regard to travelling costs” (Respondent 1). On the contrary, half of the athletes complain about the initial costs for setting up the necessary hardware, especially the smart trainer. Moreover, the athletes have to take on more responsibilities on their own, e.g., to prepare the races, as there is less or even no staf involved (in the teams as well as in the organisation), which bothered three of the respondents. With regard to the spread of COVID-19, athletes also see positive aspects in the massive reduction of physical proximity, which is inevitable in real world competition: “I think this has a very big impact on containing the pandemic. If I now imagine that every weekend there are 300 to 400 people with staf who would have met [. . .], there would have been a clearly increased risk of infection due to the physical contact one has. [. . .] It was appropriate that this did not happen at the moment” (R1). Beyond that, the athletes identified streaming platforms like YouTube as an important stakeholder in the broadcasting context. Regarding the novel broadcasting opportunities and cooperation with sponsors, most athletes positively perceive the virtual races as a chance to gain visibility for themselves and their organisation during the COVID-19 pandemic and beyond. For example, the recorded streams or self-produced highlight videos are available online after the event and “[.] you can still present your sponsors very well” (R2). In result “[.] the team management and the sponsors, who are mentioned again somewhere, are happy” (R3). Six athletes were concerned about procedures to ensure the reliability of results and to prevent manipulation and cheating.

Regarding the transformation of technological aspects of cycling competitions, the role of traditional hardware, i.e., the bike, is assumed to diminish in virtual competition, as the bike does not actually move on the smart trainer. All respondents mentioned this change, highlighting that traditional hardware (e. g., bike’s aerodynamic and rolling resistance) is irrelevant, but no one expressed a clear positive or negative position, meaning that no sentiment was attributed here. In contrast, application-specific hardware, such as a smart trainer, cameras, and fans, was identified as an emerging topic, which is necessary for participating in the virtual competition. Especially the smart trainer is regarded negatively by participants, who criticise the impact of diferent smart trainer types on the measured performance: “I know that these diferent smart trainers and power meters have an impact. In our case, some results were rather questionable” (R4). In this light, the issue of doping is assumed to become less relevant, because it is partly replaced by the emerging issue of cheating instead, which, for instance, can be achieved by entering false body weight values or through technical manipulation of the smart trainer. In line with that, all but one respondent highlight their scepticism regarding the technical reliability and are concerned about the possibility of data manipulation, e.g., “So for me, the efect of technical manipulation clearly outweighs any other. I think it is much more convenient to manipulate technically than to dope yourself” (R4). Moreover, ZWIFT-specific technical knowledge is regarded as necessary to properly compete, e.g., regarding the use of power-ups and the impacts of slipstream as well as more administrative technical knowledge in setting up the equipment. These changes in skills and knowledge are noted by the participants and criticised by one athlete: “The power-ups distort the whole thing since they have a massive impact on the results” (R1). A pivotal poin here is that no athlete mentioned that the physical skills necessary to compete difer between the real world and the virtual competition. In this regard, one athlete mentioned that: “All in all, I would say that it [virtual race] is comparable to the last hour of tough Bundesliga races” (R3). In line with that, nine respondents highlighted technical aspects of ZWIFT to increase realism, which are mostly evaluated positively, as for example: “The courses I know [. . .] it’s exciting that they are built so similar. And the fact that you can feel whether you’re going uphill or downhill with the smart trainer is very authentic” (R1). Notably, no respondent reported that the perceived realism is afected by audio-visual stimuli. Finally, the increased data transparency during and after the race enabled by the constant performance measurement is perceived mainly positively by seven athletes.

Regarding symbolic aspects of the sport, the virtualisation reduces the relevance and even eliminates many rituals, such as joint preparation for the race, team presentation, award ceremony, and cheering fans near the course: “I would say that almost all of these classical rituals are simply not present on ZWIFT” (R5). The athletes perceive the transfer of rituals into the virtual world as well as rituals dropping out as both positive and negative. Contrary, the interaction with consumers as well as the public display are estimated to be increasingly relevant. For example, social media feedback and ZWIFT-internal chat interactions during and after the virtual competition are extensive. Accordingly, athletes report an increased public recognition. Regarding consumer interaction, almost half of the respondents indicate that they positively perceive the novel forms of virtually mediated interactions, as they create a more intense relationship and allow fans to get closer to the athletes. Seven athletes perceive the virtual races positively in regard to the increased public display, as it might even increase the number of consumers of the sport: “There might even be more than at the roadside at a [real-world] bike race” (R2). Specifically, during COVID-19 shutdowns of other sports events, the possibility to compete virtually allows cycling to generate a broad display as “[it is] the only sport that’s being broadcast live at the moment and I think that’s just maybe an advantage to get to the viewers” (R6). In contrast to this public relevance, athletes have mixed opinions about the sporting relevance of the virtual races, which is emphasised by both positive and negative statements about the topic. While the rather high-performance level is positively mentioned, the negative statements go as far as stating that they “[.] wouldn’t really attach any relevance to it right now [. . .] for me it’s like playing PlayStation on a bike” (R6).

Participants identified educational aspects since novel skills are necessary for athletes and coaches. Regarding team tactics, teamwork or positioning in a virtual group of riders, the traditional knowledge is of subordinate relevance, while virtual-world-specific tactics are necessary to compete, e.g., regarding the use of power-ups and activities such as mastering the start as well as knowing specifics of the courses. One participant stated that “we had a meeting call where he [performance coach] told us exactly what we had to do with the power-ups” (R8). This quote also highlights that coaching requires new specific knowledge regarding data analysis and in-competition decision-making as well. Thereby, the possibility to utilise data analytics to optimise training and competitive success represents an important component in coaching and is perceived positively by almost half of respondents. Finally, respondents frequently indicated positive sentiment towards the fact that the virtual competition serves as a substitute for real-world races to maintain their fitness level, specifically during the COVID-19 pandemic: “There are no other (real-world) races and I think a lot of athletes feel the need to compete now and that’s just a good format” (R5). In addition, and independent of COVID-19 restrictions, virtual competition might also complement real-world training, which was mentioned by three participants.

## 4.3. Discussion

The results of study II allow insights on which drivers of sport digitalisation are specifically relevant in the context of virtual cycling events and how the resulting changes are perceived by afected athletes. We validate the theoretical considerations of Xiao et al. (2017) in the context of virtualised cycling competitions and thereby find concrete manifestations of the mentioned aspects.

Beyond that, we illuminate the efects an increasing degree of digitalisation, specifically the virtualisation of physical interaction (Flavián et al., 2019; Speicher et al., 2019) could have on diferent aspects of sports. The participation in virtually mediated competitions allows athletes to maintain their fitness and contributes to a novel level of training realism, which diferentiates virtual sport platforms from recent MR applications for professional athletes training (Vignais et al., 2015; Xiao et al., 2017). In addition, we find that the changes in stakeholder responsibilities in our context mainly concern reduction of tasks for the staf, while the athletes have to take more responsibilities in preparing for the races. Virtual sport also requires novel skills, such as specific tactics or strategic power-up use and thus, requires learning efort.

We also find that the degree of visually perceived realism is a subordinate design factor for this type of application, as realism mainly arises from physical feedback and virtually mediated interactions. These results support the proposition that applications ofering stimuli beyond audio-visual ones are deemed suitable to enhance perceived realism and immersion of virtual worlds (Bastos et al., 2018; Lin & Peng, 2015; Ribbens et al., 2016).

Moreover, we find changes that are evaluated as rather positive by the participants, such as the novel broadcasting possibilities, which increase the opportunity to present sponsors and allow for a more public display. In contrast, athletes are sceptical about manipulation possibilities regarding the input data and distortion of fair competition through powerups as a facet of gamification (Hamari & Koivisto, 2015; Schöbel et al., 2020), which makes them question the sportive relevance of the virtual competitions.

Furthermore, the athletes relate specific aspects of virtualising sports to the COVID-19 pandemic. They highlight the advantages of competing virtually and at social distance instead of conducting large-scale events with physical proximity in the real world, which is in line with current findings on COVID-19 transmission (Blocken et al., 2020; McCloskey et al., 2020). Finally, the respondents perceive the virtual events and the resulting audience as a chance to cope with financial losses due to cancelled real-life events (Horky, 2020; Timpka, 2020).

## 5. General discussion

## 5.1. Overarching discussion and theoretical implications

Combining the insights from study I and II, we find that virtual sports events are suitable to conduct realistic and serious competitions during the COVID-19 pandemic and beyond. The fact that the performances in virtual races resemble specific parts of real-world sport – in the form of time trials, or finals of races – also corresponds to the subjective athlete perception. Nevertheless, diferences in their perceptions of both types of sport exist. Summarising, this study is the first to analyse competitive, professional, and physical sport, which is transferred and performed in a virtual sphere and therefore allows for several contributions.

First, we find evidence that virtual sport can be classified as a sport according to common definitions, specifically because the necessary physical skills have been shown to be comparable (Guttmann, 1978; Jenny et al., 2017; Suits, 2007). In this way, we contribute to the discussion on whether eSport and sport are separate concepts (e.g., Cunningham et al., 2018), as we show that virtual sport lies in between both extremes, which allows for a more granular discussion.

Second, we contribute to the broader literature on sports digitalisation, as we identify transformational aspects of virtualising cycling. We thereby concretise and empirically apply the sports digitalisation framework proposed by Xiao et al. (2017). In particular, we focus on the impact of MR technologies and highlight that their application potential in sports goes beyond augmenting broadcasting and analysis or experimental training use cases, but enables serious competition (Fischer et al., 2019; Vignais et al., 2015).

Third, we also illuminate, which of the occurring changes the participants perceive as positive and which are barriers of use. It appears that the combination of objective performance data and the athletes’ perception fits well to answer this endeavour. For example, the athletes indicated their concerns regarding possible data manipulation and question the correctness of the declared weight in the virtual context. This concern seems reasonable, since the comparison in study I reveals that the weight indicated in virtual races is in some cases considerably lower than in realworld races. These combined findings are in line with the proposition of Hacker et al. (2020) that novel vulnerabilities emerge, as people more strongly engage in the virtual sphere and show that it applies for virtual sports as well.

Fourth, we highlight potential motivators and barriers to engage with a technology that visually presents a purely virtual world but augments it with physical input and haptic output of many users synchronised via interactions within the virtual sphere. Therefore, we contribute to the literature on MR technologies, which has so far focused mainly on audio-visual interaction and validate the recent framework of Speicher et al. (2019), which extends the reality-virtuality continuum (Milgram & Kishino, 1994) along the specific example of ZWIFT. Accordingly, we categorised ZWIFT and related applications of MR as a form of collaboration (Speicher et al., 2019), which might seem contradictory at first, as the athletes actually compete against each other. However, at a second glance, it becomes clear that the technology enables them to collaborate in performing a joint sportive competition without being at the same real-world location. Therefore, we identify the sports industry as a field in which this type of application can beneficially be applied (Flavián et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2017).

Finally, we emphasise how information systems can support mastering the COVID-19 pandemic in the field of sports, which is a vital part of people’s lives. Thereby, we complement the literature on technological measures and remote interaction during the pandemic (e.g., Hacker et al., 2020; Waizenegger et al., 2020). Adding to the discourse on which digital practices might endure after the pandemic (Hacker et al., 2020), we propose that professional sport events taking place in the virtual sphere might complement the traditional sport beyond COVID-19.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

From a practical perspective, this study provides guidelines on how virtual sports and virtually mediated physical interaction applications could successfully be deployed at large scale, while at the same time helping to control the COVID-19 pandemic. Virtual sports platforms enable athletes to take part in competitions in a virtual world from home, avoiding the need to travel to events, where they frequently are in contact with a large number of people. Still, they ofer enough realism to allow for credible competition. From a financial perspective, athletes and sponsors can use the virtual platform to reduce the financial losses arising from cancelled competitions. Teams can produce content independently from home, distribute it via social media and thus, supplement oficial broadcasts. Virtual sports also play a decisive role regarding psychological and social components, as the virtual races can serve as complements for real-world interactions and help athletes to better cope with COVID-19. Put into a broader context, we propose that virtually mediated forms of interaction, e.g., with fans, should be applied to diverse – pre-COVID-19 – mass-attended sport events as a part of the “new normal”.

In order to further develop and establish virtual sport in times of COVID-19 and beyond, further aspects have to be considered. We showed that the performances provided in the real and the virtual world are comparable, but that special knowledge (e. g., power-ups) is required in the virtual world. Accordingly, stakeholders have to find a common understanding on whether virtual sport should resemble traditional sport as closely as possible or whether it should be a separate discipline with its own (gamification) characteristics. In the light of the rapidly increasing attention paid to virtual sport, it must be clarified whether virtual sport should be integrated into existing sports federations or independent institutions should be established that are responsible for regulations. Thereby, it is of paramount importance to limit manipulation potential. To prevent technical manipulation, the reliability of the relevant measurements across diferent hardware setups must be ensured and there must be mechanisms (e.g., transparency and data recording via multiple sources) to identify suspicious data. Data transparency is not only important to prevent manipulation but can also be valuable for athletes and coaches to identify potential to improve performance. In addition, the performance data can also provide interesting additional information for spectators that is not available in real-world sport and should be integrated into the broadcasts of virtual competitions.

## 5.3. Limitations and further research

This study is subject to certain limitations. For our quantitative study I, we could not access individuallevel performance data from previous studies, so that it was not possible to conduct significance testing for

Regarding qualitative study II, we only surveyed participants of virtual cycling competitions organised by the German Cycling Federation. Therefore, the gathered insights might be biased due to cultural and regional influences although all participants regularly participate in international competitions. Further studies could validate our findings in other cultural contexts. Moreover, stakeholder groups beyond athletes, specifically spectators, should be subject of further studies to contribute to the broader understanding of virtual sports. Beyond that, the sample size is suficient to explore the topic and to gather relevant insights, but the quantitative counts of positive and negative sentiment do not necessarily allow conclusions about the relevance regarding individual factors and should be interpreted with caution. Larger scale studies or survey-based approaches could extend our chosen approach. Moreover, sentiment analysis is a popular use case of artificial intelligence (Liu, 2010). Thus, we propose that a hybrid approach to content and sentiment analysis could verify and complement our manual coding approach, especially when larger datasets are to be considered (Su et al., 2017).

Finally, as our study focuses on virtualised cycling competitions, findings might not be valid for sports, in which virtualisation is more dificult to operationalise, as they require more body contact or involve interactions with movable objects (i.e., a ball). Nevertheless, endurance disciplines make up a substantial share of sports and the presented type of virtual sports competitions might just be a hallmark for further applications making use of MR technologies.
