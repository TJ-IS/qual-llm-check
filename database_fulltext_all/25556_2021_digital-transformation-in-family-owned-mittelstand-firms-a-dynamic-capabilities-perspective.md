# Digital transformation in family-owned Mittelstand firms: A dynamic capabilities perspective

Jonas Soluk and Nadine Kammerlander

Entrepreneurship and Innovation Group, WHU–Otto Beisheim School of Management, Vallendar, Germany

## ABSTRACT

Digital technologies are increasingly afecting industries worldwide in many ways. Although the adoption of digital technologies by firms has been studied extensively from a technical point of view, previous research lacks insight into the managerial aspects of digital transformation, which, given the transformation’s interdisciplinarity, might have substantial implications for information systems research. In particular, it is unclear how small and medium-sized family-controlled firms (known as family-owned Mittelstand firms) with resource constraints handle digital transformation. This paper addresses this gap by drawing on rich data from 127 semi-structured interviews in a multiple case study of 15 family-owned Mittelstand firms from Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. All of these firms are active in the manufacturing industry but vary in terms of their digital transformation progress. Based on within-case and cross-case pattern analysis, we propose that the digital transformation of Mittelstand firms is a process consisting of three stages, namely, process digitalisation, product and service digitalisation, and business model digitalisation, and we reveal triggers for each stage and the dynamic capabilities needed throughout this process. Moreover, we identify three combinations of enablers and barriers that support or hinder the development of dynamic capabilities and thus accelerate or impede the advancement of the digital transformation process.

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 25 March 2018 Accepted 24 November 2020

KEYWORDS

Digital transformation; digital innovation; dynamic capabilities; technology adoption; family business; SMEs; Mittelstand firms

## 1. Introduction

The digital transformation of organisations, defined as the activities through which a company “fundamentally transform[s] business strategies, business processes, firm capabilities, products and services, and key interfirm relationships in extended business networks” with digital technologies (Bharadwaj et al., 2013, p. 471), has changed the way companies and, in particular, their information systems need to be managed (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006, 2010). Digital transformation is relevant for firms of all sizes and all sectors, as analysts predict that the “direct digital transformation investment spending [will] approach [US] 7.4 USD trillion” between 2020 and 2023 (Shirer & Murray, 2019, p. 1). Through the use of digital technologies, firms can create new business options and thereby contribute to their long-term success (Yoo et al., 2010). Bharadwaj (2000, p. 169) argued that in the 21<sup>st</sup> century, the “efective and eficient use of IT is a key factor diferentiating successful firms from their less successful counterparts.” However, despite its medium- and long-term benefits, digital transformation poses substantial challenges to businesses, for instance, due to the need to build up capabilities that are new for the firm (Nambisan et al., 2017; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Yoo et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2013). Such challenges must be successfully overcome for the firm to remain competitive in an increasingly digital environment.

Because of the resource-constrained nature of Mittelstand firms, these challenges particularly apply to such firms, which are privately owned, often small or medium in size and controlled by a small number of families—or only one (De Massis et al., 2018). These firms contribute substantially to economies across the world (Berlemann & Jahn, 2016; Bornheim, 2000; Shepherd & Zacharakis, 2000). Despite the increasing research interest in innovation management in these firms (Duran et al., 2016; König et al., 2013; Kotlar et al., 2013; De Massis et al., 2016), there is still a lack of theoretical and empirical knowledge about how Mittelstand firms approach their digital transformation and the associated development of new capabilities. This issue raises the following research questions. (1) How does the digital transformation process work in Mittelstand firms, and how can such firms develop the necessary capabilities through the digital transformation process? (2) What barriers and enablers impede or foster the digital transformation process of Mittelstand firms, and how and why do they enable or stall the development of the necessary dynamic capabilities?

Answering these questions is important because information systems research has found that the adoption of digital technologies in SMEs is diferent from that in other, larger organisations and hence requires novel theorising (Macredie & Mijinyawa, 2011; Rehm & Goel, 2017; Zhang et al., 2008). A better theoretical understanding of the diferent stages of the digita transformation process will allow us to acknowledge the heterogeneity among firms with regard to their current state of digital adoption. In addition, more nuanced knowledge about the capabilities required throughout the transformation process is needed for further theorising on the digital transformation process. Moreover, the identification of certain barriers and enablers in the digital transformation process is required to integrate general management and information systems research in an efort to comprehensively understand firms’ attempts to adopt digital technologies. We used a qualitative multiple case study design to investigate the digital transformation processes of 15 Mittelstand firms. Our data include 127 interviews with family owner-managers, (executive) managers, and experts in the field of digital technology and other primary and secondary data. Based on our findings, we develop a theoretical framework that theorises about the digital transformation of Mittelstand firms as a three-staged process with triggers, certain associated dynamic capabilities, and related barriers and enablers.

We aim to make the following contributions to the literature in general and to research on information systems in particular. First, we reveal the staged character of the digital transformation of Mittelstand firms, which consists of three mostly sequential process steps. These findings are especially relevant for information systems researchers because they illustrate how the maturity, focus, and scope of digital technologies, and thus the underlying information systems, vary through out the digital transformation process. These findings can influence further information systems research since scholars need to take those diferences into account when designing studies and analysing data. Second, we provide insights into the barriers and related enablers that afect the digital transformation of resource-constrained, family-owned Mittelstand firms. In doing so, we deliver insights for both information systems scholars and practitioners about which potential success factors and best practices exist for managing digital technologies in such firms’ transformation processes (Dremel et al., 2017; Hess et al., 2016; Karimi & Walter, 2015; Nambisan, 2017). Third, we aim to fill the gap in the information systems literature regarding dynamic capabilities in times of digital transformation. While prior information systems research has elaborated on the generic relationships between digital technologies and dynamic capabilities (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Yeow et al., 2018), we advance this research by providing specific examples of dynamic capabilities and linking them to the various stages of the digital transformation process.

## 2. Literature review

Our study builds on, integrates, and ultimately extends two streams of literature: a) the literature on the digital transformation of organisations, especially familyowned Mittelstand firms, rooted in the innovation, technology, and information systems literature; and b) the literature on dynamic capabilities.

## 2.1. Research on digital transformation in general and in Mittelstand firms in particular

Scholars agree that constant renewal through innovation can drive firm performance and growth (Gómez et al., 2017; Hashi & Stojčić, 2013; Nelson & Winter, 1982). However, there is still debate about which abilities firms need to efectively innovate in times of technological change and how such abilities can be built up. Researchers studying firms’ innovation ability in such times, for instance, highlight the importance of the firm’s resources (Gilbert, 2005), the ability to adequately respond to technological change without neglecting the core business (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013), and the necessity of dealing with the disruptive nature of technology (Christensen, 2016). Due to the widespread influence of technology on organisations in general (Remane et al., 2017) and innovation in particular (Nambisan et al., 2017), this debate is parti cularly on the rise with regard to digital technologies (Steininger, 2019). Engaging in a digital transformation by adopting digital technologies, which are techniques, skills, and processes based on binary digits (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010), ofers companies benefits yet also poses challenges. On the one hand, companies can decrease costs and increase revenue when adopting digital technologies (Bharadwaj, 2000), create firm value by introducing new business models (Remane et al., 2017), and, in general, build competitive advantage (Yoo et al., 2010). On the other hand, the adoption of digital technologies is a timeconsuming and potentially costly process, with returns that are uncertain and require time to appear (Karimi & Walter, 2016; Müller et al., 2018).

Understanding digital transformation is particularly important for Mittelstand firms, given both their relevance to the economy (De Massis et al., 2018) and their resource constraints and idiosyncrasies, which might hamper digital transformation (Kammerlander & Ganter, 2015; De Massis et al., 2018; Nambisan, 2017). Research on family firms has emphasised that those firms difer from their nonfamily-owned counterparts, particularly with regard to the role of the owner-manager and the resources available in those firms (De Massis et al., 2013, 2015; Röd, 2016) and that these firms might, as such, also difer in their digital transformation. On the positive side, a recent meta-analysis (Duran et al., 2016) has shown that family firms are more efective in their innovation processes than non-family firms due to their owners’ and managers’ identification and personal commitment (Berrone et al., 2012), their longterm orientation (Zahra et al., 2004), their low degree of formalisation (König et al., 2013; De Massis et al., 2015), their tacit knowledge, and their community of loyal employees, suppliers, and customers (Miller & Le Breton-Miller, 2006). However, on the negative side, researchers highlight that family firms might be particularly successful with incremental innovation (De Massis et al., 2013, 2015; Nieto et al., 2015) but have problems when confronted with more radical types of innovation (König et al., 2013), such as digital transformation. The reason they face such problems is their specific risk behaviour, which includes an aversion to losing socioemotional wealth (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007) and a preference for self-financing (Hamilton & Fox, 1998; Romano et al., 2001) that limits the resources such firms can invest in innovation (Duran et al., 2016).

To obtain a profound and comprehensive overview of the extant research on digital transformation in Mittelstand firms that goes beyond the more generic findings on family-firm innovation presented above, we conducted a systematic literature review. This review was based on a search for a combination of keywords, with references to both digital technologies and family business SMEs in the abstracts or titles of English peer-reviewed journal articles published between 01.01.2008 and 31.12.2018 in the EBSCO Discovery Service (EDS) database. In the first step, 764 articles were identified through this search; of these, 84 appeared in high-quality journals.<sup>1</sup> We read all of the abstracts and selected main texts and eliminated articles that were not relevant to our research questions (e.g., because they merely mentioned digital technologies in the sections on future research or managerial implications).<sup>2</sup> We ultimately identified 21 relevant articles for our research project. A synthesis of these 21 papers is provided in the appendix (Table 1).

The literature review shows that prior research on digital technologies in SMEs has focused mostly on selected drivers and consequences of digital transformation (see Table 1). With regard to drivers, the extant research revealed, for instance, that peer influences (e.g., the opinions of a decision maker’s friends, families, and colleagues) and external influences (e.g., web media) positively impact the decision maker’s willingness to adopt new software in SMEs (Macredie & Mijinyawa, 2011). Zhang et al. (2013) categorised drivers of digital technology adoption into environmental factors (information intensity and environmental uncertainty) and firm factors (having an international entrepreneurial orientation or an organisational learning orientation). With regard to the outcomes of digital technology adoption in SMEs, prior research has shown that digitalisation in SMEs can help such firms achieve complementarity in innovation networks (Rehm & Goel, 2017) and that cloud technologies can foster SMEs’ entrepreneurship (Ross & Blumenstein, 2015). Moreover, the extant research has provided evidence that adopting digital technologies might be particularly beneficial for manufacturing SMEs (Müller et al., 2018). In summary, the research on outcomes almost unanimously agrees that adopting digital technologies can be beneficial for Mittelstand firms.

Interestingly, most of this prior research has focused on selected aspects (e.g., selected drivers, outcomes, or digital technologies) of the digital transformation process in isolation. Most of the identified studies have therefore used a cross-sectional study design; we could not identify a study that investigated the digital transformation process in Mittelstand firms as a whole. With regard to theorising, we observed an accumulation of reviewed articles using theoretical lenses that are associated with (dynamic) capabilities (four articles; e.g., Zhang et al., 2013) and closely related concepts such as the resource-based view (four articles; e.g., Pratono, 2016) or adaptive capacity (one article; Ross & Blumenstein, 2015). Specifically, those articles argue that the reconfiguration of firm resources and the development of capabilities are important conditions to successfully adopt digital technologies (Mohd Salleh et al., 2017).

## 2.2. Dynamic capabilities

Dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2014; Teece et al., 1997), a concept grounded in the evolutionary theory of the firm (Nelson & Winter, 1982), might “faithfully reflect and guide” firms’ digital transformation processes (Yoo et al., 2012, p. 1405; see also Daniel & Wilson, 2003; Benitez-Amado & Walczuch, 2012; Battleson et al., 2016). Whereas operational capabilities are defined as the functional abilities needed in everyday business routines (Helfat & Winter, 2011; Zahra et al., 2006), dynamic capabilities are defined as the portfolio of specific and distinguishable processes or routines that reflect “those enabling adaptation[s] to external environments characterized by rapid or discontinuous change” (Helfat & Winter, 2011, p. 1246; see also Teece et al., 1997). They include alliancing, strategic decision making, and learning routines (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Prieto & Easterby-Smith, 2006; Winter, 2003; Zhang et al., 2008; Zollo & Winter, 2002). At its highest order, capabilities can be categorised as sensing, seizing, and transforming activities (Teece, 2018), which refer to the identification and development of technological opportunities (sensing), the mobilisation of a company’s own resources to exploit these opportunities (seizing), and the continuous renewal of the organisation (transforming; Teece, 2014). While this categorisation is often applied only to dynamic capabilities, Teece (2018, p. 41) clarifies that these three categories “aggregate and direct” both operational and dynamic capabilities and thus provide a framework for capabilities in general<sup>3</sup>.

Regarding family firms and SMEs, research shows that size constraints in particular might imply idiosyncratic (dynamic) capabilities (Burg et al., 2012; Hessels & Parker, 2013; Macredie & Mijinyawa, 2011). On the positive side, family firms possess idiosyncratic dynamic capabilities such as a veto power in decisionmaking processes (Reisinger & Lehner, 2015) and the capability of “interiorizing and reinterpreting past knowledge” (De Massis et al., 2016, p. 93). Casprini et al. (2017, p. 11) found that dynamic capabilities labelled as “imprinting” and “fraternisation” can help a family firm acquire and transfer knowledge in a context of collaboration with business partners. On the negative side, emotional ties to existing assets and rigid mental models in family firms can impede the development of (dynamic) capabilities associated with the transformation of existing assets (Chirico & Nordqvist, 2010; König et al., 2013; Teece et al., 1997). Furthermore, family firms’ reluctance to adopt externally generated technologies implies struggles with developing dynamic capabilities associated with collaboration and strategic partnerships (Kotlar et al., 2013; Teece et al., 1997). In general, their resource scarcity might constrain family firms in the development of important dynamic capabilities.

In the context of digital transformation, IT capabilities, recognised as the “ability to mobilize and deploy IT-based resources” together with other capabilities, increase firms’ profit and cost-based performance (Bharadwaj, 2000, p. 171), especially when the firm possesses the relevant dynamic capabilities to exploit them (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006). Put diferently, dynamic capabilities can help firms launch “varied competitive actions” that in turn are a “significant antecedent of firm performance” (Sambamurthy et al., 2003, p. 237). For instance, Limaj et al. (2016) and Mohd Salleh et al. (2017) argued that information systems capabilities drive innovation, while others have revealed a positive correlation between IT capabilities and an SME’s international performance (Zhang et al., 2008, 2013). As another example, Karimi and Walter (2015) provided empirical evidence from the newspaper industry that digital platform capabilities can foster firms’ adoption of digital technologies.

However, those authors also admit that their consideration of (dynamic) capabilities is “only a snapshot” (Karimi & Walter, 2015, p. 72) that does not reveal a profound understanding of the specific capabilities needed, how those capabilities are developed throughout the digital transformation process, or how they interact with internal and external influencing factors. Additionally, the enablers and barriers of such capability development remain largely undetermined. Moreover, despite the overall agreement that dynamic capabilities are an important predictor of firms’ digital transformation, there is a dearth of knowledge regarding which capabilities (Daniel & Wilson, 2003; Sambamurthy et al., 2003) and highlevel categories (sensing, seizing, and transforming; Teece, 2014) are particularly relevant in the context of digital transformation.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Research setting, research approach, and sample

The context of this study is the digital transformation of Mittelstand firms in the manufacturing industry in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. This is an appropriate context in which to investigate our research questions for the following reasons. First, Mittelstand firms are important for the economies in those countries (Schröder et al., 2015; Sommer, 2015; Welter & Schröder, 2016). Second, the manufacturing industry is at the core of the digital transformation (Dremel et al., 2017; Müller et al., 2018; Remane et al., 2017) and established manufacturing business models are increasingly threatened with being left behind if firms do not adapt (e.g., Dean et al., 2009; Sommer, 2015; Weber, 2016; Wu et al., 2002). Hence, understanding the digital transformation activities of these firms is not only relevant for research but also of interest to society and policy makers, as reflected by the creation of competence centres (e.g., Schmitt & Fischer, 2016), foundations (e.g., IfM Bonn, 2017), and institutionalised initiatives focused on this topic in various countries (Gronau & Theuer, 2015). By investigating the manufacturing industry, our study also addresses the call of Easterby-Smith et al. (2009, p. 7) to explore the concept of dynamic capabilities in “more traditional industries.”

To investigate novel research areas that ask research questions about such as “how” and “why,” case-based exploratory methods are best suited for (Eisenhardt, 1989). We chose a multiple case study research design to reveal similarities and diferences between sample firms’ digital transformation process and thus engage in extending current theory (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; De Massis & Kotlar, 2014; Yin, 2013). In this multiple-case study, we also address the call of Casprini et al. (2017) to investigate barriers to innovation, possible solutions, and dynamic capabilities in a family firm context. The in-depth exploratory research approach in the context of this multiple case study is ideally suited for capturing complex firm-level factors that cannot easily be captured through quantitative research strategies (Eisenhardt, 1989; Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007).

Our sample consists of 15 Mittelstand firms from the manufacturing industry, and we follow their digital transformation. Consistent with the theoretical Mittelstand firm definition provided above (Berghof, 2006; Block & Spiegel, 2011; De Massis et al., 2018), we relied on the following sampling criteria: (1) a privately owned, medium-sized firm, (2) a substantial family influence (operationalised as 100% ownership in the hands of the family), and (3) a self-conception as a Mittelstand firm. All of the included firms are located in Germany, Austria, or Switzerland, which are central European countries that are similar in terms of economics, culture, language, and business practices (European Commission, 2009).

The selection of case firms was a stepwise process guided by the firms’ potential contribution to our theory development. We identified case firms mainly based on databases, reports from chambers of commerce, and newspaper articles. When the first cases were analysed, we added Mittelstand firms to the sample that difered from the already investigated firms with regard to their digital initiatives and progress in order to achieve variance in the sample (“polar types;” Eisenhardt, 1989). In such a theoretical sampling approach (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Yin, 2013), cases are added to replicate or extend theory and to rule out alternative explanations for outcomes until a stable theory emerges and new data do not influence the findings, indicating that theoretical saturation has been reached (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Eisenhardt, 1989). ## 3.2. Data collection

To gather rich and insightful information on the sampled Mittelstand firms, we drew on a large number of sources (see Table 3 for an overview), including (1) semi-structured interviews with CEOs, directors, and managers of each sample firm conducted mostly in their company’s headquarters or at industry fairs (the average length was approximately one hour); (2) expert interviews with specialists from other organisations such as consultancies or firms that did not match our Mittelstand definitions (the average length was approximately 50 minutes) in order to provide an external perspective on the organisations’ initiatives and “objectively” classify their progress; (3) on-site observations and observations at industry fairs; and (4) archival sources such as web archives (“Wayback Machine,” Internet Archive, 2017), annual reports, corporate documents, current websites, press releases, articles, and analyst reports. For the on-site observations and observations at industry fairs, we completed a summary sheet for each observation. Our interviews were based on a semi-structured guideline. The interviews of the case firms were conducted by the first author and trained research assistants from the second author’s research group. All interviews were conducted in German and either recorded (58% of the interviews) or simultaneously transcribed verbatim, depending on the preference of the interviewee. Table 6 in the Appendix shows extracts of the semi-structured interview guideline, which consisted of 24 questions on the following topics: (1) digital initiatives and transformation eforts in the company in general, (2) family influence, and (3) professional capabilities, prior successes, and failures in digital adoption projects. Open questions gave the interviewees the opportunity to focus on “how [digital] things evolve[d] over time and why they evolve[d] this way” (Langley, 1999, p. 692) and to provide concrete examples and anecdotes.

Our research was based on two phases of field work: the first phase lasted from November 2016 to August 2017, and the second phase lasted from March 2018 to August 2018. All 15 case firms were investigated in the two phases of the field work, which allowed us to assess possible changes over time and to follow the digital transformation process over time. A total of three to six interviews per sampled company allowed us to gain information that was not publicly available, validate the obtained information, and mitigate potential biases. It also made it possible to address individual-level motivations and behaviours and understand organisational decision-making in detail (Easterby-Smith et al., 2009) as well as to establish “linkages between firm-specific processes and the commonalities of dynamic capabilities across firms” (Wang & Ahmed, 2007, p. 43) by comparing interview quotes from respondents with diverse professional and educational backgrounds and areas of expertise.

In addition to the firm-specific interviews, we conducted expert interviews with consultants and with CEOs, managers, and digital transformation specialists from other organisations, e.g., larger family enterprises, firms active in industries other than manufacturing, and firms with lower levels of family influence. The 51 additional expert interviews took place in parallel with the main field work and covered general topics such as digital initiatives, potential family influence, and strategic planning concerning digital technologies. For instance, we asked the experts the following questions: How do you assess the overall digital transformation process of Mittelstand firms? What challenges do they face? What (dis-)advantages might they have in comparison with large corporations and firms without family influence? What are their main barriers and how can they be overcome? How do Mittelstand firms difer from other firms regarding digital transformation? This additional information enabled us to study the initiatives of the sampled firms in their context and to appropriately interpret them.

We carefully read and coded the case summaries, the associated interview transcripts, and other material. In the first step, we applied an open coding technique (Corbin & Strauss, 2008); that is, we marked passages that referred to digital activities and the required capabilities, triggers, barriers, or enablers that were in line with our research questions. The researchers first independently coded the interviews and then compared and discussed their coding, especially in the rare case of disagreement. In this step, we also tried to decompose the digital transformation process into several stages and to identify time periods related to certain similar digitalisation eforts, in line with the “temporal bracketing” analysing strategy described by Langley (1999, p. 703). Specifically, we searched for temporal patterns of firms focusing on specific digitalisation activities, applied approaches (e.g., decentralised vs. firm-wide approaches), and capability levels. Therefore, we also diligently coded any “discontinuities” that shifted a firm’s attention and eforts to diferent aspects of digitalisation. It was through this inductive coding process that the three stages of digitalisation, as elaborated below, emerged.

Although the context of our study is family-owned Mittelstand firms in the German-speaking part of Europe, interviews with executives and digital transformation specialists from other organisations helped us “objectify” the findings, increase the validity of our conclusions, and discuss implications for organisations beyond the Mittelstand setting.

## 4. Findings

While the data analysis process followed an inductive procedure, we have opted for a deductive presentation of our findings to enhance readability and save space. We will first provide an overview of the summarised case data before we discuss the theoretical model and related propositions in section 5.1. In the following, we provide an overview of the digital transformation activities that we observed in the sampled firms, thereby identifying three sequential stages of the digital transformation process: process digitalisation, product and service digitalisation, and business model digitalisation. We also reveal the triggers of those stages and dynamic capabilities required in each stage. Next, we outline three pairs of identified barriers and corresponding enablers and elaborate on how these factors hinder or foster the development of the dynamic capabilities required for digital transformation. ## 4.1. Digital transformation process

While every sampled firm showed at least some efort exerted to adopt digital technologies in recent years, the sample firms difered substantially with regard to the degree of their digital transformation at the time the interviews were conducted. While some firms were still exploring the first applications of digital technologies (e.g., bar codes and scanners in supply chain management), others had already managed to develop much more sophisticated solutions $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ integrating virtual reality applications into after-sales services). Representatives from all 15 case firms confirmed that some kind of economic benefit, e.g., in terms of eficiency gains, increased sales, or competitive advantage, had already been achieved with digital technologies to varying degrees at the time of the interviews. However, the interviewees also consistently stated that they were still in a transition and had several stage-dependent challenges and opportunities ahead of them. In the following section, we will focus on the digital transformation process—rather than firm performance or other outcome variables. We will present our findings along the three identified stages—process digitalisation, product and service digitalisation, and business model digitalisation (see Figure 1 and Table 5).

## 4.1.1. Process digitalisation stage

We label the first stage of digital transformation process digitalisation, as it is characterised by the conversion of firm processes and information from an analogue form into a digital form. Firms initiated the transformation process when they became aware of a mismatch between (technically retarded) internal conditions and current IT standards as well as when requests from business partners occurred to align with IT standards and provide digital information, with both conditions being required. While Delta’s CEO emphasised firm-internal awareness (“Back then, we said to ourselves: ‘We can’t go on like this. The costs got completely out of control for us.”), Delta’s project manager referred to the environment (“These [digitalisation issues] are often triggered from the outside, for instance, [from business partners] at trade fairs, industry conferences, etc.”) as the main trigger. In this first stage, case firms typically enhance their established operations with the use of basic digital technologies, such as e-invoicing, RFID tags, or ordinary enterprise resource planning (ERP) tools, to increase eficiency and reduce costs. Delta’s CEO explains the firm’s digitalisation status as follows:

“At the moment, digitalisation is really at the very core of most of our activities here at [Delta]. We want to achieve process automation through digitalisation. [. . .] [However,] we are still occupied with preparing our master data, a necessary precondition for every further step [regarding the digitalisation of processes and, consequently, the digital transformation at all].”

The CEO thus emphasised the importance of process digitalisation as a first step during digital transformation. Based on their digitalisation activities, three of the sample companies (Alpha, Delta, and Iota) were classified as still operating in this stage at the time of the interviews. All the other companies had also gone through this initial stage, which was of varying duration, mostly consisting of several years. In most cases, process digitalisation was carried out in informal structures without defined responsibilities.

The activities in this stage varied among the case companies. For the metal processing company Iota, process digitalisation recently began with the modification of their own ERP system and other approaches to convert analogue data, such as product information used in supply chain management printed on paper record cards, into digital data, which one manager explained as follows: “We recently introduced barcodes in our production process to register information in a digital way.” Alpha’s department head gave another example on how data are digitalised to facilitate their further processing:

“Not so long ago, we digitalised archive data in order to have better access to it. [. . .] This involved old customer data, data from research and development, product specifications, and such things. In fact, we found analogue magnetic tapes with data that needed to be utilised. [. . .] Diskettes were also read to save the data for further processing [in our daily business processes].”

The outcomes of this first stage are digital processes. To achieve those outcomes, according to the interviewees, certain (operational) capabilities are necessary. Delta’s project manager emphasised the following:

“We’re going to need a certain skill set to succeed. [. . .] Right now, a crucial factor is the technical infrastructure. And the optimisation of existing processes, which we will carry out in a diferent way than we have done in recent years. Much more digital. [. . .] We first have to set up an operational foundation on which we can later build.”

Interviewees mentioned operational capabilities, such as the usage of already known business processes, the development of a basic technical infrastructure (in the sense of physical IT infrastructure), and the aforementioned upgrading of master data to make progress with process digitalisation. These operational capabilities are important because they represent the fundamental prerequisites for further initiatives to digitally transform a company. By utilising operational capabilities, companies are able to explore the use of digital technologies and identify areas of application within the organisation. This approach is exemplified by the insights from Alpha’s head of department:

“We are [. . .] digitalising our procurement processes in order to be able to develop these procedures further and to evaluate the data on a large scale. Then, we’ll see what we can actually do with it.”

## 4.1.2. Product and service digitalisation stage

Process digitalisation is followed by a stage that we labelled product and service digitalisation, as it is characterised by the integration of digital technologies into the firm’s products and services as well as by the digitalisation of the firm’s supplier and customer relations. Firms initiated the product and service digitalisation stage when they became aware of technological opportunities inherent in the increased usage of digital technologies for the firm’s products and services and when customers requested them to ofer partly or fully digital products or services. For instance, for Kappa’s department head, the internal awareness of technological opportunities (“At Kappa, the focus is on digitalising products since these are also particularly important for future sales.”) and requests from customers were the main triggers to engage in the digitalisation of products and services:

“There is a trend towards automation [among our customers], which also afects our internal priorities [to more intensively engage with the digitalisation of products and services and increase sales with robotic applications].”

We classified Eta, Ny, and Xi as being in between process digitalisation and product and service digitalisation. Even though the companies on the threshold of the two stages showed signs of adopting digital technologies into products and services, these eforts were still immature, and the firms’ capabilities were not yet developed suficiently to be able to truly work on digitalised products and services (i.e., the companies were still working on incorporating rudimentary digital elements that were lagging behind marketable customer products and services). Thus, although there was an increasing awareness of technological opportunities with digital products and services, these firms were not (yet) able to fulfil customer requests to provide digital products and services. For instance, Xi’s department head stated:

“We’re still having a hard time changing our strategic position. Renewing old patterns of action, dealing with the fact that we now also [aim to] sell digital products. This is a process in which we still have a lot to learn.”

We classified Beta, Theta, Kappa, and Omicron as being in the product and service digitalisation stage. In this second stage, the firms start to digitalise products and services in order to grow by generating additional revenues. In most of the cases studied here, the digitalisation activities took place in project teams but were not coordinated across functions. Eta’s director described the transition of his company from process to product and service digitalisation as follows:

“As a business, we have gone through diferent phases and are now trying to get to a new, higher level of digitalisation. The previous initiatives were very rudimentary, based on procedural processes and first industry 4.0 approaches in production. Even if we still have an incredibly long way to go, we are currently trying to find a lot more structure and thus also find out which paths are the right ones.”

The outcomes of this stage are digital products and services. For instance, some companies created a digital service platform for customers to place orders (Xi), started to ofer remote maintenance services (Kappa), or installed sensors in their products to collect and analyse data in real time (Omicron). Omicron’s manager described how they digitalise products:

“Our goal [. . .] is to create intelligent products. We aim to ofer adapter plates that have a connection to a cloud system. [. . .] Our products are prepared so that the intelligence, i.e., digital add-ons, can be added. Additionally, a Wi-Fi connection is available [for our engines and transmissions].”

In contrast with the first stage, operational capabilities in this second stage must be complemented with dynamic capabilities in order to achieve the desired outcomes of digitalised products and services. For instance, Theta’s department head and Kappa’s specialist stated:

“It is an enormous efort to push this conversion. It requires rethinking. We have to reorganise many of our routines.” (Theta)

“First, we tried what is possible. Afterwards, we initiated even bigger projects, with which we combined knowledge and used opportunities for our business.” (Kappa)

Other interviewees mention, for instance, the capabil ity to change established routines (Kappa), the capability to efectively introduce new procedures (Omicron), the capability to process information (Theta), and the capability to engage in efective strategic decision making (Beta) as important prerequisites for success with product and service digitalisation.

## 4.1.3. Business model digitalisation stage

The third stage of digital transformation that we observed was business model digitalisation, which is characterised by digitally transforming business models and creating holistic digital solutions (as opposed to developing isolated applications, as was the case in the first two stages). Firms entered the business model digitalisation stage when there was internal availability of digital talents to implement digital initiatives as well as external competitive pressure through radical market changes caused by discontinuous technologies (e.g., high-tech start-ups disrupting the automotive industry by providing novel mobility solutions, thus threatening automotive suppliers). Lambda’s CEO states:

“The objective is to create competitive advantages with radical solutions through emerging technologies that do justice to increasing global competitive pressure. [. . .] This has the potential to change our entire business model.”

Gamma, Epsilon, and Zeta were about to enter this stage at the time they were interviewed. Although these companies faced radically changing markets and high competitive pressure, they still struggled with implementing and utilising digital business models due to a lack of fully developed dynamic capabilities (such as the ability to reorganise routines) and to shortcomings regarding the availability of digital talent within the firm. Some case firms (Gamma and Zeta) had already identified the need for action and were taking measures, for instance, beginning employer branding campaigns aimed specifically at attracting digital talent to their companies.

Lambda and My were already in this stage. In this third stage, companies start to integrate digitalisation eforts across the organisation to come up with new, digital business models, as they anticipate that this will provide them with sustainable competitive advantage. For instance, in 2016, the machine and plant manufacturer My established a new business unit that focused on the firm’s digital businesses and bundled together all activities related to automation, software, IT, digital and sensor technologies, alliance management, and corporate ventures with the purpose of coordinating the digitalisation of its entire business model through this digital business unit. In this stage, companies start to apply advanced digital technologies, such as predictive maintenance techniques, virtual reality applications, and blockchain technology. My’s department head explained this as follows:

“One example is the use of virtual reality in, for instance, the repair of our plants in the Amazon, where we can use such technologies to find people in four minutes who can technically repair the defect without being physically on site. The use of other emerging technologies, such as big data and machine learning, is also part of this transformation. [. . .] These technologies enable us to radically innovate our business model.”

In the case of My, the adoption of virtual reality applications changed the business model in its after-sales services. By providing a radically improved value proposition to the customer through advanced digital technologies, the company generates sustainable competitive advantages on the basis of a digitalised business model. Lambda runs its own company builder in Berlin. According to press releases and annual reports, this spin-of coordinates Lambda’s involvement in an ecosystem of start-ups, technology companies, and researchers. Lambda’s CEO explained the technological motives behind this approach as follows:

“We are increasingly focusing on digital technologies with which we can—in a positive sense—achieve radical changes. We not only implement cloud solutions and connectivity in the sense of the IoT but also test decentralised blockchain solutions. [. . .] The idea is to create competitive advantages with radical solutions through emerging technologies that give consideration to global competitive pressure. [. . .] This has the potential to change our entire business model.”

In this third stage, we noted that there is a further shift towards dynamic capabilities. Lambda’s manager underlined that in this stage, the importance of operational capabilities declines:

“These standard questions [in the sense of operational capabilities] are no longer relevant. It will no longer be about building the umpteenth ERP mask. In the future, things will be much more complex and farreaching, such as redistributing responsibilities, gaining competencies, and reassessing partnerships.”

Instead, interviewees noted the necessity of building dynamic capabilities such as the continuous renewal of the organisation through decentralisation, local autonomy, and venturing activities. For instance, Lambda’s department head explained the following during the interview:

“It is important to have people on the ground who actively promote transformation. [. . .] Decentralised resources and complementary competencies work well together in the overall structure.” 22

Firm representatives from Lambda explained that the newly acquired capabilities allowed them to serve customers in a more personalised way, respond more quickly to fluctuating requirements or demands, and, in general, become more agile. According to company representatives, these advantages are one of the best examples of how digital business models can be utilised. In particular, dynamic capabilities such as the development of new strategic partnerships are an essential prerequisite to digitally transforming businesses. This view is aligned with the view of My’s department head, who saw strategic partnerships as a central element in transforming his firm’s business model. The capability to form such partnerships is an important prerequisite for a successful transformation, according to the company representative:

“We have taken risks to foster transformation at our company. [. . .] We are repositioning ourselves completely, with strong strategic partners and completely new approaches and organisational forms.”

Moreover, interviewees mentioned further dynamic capabilities, such as the ability of employees to learn quickly (Lambda) and modify brand management (My), as important requirements for business model digitalisation.

## 4.2. Barriers and enablers in the digital transformation process

In the following section, we will outline important barriers to and enablers of the digital transformation process. Figure 2 summarises the barriers, enablers, and associated dynamic capabilities in each of the three stages of the digital transformation process.

## 4.2.1. Family firm paternalism and cash opportunities

In nine of the case firms (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Zeta, Eta, Theta, Iota, My, and Omicron), paternalistic decision-making structures were an important barrier to developing the dynamic capabilities required in the digital transformation process. Paternalistic decision-making refers to decision-making power in the hands of a senior family member (i.e., a patriarch) who leads the firm in an authoritarian way. In such cases, the family patriarchs’ reluctance, caused by a highly sceptical personal attitude towards digital technologies, resulted in an unwillingness to engage with digital initiatives. Six of these firms have family members on the management board, and the other three firms have family members as shareholders holding significant influence over the operational business of the firm. As these family patriarchs had a central and influential role in their companies, their scepticism towards digital technologies impeded the adoption of digital technologies in their respective firms, at least temporarily. Theta’s director, for instance, explained that “the family CEO does not stand out in regard to digital transformation, so no great leap forward can be expected.” Eta’s department head stated that “basically, the owner has never truly been interested in digitalisation.” A department head at Alpha, a machine manufacturer, explained how the family CEO’s scepticism towards digital technologies stalled the firm’s development of the necessary capabilities and, consequently, the digital transformation process as well:

“The owner and managing director of Alpha does not have any understanding of digital subjects. He personally does not use a personal computer and has all his emails printed out and presented on paper by a secretary since he cannot deal with them himself. Thus, there is very little empathy in the company from our managing director for digital initiatives. [. . .] This means that we are not taking the initiative to build-up competences that could be useful to us in the future.”

As a consequence of the reluctance of family patriarchs, firms forgo opportunities to develop operational capabilities (e.g., establish technical infrastructure) and dynamic capabilities (e.g., the capability to recognise, assimilate, and commercialise new information). Similarly, Eta’s head of department perceives too little support by the family CEO to build up the necessary capabilities:

“It took 16 years for the owner to take a look at our projects because at that time the topic of digitalisation was becoming increasingly popular [in our industry]. Before that, the owner wasn’t really interested in what we were doing here. A lot has been pushed forward, but nothing regarding digitalisation and the like. If the owner keeps [the digital initiatives] so small, then you can’t be surprised if you can’t fully develop your potential.”

The lack of interest by Eta’s family CEO in addressing early digital initiatives in the company has led to limited managerial attention to digital transformation for a long period of time. The undisputed managerial power of the senior family patriarch prevented the firm from advancing in its digital transformation. Even though the increasing awareness of technological opportunities in the digital economy finally led to a partial shift in the family CEO’s cognition, the initiatives of the firm are still cautious in nature. Family firm patriarchs are often accustomed to traditional product development processes, are unwilling to alter routines with decades-long histories, and, as nondigital natives, lack intuition regarding how to use and assess digital technologies, as our interviews with industry experts confirmed. One consultant specialising in the implementation of digital tools in family businesses provided the following explanation for this phenomenon:

“Over the past decades, the oftentimes patriarchal family CEOs were spoiled for success. [. . .] This success was based on conventional approaches to research and development. These previously successful practices have become ingrained in their habits. Due to their socialisation, they often lack the knowhow to determine digital activities of their own accord.”

In addition, and most importantly, family patriarchs often perceive digital technologies to be “dubious and suspicious” techniques that require substantial investment with unclear value, as Iota’s head of department explained:

“You cannot quantify that [the likelihood of success and the return on investment of digital initiativesl. This often becomes a problem when talking to [family] management. The oftentimes unclear return on investment results in a situation in which the owners are less likely to consider such [digital] issues in budgets. This may appear stubborn to the department manager.”

Emphasising the importance of such financial considerations, Alpha’s family CEO explained the following:

“The financing of such [digital] projects in Mittelstand firms like ours is dificult because we do not have the same ample budgets that large [particularly publicly traded] corporations do.”

Family patriarchs were generally unwilling to take on debt or infuse extra equity into the firm to finance digitalisation projects. Indeed, paternalistic decisionmaking structures often lead to cautious and belated investments in digitalisation projects, particularly in the early stages of digital transformation. For instance, some firms forwent the employment of experts (Alpha) who would help advance the development of certain capabilities, such as efective strategic decision making regarding digital initiatives. As another example, some firms were hesitant to acquire technical infrastructure (Iota) to recognise, assimilate, and commercialise new information and facilitate digital progress.

However, some case firms (Beta, Gamma, Zeta, Theta, My, and Omicron) were ultimately able to overcome the challenges associated with paternalism and develop the necessary capabilities. More specifically, firms were able to overcome the reluctance of powerful patriarchs when they could benefit from cash opportunities. Cash opportunities enable resourceconstrained Mittelstand firms to provide funding for digital initiatives and thus to turn the threat-framed situation of unclear return on investment of digita transformation into an opportunity-framed situation, making it more likely that family patriarchs will agree to the engagement of the firm in digital transformation.

In three cases (Gamma, Theta, and My), cash opportunities stemmed from internal sources, and for three other cases (Beta, Zeta, and Omicron), these opportunities came from external sources. Internal sources included tunnelling from other parts of the organisation, as one of Gamma’s department heads explained: “There is a cross-subsidisation of these digital projects by our parent company.” As another example of internal cash opportunities, My financed the development of its digital business unit with the cash generated through the sales of its ownership stake in another company, as one department head explained: “The profits from the sale of our shares in this company made it possible to [finally] tackle the issue [of adopting digital technologies] very actively.” External cash opportunities that enabled digital firm activities included third-party funds, such as innovation or digital transformation grants from national or EU authorities. Beta’s department head elaborated on this as follows:

“Recently, we have also [successfully] applied for third-party funds. For instance, we co-financed our wearables project in warehousing logistics through European Union funding. This, of course, helped us in the decision-making process on whether we should actually implement such a project or not.”

These internal or external cash opportunities helped patriarchal decision makers in the company approach the idea of adopting digital technologies more openly and thus to utilise digital technologies much more intensively. In combination with the opportunity to leverage existing capital through cash opportunities (i.e., sales of shares in other entities), this enabler contributed to overcoming the challenges of patriarchal decision-making structures and ultimately helped facilitate capability building. Beta’s CEO described this result as follows: “If a [digital] project entails significant risks, then such [public] funding is certainly the trigger that causes us to greenlight it.” For traditionally oriented family patriarchs, who are reluctant to invest in digital technologies with their own money because they are unsure which precise return on investment comes along with it, this cash opportunity can be a decisive argument for promoting concrete digital initiatives after all. For instance, one Omicron manager explained this situation as follows:

“The use of such funding can help to provide a decisive impetus to implement a project that would otherwise not have been kicked of [by the family owner].”

One of My’s department heads confirmed the following:

“Without the sale of the shares, this commitment [to the adoption of digital technologies] would not exist, at least not to this extent.”

## 4.2.2. Inconsistent understanding of digital transformation and digital strategy

A second important barrier to the digital transformation process is an inconsistent understanding within the firms concerning the scope and substance of a “digital transformation.” While we observed such inconsistencies in all the studied firms, its extent and, ultimately, its implications varied. In 14 of the sampled firms (all but My), an inconsistent understanding of digital transformation prohibited the integration of digital programme management and thus impeded digital transformation progress. The interviewees suggested that this lack of common understanding might be rooted in the novelty of the new technologies, their radicalness, and their inconsistent description in publications such as newspapers and other media. One of Zeta’s department heads explained how this inconsistency slowed his firm’s progress with regard to digital transformation, and Theta’s director reported similar challenges:

“We come from diferent, isolated applications in our company. This starting point is dificult to handle because it takes a long time before these views can be aligned; only afterwards can one turn to the really big topics.” (Zeta)

“There is a conflicting understanding in the individual departments. [As a consequence,] everyone is working on his or her own initiatives. If we are not able to bring these diferent views together, it becomes very dificult to adopt a holistic approach.” (Theta)

Such inconsistency resulted in and was reinforced by many firms’ tendency to handle digital initiatives as isolated projects, especially in the mid and later stages of the digital transformation process, without superordinate control and coordination. Iota’s head of department exemplified this inconsistency as follows:

“But what I have already noticed is that we have diferent approaches, diferent understandings, as I said before. The production manager understands digitalisation as an improvement of his manufacturing processes, while our sales manager understands this as easier access to the customer. As the head of IT, I bring a completely diferent view. We have not been able to bring these understandings together so far.”

A lack of a common understanding of what digital transformation comprises (and what it does not) slowed the decision-making process in the sample firms and hindered the evolution of both operational and dynamic capabilities inside the organisations, such as the capability to continuously renew the firm, the employees’ learning capabilities, and strategic partnerships. Specifically, without a consistent understanding of terminologies, it is dificult to continuously renew the company, as the individual positions within the company diverge rather than converge. A department head of Eta explained the challenges associated with such a lack of common understanding as follows:

“In addition, many terms are unclear; there is often no central theme. [. . .] How should we renew ourselves digitally if there is no common internal understanding? This is the only way to develop an organisation further on this basis.”

Moreover, such an inconsistent understanding impedes employees’ learning capabilities, as it deprives them of a clear direction and, as a consequence, leads to a discouraging working and learning atmosphere. Indeed, a consistent understanding of digital transformation is necessary to be aware of digital transformation, work to achieve clear objectives, and create value. If employees lack such a common understanding, they might become confused about the next steps with regard to digital transformation as well as about the overall aim of digital transformation of their company. Last, an inconsistent understanding has also turned out to be a barrier for strategic partnerships since it can cause partners to shy away from collaborating due to the lack of orchestration and the missing focus of the firm’s digital initiatives.

An initially inconsistent understanding of digital transformation can, however, be overcome through the development of a digital strategy (Alpha, Gamma, Epsilon, Zeta, Theta, Kappa, and Lambda). The sampled firms varied substantially with regard to the availability of such a digital strategy. One of the most frequently provided reasons for the lack of a digital strategy was that it was seen as unnecessary and conflicting with the typically low levels of formalisation in family-owned Mittelstand firms (e.g., Beta, Eta, and Iota). As a specialist at Eta explained, “[such a digital strategy] might be too bureaucratic for us,” and one Beta manager feared that a “digital strategy might distract the organisation from the actual objectives.” While many family firm decision makers emphasise a rather opportunistic approach to the digital transformation process, which is characterised by a reaction to opportunities without formal plans or structures, a digital strategy, which involves setting goals and structuring the steps to achieve those goals, can help advance the digital transformation process. One of Zeta’s department heads explains how such a written digital strategy, when made accessible to all employees, helps create a common understanding of digital transformation:

“A digital transformation strategy is crucial in order not to lose the structure in these new paths and to keep track of what digital initiatives your company would like to approach.”

Lambda’s experience revealed a direct link between the digital strategy and the inconsistent understanding, as the CEO stated:

“The [digital] strategy makes our job easier. We are not busy explaining over and over again what we mean by digitalisation. [. . .] Now we can work [in the company] in parallel, without teams developing completely separately, to derive clear KPIs from the [digital] strategy and manage projects better.”

These insights show that a digital strategy can create a common understanding of digital transformation and, in particular, of relevant concepts and initiatives and can help overcome respective barriers. Companies digital strategies need to cover various areas of the firm. For instance, Kappa’s department head responsible for the firm’s digital strategy explains:

“We have a digital strategy for our production, which was set up about two years ago. In addition to that, we have a digital strategy for our products, which was developed about a year ago.” 2

Moreover, the strategy needs to be known to all afected firm members. In the case of Ny, a grouplevel digital strategy existed in the Mittelstand firm, yet it was not communicated to all firm members. As a consequence, there was a high level of uncertainty and confusion among the employees, as one manager stated in the interview. Similarly, one of Lambda’s department heads confirmed the important role of the firm’s digital strategy:

“We currently have 62 projects in the field of digital transformation. [. . .] Without a clear strategy, we could not control so many projects at the same time and still maintain a focus. In recent years, we have learned how to handle this.”

In addition, a digital strategy can help not only speed up the decision-making process by providing a common understanding of digital activities (as outlined above) but also foster capability building processes. One manager at Lambda explained in more detail how a digital strategy facilitates capability building within the organisation:

“Our [digital strategy] defines the role of subsidiaries and entities such as our own company builder in Berlin. By transferring competencies to specialised teams, we want to build expertise in niches, such as a start-up ecosystem, and make better use of [digital knowledge] than would be possible from the corporate ofice.”

## 4.2.3. Employee resistance and the communication of early success stories

Employee resistance turned out to be a third relevant barrier in the digital transformation process. While the severity of this barrier varied across cases, we observed in all 15 sample firms that a substantial number of employees, especially long-tenured employees (who are often seen as the core of the Mittelstand firm workforce), were very hesitant to support the digital transformation process. The reason for this behaviour was mostly uncertainty about how digital technologies would afect the individual employee, leading to feelings of fear and frustration. Thus, employees’ resistance mirrors opposition against the adoption of digital technologies that is often triggered by doubts regarding the outcome of a digital transformation. While the reluctance of a family patriarch relates more to a general scepticism towards digital technologies that is often rooted in ignorance of the technical possibilities, the resistance of employees often refers to the lack of clarity regarding what consequences a digital transformation will have for the workforce, e.g., with regard to a suspected dismissal of personnel. Xi’s head of department explained this as follows:

“We can think of a plan [for the digital transformation process], but if we do not get it implemented because our employees are not cooperative, then it is all for nothing. Therefore, we must succeed in involving every single employee, making digitalisation accessible to him or her and showing him or her why he or she should not be afraid of it.”

Firms with employees ultimately showing less resistance to change through a digital transformation (e.g., Gamma, Epsilon, and Kappa) tended to go through all stages of the transformation process more smoothly than those with highly resistant employees (e.g., Alpha, Delta, Eta, and Iota). Employee resistance impedes firms in their eforts to develop important capabilities. In particular, the reorganisation of routines and brand management are afected by employee resistance. The employees in charge of the daily business of processing operational routines play a key role in reorganising routines. Delta’s CEO emphasised the following:

“The primary barrier is the human. [Digital transformation] is an extremely dificult reorientation for employees. [. . .] Their unwillingness to face the digital change makes us lethargic and inflexible. Without appropriate countermeasures, there will be no renewal process.”

If employees resist cooperating in a reorganisation due to their resistance to digital transformation, the required changes cannot be implemented. We observed similar mechanisms with modified brand management. In the cases of Alpha, Delta, and Iota, employees showed high levels of resistance towards digital transformation and thus could not authentically represent a modified, digital-focused brand management to external business partners and markets, which hampered their firm’s success. Alpha’s department head underlined the following: “Opposition in the workforce complicates change and renewal.” In other words, dynamic capabilities such as the capability to reorganise routines and modify brand management are impeded by employee resistance.

Several examples show that the communication of early success stories in the digital transformation process can help overcome the abovementioned challenges related to employee resistance and thus spur the transformation process. While most firms in our sample had early successes related to digital transformation (all but Alpha and Iota), only nine firms broadly communicated those success stories within and outside the organisation (Beta, Gamma, Delta, Epsilon, Zeta, Eta, Lambda, My, and Omicron) and were therefore able to overcome employee resistance. My’s specialist explained why early success stories were so important in the organisation:

“We already had some successes quickly after the foundation of our digital business unit. This was rather unexpected. [. . .] One such example was the formation of a digital joint venture in the US, which has been developed by a true heavyweight of the digital scene. [. . .] With this joint venture, we have shown both internally and externally that we are serious about digital transformation and are also motivated to take the next steps.”

As the example of Zeta shows, many decision makers had already become aware of the power of those early success stories and used them purposefully to motivate their employees:

“I always like to talk about the ‘small moments of success,’ which we have to create for the staf. It’s about getting people involved in the subject and taking them with us on a journey” (Zeta, head of department).

Early success stories can help increase awareness about digital projects and demonstrate to internal and external stakeholders that the firm is able to efectively handle them. In particular, these stories demonstrate to employees that digital technologies can entail “real” benefits for the company, from which individual employees might also benefit (e.g., through increased bonuses or smoothened work routines). Moreover, by sharing early success stories, exchanges among employees related to digital technologies are intensified within the company. Such increased communication and engagement by employees can, in turn, reduce resistance to digital transformation. Positive stories about recent successes have a particularly positive efect on employees, motivating them to engage in and complete digital initiatives, as one of Gamma’s responsible managers illustrated:

“It is definitely a success that we have already developed many new products in the area of industry 4.0 and in the context of connectivity and brought them to market maturity. This helps to improve the acceptance of digital issues across decision makers and all employees in the company.”

Thus, with early success stories, companies are able to reduce employee resistance to the digital transformation process. By making digital initiatives more tangible and ofering ways to participate in early successes, Mittelstand firms can address resistance and channel the workforce into constructive ways of approaching digital transformation. Omicron’s manager explains how this mitigating efect is linked to dynamic capabilities:

“We have installed more and more digital components in our mechanical drive technology. We have also told our people why we are doing this. And despite initial scepticism, it was well received because people saw that these new products were successful. That was very important for all further product developments [with digital components]. Our customers also see that more and more is happening here, so we are being received very diferently in the market [regarding digital initiatives].”

The example of Omicron shows how employee resistance towards digital technologies can be overcome by demonstrating that digital projects lead to success for the company. As a result, employees in various divisions of the company become much more open to using digital technologies. Evidence from press releases, company brochures, and observations from industry fairs reveal that this approach supports the reorganisation of internal routines and increases a firm’s capability to modify its brand management with digital products.

## 5. Model and propositions

Our findings show that the digital transformation in family-owned Mittelstand firms is a three-step process consisting of the process digitalisation, product and service digitalisation, and business model digitalisation stages,<sup>4</sup> which are associated with diferent technologies and diferent necessary capabilities. In the following, we will briefly summarise the theoretical model, which emerged from the case-based findings detailed in section 4, and provide a set of propositions. Figure 1 and Table 5 summarise the details of the model.

The first stage of the digital transformation process of Mittelstand firms is the process digitalisation stage. It is triggered by both firm-internal (i.e., the awareness of the mismatch between internal conditions and current IT standards) and firm-external (i.e., requests from business partners to align with IT standards and provide digital information) triggers, which encourage the firm to engage in digitalisation-related activities, specifically, the digitalisation of the firm’s processes. To be able to efectively digitalise the processes, firms need to build digitalisation-related operational capabilities that have not necessarily been present in the firm so far. These capabilities include the development and usage of a basic technical infrastructure and the enhancement of master data. Based on the insights of Teece (2014), we categorise those operational capabilities as “sensing activities” because they enable companies not only to explore digital technologies but also to establish a stable and up-todate technical basis for further developments. With an emphasis on sensing activities, firms in this stage are primarily engaged in the identification and development of technological opportunities. In particular, those operational capabilities set the groundwork for comprehending and assessing the current digital opportunities of a specific firm in its specific market, and they allow the firm to understand what might be possible in the future in terms of digital business opportunities. Moreover, digital processes might also help companies scan their environment for digital opportunities. We thus propose the following:

Proposition 1a. Process digitalisation is triggered by a mismatch between the internal conditions and current IT standards (internal trigger) as well as business partners’ requests to provide digital information (external trigger).

Proposition 1b. During process digitalisation, firms need to build up operational capabilities, such as the usage of existing business processes, the development and usage of a basic technical infrastructure, and the enhancement of master data (i.e., sensing activities).

The second stage, product and service digitalisation, focuses on activities related to ofering digital products and services to customers. Our case data showed that two requirements had to be in place before firms started to enter this stage. First, the process digitalisation stage needed to have been completed. This was important, as firms in the second stage often built upon the capabilities, knowledge, and experience gained in the first stage to successfully develop and market their digital products and services. Second, the product and service digitalisation stage was often set into motion when internal (i.e., the increasing awareness of technological opportunities in the digital economy) and external (i.e., requests from customers to provide digital products and services) triggers encouraged the firms to take the next step in the digital transformation process.

In the second stage, both operational (such as the usage of existing business processes) and dynamic capabilities (such as the reorganisation of established routines, efective strategic decision making, and the establishment of organisational routines that allow the recognition, assimilation, and commercialisation of new information) are needed. When faced with challenges, firms often refer to their learning and experience in the first stage, which helps them further develop relevant capabilities in the second stage. The capabilities allow the company to further expand its technical infrastructure and simultaneously implement substantial changes regarding established routines in its organisation with digital technologies. In this second stage, the exploitation of digital technologies becomes more important. The combination of operational and dynamic capabilities allows the company to pursue seizing opportunities, that is, to activate resources to capture value from digital technologies. The focus on seizing activities mirrors the strong emphasis on the mobilisation of a firm’s own resources to exploit given opportunities. For instance, by reorganising routines, firms may identify chances to ofer digital services to their existing customers. Moreover, the newly developed ability to recognise, assimilate, and commercialise new information might ofer the opportunity to produce and sell novel digital products to existing or new markets, an entrepreneurial action for which efective strategic decision making is also necessary.<sup>5</sup> These seizing opportunities, in turn, enable companies in this stage to integrate digital technologies into products and services and thus to achieve relevant growth that is reflected in sales. Hence, we propose the following:

Proposition 2a. Product and service digitalisation is triggered by an increasing internal awareness of the technological opportunities inherent in digital technologies (internal trigger) and requests from customers to provide digital products and services (external trigger).

Proposition 2b. During product and service digitalisation, firms need to build up both their operational capabilities, such as the development of a basic technical infrastructure, and their dynamic capabilities, such as engaging in efective strategic decision making, learning how to reorganise routines, and recognising, assimilating, and commercialising new information (i.e., seizing activities).

The third stage, business model digitalisation, focuses on activities targeted at adapting the business model to include a digital core. Again, the capabilities gained in the second stage are an important input factor for the third stage. Additionally, internal (i.e., the availability of digital talent to implement and utilise digital initiatives) and external (i.e., competitive pressure, discontinuous technologies changing markets radically) triggers are important to push the firms from the second to the third stage of digital transformation.

The dynamic capabilities built up in this stage refer to substantially reconfiguring the boundaries of the company. In particular, the digital transformation stage requires changes all across the organisation, including in coordination, communication, learning, and branding. Within the dynamic capabilities framework, we can interpret this change as transformational activities. An emphasis on these transformational activities particularly involves the continuous renewal of a business. Those capabilities are important for digitalising business models because they make it possible to capture the organisation in its entirety and to initiate radical changes that are necessary for a transformation of the organisation. For instance, by relying on strategic partnerships, firms are able to substantially change their business models, as this reliance allows them to learn from best practices and identify strategic opportunities. We thus propose the following:

Proposition 3a. Business model digitalisation is triggered by the availability of digital talent to implement and utilise digital initiatives (internal trigger) and by external competitive pressure and the radically changing market (external triggers).

Proposition 3b. During business model digitalisation, firms need to build up their dynamic capabilities such as ensuring continuous renewal through local autonomy, engaging in strategic partnerships, quick learning by employees, and modifying brand management (i.e., transformational activities).

As our findings above show, the availability of certain Mittelstand firm-related barriers and enablers leads to substantial heterogeneity among those firms with regard to their digital transformation. First, paternalistic decision-making structures hinder the development of both operational and dynamic capabilities because the family patriarch’s discomfort with adopting new digital technologies afects both of these categories. The impeding efect of paternalistic decision-making structures is particularly strong in the early stages of the digital transformation process (sensing and seizing activities)<sup>6</sup> because important investment decisions are already made at an early stage by the family owners of the company.

Cash opportunities allow firms to overcome reluctance on the part of the family patriarch with regard to digital transformation projects and enable firms to reconfigure their efective strategic decision making and thus build up an important dynamic capability. More precisely, with regard to sensing and seizing activities,<sup>7</sup> these opportunities help change the family patriarch’s perspective from an unclear return on investments to the opportunities associated with digital technologies. With such funding, the process of investing in digital technologies can be significantly accelerated (e.g., the purchase of IT hardware or the hiring of experts), especially since many external financing options, such as public subsidies, are explicitly oriented towards explorative projects. This acceleration in turn leads to the development of both operational (e.g., the development and deployment of a technical infrastructure) and dynamic capabilities, such as efective strategic decision making and the capability to recognise, assimilate, and commercialise new information.

Proposition 4a. Paternalistic decision making hinders the development of both operational and dynamic capabilities. Hence, it impedes sensing and seizing activities in the digital transformation process.

Proposition 4b. Cash opportunities can mitigate the challenges associated with paternalistic decisionmaking structures.

As a second barrier, the inconsistent understanding of digital transformation impedes the development of dynamic capabilities such as the capability of the firm to engage in continuous renewal, the capability of employees to learn quickly, and the capability to form strategic partnerships. This barrier is particularly efective in the second and third stages of the digital transformation process (seizing and transformational activities) due to the higher degree of complexity and advanced technologies in these stages.<sup>8</sup> In these stages, a growing number of digital initiatives in the company are typically pursued and have to be orchestrated. Making the digital strategy available to all afected firm members can help mitigate these problems. Specifically, the increasing complexity of the projects leads to a growing desire from employees and external partners to form an unambiguous picture of the company’s goals for a digital strategy. The digital strategy plays a crucial role in building learning capabilities and enabling the business to continuously renew itself: it provides the objectives for digital developments and thereby promotes them. The absence of a digital strategy restricts this capability to learn and, consequently, the capability of the firm to continuously renew itself. Moreover, a digital strategy provides better conditions for a firm to develop strategic partnerships because collaboration partners recognise and work towards clear overarching goals in the context of the firm’s digital transformation. Moreover, a digital strategy also fosters efective strategic decision making and the ability to recognise, assimilate, and commercialise new information, whereas the reorganisation of routines and brand management are only partly encouraged through such a strategy. Hence, we suggest the following:

Proposition 5a. An inconsistent understanding of digitalisation acts as a barrier in the digital transformation process by hindering the development of operational and dynamic capabilities. Hence, it impedes seizing and transformational activities.

Proposition 5b. The existence and communication of a digital strategy mitigate the challenges related to an inconsistent understanding of digitalisation.

A third barrier to digital transformation in Mittelstand firms is employee resistance to change, which occurs regardless of the scope and depth of the digital initiatives. Employees resist the digital transformation process because of the inherent uncertainty and the unclear implications for their own work. As a result, sensing, seizing, and transformational activities are stalled when employee resistance impedes the ability to continuously renew the firm, the ability of employees to learn quickly, the ability to form strategic partnerships, (to a lesser extent) the ability to engage in efective strategic decision making and the ability to recognise, assimilate, and commercialise new information. Early success stories can serve as an enabler in this regard, as they have a positive influence on internal and external stakeholders, regardless of the complexity of the digital initiative. Specifically, they create a sense of unity, point to the positive aspects of the digital transformation process and, hence, encourage employees. Therefore, the communication of early success stories continuously helps firms build dynamic capabilities such as reorganising routines and modifying brand management with a strong digital component. Additionally, early success stories also help build the ability to continuously renew the firm, the ability of employees to learn quickly, the ability to form strategic partnerships, the ability (to a much lower degree) to engage in efective strategic decision making and the ability to recognise, assimilate, and commercialise new information. We thus propose:

Proposition 6a. Employees’ resistance to digital technologies, and the resulting lack of unity across the workforce, acts as a barrier in the digital transformation process by hindering the development of dynamic capabilities. Hence, it impedes sensing, seizing, and transformational activities.

Proposition 6b. The communication of early success stories mitigates the challenges associated with employee resistance to digital technologies.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Contribution to research

First, this study’s findings contribute to research on digital transformation by revealing the process, specifically the stages and triggers, of the digital transformation in family-owned Mittelstand firms. With these insights, our research extends the nascent stream of literature on digital transformation in information systems and management research (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Nambisan, 2017; Yoo et al., 2012). While prior research has tried to treat digital transformation more as an event or a status (Berman, 2012; Matt et al., 2015) into which firms can transition by adopting a specific form of digital innovation (Ceipek et al., 2021), our model highlights the processual dimension of digital transformation, which implies that specific information systems and capabilities might be required at diferent points in the process. Prior research has focused on specific segments of digital transformation, such as selected drivers (e.g., Macredie & Mijinyawa, 2011; Zhang et al., 2013), outcomes (e.g., Rehm & Goel, 2017; Ross & Blumenstein, 2015), or digital technologies (e.g., Ceipek et al., 2021). We contribute to the extant research by consolidating the fragmented understanding thus far and by explaining digital transformation as a process consisting of the three stages of process digitalisation, product and service digitalisation, and business model digitalisation. Therefore, we extend the organisationa approach of Yoo et al. (2012) and the findings by Nambisan (2017) on digital technology to a more integrated and interdisciplinary framework. Knowledge of the stages of digital transformation is important for information systems research because it also helps better link the technological dimension of digital technology adoption with the processual boundaries in an organisation. Moreover, an in-depth understanding of the three-staged process helps enhance theorising on the digital transformation of organisations at diferent maturity levels.

A further contribution to information systems and management research is that, based on our case evidence, we reveal and explain the barriers and enablers of the digital transformation process. Information systems research has mostly addressed the technological component of digital transformation (Yoo, 2013). However, while research from general management and innovation in SMEs and family firms has revealed that the success of the digital transformation process might be hindered by several firm-level impediments that go beyond the technological component (Casprini et al., 2017; De Massis et al., 2018; Mohd Salleh et al., 2017; Peña-Vinces et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2013), it has remained silent about the precise mechanisms. We extend this research by identifying a set of barriers and enablers as well as their underlying mechanisms.

Hence, we explain in detail how barriers stall the development of the necessary dynamic capabilities and how respective enablers help mitigate those impediments, thus extending previous studies in this field that focused on technical barriers (e.g., Casprini et al., 2017; Mohd Salleh et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2013). Specifically, the paternalism-related barrier indicates that a firm’s digital transformation is hampered by—in addition to potential technological issues—human factors that are often rooted in organisational decision making. These findings help us better understand family firms’ decision making (Chirico & Nordqvist, 2010) throughout the digitalisation process. In addi tion, we show that the inconsistent understanding of digital transformation in Mittelstand firms might be explained by the lack of formalisation that is often observed in such firms (De Massis et al., 2015). While this lack of formalisation has been identified as an advantage in certain innovation contexts (König et al., 2013), it appears to be a barrier in the digitalisa tion context. Hence, our findings reveal that specific enablers for canonical innovation (e.g., lack of forma lisation) can hinder digital innovation. Finally, the important role played by employees as well as their resistance to engaging in digitalisation projects extends the current understanding of information systems research. The extant information systems research has focused on the individual’s attitude towards the usage of digital technologies, which has been found to be afected by such technology’s per ceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Legris et al., 2003). We, however, shed light on the firm level implications of employee resistance and how it can be overcome. While previous information systems research found user resistance to the implementation of digital technologies to be less relevant when the underlying change is considered favourable (Joshi, 1991), we extend this view by providing details on how to overcome the challenges associated with employee resistance by making use of early success stories in a firm’s digital transformation.

Based on our inductive theorising, we also identify and theorise about important enablers of digital transformation—cash opportunities, digital strategy, and the communication of early success stories—which have received comparably less attention in information systems and management research. In particular, the introduction of slack resources (Troilo et al., 2013) and the communication of a digital strategy or success stories (Pratono, 2016; Yeow et al., 2018) might be important routes for theorising about and understanding what makes the digital transformation of organisations successful. Knowledge about those barriers and enablers is important, as they might explain why several digital transformation attempts have failed in real case scenarios (Sutclif et al., 2019). As such, our theorising encourages information systems researchers to take such capability-related, firm-level barriers into account when designing studies, collecting and analysing data, and designing potential bestcase solutions. By explaining how enablers help mitigate the efect of barriers and revealing insights into how these efects afect the development of capabilities, we provide further enrichment for both the information systems literature and the management literature (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Limaj et al., 2016; Nambisan, 2017; Yeow et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2008).

We also contribute to the literature on innovation in Mittelstand firms (De Massis et al., 2018). While prior research has often focused on the impact of digital technologies on new ventures (e.g., Briel et al., 2018) we shed light on the digital transformation process in Mittelstand firms. We do so by considering their idio syncrasies, such as family ownership (Berlemann & Jahn, 2016; De Massis et al., 2018). Thus, we extend prevalent findings on incumbent organisations that have established routines (and capabilities) but might still lack resources and capabilities due to familiness (Chirico & Nordqvist, 2010; Salvato & Melin, 2008) and resource constraints (Burg et al., 2012; Hessels & Parker, 2013; De Massis et al., 2018). First, a veto power regarding fundamental issues has been identified as an impeding factor in capability building in family firms (e.g., Reisinger & Lehner, 2015). We extend this prior research by showing that even without oficial veto power, patriarchs can hamper capability building and thus digital transformation. With these insights, we also address the “growing interest in paternalistic leadership research” (Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008, p. 566) by extending the knowledge on social structures and deci sion making in organisations. Therefore, we also pro vide a better understanding of related concepts based on authoritarian leadership structures, such as a matriarchy (Huang et al., 2015). We also go further than prior research by suggesting that such family influence does not, in all cases, lead to stalled digital transformation (Ho et al., 2017); for instance, cash opportunities can be helpful in overcoming this barrier (Salvato & Melin, 2008). Second, prior research on family-firm innovation (e.g., Duran et al., 2016) has argued that employees are a particularly important dri ver of innovation output in resource-constrained family firms. Our case evidence reveals, however, that, in line with theoretical arguments on the adoption of discon tinuous technologies by family firms (König et al., 2013), employee resistance is one main barrier to the digital transformation process. Going beyond prior research, we show how resistant employees can hinder the development of relevant dynamic capabilities such as the reorganisation of routines (König et al., 2013). We also show, however, that such resistance can be overcome by sharing early success stories within the firm, which is in line with but goes beyond prior research on storytelling in family firms (e.g.,

Kammerlander et al., 2015), which has so far focused on storytelling within the family. Moreover, with regard to formalisation, prior research predicted that family influence frees companies from high levels of formalisation (Carney, 2005) and argued that low levels of formalisation enable the adoption of discontinuous technologies in those firms (König et al., 2013). Our findings, however, demonstrate how an inconsistent understanding of digital technologies, which is often rooted in a lack of formalisation, can hinder the digital transformation process, thus challenging previous assumptions of formalisation in family firms. The reason for this is that the inconsistent understanding impedes the development of dynamic capabilities such as the capability to continuously renew the firm and the capability of employees to learn quickly. However, we also show that a digital strategy, if communicated to all relevant stakeholders, can mitigate this efect, thus ofering new dimensions for family business research dealing with the role of formalisation (König et al., 2013; De Massis et al., 2015).

Finally, a further contribution of our study relates to dynamic capabilities theory (Teece, 2018): to address the call of Yoo et al. (2012), we focus more closely on capability building in the context of digital technologies. While prior information systems research has shown that dynamic capabilities play an important role in the adoption of digital technologies (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Zhang et al., 2013), current research does not provide adequate responses to the question of which dynamic capabilities these are and which parts of the digital transformation process they are required for. By revealing the importance of dynamic capabilities in the digital transformation process, we confirm the assumptions of the extant literature. However, by providing detailed insights into the role of specified dynamic capabilities, we considerably extend prior research (Teece, 2014; Teece et al., 1997). We integrate diferent approaches within the dynamic capabilities research stream to facilitate a shared understanding of the difer ences among operational and dynamic capabilities (Helfat & Winter, 2011: Zahra et al., 2006), to describe the functioning of both kinds of capabilities within one integrated transformation process, and to explain the individual function of each capability (Schilke, 2014). Moreover, we shed light on the interplay among enablers, barriers, and the development of dynamic capabilities that are new for an organisation, thus extending previous research on dynamic capabilities that lacks insights into the specific associations with barriers and enablers (Teece, 2018; Teece et al., 1997; Winter, 2003). In addition to providing a list of these dynamic capabilities, we explain their influence in the three stages of the digital transformation process and categorise them as relating to sensing, seizing, or transforming activities, thereby informing the research that aims to understand the role of dynamic capabilities in digital transformation (Teece, 2018). Distinguishing among diferent types of dynamic capabilities is important for information systems research because it helps to better capture which types of capabilities afect the digital transformation process and which stages these capabilities are prevalent in (Bharadwaj, 2000; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006; Zhang et al., 2013). Moreover, our research extends previous glimpses into the role of internal and external triggers (Kuuluvainen, 2012; see also Rehm & Goel, 2017; Müller et al., 2018) by revealing a nuanced understanding of how they catalyse the digital transformation process. We also demonstrate that the development of capabilities is a basic requirement for organisations to successfully proceed in digital transformation, in line with a small but growing number of studies in this field (Karimi & Walter, 2015, 2016; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010).

Our findings also contribute insights into the idiosyncrasies of Mittelstand firms with regard to their digital transformation process. While large corporations frequently invest in building up their innovation hubs in order to digitalise (Leifer et al., 2001), such activities were rather atypical for the observed Mittelstand firms. Instead, the sample firms took a more cautious, step-by-step process, relying on internal resources, which is in line with prior research on the innovation investment behaviour and risk attitude of small- and medium-sized family firms (Duran et al., 2016; Limaj et al., 2016). Moreover, we reveal important insights into how and with whom Mittelstand firms cooperate in order to support their digital transformation. While larger corporations set a particular focus on new forms of collaboration, e.g., ecosystems, crowd sourcing initiatives, or open innovation contests (Boudreau et al., 2011; Faraj et al., 2011), these approaches are less prevalent among Mittelstand firms. This insight corresponds with the finding of extant research that family-owned Mittelstand firms are reluctant to engage with new cooperation partners (Kotlar et al., 2013) and tend to rely on long-established networks (Duran et al., 2016) as well as on their embeddedness in a local community (De Massis et al., 2018). While our findings were generated in the context of German-speaking family-owned Mittelstand firms, the expert interviews indicate that our findings are also relevant to other contexts. First, the staged process of digital transformation might also be observable in other types of firms but with potentially shorter time spans or more parallelism. Several representatives from large companies confirmed a similar process pattern for other firms in our expert interviews. The expert interviews also revealed similar motivations and focuses at each stage. Second, some of the barriers and enablers might also be applicable to other contexts, yet the severity of the barriers and efectiveness of the enablers might depend on the leadership culture, risk attitude, and individualism vs. collectivism in those contexts. An expert from an advisory firm explained that paternalism can even occur in large, non-family corporations—yet is less salient than in Mittelstand firms. Patriarchal management styles, often practised by long-tenured incumbent CEOs, and political networks inside and outside the organisation, which are reminiscent of family-like bonds, also exist in large corporations, according to the expert. As such, our findings also contribute to research on decision making (Pellegrini & Scandura, 2008) and formalisation (Mattes, 2014) in organisations. Third, the dynamic capabilities identified in our research might be useful for firms of all sizes and ownership types aiming to undergo digital transformation (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Teece, 2018). The generalisability of the necessary dynamic capabilities was also supported in our expert interviews. Both the distinction between operational and dynamic capabilities and the nature of the specific dynamic capabilities mentioned in our study were essentially confirmed to be applicable to other contexts, such as larger, non-family firms, by the interviewed experts.

## 6.2. Management and policy implications

Our findings also have important practical implications. Executives of family-owned Mittelstand firms are increasingly required to address management questions related to digital transformation (De Massis et al., 2018; Remane et al., 2017). Our model helps CEOs, CIOs, CDOs, IT and digital technologies advisors, and other decision makers and involved parties understand this process in more detail. Specifically, this study sheds light on the diferent motivations and triggers for advancing the digital transformation process as well as the operational and dynamic capabilities required in each of the stages. The proposed model might enable managers to assess their firm’s status quo in the digital transformation process. After such assessment, the managers might draw on our model to learn about specific transformation activities and technologies that can be implemented.

Moreover, by identifying both barriers and enablers, we hope to provide firm members with important insights into potential pitfalls of as well as best practices for successfully handling such complexities in relatively resource-constrained firms—insights that are equally important for general managers and managers responsible for information technologies (Sutclif et al., 2019). Specifically, managers can scrutinise whether the mentioned barriers are salient in their organisations and design responses in relation to the mentioned enablers. In particular, the designation of a digital strategy and the clear communication of early success stories appear to be relatively inexpensive yet powerful tools to unify employees and motivate them to support the digital transformation process (Remane et al., 2017). In addition, while a lack of formalisation is a key characteristic of family-owned firms and can often be considered an advantage (König et al., 2013), our findings provide clear evidence of how a digital strategy can aid in the development of valuable dynamic capabilities. As such, CIOs or CDOs might push for the existence and communication of a company-wide digital strategy throughout the organisation (Yeow et al., 2018).

To foster digital transformation, Mittelstand firms need to make this topic a firm priority that is communicated from the top throughout the organisation. Mittelstand firms can thereby build on their eficient decision-making structures and their centralised power to direct the firm towards digital transformation (Duran et al., 2016). Unlike large corporations, the sampled Mittelstand firms usually prefer not to invest in their own incubators to drive digital transformation due to resource constraints (De Massis et al., 2018). Going forward, Mittelstand firms might consider finding new efective ways of collaboration to acquire external expertise, which is essential for their digital transformation. One option is to collaborate with start-ups that possess knowledge about digital processes, products/services, and business models (Teece, 2018). Such collaboration could build on formal or informal cooperation—with or without financial stakes—and could provide mutual benefits for both the Mittelstand firms and the start-ups. Additionally, initiatives that bundle resources could help alleviate problems related to resource constraints. Participation in online communities organised by established platforms, open innovation contests coordinated by third-party hubs, and campaigns from governmental or academic institutions might be a feasible way for firms to minimise the use of their own resources.

In addition to these managerial insights, our findings carry important implications for policy makers. In particular, the strong link between cash opportunities and the reduction of family patriarchs’ reluctance highlights the important role of (external) funding in driving the digital transformation process of Mittelstand firms. Given the importance of such firms for the economy (Berlemann & Jahn, 2016; Bornheim, 2000; Shepherd & Zacharakis, 2000), dedicating public funds that are accessible to Mittelstand firms, that are easy to apply for, and that support these companies in their eforts to digitalise their processes, products, and business models appear to be an important enabler of their business transformation.

## 6.3. Limitations and avenues for future research

This study has several limitations, many of which indicate promising avenues for further research. First, as with all exploratory, case-based studies, we do not aim for statistical generalisability. Based on our eforts to strengthen the validity of our work, as described in the methods section, we are highly confident that the identified mechanisms also apply in other settings. We encourage future research to identify further barriers and enablers in other contexts; scholars might aim to investigate, based on either qualitative or quantitative studies, cultural idiosyncrasies in other, e.g., non-European, settings (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). In addition, researchers are encouraged to study the relative importance of barriers and enablers with regard to the development of diferent dynamic capabilities and in diferent stages of the transformation process. Moreover, future research might dig deeper into the interplay of various operational and dynamic capabilities. For instance, researchers might study how firms are successful or unsuccessful in reconfiguring extant operational capabilities, thereby developing further dynamic capabilities. They might also research the drivers of or hurdles in such reconfigurations.

Second, given the timing of the digital transformation phenomenon, all of the sampled firms were still in the middle of the digital transformation process at the time of writing. While our interviews and the additional data we collected already indicate sources of success and failure, we cannot objectively measure financial success with digital transformation at present, and we thus leave the examination of performance consequences (e.g., regarding market leadership, customer satisfaction, and revenue growth) to future research (see also Cragg et al., 2013; Dibrell et al., 2008; Ross & Blumenstein, 2015). Specifically, longitudinal case work (e.g., Langley, 1999) would be helpful to confirm, and potentially refine, the identified stages. Third, and relatedly, the process identified in this study consists of three stages, the last of which is business model digitalisation. Given the volatility of the environment (including the COVID-19 pandemic and the impact of the “corona crisis” on organisations) and the speed of breakthrough technological innovations, one might assume that the process does not end with this stage but might continue with subsequent process steps that are associated with new challenges and require additional dynamic capabilities (see, e.g., Venkatraman, 1994 for transformational IT processes). We leave it to future research to identify subsequent stages. Last, researchers might study the efect of further, family related enablers and barriers of digital transformation. In this context, it would be useful to build on the concept of socioemotional wealth (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007), as such non-economic considerations might further afect the willingness of decision-makers to engage in certain stages of the digital transformation process.

## 7. Conclusion

The rise of digital transformation, which is one of the major challenges and opportunities for companies today and will continue to be so in the coming years, is remarkable: although the scholarly debate on digital technologies started quite some time ago, the antecedents, processes, and implications of firms’ digital transformation for information systems and management research are still under-researched. This multiple case study attempts to contribute to the literature by advancing our knowledge of how resourceconstrained, family-owned Mittelstand firms in particular can successfully advance their digital transformation process. More specifically, our research analyses the processual dimension of digital transformation in family-owned Mittelstand firms; triggers, enablers, and barriers in the digital transformation process; and the role of dynamic capabilities in achieving digital transformation. As such, we believe that our study can stimulate an interesting stream of research.
