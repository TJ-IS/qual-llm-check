# Preventing the digital scars of COVID-19

Marco Marabelli<sup>a</sup>, Emmanuelle Vaast<sup>b</sup> and Jingyao Lydia Li<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information and Process Management Department, Bentley University, Waltham, United States; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems, Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Canada

## ABSTRACT

In this commentary, we consider the ambivalent role of ubiquitous computing during the COVID pandemic and we point to the risk that some negative, IT-related practices associated with the pandemic will endure after it. We call these lasting efects the digital scars of COVID-19. The same IT that has positive impacts for some people might have negative impacts for others – often vulnerable populations, minorities and socio-economically disadvantaged groups. Some issues stem from the longstanding digital divide that characterises modern societies. Yet, the pandemic is exacerbating these inequalities. We are worried that some debatable uses of technology will persist after the pandemic is over. We therefore point to the prominent role of the IS community in enabling positive aspects of IT use during and after the pandemic, while mitigating negative aspects, especially in the long run. Our sociotechnical background enables us to see these dynamics in a processual and holistic way. To shed light on these issues, we analyse three key technologies widely used to deal with COVID (social software, AI/ML and robotics) and identify critical topics and associated research questions where IS scholarship should focus its attention to generate novel theorising and impactful practical insights.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

On December 30 2019, HealthMap, an artificial intelligence system at the Boston Children’s Hospital and scientists at PMED (Program for Monitoring Emerging Diseases) detected anomalous behaviours of a particularly aggressive coronavirus in the city of Wuhan, the capital city of China’s Hubei province (Naudé, 2020). Wuhan’s urban area has a population of over 11 million people. One week later, the WHO (World Health Organisation) and the US CDC (Centre for Disease Control) made the announcement that a potential localised epidemic of a new coronavirus might be at its outset in Wuhan. The rest is wellknown history for people and countries across the globe.

In this commentary, we discuss how Information Technology (IT) has afected and will afect individual, organisational and societal practices during and after the COVID pandemic. IT in the last two decades has increasingly become ubiquitous (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002). Its pervasive role during the COVID health crisis has been essential, e.g., to connect people/organisations, enhance clinical research and automate services and some aspects of decision-making. Technologies have substantially helped major actors (e.g., governments, corporations, private citizens) deal with the global pandemic. They have enabled remote work and access to real-time information on the disease. They have supported diagnostic systems and clinical research more generally. During shelter-inplace periods they also have provided people with comforting amenities such as video streaming, social software/gaming etc. These are all positive aspects of the technologies at hand.

Yet, as IT has become even more vital for many, various aspects of technologies being rolled out quickly and with limited attention to broad societal efects have turned to be problematic and, in some cases, have raised ethical issues. We are concerned that these technologies or some aspects of their use in practice might remain after the pandemic is over.

Drawing on this concern, in this commentary we develop a theoretical construct that we call digital scars, which we define as ethically problematic sociotechnical innovations that outlast their emergency rollouts, when broader efects of these technologies could not be carefully examined. Digital scars are therefore diferent than more general long-term societal efects of technologies for which design did not foresee unintended uses (cf. Majchrzak et al., 2016).

In this commentary, we expand on the notion of digital scars in reference to potential issues associated with the COVID post pandemic. Various actors in our society having diferent access to IT resources represents a remarkable exacerbation of longstanding digital divide concerns (Van Dijk, 2006). This becomes even more of an issue when personal health is at stake, such as when technologies enable remote work or give the possibility to shop for groceries online but only for the haves rather than the have nots. More generally, as we articulate here, the ambivalent roles of technology during the pandemic lay out the potential for digital scars. This is concerning.

It might be acceptable that, in emergency situations, some technologies are being rolled out to try to maximise benefits for society (utilitarian approach) at the expense of the focus on potential negative consequences for each individual (deontological approach) (Freeman, 1994; Vallentyne, 1987). However, after an emergency, technologies rolled out quickly should undergo systematic vetting, to ensure a balance between a society-centric and an individualcentric approach – especially given that a utilitarian approach during the COVID pandemic is already substantially penalising disadvantaged populations. We are sceptical that this vetting will systematically happen for all technologies that have been rolled out during the COVID pandemic, potentially leading to digital scars.

The IS community is well positioned to study and prevent digital scars. The vision of systems, technologies and underlying processes and actors granted by our sociotechnical approach enables us to theorise and advise practitioners on risky impacts of IT. That the understanding of the benefits of technology also requires an awareness of potential harms is something that, as a community, we are already trying to promote through our own conferences and workshops (cf. the leading theme of the forthcoming ECIS 2021 http:// ecis2021.ma/tracks-description). Here, we challenge our community to take a substantially critical stance in examining the ubiquitous role of IT in our society. An important opportunity to do so relates to some of the technologies being used during the COVID pandemic.

Our focus here is on three main technologies laying under the umbrella of ubiquitous computing: social software, AI/ML (artificial intelligence/machine learning) applications to healthcare and robotics. We discuss positive and negative aspects of their use during the pandemic and identify underlying digital scars. We therefore view the COVID pandemic as an opportunity to reflect on various aspects of sociotechnical systems so that questionable uses of IT rolled out in this dificult period do not endure as digital scars after the pandemic is over.

## 2. THE DOUBLE-EDGED SWORD OF DIGITALTECHNOLOGIES IN THE COVID ERA

During the COVID pandemic, social software (social media and various collaborative artefacts) has supported remote communication, e.g., for work and leisure during lockdown periods. In addition, advanced algorithmic decision-making systems (e.g.,

AI/ML) applied to healthcare have largely contributed to address COVID issues by improving diagnosis capacity, modelling spreads and forecasting potential outbreaks (McCall, 2020; Van der Schaar & Alaa, 2020). Robotics too – the artefacts and their use in practice – is an area that has witnessed substantial expansion lately, because this type of automation allows people to keep physical distance (Zeng et al., 2020).

Social software, AI/ML applications to healthcare and robotics do not aim to be comprehensive of all the technologies that have had a key role during the pandemic. They however represent a large array of ubiquitous IT that has become intensely used. It is therefore helpful, for analytical purposes, to identify specific technologies for examining the positive and negative aspects of their use in practice, as we do next.

## 2.1. Social software

We refer to social software as the digital technologies enabling online communication, the creation of communities and the generation and exchange of various multimedia contents (Leonardi & Vaast, 2017; Von Krogh, 2012). With social software, people and organisations can interact with each other through various digital artefacts and infrastructures. Social software has played a relevant role in the pandemic. Here we analyse three particular types of social software: social media, tools for videoconferencing such as Zoom and Go-to-meeting (for online education, remote work and telehealth) and digital contact tracing.<sup>1</sup>

## 2.2. Social media

Probably one of the most evident and largely positive efects of the widespread difusion of IT in our society, which has improved the lives of individuals worldwide during the first wave of the pandemic, was the possibility to connect with each other through social media. Research on people’s struggles when dealing with more or less mandatory isolation has revealed interesting dynamics. First, since the start of the pandemic there has been a substantial increase in the use of traditional social media such as Facebook, Instagram and Twitter (Koeze & Popper, 2020). Second, we have witnessed the emergence of new social software (COVID-related apps, such as digital contact tracing, discussed later) and the increased use of some apps that were not so popular until a few months ago, such as TikTok (Levine, 2020).

Positive aspects of social media associated with decreased feeling of loneliness and seeking health information (cf. Xu et al., 2020) need however to be balanced with potential issues such as people spending too much time online. Overuse of social media, especially during the pandemic, is associated with an increased risk of depression (Ellis et al., 2020). People may become overly attached to these platforms and alienated from in-person social relationships as extensively documented in existing scholarship (e.g., Balakrishnan & Grifiths, 2017; Osatuyi & Turel, 2018; Slonje & Smith, 2008). Applied to the context of COVID, the efects of intense use of social media initially intended for dealing with long hours at home during lockdown have contributed to increased addictive behaviours (Koeze & Popper, 2020). What is more, intense social media use during the COVID era and the associated anxiety of people looking for online solutions to contingent health problems, created the perfect storm for augmenting spread of fake news, conspiracy theories and, more generally, misinformation (Laato et al., 2020; Mitchell et al., 2020).

## 2.3. Videoconferencing tools

Besides connecting families and friends (as per social media described above), real-time videoconferencing systems such as Zoom, Go-To-Meeting and others have become extremely popular to professionally connect people with their schools, companies and doctors during lockdown periods. All this has come with positive and negative consequences (Hacker et al., 2020).

K12 education: According to the United Nations Educational Division, during the first wave of the pandemic, up to 138 countries worldwide shot down their schooling system, afecting roughly 80% of children worldwide.<sup>2</sup> However, many children could not keep up with this change because of their own inability to connect remotely, mostly because of lack of computer literacy, access to usable devices or broadband Internet (Van Lancker & Parolin, 2020). For instance, a recent study reports that in 2019, over 162 million Americans (roughly half the country’s population) were not able to use Internet at broadband speed, because it was either unavailable (mainly in rural areas) or unafordable (Microsoft, 2019).

Because digital technologies have been essential for people to survive in the pandemic (Volges et al., 2020), students without broadband, laptops or with parents unable to set up connections are marginalised. People at the margins often live in communities where these resources are not available, because the communities themselves are in disadvantaged conditions (e.g., the schooling system cannot provide students with laptops). These communities are often particularly populated by minorities; the unequal availability of IT resources hits already struggling populations and geographical areas; racial discriminations are proxied by economic disparity.

Another problematic efect of using technologies for remote learning concerns the fact that educational activities are often largely left to the parents. Here there are two key issues. First, not all parents are able to help their children attend school remotely. This further penalises poorly educated families, once again often including minorities or economically disadvantaged populations. Second, helping children (or partially homeschooling them) tends to fall on women’s shoulders, with the potential to exacerbate pre-existing gender disparities in terms of job position and salaries – the woman becoming too busy to work online or even facing the choice of either homeschooling her children or performing salaried work (Malisch et al., 2020). For instance, in academia there has been already a decline in women’s research productivity as measured in terms of submission in the first four months of 2020 (Amano-Patiño et al., 2020).

College education: The availability of broadband and supporting devices also afects college students. For instance, according to a report from the National Student Clearinghouse Research Centre (NSCRC), in 2020 there has been a general drop in enrolment in the US (around 1.8%), but community college enrolment dropped 8% (NSCRC, 2020). This, once again, provides evidence of IT-based discriminating factors associated with COVID; students having socioeconomical challenges (often from minorities) are more likely to attend community colleges where tuition is more afordable. The issue of lack of broadband and devices has afected prevalently students in rural areas (either commuter students or campus residents) penalising parts of society that already experience challenging situations.

Remote work: From March to June 2020, most knowledge workers could perform their job from home although, according to a recent Harvard Business Review study, their readiness for doing so was unclear (Chakravorti & Chatuverdi, 2020). Even if adjusting to remote work required redesigning organisational processes, most highly paid jobs could be performed online. Other, less knowledge-based, jobs, however, required additional adjustments and training. Their transition was thus neither smooth nor without organisational costs (Brynjolfsson et al., 2020).

The possibility to work remotely increases with more stable and permanent jobs, which also tend to be better compensated than jobs performed on site. Lower-paid jobs such as loading/unloading trucks and cargos, delivery jobs, public transportation jobs, stores/retailer jobs etc. typically require onsite physical presence. Remote work limits potential exposure to COVID, but this option it is not available to everyone. People who have fewer resources to deal with the virus are often the most exposed to it.

Telehealth: It is an option that limits access to hospitals and carries benefits for both patients (lower risk of exposure to COVID) and clinicians/hospitals (doctors have fewer contacts with patients and hospitals achieve eficiencies when handling virtual inpatient processes). Yet, telehealth is available solely to those who can aford it – people with enough knowledge to download and instal videoconferencing tools and, once again, people who have access to computers and broadband (Ramsetty & Adams, 2020). Unfortunately, along with economically disadvantaged people, most people who struggle with telehealth are the elderly, the category most at risk of sufering complications and dying of COVID (Onder et al., 2020).

A recent PEW study revealed that, in the US, only half of the people over 65 had broadband at home (Anderson & Perrin, 2017). A JAMA study published in August 2020 reported that 41% of people aged over 65 in the US do not have the ability to connect remotely with their doctors because of the lack of either broadband or computers/smartphones (Roberts & Mehrotra, 2020). In addition, people without COVIDrelated issues and unable to access telehealth may need to head to hospitals for other medical reasons, with the associated risks of contracting the virus. In other cases, they do not go to the hospital at all, even when they should, which is a source of increased health issues for causes not related to COVID (Wong et al., 2020).

## 2.4. Digital contact tracing

Digital contact tracing is a technique aimed at determining proximity between an infected person and others through mobile devices (Ferretti et al., 2020). “Contacts” occur via Bluetooth or GPS systems and with data stored either on the users’ phone or in a centralised fashion – e.g., in a cloud managed by the system’s owners (a company or government). Recent research has documented a successful case of digital contact tracing in Kuikuru, an indigenous territory in Brazil (Dias, 2020). While a key issue of digital contact tracing concerns adoption, its outcomes are potentially meaningful only if privacy issues are addressed properly and users trust the way the data is handled (Rowe et al., 2020; Trang et al., 2020).

In addition, for a community to benefit from this technology, most people need to be equipped with mobile devices (Riemer et al., 2020). This applies to only 80% of the US population, 65% of Russians and 45% of Brazilians, just to give three examples. Once again, in underdeveloped areas of these countries, digital contact tracing cannot work, even if adopted by all smartphone users.

## 2.5. AI/ML applications to healthcare

The healthcare domain – drained by the overwhelming demand of patients in need of urgent care – has benefitted substantially from mainstream as well as cutting-edge technologies. Here we focus on AI/ML applications in two relevant healthcare areas:

algorithmic decision-making processes associated with triaging patients and performing diagnoses; and AI/ML’s ability to generate predictive models of COVID spreads.

## 2.6. Algorithmic decision-making for COVID patients

An emerging healthcare area that has become prominent during the COVID era is that of algorithmic decision-making through AI/ML systems (Wynants et al., 2020). These systems triage COVID patients through the examination of chest X-ray scans, for instance, (Feng et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2020). However, the efectiveness of these systems directly relies on the data they are fed with. If the training data includes only some populations, the algorithms will be good at triaging just some people (Obermeyer et al., 2019). This stems from a longstanding problem; people of colour and indigenous communities are generally underrepresented in medical trials (George et al., 2014). This is one of the various reasons why in the US African Americans have been hit disproportionally by COVID in terms of infections and mortality (Shah et al., 2020). Worldwide, data referring to minorities disproportionally hit by the virus and associated deaths for similar reasons follow suit (Williamson et al., 2020).

More recently, and because of the high lab costs of testing patients for COVID, a strategy called pooling was implemented where specimens from several patients are tested together when all people involved are at low risk of being COVID positive. If that one collective test is negative, all patients are cleared at a minimum cost. If the test is positive, individual tests are required, with the already collected specimens. ML can optimise these tests by making predictions with respect to population sampling (Augenblick et al., 2002). This too appears to be a cost-efective solution while addressing medical concerns. However, populations more at risk to be infected (for instance, essential workers) are generally not included in automatic polling strategies, making these tests less available or afordable for those who most need them.

## 2.7. Predictive systems to generate spread trends

AI systems have also been used to predict COVID spreads and spikes worldwide (McCall, 2020; Van der Schaar & Alaa, 2020). The results of these models have not always been perfect, mostly because spread predictions of such a vast scale do not have historical data to rely upon (COVID is one-in-a-century event). Nevertheless, in several cases these models helped policymakers set countrywide plans to react to the pandemic (Eker, 2020). Here, too, incomplete data may generate issues (Pietz et al., 2020). Available data can for instance, cover some geographical areas at the expense of others and many models are based on hospitalisations.

Yet, for example, in the US African American patients are hospitalised less often than Caucasians because of historically disproportionate access to health services and insurance (McClelland et al., 2017). This potentially compromises models related to hospitalisation and makes them more fallible in areas populated by minorities, where infections might be high, yet not acknowledged by algorithms that interpret skewed data. That health technologies do not work well for minorities is a longstanding issue, which stems from the fact that these populations have long had less access to care. Even oximeters (over the counter devices that applied to a finger provide blood oxygen reading and that have become extremely popular in the COVID era) are designed for light skins and do not perform well for people of colour (Moran-Thomas, 2020).

## 2.8. Robotics

The COVID era sped up the already growing reliance on automation, prominently for increasing safety in a number of settings, from hospitals to retail businesses. Robots have taken over cleaning tasks in hospitals treating COVID patients, to limit human exposure to the virus. They can also provide infected patients with basic services such as bringing medical equipment in and out of the hospital’s rooms (Khan et al., 2020). With regards to retailers, the robotic industry has grown substantially since the start of the pandemic for similar reasons; to minimise human contact (Coombs, 2020). For instance, in South Korean restaurants, robots serve customers and enforce physical distancing among guests (Jang & Lee, 2020).

Yet, the “vision of robot-enabled, touchless work is a mirage” (Beane & Brynjolfsson, 2020). One of the main issues is the lack of comprehensive training data to improve a robot’s efectiveness and responsiveness (Gupta et al., 2018). The COVID pandemic has nevertheless led to a substantial commitment from companies to increase automation in various settings (Corkery & Gelles, 2020). For instance, in August 2020, Amazon and FedEx, which have developed robots for a while, took important steps to obtain governments’ authorisation to operate autonomous vehicles to support their businesses (Marshall, 2020).

Automation has become part of the efort to pursue physical distancing, both in healthcare settings and in society more generally (e.g., retail stores). This has caused a natural shift of jobs from doing to controlling and supervising, one example being the boost to massive introduction of automatic checkout systems at most supermarket chains in the US (Shekel, 2020).

However, this shift has also created two issues: a capacity issue, where one (former) cashier can supervise multiple self-checkout stations (so supermarkets need fewer (former) cashiers); and a competence issue, which reveals a potential gap between one’s ability to execute mechanical tasks such as scanning items and charging customers to that of troubleshooting when customers have issues with automatic checkout stations. The obvious consequence is that less skilled people risk being left unemployed.

What is more, in developed economies automation is generally not evenly distributed. A recent Brookings report highlights how, in the US, robots-based automation prevails in urban and coastal areas, leaving most of the inland behind (Muro et al., 2019). In these rural areas, automated checkout systems in supermarkets are not commonly adopted. Cashiers thus need to remain in constant contact with customers instead of supervising self-checkout machines, which would limit their infection risks. Thus, while automation overall can prevent people from being in contact with each other, it has not been homogeneously adopted, putting people more at risk to contract COVID in areas where automation is limited.

Here, concerns do not arise only with respect to competences and skills; poorly educated workers may not be able to turn from cashiers to supervisors of selfcheckouts. People with physical disabilities could stand or sit behind a cashier. However, walking through a store to supervise self-checkout systems whenever customers have issues can be close to impossible for these individuals. This creates another discriminatory problem associated with automation. While this issue of the future of work is not strictly related to COVID, the current pandemic and the push to replace humans with systems for health/safety reasons may generate long-term issues associated with the shift to automation/supervision.

## 3. DIGITAL SCARS: WHAT CAN THE IS COMMUNITY DO TO PREVENT THEM?

Digital scars represent the perpetuation of problematic uses of some technologies rolled out quickly for emergency reasons. The healthcare field is well aware that, because of the COVID emergency, there has been a temporary shift from a patient-focused (deontological) to a society-focused (utilitarian) approach, for instance, because of scarce resources (Michalsen et al., 2020) – and this also concerns IT used in healthcare. However, a broader and long-term examination of technologies being used during the COVID pandemic is currently lacking. This is a source of great concern.

Problematic uses of technologies may well remain the norm once the pandemic is over. This can potentially advantage powerful groups that can obtain financial and political benefits from perpetuating the use of IT with questionable efects for society. These actors are highly varied and may include, e.g., governments, social media companies but also organisations aiming to pursue short-term eficiency goals (working from home has turned to be cost-saving for many organisations); online education providers, healthcare organisations aiming at pursuing eficiency (perhaps at the expense of some patients); and retailers that benefit as well from eficiencies based on automation.

We do not believe that all these actors will purposely push for prolonged use of questionable IT once the pandemic is over. Yet, most if not all IT developed and rolled out during the COVID pandemic have fast become interwoven with our everyday life. Therefore, we need to make sure that emergency-based rollouts of IT during the pandemic are subsequently vetted.

We as a community stemming from various backgrounds (computer science, management, sociology, law etc.) are called to bring together technical and behavioural insights into the sociotechnical perspective that characterises our field (Ciborra, 2000; Land, 1976; Mumford, 2006). We need to develop a more profound understanding of how these technologies unleashed to society in emergency situations interact with society and whether they will generate digital scars. The IS community has solidly moved from a discipline that borrows from other reference disciplines to one that stands on its own (Baskerville & Myers, 2002). Our scholarship can therefore efectively make substantial theoretical and practical impacts to the study of the perpetuation of questionable uses of technologies during the COVID pandemic. We next examine potential digital scars of novel or more intense use of social software, AI/ML applications in healthcare and robotics.

## 3.1. Social software

## 3.1.1. Social media

Long lasting consequences of the heightened reliance on social media may lead to magnifying issues associated with Internet addiction as well as negative side efects such as cyberbullying, the proliferation of fake news etc. Recent COVID-related literature indicates that this type of emerging hyper-use of social media builds upon, yet difers from what we have seen so far with respect to social media addiction and associated consequences. Mental health issues associated with self-isolation are more acute because of lockdowns (Galea et al., 2020) and may lead to a number of longterm social concerns (Ågerfalk et al., 2020).

We still need to understand how to lower the rising curve of social software use to healthy and balanced levels after the pandemic. There is an open debate, for instance, on whether algorithms employed by Facebook and Google to increase user confirmation biases are ethical, because they lead to polarisation of opinions, radicalisation and fake news (Seneca, 2020). Scholars here are already trying to do their part; for instance, Facebook’s external oversight board (https://www.oversightboard.com) is formed mostly by academics and aims to help the company make dificult ethical decisions with respect to speech an online safety. Yet, this is an external board and Facebook does not have to mandatory follow its recommendations. Clearly these eforts need to be taken further to make sure that ethical principles are followed and prevail, when other economic interests are at stake. Governments too need to engage in this conversation and generate laws that protect citizens, while promoting companies’ value creation.

It is worth nothing that to this end initiatives on how to limit inappropriate uses of social media platforms (e.g., fake news) are underway. On the one hand, Facebook and others (not always successfully though) are making huge investments in trying to detect automatically and address misuses of their platform, for instance, using AI/ML (Hao, 2020). On the other hand, the US Congress is discussing whether to revise Section 230, a 1996 Internet legislation that gives immunity to website publishers from thirdparty content. Yet, changing or repelling Section 230 is not free from negative consequences, as it will potentially limit free speech online (Chavern, 2020). It is for instance, important that academics actively work to mediate this dialog between large Internet companies and government bodies to reach agreements that are fair for both businesses and society. We urge the IS community to engage in these debates by addressing the following questions:

(1) What are efective ways to reduce social media addictions that were exacerbated by lockdowns due to COVID, while retaining the positive aspects of sharing habits to relieve social isolation?

(2) How can academics and companies increase collaboration and mutual engagement to limit the improper uses of social media platforms that has been exacerbated during the COVID pandemic?

## 3.1.2. Videoconferencing tools

The potential digital scars arising from practices associated with the use of videoconferencing tools are immense. Schooling (K-12) will likely resume as traditional face to face teaching, once the pandemic is over. However, we wonder whether superintendents and school boards will think of the possibility to capitalise on the vast investments made in online K-12 education (teacher training, devices and infrastructures). For instance, routine school interruptions (snow days, voting days etc.) but also other periods of the year (very cold or hot days) that generally used to be regular class days could be replaced by online sessions. This poses two distinct issues.

First, some children (generally already socioeconomically disadvantaged) will struggle from missing school days because of their lack of technology/ resources – days where other children can attend online. Second, in case regular school days are moved online, from time to time, the same children will also miss important school support such as the daily meal. For instance, over 11 million children in the US rely on school lunch as the only meal of the day while a total of 22 million children rely, even partially, on free or reduced-price lunch at school.<sup>3</sup>

Depriving these children from this school service even a few days per school year might become extremely problematic. While the problem of poor children’s daily meal cannot be solved using IT, the fact that pervasive IT is now a protagonist in K-12 environments calls for IS scholars to reflect also on these side problems that have broad societal impact. Ideally, central governments and local administrations should work jointly with schools to identify “workarounds” and compromises that will take advantage of the progress made in remote education while ensuring that particular populations will not be penalised. We thus ask:

(1) Which strategies can help capitalise on pandemic-related IT investment and training on online teaching without leaving behind disadvantaged students?

In relation to higher education, since the 2010s most institutions have ofered online or hybrid courses, especially at the graduate level (Cook & Grant-Davis, 2020). Yet, the face-to-face college experience, for learning and the development of social skills, is still considered the standard (Castle & McGuire, 2010; Xu & Xu, 2019). The uneven availability of broadband and devices risks to generate negative efects. Remote learning represents an opportunity as well as a challenge for students and instructors. While research shows that higher education can be partially delivered online in an efective manner (Keengwe & Kidd, 2010), an enduring fully online system is neither easy to develop, nor does it seem sustainable.

The IS community can contribute with research stemming from online communication and collaboration, along with our own pedagogical resources (cf. https://aisnet.org/page/Education and the recent CAIS special issue on Learning, Pedagogy and Educational Systems in the COVID era, https://aisel.aisnet.org/ cais/covid\_specialsection.pdf). IS scholars could focus on how technical disciplines such as computer programming as well as typical MIS (Management

Information Systems) topics that relate to business can be taught online efectively. For instance, technical skills generally need collective class exercises and we are concerned that the current Zoom capabilities are suficient to do so. Similar concerns apply to managerial topics, which (especially at the graduate level) are taught using teaching cases and lively class discussions.

Another aspect of online teaching and learning that is worth examining has to do with emerging discussions on how, or, even, whether, ethics is efectively taught in IT-related classes, for instance, to future data scientists who engage in analytics programe (but also to perspective IT managers) (Markus et al., 2019). The lack of strong ethical components in IT-related classes is already a problem in face-to-face classes, and partly explains the incapacity of current leaders to uphold their companies to high ethical standards (Marabelli & Markus, 2017). Students need to be exposed to highly interactive classes where managerial problems involving ethics are openly discussed with real word cases, and among peers rather than only with the instructor (Gentile, 2010).

In addition, ethics is a discipline dificult to teach per se even in person, because it does not relate to formulas and processes with a unique solution. Teaching it online, therefore, poses additional challenges to the current education system. The IS community is certainly well equipped to address the following research questions:

(1) What will be the gaps between current and future business and IT professionals, if online higher education sticks for years?

(2) How can challenging topics such as IT ethics be taught online? What are potential IT solutions that might fill the “interactions gap,” that is needed for managers to learn how to translate ethical principles into practice?

In relation to remote work, the COVID era has led to important changes that might endure, which concern the way we work and collaborate online and bring to the surface the even more prominent role that IT will have in the post pandemic era (Carillo et al., 2020). We should reflect on the potential loss of informal organisational processes, given that informality and social networks promote knowledge sharing, creativity and innovation (Labianca & Brass, 2006; Tsai, 2001). The current trends suggest that companies have come to the realisation that their workforce can be efectively employed online, and that people can be easily monitored when everything is quantifiable and recorded because interactions happen online (Pan & Zhang, 2020).

Yet, this, in our opinion, accounts for short-term eficiency and overlooks potential long-term pitfalls such as the lack of innovative power of firms. Creative internal processes often emerging in a bottom-up fashion via informal interactions (these happening in ofice aisles, by the cofee/vending machines etc.) and leading to the generation of novel ideas may not survive virtually, in the long term. Even informal encounters with superiors and colleagues from other business units cannot happen casually online, because now every time we want to talk with someone, we need to schedule a (formal) Zoom meeting. The IS literature has long studied virtual teams’ ability to innovate, with mixed results (Dissanayake et al., 2015; Malhotra et al., 2001; Van der Meulen et al., 2019). IS scholars should capitalise on this prior knowledge to try to address current, more acute, problems associated with online innovation in the (post) COVID era.

An additional issue is about employee control when they work remotely, which can easily lead to surveillance (Rosenblat & Kneese, 2014; Zubof, 2019). The availability of digital traces will abound. Supervisors can now accurately check out meeting attendance and interactions that always happen “formally” and are often recorded. This underpins an additional trend negatively afecting innovation, because monitoring is detrimental to well-being and is demotivating (Kellogg et al., 2020). IS scholars should attempt to address the following questions:

(1) What might be the organisational consequences (e.g., mechanisms, reporting systems, level of formalisations of tasks and processes) of increased online workplaces because of COVID, where informality decreases? How far will this afect innovation and creativity?

(2) How can information systems and associated online interactions aford the sharing of ideas in safe environments when meetings can be now systematically recorded and scrutinized?

(3) What can be done to avoid heavily employment of online meetings becoming detrimental to wellbeing in the workplace?

In relation to healthcare, some view the crisis generated by COVID as an opportunity to accelerate the development of innovative processes such as telehealth (Serper et al., 2020). This shift holds benefits associated with eficiency. However, the negative consequences of telehealth after the pandemic will be not just limited to these typical of the digital divide where citizens lacking broadband and devices cannot access these services. Beyond accessibility issues, many privacy- and security-related procedures have been relaxed due to the emergency, posing potential patient-doctor confidentiality issues (Turner & Yaraghi, 2020).

On the one hand, promoting telehealth in the postpandemic era is important to capitalise on the various investments made for training doctors to practicing online. One the other hand, the general public might refrain from accessing telehealth services once the pandemic is over, because either they might prefer to “see” a doctor, or because they are fearful of the myriad of privacy issues surrounding telehealth (Hall & McGraw, 2014). Therefore, telehealth system developers should build safer and more comprehensive platforms that, e.g., allow patients to submit documents, photos, logbooks of blood pressure and glucose readings etc. in a secure fashion. Some EMR (electronic medical record) systems already allow a certain degree of doctor-patient online interactions, MyChart from $\mathrm { E P I C } ^ { 4 }$ being an example. However, to make telehealth a true substitute for many in-person visits, system integration needs to be taken further, allowing for platforms supporting videocalls, availability of medical records and file exchange capabilities using state of the art security protocols.

What is more, the consequences associated with the virtualisation of healthcare during the pandemic has hit health centres heterogeneously. For instance, in the US, Community Health Centres – that serve more than 28 million low-income and often uninsured patients in rural and underserved urban areas – have been heavily penalised by temporary layofs of staf and nurses, as a consequence of the growth of telehealth. This trend has two important long-term consequences. The first has to do with unemployment, as customer-facing personnel (nurses for instance) might be laid of for good (Kim et al., 2020). The second is even more worrying in the long-term, and afects sick patients who need to see a doctor in person but cannot aford a private structure that keeps in-hospital personnel properly stafed. We therefore suggest a focus on the following research questions:

(1) What are privacy and information security implications associated with continuing to make telehealth widely available under poorly regulated regimes?

(2) What are potential sociotechnical solutions and integrated systems that can make telehealth visits more interactive and comprehensive?

(3) How can healthcare facilities balance costefective strategies, tied to continued delivery of health services virtually, with issues related to potential shortening of stafing, especially in public healthcare systems?

## 3.1.3. Digital contact tracing

Digital contact tracing technologies currently pose concerns related to the balance between security, health and privacy (Trang et al., 2020). Associated digital scars may relate to governments and institutions keeping long-term unlimited access to citizens’ whereabouts, if used to monitor individuals for surveillance purposes (Rowe et al., 2020). For instance, in Pakistan the government has already turned its ISI (Inter-Service Intelligence, the equivalent of the US CIA) capability into contact tracing of various types, depriving citizens from their privacy rights (Siddiqui, 2020).

It is unclear whether digital contact tracing systems will be dismissed when the pandemic is over or will be rather turned into surveillance systems, useful for governments but at the expense of citizens’ privacy because, overall, the end justifies the means (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Aroles, 2020). One such example of endured control of emergency actions is the US Patriots Act, a set of temporary measures established by the Bush administration soon after 9/11 allowing federal and local law enforcement to surveil and control people in ways that might however compromise one’s privacy and/or basic rights. Almost two decades after its approval, and 15 years after its expected dismissal, several surveillance systems are still in place.

More research is needed to mobilise people towards a collective understanding of the key role that democracy plays in protecting citizens from mass surveillance, in the online workplace as we pointed out before while discussing remote work, and at a larger scale when a smartphone’s app can report to governments the minutiae of people’s lives. Being surveilled in emergency situations might be seen as a necessary measure, temporarily shifting the balance of privacy, security and health. Yet, it is relevant to address longterm consequences of monitoring citizens’ whereabouts (Newell & Marabelli, 2015). This is especially true nowadays, given the new developments arising from technologies becoming popular during the pandemic, such as digital contact tracing apps.

Government-based security initiatives in the hand of local police could substantially benefit from the difusion of contact tracing apps, for instance, to locate missing people or monitor crimes without the need of a warrant against the phone company, which might take time. Yet, the development of trust and increased transparency is needed with respect to how institutions handle the data (Riemer et al., 2020) and with respect to whether the use of these data beyond the purposes they were collected for is allowed (Toots, 2019). Otherwise, the lack of confidence in the institutions that manage user data will prevent their difusion.

One last consideration concerns surveillance issues associated with digital contact tracing being countryspecific (Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020); while GDPR<sup>5</sup> may reasonably protect EU citizens, this may not happen in other parts of the world. Potential questions focusing on the long-term consequences of using social software in the way it has been used during the pandemic are:

(1) What will be the consequences of the proliferation of digital traces available to governments and institutions and leading to control and surveillance, originating from digital contact tracing apps?

(2) How can sociotechnical solutions stemming from digital contact tracing enable the eficient use of various government-managed and locally operated apps, while minimising privacy risks for citizens and increasing security?

## 4. AI/ML in healthcare

## 4.1. Algorithmic decision-making for COVID patients

Algorithmic decision-making in healthcare may have negative impacts afecting mostly minorities and disadvantaged people, as noted supra. Patient data from clinical trials often come from Caucasian subjects (George et al., 2014). AI/ML systems, therefore, provide poor outcomes for other populations (Obermeyer et al., 2019). Before the pandemic, healthcare AI/ML was already a booming area of research but privacy concerns related to collecting data to train systems and algorithmic biases represented significant obstacles to their broad implementation. Now, COVID has sped up these rollouts for emergency reasons.

However, if these systems remain used intensively, e.g., to triage ER (emergency room) patients, we need to expand the current debate on ethical issues associated with AI/ML applications in healthcare (e.g., Sun & Medaglia, 2019; Yu & Kohane, 2019). Recent research has attempted to generate frameworks addressing ethical design of AI systems aimed at minimising biases (d’Aquin et al., 2018; Martin, 2019). The IS community has extensively contributed to design science (cf. Rai, 2017) and thus should join this conversation.

Along with attempting to develop more “ethical” algorithms, processes should be designed that enable people in decision-making positions to vet these systems, to make sure that potential unfair outcomes are mitigated. Vetting and repeatedly checking these systems would also allow going back to the design point at any time and adjusting the underlying algorithms. Organisations should establish rigorous procedures to make the AI/ML development lifecycle a circular one, where unintended behaviours of the systems or emergent problematic applications to “real settings” are addressed with redesign. It is therefore important to enforce processes that keep (or reinstate) humans in the loop, even if it may result in less eficient processes, because of human supervision. To this end, we urge IS scholars to expand on recent disciplinary conversations on this topic (cf. Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020) to address future AI/ML challenges in healthcare (and beyond).

The current pandemic and the rush to deploy cutting-edge technologies (e.g., ML) in delicate settings such as healthcare should not happen at the expense of the unfair treatment of patients. Instead, the COVID era to this end should be seen as an opportunity to improve the design and development of such systems in a more ethical fashion, pursuing eficiency, yet minimising potential side efects. Important related questions are:

(1) Given the use of AI/ML for triaging and diagnosing COVID patients, what are novel processes that institutions and organisations can implement to allow repeated vetting of algorithmic systems?

(2) How to deal with eficiency-based challenges associated with the (re)introduction of the human in the loop, in AI/ML decision-making processes, to face the increased use of these systems because of the pandemic?

## 4.2. Predictive systems to generate spread trends

Predictive models forecasting COVID spreads are not very efective in areas where IT has a weak presence, because they often rely on search engines and social media analysis as well as other data collected via ubiquitous computing (GPS-equipped devices etc.). The idea that the intense use of these modelling strategies will continue in the future (for instance, for predicting flu spread or, more generally, for assessing health issues at the country level) becomes therefore problematic; if predictive modelling in healthcare keeps being used broadly after the pandemic is over, people will be able to benefit from these models in unequal ways. This raises important questions around the possibility to make broadband available to populations living in rural areas through breakthrough technologies such as helium-filled balloons – a project currently pursued by Facebook and Google (Reynolds, 2020).

A related issue is that models generally rely heavily on data visualisation, especially if the data needs to be shown to the general public, where trends are better understood through charts rather than numbers (Donalek et al., 2014). To this end, in addition to these predictive systems still being imperfect (Pietz et al., 2020), concerns relate to potential data massaging, often associated with political interests and exploitation of information when its visualisation is exposed to citizens, for instance, by government websites, news channels etc. Knowledge is highly politica (Swan & Scarbrough, 2005) and in the era of big data it is easy to turn complex numbers into desired outcomes via visualisation. The COVID era has accelerated this process of adopting big-data -based models and has exposed them to the general public. If this trend continues after the pandemic is over, it might become problematic for the average citizen to understand the extent to which the information provided is real or manipulated for political purposes.

The IS community has an opportunity to join this important conversation and examine in detail the reliability of predictive modelling systems, who should develop them and how. In addition, IS scholars should carefully examine the efects of sociotechnical systems such as data sharing (in visual mode) by institutions and news channels and their ubiquitous efects to society – a phenomenon that has been magnified by the COVID era. Below are two questions that should be addressed:

(1) Given the increased use of predictive models on diseases spread, how can we improve their comprehensiveness to ensure the coverage of most areas and populations?

(2) As the COVID era has magnified the power of data visualisation, what are ways in which IS scholarship can limit its negative impacts to society, when the data is ad-hoc “massaged”?

## 4.3. Robotics

Temporary increased automation through robots that limit physical contact has been useful during the COVID era. However, the fact that robots also create eficiencies makes us think that some of these automations will remain in place in the long term. While the race for mass automation might be acceptable in times of emergency, the long-term perpetuation of this trend raises issues. Vulnerable populations such as task workers, unable to switch from doing to controlling/ supervising are particularly penalised by automation. The increasing introduction of robots in our everyday life, at home, at the workplace and in our social life holds positive efects (Willcocks, 2020). A key problem, however, is about the pace of these changes, as shifting too rapidly from task to knowledge work in some industries might create disruptive unemployment of populations that already experience issues with finding and keeping a job.

Robots as sociotechnical systems have been examined by IS scholars and beyond (e.g., Anderson, 2007; Garmann-Johnsen et al., 2014). IS scholars are well positioned to study their deployment in society. The post pandemic era will represent a delicate phase where scholars as well as practitioners need to ensure that the transition to automation is socially sustainable. We therefore suggest that the following questions be addressed:

(1) How can robots and the underlying automations be introduced in society progressively rather than disruptively (as it happened during

COVID) so that the shift from “doing” to “supervising” does not penalise disadvantaged individuals?

(2) What are possible opportunities and incentives for companies that have already automated operations (e.g., retailers, supply chain), that would make them slow down the shift?

## 5. Overarching challenges, opportunities and questions arising from the COVID Era

Along with the above issues, we believe that there is room for a debate at large and more general questions related to the potential misuse of technologies and innovation in the post-pandemic era. One relevant overarching theme, which involves digital scars associated with social software, AI/ML applications and robotics, is about problematic data management in relation to various COVID aspects.

In the short-term, better data management would help address some contingent problems. Just to give two examples, in the US, states currently (November 2020) collect COVID data (positives, infections, hospital beds, deaths) with diferent criteria making it impossible to do comparisons and assessments at the national level. In addition, some states (e.g., New York) make their data available on a daily basis (cf. https://github.com/nychealth/coronavirusdata) while others do so less frequently (Patel, 2020). Across the ocean, the UK system has so far collected data about testing and infections partially manually (paper and pen). Statistics have been inaccurate, which has made it dificult to assess the real status of the progress of the disease in the country (Conway, 2020).

In the long-term, the poor management of COVID data might lead to scars (because of the incapacity to handle future pandemics because of the inability to turn a crisis into lessons learned) but also to opportunities; the exposure of weak COVID-related data management systems might bring to the surface the need to accelerate consolidation processes of health data more generally. For instance, initiatives such as the US National Centre for Data Health<sup>6</sup> and the National COVID Cohort Collaborative Data Enclave<sup>7</sup> have attempted to build a US national centralised database. Its aim is to go beyond addressing current health issues (COVID) and promote comprehensive and data-driven research for addressing future health threats of various kind (Westman, 2020).

Improving data management at the national level might also help addressing AI/ML-related longstanding issues associated with medical trials for non COVID-related diseases as well as supporting better predictive modelling related to the spread of infections (Pietz et al., 2020). It might also help deal with data training issues and improve AI capabilities to identify problematic social media content. For instance, so far AI’s ability to identify, tag as inappropriate or even remove posts from platforms on conspiracy theories, misinformation, fake news and hate speech has been weak (Kirchner & Reuter, 2020; Marcus & Davis, 2018). Improving data management will also help expanding robotics’ ability to learn from high quality and comprehensive training data (Gupta et al., 2018).

Another data management issue has to do with information control on social media and beyond, lead ing to censorship of news by institutions. For instance, in June 2020 Brazil’s president Jair Bolsonaro was accused of controlling health reports and of withholding infection data for political purposes (Londoño, 2020). The human and digital rights watchdog Freedom House,<sup>8</sup> in its 2020 annual report, noted that in at least 45 countries activists, journalists and other members of the public were arrested or charged with criminal ofences associated with social media posts and newspaper articles related to COVID. In addition, in at least 20 countries, governments used the pandemic emergency to impose overly broad speech restrictions. Finally, governments in at least 28 countries censored websites, forums and social media posts reporting unfavourable health statistics, corruption allegations and other COVID-related content (Newman, 2020). It is important to address data consistency and management issues and the underlying political dynamics preventing data sharing.

Academics should advise and/or work jointly with governments and institutions to help them develop transparent, high-level frameworks that assess the status quo of technologies rolled out for emergency reasons (from deregulated telehealth systems to AI for decision making and robots to replace humans) and with only a partial understanding of their side efects (Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020). This can be done by promoting engaged scholarship (Van de Ven, 2018) – ways for academics to partner with practitioners – to identify strategies to address digital scars. One starting point could be for academics engaged in IT related disciplines to synthesise the vast number of codes of ethics – more than 90, as Jobin et al. (2019) report – and ofer to companies a selected number of principles (i.e., a framework) that relate specifically to managing IT-related post-emergency situations.

Moving to more practical actions such as actively working with companies to solve ethical problems, institutions and large companies could release resources in the form of grants promoting collaborations between academics and industry partners. For instance, such initiatives in Europe have been realised through Research Projects under Framework Programs.<sup>9</sup> The creation of a specific call for transdisciplinary research on the post-pandemic role of IT under the current program (Horizon 2020) seems therefore appropriate. In the US, the NSF (National Science Foundation) is home to a specific division (IIP, Industrial Innovation and Partnership<sup>10</sup>) that promotes collaborations with academia. Here, too, we call for scholars involved in the agency to push for the creation of ad-hoc funding opportunities to expand on digital scars.

To magnify the efectiveness of regional projects (e.g., EU/US), it is important that scholars promote knowledge exchange across continents related to lessons learned on how to control and mitigate unwanted efects of digital scars. For instance, one may argue that the EU will be initially more efective in handling post-pandemic consequences of digital contact tracing, because of GDPR. It is therefore important to avoid working in silos, even if these silos include large countries and federations. This will also unfold the potential to create new transdisciplinary fields of research (Boland et al., 2007; Bruns, 2013). In all of this, the role of academics should be that of advisors who are intellectually independent and are therefore not money or power-driven, as companies and governments may be (Van de Ven, 2018).

The frameworks resulting from collaborations via engaged scholarship however need to not simply focus on ethical problems associated with scars. They should also promote long-lasting efects of eficiencies, innovation, economic implications and ultimately progress – also because most companies that have unleashed innovative yet problematic technologies employ a large number of people.

We therefore call for IS scholars’ engagement in this conversation, within the scientific community but also with policy-makers and companies’ executives and their ethical boards. IS scholarship already provides some guidance (cf. Barrett & Oborn, 2018; Marabelli & Vaast, 2020). The following are among the several overarching questions to be addressed, related to digital scars:

(1) Given the opportunities to reflect on data management because of the pandemic, how can governments and institutions promote future healthcare data integration beyond COVID, while dealing with political influences?

(2) What should be the ethical criteria guiding decisions to keep vs. discard IT innovations that emerged during the pandemic?

(3) How can academics engage more with practitioners from all domains of social life to ensure a responsible use of IT after the pandemic?

## 6. CONCLUDING REMARKS

In this commentary we presented ambivalent aspects of using technologies during the COVID era. The IS community is well positioned to address many of the problems arising from unequal and unfair use of IT to assist populations afected by the pandemic. Having identified three main areas (social software, AI/ML applications to healthcare and robotics), we outlined outstanding issues that might lead to COVID digital scars. It can be painful to discuss IT applications as potential digital scars. Yet, we need to make sure that not only the benefits of innovation outweigh the drawbacks, but also that all these new systems or novel uses of existing systems are deployed and used responsibly.

One person being discriminated against because of automatic systems is one too many. One child deprived from basic education and left behind due to lack of access to technologies to attend class is one too many. While some of these issues related to the applications of IT to society are well-known, here we stand for avoiding the exacerbation of these issues as they turn into digita scars. We therefore outlined research questions that IS scholars should consider to advance theory and practice in our discipline, while contributing to resolve crucial societal problems such as that of the digital scars that might characterise the post COVID era. Examining these questions will also help address some current and future problematic uses of technologies that go beyond the COVID pandemic. Digital scars may appear anytime a technology is rolled out quickly, in an emergency context, with little considerations for long-term societal consequences. As IS scholars, it is within our role to work towards alleviating these digital scars.
