# Co-creating business value through IT-business operational alignment in inter-organisational relationships: empirical evidence from regional networks

Simon Trang <sup>a</sup>, Markus Mandrella<sup>b</sup>, Mauricio Marrone <sup>c</sup> and Lutz M. Kolbe<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Chair of Information Security and Compliance, University of Göttingen, Goettingen, Germany; <sup>b</sup>Chair of Information Management, University of Göttingen, Göttingen, Germany; <sup>c</sup>Department of Accounting and Corporate Governance, Macquarie University, Sydney, Australia

## ABSTRACT

IT alignment research has remained limited primarily to intra-organisational alignment, leaving much to learn about the value-creation potential of its largely neglected inter-organisational counterpart. This study addresses this gap by investigating the role of IT-business operational alignment in inter-organisational relationships (IOR). Drawing on IT-based value-co creation and IT alignment literature, we propose that IOR IT-business operational alignment is an immediate source for co-creating business value and can be established through a capabilitybuilding process. Using a sample of 241 regional network collaborations in Germany, we find that IOR IT-business operational alignment directly afects relationship performance. Moreover, we find that three IOR IT capabilities – IT infrastructure integration, information exchange capability, and IT-enabled coordination – enable the development of IOR IT-business operational alignment. This study contributes to IS research on value co-creation by integrating the concept of IT-business alignment into a nomological network with well-established IOR IT capabilities. We provide theoretical explanations and empirical evidence that IT infrastructure integration, information exchange capability, and IT-enabled coordination indirectly afect relationship performance over an operational alignment process. For practice, we highlight the importance of monitoring IT-business operational alignment in IOR and provide recommendations on how to expand existing alignment eforts.

## KEYWORDS

IT capabilities; interorganisational relationships; IT-business operational alignment; business value co-creation; networks; the business value of IT

## 1. Introduction

In highly competitive environments, organisations tend to focus on their core competencies, while close collaboration with multiple partners becomes essential for coordinating and combining resources. Accordingly, the focus of traditional supply chain management on the strategic choice between “make” or “buy” has been extended by the dimension of long-term collaboration in inter-organisational networks (Sydow & Duschek, 2011). Studies report that these forms of multi-partner alliances, in comparison to classical dyadic alliances, make up 30– 50% of all alliances (Heidl et al., 2014; Makino et al., 2007). Today, they are common practice in a variety of industries and functions, such as joint marketing activities in the aviation industry (Götsch & Albers, 2017) or in the area of research and development in the pharmaceutical industry (Xu & Cavusgil, 2019).

Inter-organisational networks are composed of three or more organisations that are largely autonomous, geographically distributed, and heterogeneous regarding their operating environments, cultures, and social capitals (Sydow & Duschek, 2011). In network collaborations, organisations profit not only from the flexibility of resource integration but also from complementary competences and the exchange of knowledge (Constantinides et al., 2018; Dyer & Singh, 1998). However, due to the dificulties of coevolving with network partners and creating value from inter-organisational relationships (IOR), cooperative networks sufer from a high failure rate (Lewin et al., 1999) – often well in excess of 50% (Gulati et al., 2012). Information systems (IS) researchers therefore strive to understand how information technology (IT) resources can lead to co-created value to support IOR (Grover & Kohli, 2012).

Research on IT-based value co-creation explores how multiple firms with diferent IT systems jointly create superior value (Grover & Kohli, 2012). Studies often build on resource-based theoretical lenses when examining the phenomenon of value proposition through inter-organisational IT resources (Paulraj et al., 2008; Rai et al., 2006; Saraf et al., 2007). Prior research has argued that inter-organisational networks must develop IT capabilities through synergistic combination with other resources and capabilities to address the specific challenges that arise within the network context (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Rai et al., 2012). Recent studies have identified various IOR IT capabilities, such as IT integration (DQ Chen et al., 2013; Saraf et al., 2007), information exchange capability (MC Dong et al., 2017; Wong et al., 2011), and

IT-enabled coordination (Im & Rai, 2014; Nicolaou et al., 2011). Yet, despite the considerable eforts and findings in this research field, advanced knowledge on IT capability development for co-creating value remains limited. This is reflected in inconsistent research findings regarding direct vs indirect efects (S Dong et al., 2009; Rai et al., 2006) as well as by some insignificant relationships found between IOR IT capabilities and co-created value (Saldanha et al., 2013). Therefore, we identify an area of research opportunity for developing a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving IT-based value co-creation (Grover & Kohli, 2012).

The concept of IT-business alignment refers to the general fit between IT and business components (Luftman & Brier, 1999) and is considered an impor tant source of IT business value among the foremost topics of management concern in recent years (Kappelman et al., 2019). A multitude of studies have extended our understanding of the antecedents, con sequences, conceptualisation, and operationalisation of IT-business alignment, providing ample empirica support for its role as a key driver of organisational performance (Gerow et al., 2014; Luftman et al., 2017) Despite these eforts, IT-business alignment is rarely investigated from the inter-organisational perspective outside of its conceptualisation (Katzy et al., 2016; Zarvić et al., 2012) and influence on supply chain performance (Sanders, 2005; Wu et al., 2006). However, a deeper understanding of IT-business alignment’s role in the value co-creation process is important for two reasons (Coltman et al., 2015) First, organisations increasingly depend on value cocreation in networks and enabling IT resources. It is hard to imagine that any modern multi-process orga nisations will succeed without an innovation ecosys tem that comprises suppliers, business partners, and customers working together. A variety of inter organisational IT resources, such as e-business plat forms, electronic data interchange (EDI), and colla boration systems are the enablers for these ecosystems (Chi & Holsapple, 2005; Constantinides et al., 2018). Second, the value co-creation context creates a new degree of complexity to the alignment process. This includes, e.g., diferent IT resources with heteroge neous processes, capabilities and diverging strategies, challenges in the cross-company communication for alignment initiatives, or network governance issues due to foreign ownership of processes and IT resources (Rai et al., 2012). Specific IOR IT capabilities are required to meet these alignment challenges Moreover, the consequences and value creation of alignment need to be rethought (Kohli & Grover, 2008). In contrast to the intra-organisational case, net work participants need to ensure that they capture their respective share of any newly created business value from IOR IT-business alignment eforts.

Research, therefore, underlines that the subject of IOR IT-business alignment has yet to fully explore its potential for transforming IT resources to meet interorganisational relationship requirements (Coltman et al., 2015; Katzy et al., 2016). To the best of our knowledge, there is no study that theoretically derives causal explanations regarding the role of IT-business alignment in the process of IT capability-building for value co-creation.

Based on these two research gaps in the largely independent research fields of IT-based value cocreation and IT-business alignment, this study aims to integrate the concept of IT-business alignment into research on inter-organisational IT capabilities. In this study, we focus on operational IT-business alignment in IOR, which is defined as the fit between interorganisational business and IT infrastructures and processes, reflecting a focus on performance enhancement through IOR IT capabilities (Katzy et al., 2016). The motivation for this research stems from two core observations from relevant literature. First, the concept of IT-business alignment, especially operational alignment, as a driver for organisational performance is well established and finds empirical support in individual firms (Gerow et al., 2014; Luftman et al., 2017) providing a solid conceptual starting point for analysing IT-based value co-creation mechanisms. We expect that IOR IT-business operational alignment can enrich our understanding of joint creation of superior value by leveraging common IT resources (Coltman et al., 2015). Second, typical characteristics of inter-organisational networks, such as insuficient information integration among partners, instability in alliances, and a lack of hierarchical decision-making (Winkler, 2006) diferentiate IOR IT-business alignment from the intra-organisational context and further emphasise the challenges of IT-business alignment (Coltman et al., 2015; King, 2013). Thus, there is both a potential and a need to examine IT-business alignment in inter-organisational contexts. We, therefore, aim to answer the following research question: What is the role of IOR IT-business operational alignment for co-creating value and how can such a capability be developed?

This research question is addressed through empirical validation of a theoretically derived research model. Drawing on the resource-based view (RBV) and the capability-building process in IS literature (Barua et al., 2004; Rai et al., 2006; Sambamurthy et al., 2003), we interpret IOR IT-business operational alignment as a higher-order IT capability and an immediate source of performance outcomes realised by network members (i.e., relationship performance). Furthermore, we identify three lower-order IOR IT capabilities that can be leveraged to develop IOR ITbusiness operational alignment (see Table 1 for construct definitions). First, IT infrastructure integration refers to the IS application integration between network partners. Integrated IT infrastructures provide the technical foundation for initiatives that align interorganisational IS and inter-organisational processes. Second, information exchange capability refers to the extent of IT-enabled information exchange and provides required knowledge about inter-organisational processes, IT systems and interdependencies. Third, IT-enabled coordination, i.e., the extent of IS usage for network planning, improves planning and decisionmaking of network partners to better allocate and utilise common IT resources. Drawing on a survey in the context of organisations in regional networks in Germany, we find empirical evidence for our research model and the capability-building process.

This study makes two major research contributions. First, our analysis extends the conceptualisation of operational IT-business alignment beyond the firmlevel source of value to the relational level. We stress the importance of IT-business operational alignment for organisations in inter-organisational networks and identify specific characteristics of achieving and creating value through IT-business operational alignment in this context. Second, we extend existing research on IT capabilities and IT-based value co-creation by interpreting IOR IT-business operational alignment as an important explanatory factor in the relationship between IOR IT capabilities and co-created value. By explaining this relationship within a theory-based conceptual framework, we contribute to the clarification of conceptual inconsistencies in previous research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. IT-based value co-creation

The concept of co-creation generally refers to collaboration among multiple stakeholders (Ranjan & Read, 2016). In the IS context, the term “co-creation of IT value” was introduced by Kohli and Grover (2008) as an extension of IT business value research in multi-firm environments. IT-based value cocreation extends the level of analysis of IT business value research to inter-organisational relationships, “examining how diferent companies with perhaps diferent IT can join together and create new value that either organisation is unlikely to create on its own” (Grover & Kohli, 2012). Examples of this include the integration of digitalised supply chain processes with the help of supply chain collaborative systems (Hadaya & Cassivi, 2012; Jiang & Zhao, 2014), collaboration with third parties on IT-based platforms (J Kim et al., 2019; F Wang et al., 2017), and the development of new products in innovation networks using networked technologies (Prince et al., 2014). This research aims to contribute to the stream of literature on IT value co-creation. In the following, we will explain its theoretical foundations.

## 2.2. IT capabilities

The resource-based view (RBV) highlights the unique resources of a firm as the central source of organisational performance, arguing that valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable resources lead to sustained competitive advantage (Barney, 1991). This can be achieved by developing organisational capabilities, which refer to the abilities of an organisation to integrate, mobilise, and utilise resources, usually in combination with other resources and capabilities (Grant, 1991). Transferring this view to the IS context, the core value of IT resources is derived from its synergistic combination with other IT- and non-IT-related resources, rather than the IT investments themselves (Benitez-Amado & Walczuch, 2012). The capability to apply such IT resources eficiently and efectively is regarded as the key to competitive performance (Goh & Arenas, 2020; Mata et al., 1995; Wade & Hulland, 2004). Following this perspective, an IT capability can be defined as “the ability to mobilize and deploy IT-based resources in combination or co-present with other resources and capabilities” (Bharadwaj, 2000). According to Wade and Hulland (2004), IT capabilities can be classified into three types: (1) inside-out capabilities are developed from inside the firm in response to requirements of the market; (2) outside-in capabilities are externally oriented and focus on managing external relation ships and resources; and (3) spanning IT capabilities integrate inside-out and outside-in IT capabilities by invo lving both an external and internal perspective.

The RBV further states that firms can create business value by developing a capability-building process (Grant, 1996; Teece et al., 1997). A capability-building process results in a hierarchy of capabilities in which lower-order capabilities<sup>1</sup> shape the development of higher-order capabilities, which creates business value. The notion of a capability-building process has been adopted in prior IT business value research. Rai et al. (2006, p. 225), e.g., refer to the IT-enabled organisational capabilities perspective to explain “how and why IT shapes high-order process capabilities that create performance gains.” Benit ez-Amado and Walczuch (2012, p. 667) refer more broadly to the so-called mediation hypothesis, “IT resour ces and capabilities do not seem to help the firm directly to improve competitive position, but can do so indirectly through the mediation of higher-order organizational capabilities.”

In the following, we adopt the capability-building pro cess of the RBV and the classification by Wade and Hul land (2004) to theorise the role of IOR IT-business opera tional alignment and other IOR IT capabilities (i.e., IT infrastructure integration, information exchange capability and IT-enabled coordination) in co-creating value.

## 2.3. IT capabilities in inter-organisational relatinonships

In the following, we identify three lower-order capabilities and a higher-order capability that lie at the interorganisational level of analysis and are related to organisational performance. All the capabilities are conceptua lised as IT capabilities, especially as IOR IT capabilities. In particular, higher-order IOR IT capabilities blend inter-organisational resources with inter-organisational processes and are embedded in the social, structural, and cultural contexts of networked firms (Rai et al., 2006). In contrast, lower-order IOR IT capabilities shape the development of higher-order IOR IT and organisational capabilities, leading to sustained performance gains for networked firms. Construct definitions for the core research variables are summarised in Table 1.

IT infrastructure integration is defined as, “the extent to which the IS applications of a focal firm work as a functional whole in conjunction with the IS applications of its business partners” (Saraf et al., 2007). It requires internal technical skills and is developed from inside the firm (S Dong et al., 2009). An integrated IT infrastructure connects diferent databases and applications of network partners by establishing communication platforms and common data standards and is regarded as the “nerve system” of interfirm collaboration (S Dong et al., 2009; Prasad et al., 2013). Accordingly, IT infrastructure integration has been identified as an important capability in interorganisational relationships. For example, Rai et al. (2006) find that IS integration between supply chain partners has a positive impact on supply chain capa bilities.

Information exchange capability refers to the abilities of a firm to exchange information with network partners based on IS applications (Barua et al., 2004). Information exchange capability represents an outside-in capability because it strengthens the interorganisational flow of knowledge and aims to recognise valuable information from network partners (Roberts & Grover, 2012). Due to the critical importance of information sharing to inter-organisational network functionality and subsequent reduction of uncertainty, its support through information systems is regarded as substantial IOR IT capability (Wong et al., 2011). Consequently, beneficial outcomes resulting from information exchange capability have enjoyed much attention from research studies (Barua et al., 2004; Malhotra et al., 2005; Wong et al., 2011).

IT-enabled coordination refers to the extent to which IT is used for planning and arranging the interdependent activities in which network partners collectively engage (Nicolaou et al., 2011). This includes, for example, interpretation support systems for efective decision-making (Malhotra et al., 2005) and IS for collaborative planning (ETG Wang et al., 2013). Such systems provide the basis to manipulate, assimilate, and blend internal and external information of a firm collaborating in an inter-organis ational network, thus representing a spanning IT capability (Roberts et al., 2012). IT-enabled coordination facilitates the management of complex interdependencies between networked organisations and is recognised as an important IOR IT capability (Im & Rai, 2014; Nico laou et al., 2011).

Research findings regarding the relationships between IOR IT capabilities and co-created value demonstrate inconsistencies (Table 2). First, while some studies assu me direct impacts (Wong et al., 2011; Zhu & Kraemer, 2005), other studies postulate indirect relationships throu gh intermediate factors (Rai et al., 2006; Wong et al., 2015). Second, most studies find positive relationships between IOR IT capabilities and co-created value (MC Dong et al., 2017; Saraf et al., 2007), whereas some reveal insignificant relationships (Saldanha et al., 2013; Truman, 2000). These inconsistencies result in limited understanding of how IOR IT capabilities lead to value in inter-organisational networks, calling for a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of IT-based value co-crea tion through further research (Grover & Kohli, 2012). Importantly, studies exploring IT capabilities have indicated that higher-order capabilities, such as supply chain integration (Rai et al., 2006) and contextual ambidexterity (Im & Rai, 2014), can explain how these capabilities enhance co-created value. We argue that IOR ITbusiness operational alignment can be a further explanatory factor in this value co-creation process and may help clarify prior inconsistencies in the literature.

## 2.4. IT-business operational alignment in interorganisational relatinonships

IT-business alignment can generally be described as, “applying IT in an appropriate and timely way and in harmony with business strategies, goals, and needs” (Luftman & Brier, 1999, p. 109). By facilitating eficient and efective use of information systems, ITbusiness alignment can be regarded as a firm’s capability to align IT- and business-related resources to achieve a competitive advantage (Avison et al., 2004; Schwarz et al., 2010). The Strategic Alignment Model (SAM) by Henderson and Venkatraman (1993) classifies information systems into four business and IT components (i.e., business strategy, IT strategy, organisational infrastructure and processes, and IS infrastructure and processes). Accordingly, IT-business alignment is defined as the fit between two or more of these components to ensure that business and IT remain in harmony (Chan & Reich, 2007; Luftman & Brier, 1999), resulting in three types of IT-business alignment: intellectual, operational, and cross-doma in alignment. Whereas intellectual alignment ensures a close link between business strategy and IT strategy, cross-domain alignment refers to the integration of infrastructures and processes with strategies. In this study, we focus on operational alignment, which descr ibes “the link between organisational infrastructure and processes and I/S infrastructure and processes” (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993).

IT-business alignment at the inter-organisational level involves multiple firms, leading to additional alignment levels and interdependencies due to the heterogeneity of structures, processes, systems, and even alignment capabilities within the member organisations (Coltman et al., 2015; Zarvić et al., 2012). Drawing from the conceptualisation of IT-business alignment in inter-organisational networks by Katzy et al. (2016), we analyse the alignment between inter-organisational business and IT infrastructures and processes, i.e., IOR IT-business operational alignment. Our conceptualisation, therefore, corresponds with the perspective of “functional integration” of the SAM (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993), which has also been labelled “internal” (Chan & Reich, 2007) and “operational” (Gerow et al., 2015) alignment. In contrast to intellectual or strategic IT-business alignment, where IT and business strategy can also be measured at the pro cess-level (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), we analyse processes and infrastructures at the operational level: The inter-organisational IT component encompasses IT architectures and applications for networked firms (e.g., common IT platforms, cloud applications, and interorganisational systems) and supportive IT processes (e.g., IT incident management process to restore the operation of an inter-organisational IT system, or operative IT change management processes to reliably implement minor IT changes). The business component comprises inter-organisational business processes, such as customer order or network management processes, and network infrastructures, such as common financial controls and transportation services of network partners (Gerow et al., 2016; Katzy et al., 2016; Riemer & Klein, 2006). The concept of IOR IT-business operational alignment should be distinguished from integration or “accordance” (Katzy et al., 2016), which refers to the correspon dence of infrastructures and processes of network members with network-level systems and infrastructures.

Thus far, although some conceptual research on IOR IT-business alignment exists (Katzy et al., 2016; Mandal et al., 2003; Zarvić et al., 2012), the topic has received limited overall attention. IT-business alignment has been subject to investigations in the context of supply chains (Sanders, 2005; Seggie et al., 2006; Wu et al., 2006). For example, Wu et al. (2006) show that IT-business alignment across the supply chain leads to advanced supply chain capabilities, which, in turn, enhance market and financial performance. However, dominant firms in the supply chain can impose strategies and information systems on other companies, diferentiating the context from inter-organisational networks (Katzy et al., 2016). Power and Gruner (2017) examine inter-organisational IT alignment in Australian SMEs, however, focusing on the dimension of intellectual alignment, i.e., the alignment between a firm’s strategy and its IT-related decisions.

While operational IT-business alignment is already underrepresented in contrast to intellectual alignment in IS literature (Gerow et al., 2016), we found no study examining operational IT-business alignment with regard to other IT capabilities in IOR. The conceptualisation of IOR IT-business operational alignment as the fit between inter-organisational business and IT infrastructures suits the context of our study for two key reasons. First, we view IOR IT-business operational alignment as a capability and aim to further investigate its role in the value co-creation process with other IOR IT capabilities. We, therefore, argue that employing a tangible fit–related conceptualisation of alignment is more consistent with our theory than intangible alignment concepts, such as social alignment (Gerow et al., 2016; Wagner et al., 2014). Second, in contrast to single firms or market relationships, inter-organisational networks lack hierarchy and control, which often leads to informal, emergent, and implicit strategies (Sturm et al., 2004). In addition to the resulting dificulties in conceptualisation and measurement, strategic alignment in inter-organis ational networks may not always exist or serve as a desirable or efective solution (Katzy et al., 2016).

## 3. Research model

We seek to understand the role of IOR IT-business ope rational alignment in co-creating value and how it can be established. The research framework and research model of this study are presented in Figure 1. Our research mo del draws on the RBV and the capability-building process in IS research (Rai et al., 2006). First, we interpret IOR IT-business operational alignment as a higher-order IOR IT capability and, therefore, as an immediate source of relationship performance. Second, this study conceptualises IT infrastructure integration, information exchange capability, and IT-enabled coordination as lower-order IOR IT capabilities that can be leveraged to develop the higher-order capability of IOR IT-business operational alignment, in turn leading directly to relationship performance. According to the distinction between higherand lower-order capabilities, the higher the order of capabilities, the more direct the impact on performance outcomes (Benitez et al., 2018; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006; Rai et al., 2006). Following this logic, we propose that the lower-order IOR IT capabilities IT infrastructure integration, information exchange capability, and IT-enabled coordination help to afect relationship performance through the development of IOR IT-business operational alignment, a higher-order IOR IT capability.

## 3.1. IIT-business operational alignment as a source of relationship performance in interorganisational relatinonships

In our research model, we identify relationship performance as the outcome variable. Research on IT business value argues that IT impacts should be assessed at the specific domain of interest where immediate efects are expected (Klein & Rai, 2009; Ray et al., 2005). In our context, we aim to examine superior benefits resulting from collaboration in inter-organisational networks. Considering that relationship performance refers to superior benefits which cannot be attained individually by a network partner and are eventually absorbed by network members to realise the firm-level value (Dyer & Singh, 1998; Lavie, 2006), we integrate it in our resea rch model as an outcome variable (Prasad et al., 2013; Rai et al., 2012). Consistent with previous research, we consider both tangible economic outcomes (i.e., reduced ope rational costs, increased revenue, and productivity) as well as intangible dimensions (i.e., increased flexibility, improved launch of new products and services, and bet ter service quality) (Im & Rai, 2014; Klein & Rai, 2009).

Drawing on the capability-building process of the RBV, we argue that IOR IT-business operational alignment leads directly to superior performance outcomes for network partners. Although inter-organisational IT resources such as collaborative platforms or supply chain systems can be beneficial for network partners (Chi & Holsapple, 2005; Sambamurthy et al., 2003), these IT resources are widely available on the market and can easily be replicated by competitors (Barua et al., 2004; Mata et al., 1995). To achieve and maintain a competitive advantage, partners in inter-organisational networks mu st align their IT resources with business infrastructures and processes, in accordance with the business needs of the network. IOR IT-business operational alignment is the capability to ensure a fit between inter-organisational IT and business infrastructures and processes, resulting in increased efectiveness and eficient use of collaborative IT resources. This strengthens the financial positions of network partners while reducing their operational costs (Sanders, 2005). Moreover, such a capability is unique to the network and therefore dificult for competitors to imitate, thus strengthening the competitive and market position of network partners (Dyer & Singh, 19 98; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). These arguments are supported by studies that have emphasised the important role of IT-business alignment in inter-organisational networks (Katzy et al., 2016; Zarvić et al., 2012) and revealed its performance-enhancing impact in supply chains (Sa

## 3.2. Antecedents of inter-organisational relationships: IT-business operational alignment

Based on the capability-building process, we specify IT infrastructure integration, information exchange capability, and IT-enabled coordination as antecedents of IOR IT-business operational alignment. Individual hypotheses are developed in the following sections.

## 3.2.1. IT infrastructure integration

An integrated IT infrastructure acts as an enabler for IOR IT-business operational alignment (Katzy et al., 2016). Only when a certain degree of IT integration between net work partners exists can inter-organisational systems be integrated into inter-organisational processes and infrastructures (S Dong et al., 2009; Saraf et al., 20

07), leading to a better fit between inter-organisati onal businesses and IT processes and infrastructures. For example, adaptations in supply chain processes, su ch as inventory stock levels, transportation routes, and production plans, are facilitated by smooth information flows and seamless data access through integrated information systems (Bharadwaj et al., 2007). Furthermore, IT integration can be seen as a relation-specific investment, as it is customised and specific to the network (DQ Chen et al., 2013; Saraf et al., 2007). Accordingly, a high level of IT infrastructure integration increases the willingness of network partners to engage in value-adding initiatives and develop further IT-business operational alignment within the network. IT infrastructure integration has been thoroughly researched as an important antecedent to performance-enhancing IOR capabilities, such as supply chain capabilities (Rajaguru & Matanda, 2013), supply chain integration (DQ Chen et al., 2013; Rai et al., 2006) and knowledge desorptive capacity (Braojos et al., 2020). This might indicate that IT infrastructure integration also has a positive impact on IOR IT-business ope rational alignment. We, therefore, propose our second hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2 (H<sub>2</sub>): IT infrastructure integration positively afects IOR IT-business operational alignment in a context of regional network collaborations.

## 3.2.2. Information exchange capability

To successfully align inter-organisational processes and information systems, network partners require knowle dge about each other’s processes and IT systems and their interdependencies (Zarvić et al., 2011). IT-enabled exch ange of information provides resources for facilitating the transfer of higher value-adding and rich information sharing (Malhotra et al., 2005). The increased level and higher quality of information exchange provide interorganisational networks with the conditions needed to achieve IOR IT-business operational alignment as network members can more eficiently refine and synchronise processes and structures (Im & Rai, 2008) and impr ove resource allocation (Klein & Rai, 2009). Furthermore, information exchange capability increases transparency between network partners, thus reducing uncertainty and opportunistic behaviour (Chi & Holsapple, 2005), while enhancing the willingness to develop the joint capability of IOR IT-business operational alignment. These arguments are supported by various studies that investigate the efect of information exchange capability on valueenhancing organisational capabilities, such as the digitisation level (Barua et al., 2004) and supply chain flexibility (Y Wang et al., 2015). Considering these findings, it can be argued that information exchange capability also facilitates the capability of IOR IT-business operational alignment. We, therefore, propose a third hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3 (H<sub>3</sub>): Information exchange capability positively afects IOR IT-business operational alignment in a context of regional network collaborations.

## 3.2.3. IT-enabled coordination

Coordination has been recognised as critical in the context of inter-organisational networks (Provan & Kenis, 2008). IT-enabled coordination between network partners is carried out by identifying, processing, and analysing huge amounts of data, leading to improved planning and decision-making (ETG Wang et al., 2013; Im & Rai, 2014). Thereby, IT-enabled coordination contributes to developing an efective and eficient use for common resources in an inter-organisational network, finding synergies between network partners, and solving complex problems (ETG Wang et al., 2013; Provan & Kenis, 2008). Efective and eficient decisions ensure that network partners invest in the appropriate information sys tems and infrastructures to support inter-organisational processes and structures (Hadaya & Cassivi, 2012). Mor eover, when managing inter-organisational resources and activities, opportunistic behaviour can occur because of conflicts and distrust between network partners. In this context, self-enforcing safeguards serve as cost-efec tive and practical preventative means (Chi & Holsapple, 2005; Dyer & Singh, 1998). The capability of IT-enabled coordination can be regarded as a safeguard, leading to less opportunistic behaviour of partners in inter-organi sational networks (ETG Wang et al., 2013; Grover & Ko hli, 2012). In addition to the resulting shared responsibility and accountability, this enhances the willingness and motivation of network partners to engage in valueadding initiatives (Dyer & Singh, 1998; Prasad et al., 20 13) and increases the level of IOR IT-business operational alignment. Therefore, we propose the fourth hypo thesis:

Hypothesis 4 (H<sub>4</sub>): IT-enabled coordination positively afects IOR IT-business operational alignment in a context of regional network collaborations.

## 4. Research design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted on 241 organisations, collecting regional network collaboration data acro ss inter-organisational networks with standardised questionnaires. We chose organisations and their direct relations with other organisations within a regional network cluster as the research context. To be specific, the resear ch design examines the capability of an organisation wit hin a regional network to align IT resources with relationships to other organisations in the same regional net work.

## 4.1. Research context

Regional networks are defined as a set of organisations that collaborate for common goals. These networks are characterised by their local proximity and common social milieu in terms of a similar understanding of cooperation, communication, and conflict resolution (Sydow & Duschek, 2011). The nature of collaboration lies in an extensive exchange of not only tangible assets but also intangible assets, such as information and knowledge. Typical objectives for networks lie in common product development activities (e.g., in optical industry clusters), common marketing activities (e.g., broker networks), or industry networks for resource sharing (e.g., industrial symbiosis).

Collaborations in regional networks often involve different forms of cooperation. For example, in an optical la ser network, diferent organisations link together to achi eve the common goal of product development. Each organisation provides a specialised resource to the network, such as lenses, transformers, or microelectronics. The area of cooperation for such a network lies in research/development and production. To achieve the common network goal, the regional network shares a co mmon set of network-wide business processes (Sydow & Duschek, 2011), such as a resource allocation process or a quality management process. Inter-organisational IT that supports a distributed product development can include collaboration platforms for the innovation process or systems for quality management and control that are integrated across network partners.

## 4.2. Unit of analysis

Provan et al. (2007) classify inter-organisational network research along the independent variable and the dependent variable, and the unit of analysis (i.e., organisational variables or network variables). For example, research on whole networks, in which the independent and the depen

dent variables lie at the network level, examine the impa ct of network structures such as centrality on aggregated network-level outcomes. In this study, the IOR IT capabilities refer to capabilities of a single organisation to either integrate their systems with their network partners, exchange knowledge within their relations, coordinate their relations using IT-based capability, or align the ir IT resources with their relationships to other organi sations.

Moreover, relationship performance refers to the aggregated outcome of a single organisation with its specific relations within the network cluster. According ly, all research variables lie at the organisational level. This perspective of organisational inter-organisational IT capabilities is consistent with existing IT value cocreation studies (Klein & Rai, 2009; Nicolaou et al., 2011; Saraf et al., 2007; Zhao & Xia, 2014). Within the group of research on IT value co-creation and organisational capabilities and outcomes, a further diferentiation can be made regarding the structure of the relationship. While some studies focus on dyadic relationships (1:1), e.g., between a focal organisation and a specific business partner (Klein & Rai, 2009), other studies focus on a specific set of relationships of a single organisation (1: n), e.g., between a business unit and their channel partners (Saraf et al., 2007). This decision is typically dependent on the network type and the research purpose. We aim to examine organisational capabilities and outcomes.

Our sample comprises organisations within a regional network cluster. Organisations were surveyed regarding a specific regional network that has emerged in their regional cluster. We chose to refer our measurement to the aggregated level of all relations of a single organisation in its specific network (see Figure 2). This approach is consistent with other research on IT value co-creation (Nicolaou et al., 2011; Saraf et al., 2007; Zhao & Xia, 2014) and appropriate because the relationships work together to achieve the common goal of this specific regional network and network-related business processes run through all relationships. The IOR IT capabilities of a single organisation equally afect all relationships. Moreover, relationship outcome refers to the sum of relationships rather than to single relationships. Thus, measuring our variables at the aggregated level better captures specific characteristics of our research context and reduces bias from relationship-specific influences.

## 4.3. Measures

The survey instrument was developed based on a comprehensive review of appropriate measurement scales for each theoretical construct. All scales were adapted from prior literature, and the items were selected based on their consistencies with the construct definition in this research, measurement quality, and application in other studies (Appendix A). To adjust the scales to the research context of this investigation, the items have been carefully reworded. Construct specifications in terms of composite-formative, reflective, and causal-formative measurement are based on personal evaluations (Benitez et al., 2020) and followed guidelines in Henseler (2017).

## 4.4. Sampling frame and data collection

The sampling frame in this investigation was provided by Cluster Observatory (www.clusterobservatory.eu), an online database for regional network clusters in Europe consisting of a variety of regional network types, such as research networks, supply networks, and engineering networks. German clusters were randomly selected, and a list of organisations from these clusters was obtained from this database. Email invitations were sent out to organisation members responsible for network cooperation with other organisations within their respective clusters.

A web-based survey questionnaire was developed for this investigation, with measurement scales translated to German. Data were collected in August and September 2017 and included a follow-up email to encourage responses. The personalised emails asked the managers to refer their answers in the questionnaire to the network that emerged from the specific cluster, as stated on Cluster Observatory. In the cases of several networks, participants were asked to base their answers to the questionnaire on the network formation with which they are most familiar. Moreover, participants were given a brief description of examples of network instances within a typical cluster. Participants were asked to answer all questions through the lens of the organisational entity where they might be employed. The survey instrument also checked whether each respondent had the necessary knowledge to serve as a key informant. Responses from those who stated that they had insuficient knowledge of network processes or network IT were discarded. All IT alignment measures were supplemented with explanations and various examples of inter-organisational processes and systems. The structure, clarity, ambiguity, and completeness of the questionnaire were reviewed by three academic researchers and five field experts. The pre-tests resulted in minor wording alterations to improve clarity and modifications of the layout.

## 4.5. Descriptive statistics and data screening

The sampling method above resulted in an efective sample frame size of 2,830 firms, of which 251 surveys were completed (see Table 3 for sample characteristics). Ten questionnaires were discarded due to quality criteria, such as missing values, the implausibility of demographics, or network characteristic answers, reducing the sample size to 241 firms. We conducted several tests to check for non-response bias, common method bias, and respondent validity. The results indicate no dedicated threat in one of these areas (Appendix B).

## 5.1. Measurement model assessment

The measurement model comprises reflective and formative constructs. All formative constructs were modelled as composites that are estimated using Mode A. We used Mode A instead of Mode B due to some collinearity between our indicators (Becker et al., 2013). All reflective constructs were estimated using consistent PLS.

We first checked whether the PLS estimations had converged properly. The results show that the interconstruct correlation and the implied correlation matrix of the models are both positive semidefinite (i.e., all eigenvalues > 0). Moreover, all factor loadings range between −1 and 1 and the reliability scores are below 1.

For all formative constructs, we first evaluated the relative and absolute contributions of their indicators. All indicators were revealed a significant factor weight and factor loading. We then evaluated the reflective constructs for indicator reliability, composite reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. We excluded one item from the IOR IT-business operational alignment scale, two items from the information exchange capability scale, and one item from the ITenabled coordination scale. The resulting measurement model reveals that all items significantly load on their respective construct above the threshold of .707 (Hulland, 1999). Furthermore, all composite reliabilities in terms of Dijkstra–Henseler’s $\rho _ { \mathrm { A } }$ lie above the acceptable limit of .707 for reliabilities (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994), and each AVE also exceeds the lower bound of .50 (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988). The detailed results are depicted in Appendix B. We then assessed discriminant validity using the heterotrait–monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT) indicator (Henseler et al., 2014). The highest pairwise HTMT value is .608, which is below the threshold of .85. Additionally,

the 95% quantiles of the bootstrapped HTMT values are below one. The results suggest that discriminant validity can be confirmed.

We then evaluated our data’s overall fit with the theoretical constructs and estimated a saturated model (Table 4). As our model includes both compositeformative measurement and reflective measurement, we used a combined confirmatory composite/factor analysis (Henseler, 2017). The standardised rootmean-square residual (SRMR) lies at .038, which is below the threshold of .08 (LT & Bentler, 1999) and below .039, which is the bootstrapped 99% quantile of their reference distribution (HI99). We also inspected two measures $( \mathrm { d } _ { \mathrm { U L S } }$ and $\mathrm { d } _ { \mathrm { G } } )$ that reflect the discrepancy between the empirical- and the model-implied variance-covariance matrix. ${ \tt d } _ { \mathrm { U L S } }$ is below the bootstrapped HI99 reference distribution and $\mathrm { d } _ { \mathrm { G } }$ is below the bootstrapped HI95 reference distribution. In sum, our data reveal to have an acceptable level of model fit.

## 5.3. Mediation analysis

To check if variance shared between the lower-order capabilities and relationship performance is mediated over alignment, we conducted mediation analysis (Zhao et al., 2010) and computed a partially mediated Model 3. In contrast to Model 2, it includes direct paths between the lower-order capability and relationship performance. For all three lower-order capabilities, we find a significant indirect efect on relationship performance (see Table 5 – Model 3 – Indirect Efects). Accordingly, we find support for our mediation hypotheses (H5a–H5c). As a next step, we classified the mediation and examined whether the direct efect was significant. The direct efect of information exchange capability is insignificant. This result suggests a full mediation (i.e., indirect-only mediation). Furthermore, we find a significant efect for IT infrastructure integration and IT-enabled coordination. These results suggest that the mediation is complementary in nature, which can be classified as a partial mediation.

## 5.4. Robustness checks

As robustness checks, we estimated and compared two more models (Table 6). First, we estimated IOR ITbusiness operational alignment as a hierarchical component model. We used the two-stage approach as our preferred estimation procedure (Ringle et al., 2012). To estimate the first stage, we used the repeated indicator approach to obtain the variable scores of the thr

ee lower-order capabilities. The measurement models of the first-order parts were kept according to their existing specifications. The latent variable scores of the first stage were used as manifest variables for IOR ITbusiness operational alignment using PLS Mode B to estimate the second stage. The results (see Model 4) reveal that all three first-order constructs significantly contribute to the second-order construct. Moreover, IOR IT-business operational alignment significantly explains relationship performance. Accordingly, the interpretation of the results does not change.

Second, we estimate the efect of common method bias (CMB) on our structural path estimates using the construct level correction approach (Chin et al., 2013). Based on a theoretically unrelated marker variable (i.e., an individual’s willingness to learn), we created a CMB control construct for each latent variable. The new model reveals that the CMB control constructs impacts on the research variables are insignificant and small (ranging from −.076 to .058). Moreover, the inclusion of the CMB control constructs only lead to minor changes in our path estimates $( \Delta < . 0 5$ , see Model 5). Overall, our results appear to be stable in terms of the modelling of IOR IT-business operational alignment and do not sufer from CMB.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Summary of findings

Modern organisations rely heavily on the joint creation of superior value in inter-organisational networks by leveraging common IT resources. However, the failure rates of such cooperation are high (Gulati et al., 2012). This research was spurred by the idea of examining the role of IOR IT-business operational alignment for co-creating value in regional network collaborations and how firms can develop this capability. We argued that organisations are required to build distinct IOR IT capabilities to align network IT infrastructures and processes. Moreover, we explored how an IOR IT-business operational alignment capability can help to generate co-created value.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions

The overarching contribution of this study lies in the integration of IT-business alignment into IT-based value co-creation research. We thus implicate two major contributions of our study to the IS literature.

First, we contribute to research that calls for considering the specific type and context of IT-business alignment. For instance, Gerow et al. (2015, p. 15) diferentiate between six types of IT-business alignment and conclude that researchers should “pick the type of alignment that is most suited to that situation.” Queiroz (2017) found mixed results regarding IT-business alignment at the infrastructural and process levels, concluding that the value of alignment at this level may highly depend on the context and conceptualisation of IT-business alignment. We argued that operational IT-business alignment is the type of alignment that best suits the context of interorganisational networks. To the best of our knowledge, no study has examined operational IT-business alignment with other IT capabilities in the context of IOR in networks. Our results demonstrate the value-enhancing efect of IOR IT-business operational alignment for interorganisational networks, extending findings from single firms (Gerow et al., 2014) and supply chains (Sanders, 2005; Seggie et al., 2006; Wu et al., 2006). Accordingly, inter-organisational networks might be a context in which this type of alignment seems to have positive performance efects. Furthermore, by examining antecedents, we can show that developing IOR IT capabilities, i.e., IT infrastructure integration, information exchange capability, and IT-enabled coordination facilitate the complex challenge (Katzy et al., 2016) of achieving IOR IT-business operational alignment.

Second, we contribute to the IT capabilities and the IT-based value co-creation literature by theorising a hie rarchy of IOR IT capabilities. Scholars have employed mixed approaches to investigate the relationship between IOR IT capabilities and co-created value. Some studies assume that IOR IT capabilities and co-created value are directly interrelated; others conceptualise the relationship as indirect (S Dong et al., 2009; Rai et al., 2006). These diferent conceptualisations limit the comparability of results, which is further undermined by insignificant findings (Saldanha et al., 2013). To improve comparisons and our understanding of how IOR IT capabilities lead to superior performance outcomes, our study utilises IOR IT-business operational alignment – the higher-order IOR IT capability to ensure a fit between IT and business infrastructures and processes in networks – as a diferen tiator between the two conceptualisations. Our findings reveal that IOR IT-business alignment improves the explanatory power of the model by mediating the relationship between IOR IT capabilities and relationship performance. Thus, we contribute to the view that the value of inter-organisational IT is derived from a capa bility-building process (Rai et al., 2006; Zhu et al., 2015). Another distinct feature of this study and the IT-based value co-creation literature can be seen in our unit of analysis and sample. Extant literature primarily studies supply chain relationships in the American or East Asian contexts. This study provides empirical evidence for regional network collaborations in a central European country, an area and market with significant economic importance (Sydow & Duschek, 2011).

## 6.3. Managerial implications

Given the high failure rate of network collaborations, this study provides important insights into how organisations can achieve IOR IT-business operational alignment for favourable network-level outcomes. Our study informs business executives who are responsible for the design, evaluation, and maintenance of the coordination of IOR. The study suggests that they should recognise IOR IT-business operational alignment as a central source of business value. While achieving IT alignment among net

work partners might be particularly challenging and conflicting, due to informal decision-making or diverging goals among the member organisations (Winkler, 2006), this is the central mechanism that transforms IT investments into value. Organisations already spend much efort on continuously monitoring intraorganisa

tional IT alignment (Gerow et al., 2014; Luftman et al., 2017); these eforts should be broadened and explicitly include IOR. Organisations can, for example, enable network managers, who are the central strategic link betwe

en an organisation and its network, to continuously mo

nitor the alignment process and, if appropriate, initiate alignment activities. Management capabilities should be built to handle and forecast the ongoing dynamics of I

OR, such as partner (de-)integration, and transform network IT resources in line with changing requirements.

This study also ofers recommendations for such capabilities that foster the establishment of IOR IT-business operational alignment capabilities. First, IT and business executives should benchmark and, if necessary, improve their IT infrastructure integration capabilities. Elements can include integrated IT networks, shared databases, or common technical platforms (Trang et al., 2013). Second, organisations should pay attention to their information exchange capability. Specific network information systems, such as collaboration portals, open knowledgebased systems, or even simple network catalogues provide transparency on operative information such as network (IT) resources, processes, and services. Moreover, through the exchange of tactical information, such as order prediction and product development plans, relevant network changes can be predicted more accurately. Third, organisations in IOR should evaluate and continuously improve their IT-enabled coordination capabilities. Network-specific management information systems can include network-specific data warehouses with historic collaboration data. These are important to facilitate future planning and continuous improvement, or coordinate tasks (Nicolaou et al., 2011).

## 6.4. Limitations and avenues for further research

As with other studies, it is important to discuss limitations in applying the insights from this study. The target population of regional networks in Germany might be considered a limitation of this investigation. Although we found substantial variations in IT-related capabilities and their respective IT alignments, regional networks have unique characteristics that may not be generalisable to other areas or cultures. As discussed earlier, these networks are characterised by high levels of trust. In addition, the results indicate that members of regional networks prefer face-to-face meetings, rather than communication through IT. However, regular meetings might be dificult in geographically distributed networks (Provan & Kenis, 2008), resulting in increased complexity of IT alignment. Additional research analysing other types of collaboration and in a diferent cultural context is necessary to substantiate our findings.

Moreover, using correlations of IT capabilities and IT success measures that are collected at a single point in time poses another potential limitation, as efects may be time-sensitive. This can be particularly relevant for the context of inter-organisational networks because they might be even less responsive to procedural and structural changes than changes within single organisations (Kim et al., 2006). While a longitudinal measurement is preferable, studies that follow a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based, single informant design – as ours does – often succeed in taking early steps in a new direction, forming a solid base for further research.

We would also like to note that the results need to be interpreted in light of modest model fits values for the fully mediated model and model fit values of the partially mediated model that are still close to the respective thresholds. We see this as an opportunity for further research to develop further and refine the proposed role of IOR IT-business operational alignment.

More generally, we believe that this study ofers further interesting avenues for research on ITbusiness alignment, IOR, and IT-based value cocreation. Future IS research can examine the economic consequences of IOR IT-business operational alignment in both more and less dynamic network contexts (e.g., strategic alliances vs. ad hoc networks). For instance, research on IT business value provides specific insights into how the intra-organisational valuecreation process is afected by internal factors such as IT governance decentralisation (Mikalef et al., 2020) and external factors such as environmental complexity, dynamism, and hostility (Y Chen et al., 2014; Mikalef & Krogstie, 2020). Translated to the network level, there is much to learn about how network properties such as power distribution and network rigidity (Provan et al., 2007) afect the value creation process from IT-business alignment beyond the direct efect. A further interesting question that emerges from this research is that of the social dimensions for the capability-building process of IOR IT-business operational alignment. Trust and mutual understanding within an organisation have remained a topic of intraorganisational IT alignment (Chan & Reich, 2007). Research on inter-organisational networks clearly shows that network trust and network-level competencies are central key predictors for network configurations, processes, and outcomes (Provan & Kenis, 2008). Our findings are expected to inspire future IS research to investigate how social dimensions between network partners afect the capability-building process of IOR IT-business operational alignment.

## 7. Conclusion

This research investigated the role of IOR IT-business operational alignment in co-creating value among organisations in inter-organisational networks. A survey data set of 241 organisations in regional network collaborations in Germany was collected and analysed. The results suggest that IOR ITbusiness operational alignment is a higher-order capability through which three IT-related IOR lowerorder capabilities afect relationship performance. IT infrastructure integration, information exchange capability, and IT-enabled coordination enable network organisations to establish IOR IT-business operational alignment between network processes and IT resources, which, in turn, is an immediate source of relationship performance through IT. Given these findings, we conclude that the concept of IT alignment, which has been heavily examined in the intraorganisational context, should receive more attention in the inter-organisational context.

## Appendix A. Non-response bias, common method bias, respondent validity

Overall, return rates of around 10% are typical for emailbased surveys in supply chain and network research (DQ Chen et al., 2013; Im & Rai, 2014). However, low response rates bear the risk of non-response bias. To test for nonresponse bias, we aimed to compare firm characteristics of the survey sample and the database. To accomplish this, we took another random sample (n = 250) from our database and collected information on firm size and industry (see Table 3). A chi-squared test on homogeneity revealed no significant diferences for either firm size (chi-squared = 1.29, p > .05) or industry (chi-squared = 2.55, p > .05). We conclude that non-response bias does not appear to be an issue in this investigation.

To counteract biases caused by data collection by a single informant, we employed diferent procedural remedies for common method variance as suggested in Podsakof et al. (2012). Procedural remedies included a cover story with detailed descriptions and examples as well as the usage of well-developed scales, which both decrease bias through ambiguous questions. Respondents were further motivated to respond accurately by saying that their opinions were valued and telling them that there were no wrong answers, which have been shown to decrease social desirability bias. We underlined the benefit for the responding organisation by giving them the opportunity to receive a full analysis and report, as promising feedback has been shown to motivate participants to respond more accurately. We allowed for scale types and anchor label variation in the questionnaire to decrease motivation to respond stylistically.

We also tested if individual characteristics of the respondents potentially bias estimations by conducting a series of t-tests for position, age, job experience, and IT competence on the items of the research variables. IT competence was measured by splitting the sample between IT and non-IT positions. We find no significant diferences. In summary, we conclude that common method bias and respondents’ individual characteristics are not a major concern for the validity of our results.

Lastly, the research design could yield diferent answers in the data set referring to the same relations. In this case, the statistical analysis must account for the clustered data structure. We therefore checked whether the data sets were equal in terms of network characteristics (“area of cooperation,” “network duration,” “network size,” “revenue,” “IT support”). As we found no concerning similarities among any data sets in our sample, we conclude that clustered data does not have to be considered for the following statistical analysis.
