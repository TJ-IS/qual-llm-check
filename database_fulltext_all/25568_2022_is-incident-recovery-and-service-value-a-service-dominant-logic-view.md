# IS incident recovery and service value: a service-dominant logic view

Mohammad S. Najjar<sup>a</sup>, William J. Kettinger <sup>b</sup> and Lynda D. Kettinger<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Business Information and Technology, The University of Memphis, Memphis, TN, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Management, Powers College of Business, Clemson University, Clemson, SC, USA; <sup>c</sup>Kettinger Associates, Isle of Palms, SC, USA

## ABSTRACT

IS service delivery failures inside companies are value diminishing events. Information systems service providers seek to limit this value destruction but lack guidance on IS incident resolution actions that satisfy users and preserve IS service value. We apply Service-Dominant Logic (SDL) to explore what is relevant to users’ recovery service experiences while interacting with IS service providers. We use an integrated research approach including qualitative and quantitative data. We uncover actions that lead to a satisfying incident recovery and categorise them into three recovery components (responsive handling interactions, supportive communicating interactions, and efective resolving actions) that reflect IS provider resource exchanges of information, knowledge, and service skills with users in resolving incidents. We integrate these three recovery components as factors in our research model and test direct and moderating efects on value outcomes. We find that users’ recovery satisfaction results from both a “fix it fast and fully” perspective and a sense of efort and fairness conveyed. Results point to managers preserving overall IS service satisfaction and service quality by facilitating proper resource exchanges during an incident recovery.

## 1. Introduction

Today’s workplace depends on “IT for everything”, where IS users demand constant access to IT hardware, data, and application services. Unexpected IS service interruptions are value-diminishing events that cost a company time, resources, and potential revenue. While large-scale operational failures dominate the news, such as British Airways’ global IT shutdown costing the firm over 102 USD million (Reuters, 2017), most failure incidents are smaller in scale and impact firms of all sizes. According to Everbridge, IT Alerting’s (2019) IT Incident Management Report, a survey of IT managers reveals 91% of firms experienced at least one major operational incident in a year, and the average cost per minute of unplanned downtime was 8,662USD. Network outages, hardware failures, and internal business application issues occurred in over half of the firms surveyed, more frequent than data centre outages (26%) or cybercrimes (14%).

Managers must also deal with intangible costs. IS failures interrupt work and the quality of service (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015) and may lead to user dissatisfaction, negative views of IS service quality, and reputation loss (Goode et al., 2017). Contemporary internal IS service delivery is a collaborative, relational process with frequent interactions among users, IS service providers (ISSPs), and other stakeholders (e.g., Sun et al., 2012; Winkler & Wulf, 2019). When IS systems are running well and interactions with the IS unit are responsive with knowledgeable, competent staf who understand users’ needs, both IS units and users experience benefit and accomplish business goals. However, little is known about how this overall value dynamic can be preserved when an incident occurs causing users to interact with ISSPs to address a service failure.

Managing a successful IS incident response is more than just a focus on a technical fix. It is “inherently people-oriented . . . the problem may be technical in nature but resolving it and keeping stakeholders informed involves interacting and communicating with one another” (Everbridge, IT Alerting, 2019). Failures are costly and demand resources of IS units and users. Thus, managers are challenged to ofer task–support interactions that integrate operant resources such as technical knowledge and service skills combined with users’ information and eforts during a response. To create a satisfying recovery experience and avoid overall value losses, it is essential to know what recovery components are most valuable for users and how they should be ofered.

Guidance is limited on specific interactions during an incident recovery that facilitates (or diminishes) satisfaction and recurring value in internal IS service delivery contexts. IS service failure recovery literature is scarce (Goode et al., 2017; Tan et al., 2016). A review of articles in the field’s prominent journals from 2011 to 2019 found two critical gaps (See Table A1, Appendix A): 1) Few operational IS failure studies are at the individual level, and most are limited to data breaches or privacy failures impacting external customers. 2) A theoretical framework is lacking to explain collaborative resource exchanges during a recovery efort that enable service value. Data breach studies have focused on firm-delivered remedies and theorised the roles of compensation (Goode et al., 2017) and psychological mechanisms (e.g., Bansal & Zahedi, 2015; Choi et al., 2016) to restore customer relationships where contacts are episodic and transactional. However, unlike external contexts where dissatisfied customers can switch to other businesses, users and IS units in internal settings are expected to continue interacting beyond the incident recovery. The role of beneficially applied resources in a collaborative incident recovery has not been theorised. Finally, the link between incident recovery satisfaction and overall IS satisfaction has not been explored, yet both may influence provider-user interactions after a recovery (Gelbrich & Roschk, 2011).

Our study addresses these gaps by drawing on the lens of Service-Dominant Logic (SDL) and is guided by the following questions: 1) What recovery components influence IS users’ satisfaction with an IS incident resolution service? and 2) How does users’ satisfaction with an incident recovery experience influence their views of overall IS service value? We used an integrated research approach that includes qualitative and quantitative data (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2019). Given scant research on our topic, we conducted a precursory study including interviews with IS users and scale development of our antecedent constructs. Analysis of qualitative data from our interviews identified three key recovery components (responsive handling interactions, supportive communicating interactions, and efective resolving actions) representing user-ISSP exchanges of resources most meaningful to users for recovery satisfaction. We incorporated these components as factors in our research model, indicating their three direct and two moderating efects on recovery satisfaction and its impact on value outcomes (overall IS unit satisfaction and IS service quality). We validate our model using quantitative data from a survey of IS users in a financial services firm. Findings indicate five of our seven hypotheses were supported.

This study ofers three novel contributions. First, using SDL with its focus on on-going service exchanges provides an alternative theoretical lens to the IS service recovery literature. We illustrate incident recovery’s collaborative nature and reveal specific value-enabling exchanges of service and technical skills by IS units with users. Second, we develop the concept of incident recovery satisfaction as an essential contributor to overall IS-unit service value and show that a satisfying IS incident recovery helps to avoid diminishing overall IS satisfaction and service quality. Third, we extend the theoretical and empirical applicability of SDL to IS service failure recovery research in internal IS delivery settings, focusing on proper resource integration as a facilitator of value cocreation.

## 2. Theoretical development and research model

In this section, we first describe our theoretical approach. Next, we introduce SDL and describe a collaborative IS incident resolution service. We then explain key resource integration concepts, develop our hypotheses, and present our research model (Figure 1).

## 2.1. Theoretical approach

To develop our theoretical model, we first use SDL as a meta-theoretical lens to identify and define concepts that explain incident recovery satisfaction and overall value outcomes. We then use exploratory interviews with IS users to help develop context-specific recovery components.

Limited research exists empirically applying SDL’s general constructs and factors influencing IS users’ recovery satisfaction. While the marketing discipline reveals much on service failure recovery mechanisms and attributes that influence satisfaction and mitigate negative efects (see Table A2, Appendix A), our understanding of recovery eforts, including joint customer-provider recoveries, is mostly with external customers concerning complaint-handling, selfservice technology, or online service failures. Both IS and marketing studies examine recovery attributes primarily through a lens of justice (fairness) or service expectations as cognitive antecedents of recovery satisfaction. Missing from both streams is a set of actionable customer-derived antecedents that afect on-going value co-creation, or the how-to’s of communicating, handling, and resolving IS users’ incidents in the context of a joint recovery in internal IS settings.

Therefore, we inform our theorising with qualitative data regarding IS users’ unique experiences interacting with IS staf to understand the specific recovery interactions and actions that impact users’ incident recovery satisfaction in our context. We use interview findings to interpretably detect specific dimensions for each recovery construct that reflects properly applied resources posited by SDL. These include the persistence of efort displayed by IT help desk/unit staf, fairness applied during the recovery service, the quality of apologies ofered, appropriateness of explanations of the problem, the speed of resolving the incident, and level of restoring the IT function completelysee Section 4.1. We also use service failure recovery and value co-creation (destruction) literature to refine our antecedents further and justify their linkages to incident satisfaction. Finally, we integrate interview findings to illustrate resource integration concepts in our context and develop the antecedent constructs and hypotheses of our model.

## 2.2. Service-dominant logic and value co-creation

Service-Dominant Logic (SDL) provides a foundation for understanding a collaborative path to co-create value (Vargo & Lusch, 2004). It focuses on on-going service exchanges where individuals jointly apply operant resources, such as knowledge and skills, sometimes along with operand resources such as IT hardware or software to meet others’ needs or their own (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015). We think of IS incident resolution as a service and the context for our study. Five premises (from Vargo & Lusch, 2008, 2016, p. 8) are applicable and explained in the organisational context of our study: 1) Resource integration, such as sharing information, applying specialised knowledge or competencies to leverage other intangible or tangible resources to get benefits, is the underlying driver of exchange and value co-creation. 2) Value is co-created by service providers, customers, and others through on-going interactions of service. Customers are always participants in the process, at varying levels depending on the context. 3) A service-centred view is inherently beneficiary-oriented and relational, beyond transactional or periodic contacts (Vargo et al., 2010). 4) All customers, service providers, and managers in an organisation or network are resource integrators, meaning all have knowledge or skills they may apply to benefit each other or the firm. 5) While benefits are mutual, value is uniquely determined by the beneficiary through perceptions “in use” of the service experience and outcomes.

Simply put, as applied in our study, SDL posits that value is defined by IS users, and the focus of IS units is helping them to “get their job done” (Bettencourt et al., 2014, p. 51). Each instance of users’ service exchange with IS providers and other stakeholders may be value-creating (Chandler & Vargo, 2011; Lusch & Vargo, 2006; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004). The process is holistic and accumulates over time through many interactions and highlights the interdependence of IS users and IS units in organisations (Vargo et al., 2010).

A typical incident response service within organisations is a co-operative process, where both IS users and ISSPs exchange operant resources at varying levels to solve the problem and realise mutual benefits, as depicted in Table 1. For example, when a user’s PC starts freezing or crashes, prompting a call to the Help Desk to help diagnose the cause, a user may engage with the ISSP by providing details about the failure and suggesting a suspected malware from a suspicious software download. IS units depend on users detecting and reporting operational IS failures to enable a prompt recovery and restore the business unit’s function. With a successful incident resolution, users receive reliable services, such as getting their PC backed up and running. Likewise, IS units benefit from reduced down-time costs and users being satisfied.

IS scholars recognise the importance of a service perspective to design and manage IS services to cocreate value given the high level of involvement of individuals jointly applying knowledge and skills to deliver and use IS services (e.g., Alter, 2010; Bardhan et al., 2010; Lusch & Nambisan, 2015). Emerging research has applied SDL concepts to examine value co-creation (e.g., Kathuria et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2012; Winkler & Wulf, 2019); however, none to our knowledge has applied SDL to explore operational IS service failures.

## 2.3. Resource integration in an incident recovery service

We focus on the fundamental SDL premise of resource integration and IS units’ potential to facilitate a positive service experience leading to satisfaction (value). We utilise the work of Peters et al. (2014) “Theorizing on Resource Integration through SDL”, which provides ontological and epistemological guidance for researchers (see Table B1 in Appendix B). Given the on-going exchange dynamic characterising the user-IS unit service relationship, the concept of how resources can be integrated and co-create value can be mapped to explain a successful incident resolution that requires both actions by IS providers to fix the problem and interactions performing the process and communicating with users.

ISSPs integrate their knowledge and skills interacting with IS users through “deeds, processes and performances” (Vargo & Lusch, 2004, p. 2), and the “heart” of these interactions is communication (Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004). Value co-creation results from the use of ISSPs’ or users’ resources applied during interactions as well as from experiencing the outcome of integrating resources (Grönroos & Voima, 2013; Gummerus, 2013; Vargo & Lusch, 2004, 2008). We explore responsive handling interactions, supportive communicating interactions, and efective resolving actions as three over-arching IS recovery constructs in which value-enabling technical and service skills, information, and other resources are applied.

Exploring factors that create (or diminish) value can be assessed in customers’ and providers’ resource exchanges (Vafeas et al., 2016). Here, we take the IS user’s view as the primary beneficiary of incident recovery and explore the adequacy of providers’ ofering of their resources. Compared to some project collaborations among users and IS units such as requirements determination, most incident resolutions heavily rely on the IS providers’ technical and service skills. Users’ evaluation of the experience will be based mostly on ISSP competencies and interactions (Liao, 2007). While this imbalance of knowledge and skills is seemingly provider-driven, as both users and the IS unit realise the benefits of service recovery, each recovery component may be conceptually considered as collaborative (Aarikka-Stenroos & Jaakkola, 2012; Echeverri & Skålén, 2011; Edvardsson et al., 2011; Vargo et al., 2008).

Several key concepts of resource integration apply to an IS incident recovery that may foster (or diminish) value in users’ experiences. First, as value creation is a relational and dynamic process, it may include both value creation and destruction phases (Grönroos & Voima, 2013). Service failures may be viewed as a value-destroying event. Echeverri and Skålén (2011) show that value can be jointly created or destroyed depending on the customer-service provider’s degree of congruence on interactional value practices such as informing, delivering, and helping during service encounters. In on-going B2B relationships, diminished value co-creation was found to result from resource deficiencies, such as inadequate communication or deficient knowledge and skills by either partner, leading to dissatisfaction (Vafeas et al., 2016), and an imbalance of specialised knowledge and information asymmetries can challenge the problemsolving process (Aarikka-Stenroos & Jaakkola, 2012). In general, when resources of any type expected or desired by customers (un)intentionally or accidentally fail, or when service processes are misaligned, value co-destruction may result (Lefebvre & Ple, 2011; Plé & Chumpitaz Caceres, 2010; Smith, 2013).

The link between the deficiency (adequacy) of information and service skills in communicating and handling interactions and resolving actions during an IS incident recovery service and value diminishment is illustrated by a user in our interviews who complained the IS staf

“did not provide any explanation, nor did they respond to my initial emails,” and when the user “finally got hold of someone” s/he felt “they were not to be bothered with my problem. Not fast, not courteous, not caring”.

Second, resources are “producers of efects”, they are generally intangible and dynamic, i.e., “in the moment of time and place of service use” (Vargo et al., 2010, p. 15). Recovery eforts such as apologising, solving problems, explaining, or displaying initiative can be viewed as resources and influence customers’ satisfaction perceptions directly or indirectly through fairness perceptions (e.g., Liao, 2007; Roschk & Gelbrich, 2014; Smith et al., 1999; Tax et al., 1998) and emotional impacts (Del Rio-Lanza et al., 2013; A. K. Smith & Bolton, 2002). A proper apology, for example, reflects the ISSP’s service skills when communicating. It is not the apology per se that fosters value co-creation, but what the provider is trying to “efect” for a user with this social resource exchange, e.g., show respect, diminish frustration, or sustain a collaborative relationship.

Third, service interactions do not have to be face-to -face or repeated encounters; instead, Vargo and Lusch (2016, p. 9) explain that interaction means “mutual or reciprocal action or influence.” While IS users are always participants in creating value for themselves and the IS unit, they may be more or less actively engaged in resolving actions and processes. Information and efort users contribute to report and provide details about an incident triggers the application of the IS unit’s technical knowledge and skills to fix their failure and may lead to a quicker resolution (Dong et al., 2008), or create applied knowledge for both users and the IS unit to prevent a future failure. Some users may apply proactive efort in handling interactions to access the IS provider’s resources (e.g., information) to gain a sense of fairness and control over his/her delayed work, as one user in our interviews stated,

“If I visited them in person about the complaint, they were able to tell how much delay is expected if any, depending on the work at hand.”

Finally, Vargo et al. (2008) define value co-creation as improving an individual’s, unit’s, or firm’s “wellbeing” and occurs when a potential resource is applied and contributes to a specific goal or need. Our field interviews revealed that each user uniquely determines value. Some perceive value in practical terms (e.g., fixing my software bug quickly or sharing information so I can prevent causing a similar failure), and others determine value through respectful and courteous interactions aforded through the ISSP’s service skills demonstrating an understanding of their inconvenience from the incident. While we do not assess emotional responses to an IS failure, most users will experience some degree of negative emotions from the disruption, varying from annoyance, frustration, anger to stress. We expect users will perceive a satisfying experience when resources are adequately applied by ISSPs and viewed relevant to restoring their functional (task-support) and non-functional (social) needs that result from the failure (Smith et al., 1999) so they can do their job successfully (Au et al., 2008; Bettencourt et al., 2014; Smith, 2013).

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1. Responsive handling interactions’ influence on recovery satisfaction

Responsive handling interactions reflect users’ perceptions of how the entire resolution service is experienced, specifically the degree to which they view resources are beneficially applied to guide the process. IT staf are expected to interact with users throughout the resolution and requires both their service skills and technical competence in coordinating and prioritising each step efectively to responsively address users’ IT service failures and disrupted work. Displaying an interest to help during service encounters facilitates co-created value by showing commitment to a mutual agreement on the procedures of what is needed and a willingness to do what is required to fix the problem (Echeverri & Skålén, 2011). Demonstrating efort conveys understanding and respect (Smith et al., 1999) and positively influences customers’ perception of how the recovery process is handled (e.g., Xu et al., 2014a). Perceptions of inadequately handling the problem-solving process, such as from a provider’s lacklustre efort, can negatively afect customers’ satisfaction with a firm’s recovery response (Del Rio-Lanza et al., 2013), and can be valuediminishing resulting in dissatisfaction with interaction experiences in on-going service relations contexts (Vafeas et al., 2016).

Our interview data also suggest that handling interactions are more likely to be value co-creating when users perceive that ISSPs appropriately, or fairly, consider their incident relative to other users or the unit’s workload and display efort to address users’ specific IT work-related needs. When service recovery processes are handled responsively, it conveys respect and concern for customers’ needs and restores equity to relationships (e.g., Smith et al., 1999; Tax et al., 1998). As indicated by justice theory, a sense of fairness in handling a service recovery is a predictor of satisfaction and positive outcomes for external customers (e.g., Bansal & Zahedi, 2015; Choi et al., 2016; Kau & Loh, 2006; Xu et al., 2014b). In B2B services, an external customer context where longer-term customer relationships exist somewhat similar to internal IS delivery services, unfairness displayed during interactions was a key value-diminishing factor, reducing a willingness to cooperate and integrate resources (Samaha et al., 2011). For some users, fairness and efort perceptions may enable value by relieving negative emotions (Del Rio-Lanza et al., 2013; A. K. Smith & Bolton, 2002), such as stress over potential missed deadlines.

Therefore, the more responsive handling interactions convey ISSPs are doing their best to help users address their IS failure and disrupted work, the more users will be satisfied with the recovery experience.

## 3.2. Supportive communicating interactions’ influence on recovery satisfaction

Supportive communicating interactions refers to the extent information-sharing and service skills ofered by ISSPs display concern and understanding of a user’s failure problem and needs. Supportive communicating implies exchanges of dialogue, joint listening, and willingness to share information (Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004). ISSP’s information sharing and service skills, such as ofering explanations and apologies, reflect an understanding of how to address the customers’ needs and are viewed as an enabler of a positive service experience (Ballentyne & Varey, 2006; Montoya et al., 2010; Salomonson et al., 2012). This co-creates value by providing a learning mechanism and promoting a sense of partnership (Ballentyne & Varey, 2006; Vafeas et al., 2016; Vargo et al., 2010).

In the SDL view, information is a crucial operant resource used to influence other resources, and information sharing through clear and timely explanations is a critical component of recovery communication. Explaining how failures occurred can help restore a sense of control and understanding, such as how long a recovery may take (Tax et al., 1998). In other service contexts, explanations and apologies are well known to directly or indirectly impact recovery satisfaction (e.g., Bradley & Sparks, 2012; Del Rio-Lanza et al., 2013; Liao, 2007; Roschk & Gelbrich, 2014; Tax et al., 1998). Apologising conveys courtesy and empathy, and for users seeking relational support may be viewed as a form of social or psychological compensation, acknowledging the disruption experienced from a service failure (Tax et al., 1998). Proper apologies imply the quality of user-ISSP communication and can restore a sense of balance in their relationship (Smith et al., 1999). Apologies can set the tone to facilitate better information sharing from users to refine the diagnosis and resolve the problem more quickly.

Data from our interviews also suggest that receiving explanations about the cause of IS failures and displays of courtesy through apologies are important for users satisfaction. Explanations may be particularly valued by internal IS users seeking information about pursuing alternatives for completing their work assignments or informing managers and colleagues of their potential to miss deadlines. Explaining the IS service failure reasons may be valued to prevent future incidents by changing work routines such as security or data backup practices. A user illustrates this in our interviews:

“I would have appreciated more information about the virus as I often do research on blogs that may not have the best security and would like to avoid future problems.”

Inadequate information sharing during communicating interactions can lead to dissatisfaction in the context of on-going relations (Vafeas et al., 2016). A dissatisfied user in our interviews illustrates valuediminishment with a poor-quality communication experience due to an improper explanation and no apologising, saying:

“The service provider claimed it was my fault for using p2p services, and never apologized for cutting of my connection.”

Therefore, the more supportive communicating interactions convey ISSPs are understanding of users’ IT failure and disrupted work, the more users will be satisfied with the recovery experience.

Hypothesis 1b: Supportive communicating interactions are positively related to IS users’ incident recovery satisfaction.

## 3.3. Efective resolving actions’ influence on recovery satisfaction

Efective resolving actions refers to the knowledge and technical skills applied by IS providers that successfully resolve users’ incident so they can achieve their work goals. A recovery outcome is critical in determining a satisfying recovery experience (McCollough et al., 2000). Customer satisfaction is highest when recovery eforts result in a renewed or completely restored service (Roschk & Gelbrich, 2014). Successful incident resolving actions can be viewed as a form of functional value. This is a fast and complete resolution for IS users, facilitating satisfaction by quickly removing negative impacts and preserving users confidence in ISSP’s technical skills. Empirical evidence in a cellular phone service failure context suggests that resolving actions that result in fixing the problem completely and quickly influences users’ satisfaction by reducing negative emotions associated with the disrupted service (Del Rio-Lanza et al., 2013).

Users may provide details to help providers efectively diagnose the cause or assist in coproducing actions such as working with the IS unit to restore their data loss. In B2B service contexts, positive perceptions of collaborative activities to diagnose and solve problems foster clients’ intent to continue collaborations (Aarikka-Stenroos & Jaakkola, 2012), while perceptions of inadequate knowledge and skills lead to feelings of dissatisfaction (Vafeas et al., 2016).

Our interview data suggest that users are more satisfied with the outcome of incident resolution when ISSP’s technical actions fix the incident promptly and completely. Many users appreciated the speed of resolution, as illustrated by this user:

“The amount of time it took to put in the request and have the problem fixed was about 30-45 minutes . . very impressed. Thanks for the speedy response!”

Alternatively, an inadequate and slow response can lead to an unsatisfactory experience and value diminishment as one user expressed:

“I am still waiting for someone to get back with me regarding my inability to connect to the SPSS application from my personal laptop. That has been about 4 weeks ago.”

IS users also appreciate ISSP’s actions to completely restore their problem to the level of hardware or software functions they had before the incident. For example, users who lose data will be more satisfied when they promptly get all, not a portion, of the data back. Several users illustrate the value-diminishing (enabling) role of recovery level in influencing satisfaction. One stated:

[I felt unsatisfied] “That the files were still on the old drive that was copied to a flash drive that I could copy onto my laptop. Would have preferred the files to be put back on the laptop by the provider support.”

Another said:

“Didn’t realize how much would be lost when the CPU was erased. Still without shortcuts and other apps that simplify my work”.

Therefore, the more incident recovery knowledge and technical skills are efectively applied and successfully restore users’ IS function or service, the more users will be satisfied with the recovery experience:

Hypothesis 1c: Efective resolving actions are positively related to IS users’ incident recovery satisfaction.

## 3.4. Substitution efects of responsive handling interactions and supportive communicating interactions

Beyond direct efects on recovery satisfaction, we examine the interplay of responsive handling and supportive communicating interactions, and efective resolving actions. Our interview data suggests that even if users are less satisfied with the outcome of resolving actions, ISSPs have an opportunity to compensate by leveraging their knowledge and skills while communicating and managing the resolution, influencing recovery satisfaction. For example, one user who was only “somewhat satisfied with the response time” suggested s/he “would like to have received updates and information about solutions.” This implies that for some users, responsive handling interactions conveying efort throughout the incident recovery or supportive communicating interactions sharing helpful information may substitute for a less successful resolution’s influence on recovery satisfaction.

This assumption is consistent with prior service recovery research indicating when customers are disappointed with the outcome of a recovery service, it is possible that applying other resources mitigates their negative reactions and influences satisfaction (Bitner et al., 1990; Edvardsson et al., 2011; Mattila, 2001). In these cases, displaying an understanding of customers needs, such as ofering meaningful apologies and explanations or displays of efort are important contributors to customers being more satisfied. This is the case even when the resolution is not optimal, as these skills convey to customers a sense of perceived control and understanding and shape perceptions of the experience (Edvardsson et al., 2011). Joireman et al. (2013) further find that customers are more forgiving of a failed recovery outcome when interactions convey a provider’s positive motives, i.e., an intent to help.

Sometimes a successful incident resolution is beyond the ISSP’s control, while accessing and conveying his/her knowledge and service skills is more readily available to influence users’ satisfaction. We ofer an example to illustrate the compensatory role of responsive handling interactions. If a user’s tablet is so old that it cannot be upgraded with a required software release causing it to be useless, there is likely no immediate technical fix. The old device will have to be replaced with an updated model, which may take time to achieve, preventing the user from immediately having a solution precisely like the one s/he had before the required software update. ISSPs can ofer options such as cloud-based access to apps and some data via the user’s smartphone to substitute. Even though the solution may not ofer all the functionality needed, some users will appreciate the ISSP’s persistent eforts and fairness to help them provide a “stop-gap” solution, minimising their disappointment over the delay. Alternatively, when handling skills do not convey helpful intent, users will feel more dissatisfied, as illustrated by this user in our interviews:

“I am dissatisfied that the problem has not been solved and there was no follow-up to see it through to correction.” 22

Based on a service-dominant view and past research, ISSPs who apply their knowledge and service skills in handling the recovery relevant to solving users’ incident can compensate (substitute) for the lack of a preferred resolving outcome or make their efective resolving actions less important in influencing recovery satisfaction. Therefore, we propose that the ISSP’s responsive handling interactions and efective resolving actions function in a compensatory (substitutive) manner such that responsive handling interactions can mitigate limitations in efective resolving actions outcomes:

Hypothesis 2a: Responsive handling interactions will moderate the efect of efective resolving actions outcomes on recovery satisfaction, such that efective resolving actions will have a weaker efect on recovery satisfaction when responsive handling interactions are high (and a stronger efect when responsive handling interactions are low).

Similarly, we suggest that users may accept less optimal recovery outcomes with supportive communicating interactions. For example, if a laptop’s hard drive “crashes” causing data loss and the user did not maintain back-ups, some users may feel stressed, even embarrassed. Ofering an apology that shows concern and understanding of the user’s problem can be appreciated (e.g., “I am so sorry. This could happen to anyone”), and ofering explanations gives users a better understanding of why all their data cannot be retrieved. While users prefer to get all their data back, the positive intent conveyed by the ISSP can attenuate their disappointment with the outcome, at least to some degree.

In these cases, the “cues” provided by a meaningful apology and relevant explanations of the problem can be appreciated by users, as these skills show providers are understanding of their disruption and may help create a sense of control of the situation. Thus, we propose that the ISSP’s supportive communicating interactions and efective resolving actions function in a compensatory (substitutive) manner such that supportive communicating interactions can mitigate limitations in efective resolving actions outcomes:

Hypothesis 2b: Supportive communicating interactions will moderate the efect of efectiveresolving actions outcomes on recovery satisfaction, such that efective resolving actionswill have a weaker efect on recovery satisfaction when supportive communicatinginteractions are high (and a stronger efect when supportive communicating interactionsare low).

## 3.5. IS incident recovery service value co-creation outcomes

In our organisational context, incident recovery satisfaction, overall service satisfaction and service quality can be indicators of co-created value (e.g., Dong et al., 2008; A. K. Smith & Bolton, 2002; Ostrom et al., 2010; Roggeveen et al., 2012; Sun et al., 2012; Van et al., 2014).

Incident recovery satisfaction is the extent that a user is satisfied with a specific recovery service experience and outcome. Users will evaluate the quality and adequacy of technical and service skills and other resources applied during interactions and resolving actions with IS staf and formulate a level of incident recovery satisfaction based on the benefits (value) facilitated. In this way, recovery satisfaction is a one-time transactional evaluation that contributes to the overall service-value picture (Maxham & Netemeyer, 2002). In contrast, overall service satisfaction and service quality are perceptions of multiple types of experiences with the IS unit. As value cocreation is a holistic and temporal process in the ongoing user-IS services relationship, we include both incident-level and unit-level assessments as transactional and holistic value indicators as suggested when applying an SDL view (e.g., Helkkula et al., 2012).

With organisation supported IS, users expect a level of IS service they can rely on to efectively do their jobs (Sun et al., 2012; Van den Hoof & de Winter, 2011). Most IS units depend on users’ views of satisfaction and quality of their IS services as indicators of overall success. While unique constructs, IS service quality and IS service satisfaction represent users’ unit-level, holistic perceptions of past, present, and expected exchanges with ISSPs resulting in mutual benefits (Chandler & Vargo, 2011; Vargo & Lusch, 2004). Examples include planning, developing, implementing, maintaining IT artefacts and data assets (Sun et al., 2012), and resolving IS incidents. The more users are satisfied with a collaborative recovery, the greater their future intent is to participate in future value co-creating interactions (Dong et al., 2008).

## 3.6. Recovery satisfaction’s influence on perceived IS service quality

IS service quality represents users’ assessments of experiences with all IS unit services and includes interactions with an ISSP during a specific service. This holistic assessment includes perceptions of service reliability, responsiveness, competence, assurance, and empathy (Kettinger & Lee, 1994, 1997). Because an incident interrupts a user’s service expectations, it can disrupt the user-IS service value-creating dynamic (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Smith, 2013) and cause a perception of inferior quality, especially for failures not attributed to users. Therefore, users’ views of IS service quality could also be lower (Goode et al., 2017). Resolving IS incidents is a unique service requiring diferent resource exchanges and levels of interaction between users and ISSPs than routine or businessrelated interactions. The extent to which an unexpected IT failure event potentially diminishes (preserves) overall IS service quality is indicated by users’ perceptions of the appropriateness of resources applied during interactions and the success of resolving actions as measured by incident recovery satisfaction.

In a collaborative service environment, the quality of interactions and service processes weigh significantly in customers’ assessment of the experience (e.g., Aarikka-Stenroos & Jaakkola, 2012; Vafeas et al., 2016). Service skills such as responsiveness, empathy, information sharing, and technical competencies are value-driving resources for IS users. When their interactions with ISSPs result in a satisfying recovery experience, they are likely to continue to assess the overall IS service quality more favourably. The positive impact of the quality and expertise applied in resolving actions in preserving confidence in the IS unit is illustrated by a satisfied user in our interviews:

“The fact that they are experts in the field and know exactly what the problem is without hesitation. I am very satisfied and very confident in their expertise in computer knowledge.”

A fast, fair, and informative recovery restores trust (Tax et al., 1998) and a sense that the IS unit has competent, knowledgeable staf and reliable service capabilities. Users will continue to believe that the IS unit is capable of delivering quality IS services, and in future service encounters, they will be treated empathetically and fairly. In essence, a positively perceived incident resolution service restores equity in the user-IS unit relationship (Smith et al., 1999), thus sustaining a user’s perception of holistic service value. Therefore:

Hypothesis 3: Users’ satisfaction with an individual IS incident recovery service is positively related to their overall perception of IS service quality with the IS unit.

## 3.7. Recovery satisfaction’s influence on overall IS satisfaction

SDL emphasises the dynamic process of value cocreation. Overall satisfaction is a fundamental dynamic value indicator of a customer’s continuing attitude towards a service provider (Parasurman et al., 1994) and is updated after each service encounter (Jones & Suh, 2000; Maxham & Netemeyer, 2002; Van et al., 2014). We define overall IS service satisfaction as the level of users’ afective responses to needs fulfilment through all prior types of experiences interacting with IS services, including the most recent incident recovery (Oliver, 1993, 1996; Sun et al., 2012).

The duration of the customer-provider relationship and service type may influence the link between incident recovery satisfaction and overall satisfaction perceptions. Maxham and Netemeyer (2002) find a positive link among established banking customers, but not for new home construction firm customers, suggesting that in less established relationships customers weigh recovery satisfaction less in deriving overall satisfaction perceptions. As such, it is a critical summative indicator of customer relationships at any point in time (Olsen & Johnson, 2003). Given its additive nature, we expect that when users experience a satisfying incident recovery service, it will positively contribute to their holistic evaluations of IS service satisfaction. Thus, in the context of on-going IS unitusers collaborative relations, we expect:

Hypothesis 4: Users’ satisfaction with an individual IS incident recovery service is positively related to their overall perception of satisfaction with the IS unit.

The positive influence of service quality on overall service satisfaction is well known (e.g., Cronin et al., 2000; Kettinger & Lee, 1994; Sun et al., 2012). We do not hypothesise this relationship but include it in our research model for theoretical completeness.

## 4. Research methods

We use field interviews and surveys with IS users in internal IS delivery settings who experienced a recent operational IT/IS failure (incident), prompting them to contact the help desk. While IS services can be delivered from multiple service providers, including external/outsourced help desks and informal sources, we focus on the internal organisational entities formally empowered to provide services to a specific IS user.

Because of the sparse research on internal IS service recovery and the importance of understanding the unique context, we conducted a precursory study including field interviews and scale development of our three antecedent constructs. Earlier, we illustrated how qualitative data from exploratory interviews informed our theoretical development. Below, we explain how the qualitative data informed our main empirical study through the selection of first-order dimensions of responsive handling interactions, supportive communicating interactions, and efective resolving actions. We then explain our measures development and validation procedures for our antecedents, followed by the methodology for our Main Study to test our research model. Appendix C provides the details of our precursory work.

## 4.1. Precursory study

## 4.1.1. Antecedents dimension identification method and procedure

We interviewed fifteen faculty members, staf, and doctoral students at the business school of a large U.S. research university who had all been users for more than one year. IS usage ranged from purely Internet and infrastructure access to administrative transactions dependent on IS services to complete their jobs. Participants were asked to recall their service recovery experience during interactions with the IS unit. Open-ended questions included: How do you feel when such a problem occurs? What are the factors that will influence your incident recovery satisfaction? and How many points of interaction did you have with the ISSP? Users’ described both positive and negative experiences from which we inferred value-creating elements.

We applied an inductive approach to identify pertinent dimensions, grouped related ones into each higher-order incident recovery factor and determined their fit. We analysed the interview notes using a statement analysis technique (Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005) by considering each resource element’s nature and frequency, which revealed promptness, level (completeness), efort, fairness, explanations, and apologising as potential dimensions. We then deductively confirmed these dimensions by comparing them to those identified in past service recovery literature. Table 3 presents the results of our content analysis. While most IS users’ comments refer to several recovery practices, the sample quotes reflect specific resources (dimensions).

Following the process outlined above, dimensions were chosen as they (1) were particularly relevant to IS users in our interviews, (2) have received empirical support in the service recovery (e.g., Liao, 2007; Smith et al., 1999; Tax et al., 1998) and the services literature (e.g., Echeverri & Skålén, 2011; Vafeas et al., 2016), (3) can be acted upon by managers, and (4) may be incorporated in quantitative methods to address our research questions. Completeness or level of resolving the problem was not a dimension identified in prior literature; however, our qualitative data suggested it is a value-enabling factor. We include this new dimension as a resolving action.

## 4.1.2. Instrument development and measurement

Reflective items were developed to measure each dimension. Most measures were adopted from prior service recovery work. New items were developed for recovery level as there were no existing scales, and for apologising as existing scales were deemed incomplete for our context. Measures for recovery explanation (Kau & Loh,

2006), fairness (Carr, 2007), speed (J. S. Smith & Karwan, 2010), and efort (Mohr & Bitner, 1995) were developed from similar constructs found in the literature and field interviews as discussed earlier.

We followed the methodology proposed by Lewis et al. (2005) to develop new items for apology and recovery level. We constructed an instrument by converting statements in the constructs’ domains into items. Although a minimum of three items per construct is recommended for adequate internal consistency (Cook et al., 1981), it is wiser to include an additional item for potentially deleting a problematic item (Robinson, 2018). Items are based on a 7-point Likert agreement scale. Demographic variables (gender, age, job role, tenure), problem severity, and attribution were included as controls. The instrument was pre-tested with 10 IS users, revised and re-tested with five MIS Ph.D. students for further instrument purification and adjusted based on comments. Two IS scholars screened the items to assess the degree to which each item adequately represents the construct’s content domain (See Appendix D, Table D1).

## 4.2. Main study

## 4.2.1. Sample and data collection

A field study was conducted in a leading U.S. financial services company. The company has a dedicated technology helpdesk function responsible for interacting with users and maintaining a smooth delivery of IS services. Following an IS service failure, users contacted the internal technology helpdesk to initiate an IS incident recovery. During the recovery, users could interact with the local IS unit team member(s) assigned to resolve the incident. A list was assembled of 1031 employees who had an IS service incident/ failure during the most recent two-month period and contacted the technology helpdesk to fix their problem. Two months were chosen as the sampling time frame to minimise recall bias. An invitation to participate in the study was emailed to these employees, and a reminder was sent after a week. Overall, 596 employees clicked on the survey link, and 270 completed the survey resulting in a 26% response rate.

To test for nonresponse bias, we compared the demographic characteristics from responses received before the reminder email (i.e., the first round of questionnaires) and those received after the reminder email (i.e., a second round of questionnaires), with the assumption that later respondents demonstrate characteristics similar to non-respondents. We found no systematic diferences between the two groups, suggesting that nonresponse bias may not be an issue (Armstrong & Overton, 1977). The respondents demographic data are displayed in Table 5.

## 4.2.2. Measures

The same measures of the IS service recovery constructs and corresponding dimensions developed in our precursory work described above were used. We incorporated items in the survey (Appendix D) to measure incident recovery satisfaction (incident level), IS service quality (unit level), and overall satisfaction (unit-level). Consistent with one stream of modelling IS service quality (Kettinger et al., 2009), IS service quality was modelled as a second-order reflective, four first-order reflective, multidimensional construct. All other constructs were modelled as unidimensional and reflective. We slightly modified the survey instrument to fit the main study’s context of a financial services firm. All measurement was made based on individual assessments shortly after a service incident had occurred.

## 4.2.3. Control variables

To help rule out alternative explanations of IS incident recovery satisfaction and overall IS satisfaction in internal IS delivery settings, we included several factors as control variables established in the IS and service recovery literature. The collaborative and relational nature of IS service delivery and the high degree of social interaction between IS users and IS unit team members may also indicate that relationship quality influences recovery satisfaction (Hess et al., 2003) and overall satisfaction (Sun et al., 2012). To control for the role of these relational factors, we included cognitive capital and relational capital (relationship quality indicators) as control variables. Cognitive capital refers to the capabilities for shared representations, interpretations, and systems of meaning among employees; it reflects the extent to which IS users and ISSPs share a common perspective or understanding. Relational capital reflects the nature of employees’ connections, including trust and trustworthiness, norms, obligations, and identification. Failure severity and failure attribution (Smith et al., 1999) were included as incidentrelated variables. Demographics and job-related factors (gender, age, job role, and tenure) were also included.

## 5.3. Hypotheses testing

## 5.3.1. Efects of recovery components

We theorised that the three recovery components would facilitate users’ value creation and positively influence recovery satisfaction (H1a, H1b, H1c). Responsive handling interactions and efective resolving actions have significant efects on recovery satisfaction (H1a, H1c supported), explaining 86% of the variance in IS incident recovery satisfaction. Supportive communicating interactions had no significant efect on recovery satisfaction (H1b not supported).

## 5.3.2. Substitution efects of responsive handling and supportive communicating interactions

We theorised that the efect of efective resolving actions outcomes on recovery satisfaction would lessen with an increase in responsive handling interactions. As expected, responsive handling interactions weaken the efect of efective resolving actions on IS incident recovery satisfaction (H2a supported), suggesting this component can substitute (compensate) for the efect of less successful efective resolving actions on incident recovery satisfaction (see Figure 3 below). We predicted a similar role of supportive communicating interactions on resolving actions; however, supportive communicating interactions did not have a moderating efect on the relationship between efective resolving actions outcomes and recovery satisfaction (H2b not supported).

## 5.3.3. Efects of recovery satisfaction

We proposed that IS service quality (H3) and overall IS satisfaction with the IS unit (H4) would be positively influenced by recovery satisfaction. As expected, recovery satisfaction has a significant direct efect on IS service quality (H3 supported), explaining 47% of its variance, and has a significant efect on overall satisfaction (H4 supported). While not hypothesised, the efect of IS service quality on overall satisfaction was significant (Figure 2), replicating previous research findings. Together, recovery satisfaction and IS service quality explain 73% of overall satisfaction’s variance.

## 5.4.2. Robustness checks

Appendix F provides the details of our robustness checks. Since our sample is cross-sectional, the direction of causality may be a concern. For example, we hypothesised that recovery satisfaction results from evaluating the three recovery components; however, an inverse direction of this relationship is possible in which recovery satisfaction influences the three recovery components. We acknowledge that causality cannot be established from a cross-sectional study. Nevertheless, we compared our research model M2 with two rival, alternative models: 1) a baseline model M0 with direct paths between the three recovery components and service quality and overall IS satisfaction, i.e., IS recovery satisfaction is removed from the model, and 2) a model M1 where incident recovery satisfaction is added to the baseline model as an antecedent of the three recovery components, i.e., incident recovery satisfaction as an exogenous construct. Our research model M2 had the highest GoF value among the three models (0.72 compared to 0.70 for M0 and 0.69 for M1).

## 6. Discussion

Motivated by gaps in IS service recovery research, this study aims to advance our understanding of critical components of an incident resolution service in the internal IS delivery setting that contribute to IS users recovery satisfaction and value outcomes important to managers. Drawing on Service-Dominant Logic, exploratory interview insights, and prior literature, we theoretically develop hypotheses that ISSPs’ knowledge, technical and service skills applied in responsive handling interactions, supportive communicating interactions, and efective resolving actions directly influence users’ incident recovery satisfaction (H1a, H1b, and H1c), and that handling and communicating components moderate resolving actions afecting recovery satisfaction (H2a and H2b). We also proposed that recovery satisfaction positively contributes to overall IS services quality (H3) and IS service satisfaction (H4).

Overall, we found convergence among the subjective interviews’ findings and the objective main study testing our hypotheses for the importance of efective resolving actions and responsive handling interactions’ influence on recovery satisfaction. Results indicate that responsive handling interactions (H1a) and efective resolving actions (H1c) are positively related to recovery satisfaction, and responsive handling interactions negatively moderate (i.e., substitute) efective resolving actions’ influence on recovery satisfaction (H2a). As predicted, incident recovery satisfaction is positively related to IS service quality (H3) and IS service satisfaction (H4).

These positive findings support the fundamental SDL premise that proper knowledge, technical, and service skills exchanged during a service encounter enable mutual benefits and recurring value cocreation if they are beneficially applied. Our findings indicate that efective resolving actions successfully fixing the problem is most important to users, not just promptly but also restoring a user’s IT function to its pre-incident level. Conveying efort and fairness during interactions while handling the service are also influential for recovery satisfaction and play an important role.

The efect of responsive handling interactions on the link between efective resolving actions outcomes and recovery satisfaction recognises contingency when the success of actions difers in collaborative recoveries. This finding suggests the feasibility of leveraging service skills to influence satisfaction to compensate in cases when users are less satisfied with the outcome. This is consistent with the view that the beneficial role of resources applied is contextual, “in the moment of use” (Chandler & Vargo, 2011). Understanding that demonstrating efort and fairness to help users resolve their unique IS incident can attenuate their negative reactions in the case of a less than optimal fix, and cue IS providers that an adaptive response is needed.

Our findings indicate that users’ incident recovery satisfaction plays a role in contributing to users’ views of overall IS unit satisfaction and service quality. This suggests that by focusing on providers’ actions to ensure a successful outcome and helpful interactions with users while handling the recovery, it is possible to overcome a disruptive event and preserve an on-going co-creation dynamic between users and IT services staf. Examining these holistic outcomes is consistent with a service-dominant perspective that the value cocreation process is inherently dynamic, relational, and emerges beyond transactional contacts. As such, we respond to calls to assess both transactional and holistic views as indicators of individuals’ service experience and intent to collaborate in the future (e.g., Gelbrich & Roschk, 2011; Helkkula et al., 2012).

We attempt to capture the dynamic and relational nature of value co-creation (diminishment) by simultaneously assessing users’ experience with one encounter (incident recovery satisfaction) along with their recollection of past experiences of varied encounter types with various IS unit staf (service quality and overall IS satisfaction). In doing so, we also begin to address calls for multi-level research in service recovery to examine how individuals’ actions may impact collective constructs (Van Vaerenbergh & Orsingher, 2016). Because Help Desk staf are an important “face” of the IS unit, individual IS providers’ actions and interactions can influence users’ perceptions of the overall IS unit (measured as overall satisfaction and service quality). As we only capture users’ assessment at one point in time and given possible recency efects, our measure could over-estimate the positive link of recovery satisfaction to overall satisfaction and service quality over time. However, our findings provide initial insights that recovery satisfaction is an important contributor to holistic views of the IS unit’s services. Future studies can build on this finding to enhance managers’ strategic guidance, such as assessing how recovery satisfaction from a single recovery incident impacts overall IS satisfaction and service quality over time. Also, repeated temporal assessments incorporating the frequency and types of resource exchanges with the IS unit, multiple service recovery incidents, and other value indicators such as intent to collaborate (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017) will more fully capture the value co-creation dynamic.

Unexpectedly, supportive communicating interactions were not significantly related to recovery satisfaction (H1b) and did not have a moderating efect on efective resolving actions (H2b). These main study findings diverge from the qualitative data. While essential to co-create value in an external recovery service, supportive communicating interactions is not suficient as a recovery component to influence satisfaction in IS internal contexts. Prior research has found mixed results with supportive communicating interactions. Liao (2007) finds service failure explanations were not valued in complaint handling contexts and suggests customers view them as a waste of time once a problem is fairly and quickly resolved or view the explanation as avoiding responsibility. It is also possible that the quality and content of apologies and explanations did not align with IS users’ needs (Edvardsson et al., 2011). Interactional dynamics may diminish value if customers expect supportive communicating interactions to be genuine, easy to understand, and relevant to their needs, but instead they receive a perfunctory, non-empathetic apology or an explanation that is time-consuming, too technical, or insuficient to address what is important to them (Echeverri & Skålén, 2011; Vafeas et al., 2016). Finally, apologies and explanations may be hygiene factors, meaning users may expect them to be ofered, as was the case for some users in our interviews, but they do not influence satisfaction when ofered (Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2018).

Instead of removing the supportive communicating interactions construct, future research might refine the measurement or expand it to draw out unique internal IS interaction communication contexts. We chose explanations and apologies from both users’ explicit and implicit narratives and because they are well-studied as factors influencing recovery outcomes. Given the salience of information as a resource in the SDL view, and mentioned by users in our interviews, information sharing may be a possible future dimension. Or, exploring the supportive communicating interactions construct from a dialogical perspective may be more fruitful. Researchers using text mining may closely observe conversations between users and IS providers to identify less meaningful communication. Managers might then better engineer dialogues during interactions to help ensure user benefit.

## 6.1. Research implications

This study ofers three important theoretical contributions. First, our work contributes to the IS literature on operational IT failures and service recovery by introducing SDL as a novel alternative theoretical lens to facilitate beneficial outcomes following a service failure. By positioning recovery interactions and actions in a service-dominant view, we provide an interactional perspective to explore IS service failure recovery beyond the well-studied fairness (justice), attributions, and expectations views previously applied (e.g., Bansal & Zahedi, 2015; Choi et al., 2016; Goode et al., 2017). While these views contribute to service recovery theory and practice in data breach contexts where the resource contribution is one-way from service provider to customers in transactional relationships, they are insuficient to pose and position unique constructs in a collaborative service recovery with mutually derived benefits. In contrast, we view IS users (customers) as service recovery participants in on-going work relationships. The empirical development of three recovery components, born from a customer perspective, ofers critical insights and actionable guidance for managers on how properly applied knowledge and skills exchanged in specific interactions and actions afect users’ recovery experience and satisfaction.

SDL’s broad concepts required a multi-step approach to develop our model and explain how resources are beneficially exchanged to influence value co-creation. Like past researchers, our findings suggest that actions can be objectively assessed, but subjective methods are particularly useful for uncovering interactional elements (Echeverri & Skålén, 2011; Vafeas et al., 2016). The inclusion of dyadic perspectives and relevant mid-level theories can help further explain the constructs’ links and enhance theoretical development (Vargo & Lusch, 2016). Mechanisms that explain how beneficially applied resources facilitate value co-creation and satisfaction in collaborative contexts should be further explored, such as the mediating role of goal and resource alignment (Winkler & Wulf, 2019) or intent to help (Joireman et al., 2013). Alternatively, a study might theoretically tie in Addas and Pinsonneault (2015) work on IT-related interruptions, exploring users’ work disruptions from IS failures. SDL’s flexibility to “zoom in or out” with a multi-actor depiction of resource integrators allows for varying levels of exploration and beneficiaries views; this is a particular strength that should be leveraged in future IS research.

Second, we develop the novel concept of incident recovery satisfaction as a contributor to overall IS-unit service value and show that a satisfying IS incident recovery helps avoid diminishing overall IS satisfaction and service quality. As such, we build on prior IS research in internal IS service relationship contexts that have drawn on relational concepts of servicedominant views to explain overall IS satisfaction and value outcomes (e.g., Sun et al., 2012). While these works focus on users’ satisfaction across all IS service oferings, our study demonstrates the important contributing role of a specific IS service, incident resolution, in facilitating users’ overall IS value views.

Third, our study contributes to emerging servicedominant research in IS by empirically applying it to the IS service failure domain. We complement recent IS research that has examined the integration of operant and operand resources as cloud service capabilities’ influencing firm value creation (Kathuria et al., 2018) and Winkler and Wulf's (2019) work demonstrating the mechanisms through which firm-level outcomes evolve from service capabilities of IS service management to facilitate and co-create value. Our study provides an individual-level illustration of how a service-dominant perspective may be applied in a joint customer-provider recovery service and provides a validation of what resources may be beneficially exchanged in contexts of on-going relationships. In particular, given our focus on identifying the information, eforts, and technical and service skills of IS staf exchanged with users during interactions and resolving actions as variables relevant to the operational IS failure recovery domain, we believe that our study succeeds in presenting a nuanced version of resource integration of relevance to IS scholarship and practice.

## 6.2. Practical implications

For IS managers, feedback is a component of incident resolution and a key metric assessed is customer satisfaction. Our findings inform managers how to facilitate user satisfaction by encouraging help desk staf and other providers to consider ways to fully restore a user’s problem in addition to “time to resolution” (speed) and to proactively demonstrate a willingness to help through efort and fairness while handling the incident recovery process. Providers should also understand how they can adapt or leverage their service skills, especially in a delayed and less than complete resolution. To facilitate on-going collaboration and mutual benefits, managers should consider recovery satisfaction as an additional critical contributor to their overall service quality and customer satisfaction metrics and ensure IS providers understand that their knowledge, eforts, and technical skills used in resolving each incident also contribute to the IS unit’s value outcomes.

Our qualitative data reveal the importance of information to users. Providers should recognise that some users also want updates and information about solutions in addition to explanations of the problem. Users’ desire for frequent updates or information may conflict with ISSPs’ value-needs for a fast, technical fix at the expense of more proactive handling and communicating, given their need to balance workload and priorities. In this case, staf should explain “their side” so users will have a better understanding. Finally, users also need to know how to be “good” resource integrators and be encouraged to share timely, relevant information or change their future IT usage practices. Training, feedback, and clear guidelines for addressing these priorities may better assist users and providers.

## 6.3. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, crosssectional research designs pose limitations concerning claims of causality. By including several controls found to influence recovery satisfaction (failure severity, attribution) and overall satisfaction (cognitive and relational social capital) as well as robustness checks with alternative models, we attempted to rule out other explanations. However, due to methodological limitations, causality cannot be tested and directional reasoning is based solely on theoretical arguments with these alternative models (Markus & Rowe, 2018). Robustness checks with controls give only indications about possible cross-sectional associations. Second, incident recovery satisfaction fosters but does not fully explain all variance in the additive overall satisfaction construct (Maxham & Netemeyer, 2002). In addition to IS service quality, other possible predictors may include user-provider perceptional alignment or misalignment on IS service quality factors (Benlian, 2013), relational factors (Sun et al., 2012), perceived fairness and quality of interactions, or trust over time (e.g., Ballentyne & Varey, 2006; Gelbrich & Roschk, 2011; Samaha et al. 2011: Zhao et al. 2012) Third. the IS service recovery antecedents were validated using data collected from a research university, whereas the complete model was validated using data from a U.S. financial service company. While we uniquely tried to study this phenomenon in multiple industries, future research might investigate new industries and across alternative samples of IS users to further strengthen the external validity.

## 6.4. Future research

Our study reveals intriguing research questions to explore concerning IT failure service recovery and resource integration: What are drivers of interactions and resolving actions for a successful outcome? What is the role of IT as an operand resource in service recovery in internal settings? What other operant resources at the individual or IT service system-level influence value co-creation? How do IT providers view interactions with users during the service? Does a joint recovery provide the IS unit opportunities to identify training needs (for both users and staf) and refine their incident management and response, and how do these refinements impact value cocreation outcomes?

As SDL recognises technology may be both an operant and operand resource (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015), research could explore how IT directly or indirectly influences time to resolution using emerging technologies like artificial intelligence (e.g., chatbots and live chats). SDL highlights the interdependence of actors at varying levels of the service system. A greater understanding is needed of how individual providers’ capabilities and personalities influence users’ satisfaction and value co-creation, such as the ability to sense emotions and ask questions to provide adaptive responses (A. K. Smith & Bolton, 2002). Other contextual variables may be explored, such as the number of past failure events (found to negatively interact with the problemsolving dimension of service recovery performance, Liao, 2007), the type of IS failure (e.g., personal vs. institutional), degree of dependency a user has on the IT (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015), and sources of IS provision (in-house vs. outsourced or in the cloud). Finally, researchers should measure this phenomenon longitudinally with panel or time series techniques to better understand the long-term causal relationships proposed by our incident level and unit-level model.

## 6.5. Concluding comment

An operational IS service incident response’s key objective is to mitigate negative impacts and enable value for both IS customers and IS units. There is much to learn about achieving this objective in both internal and external settings. Our study provides a foundation for future work, examining these topics in a service and customer-oriented way.

We compared our research model $\mathbf { M } _ { 2 }$ with two rival, alternative models: 1) a baseline model $\mathrm { M } _ { 0 }$ with direct paths between the three recovery components and service quality and overall IS satisfaction, i.e., IS recovery satisfaction is removed from the model, and 2) a model $\mathrm { M } _ { 1 }$ where incident recovery satisfaction is added to the

baseline model as an antecedent of the three recovery components, i.e., incident recovery satisfaction as exogenous construct (Table 9). We used several criteria to compare the models. Since $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ and adjusted $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ values for the dependent variable are almost identical among the models, we compared the models on several other fit indices, e.g., the goodness-of-fit (GoF) index (Tenenhaus et al., 2005; Henseler & Sarstedt, 2013). While GoF does not separate valid models from invalid ones, its application makes sense for some models (Henseler & Sarstedt, 2013). Our research model M had the highest GoF value among the three models (0.72 compared to 0.70 for $\mathrm { { M } } _ { 0 }$ and 0.69 for $\mathbf { M } _ { 1 } )$ ).

Furthermore, Sharma et al. (2019) suggest that PLS criteria of $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } ,$ adjusted $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } ,$ , and GoF cannot be relied on to appropriately select among models; they strongly favour asymptotically consistent criteria, especially Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and the Geweke-Meese criterion (GM), for their high model selection accuracy. We computed BIC, GM, Hannan & Quinn’s criterion (HQ), and McQuarrie & Tsai’s corrected HQ criterion (HQc) for the three models. A model with the smallest value on these criteria should be selected (Sharma et al., 2019). The values of these four criteria were the lowest for our current research model $\mathbf { M } _ { 2 }$ (see Table F1).
