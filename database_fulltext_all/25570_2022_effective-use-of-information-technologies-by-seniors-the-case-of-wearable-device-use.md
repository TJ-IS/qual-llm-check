# Efective use of information technologies by seniors: the case of wearable device use

Mohamed Abouzahraa<sup>a</sup> and Maryam Ghasemaghaeib<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>College of Business, California State University, Sea Side, USA; <sup>b</sup>McMaster University DeGroote School of Business, Hamilton, Ontario, Canada

## ABSTRACT

Healthcare is an area that has benefitted from the developments in wearable device technology. Seniors, who usually sufer from multiple comorbidities, are among the target users of these devices, and research has shown potential health benefits for seniors when they use these devices efectively. However, the adoption rate of wearable devices is low, especially among seniors, preventing the full utilisation of their data in healthcare. In this study, we interviewed forty-four seniors across North America and collected data from their wearable devices to develop a theoretical afordance network-based model to explain seniors’ efective use of wearable devices. Our model indicates that despite the apparent simplicity of wearable devices, they have multiple afordances that help seniors achieve several goals, including activity monitoring, activity planning, and activity improvement. Furthermore, we identified factors that enable seniors to actualise the afordances of wearable devices and achieve their goals. The results of this study suggest a strong relationship between seniors’ mental and physical capabilities and their willingness to use and benefit from wearable devices. We join other researchers in their call for a contextual study on consumer technology use.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems (IS) use is among the most studied areas in the field of IS. The study of IS use began in the 1980s (Davis, 1989) and has been remained active to this day. Research in IS use has drawn much attention because IS implementations typically require notable investment and have the potential to create value for both organisations and individuals. To justify the investment in and reap the benefits of IS, it must be used for an extended period of time (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Venkatesh et al., 2011). However, as many researchers have noted (e.g., Benbasat & Barki, 2007; Burton-Jones & Grange, 2012; Seddon, 1997), reaping the benefits of IS requires not only the use but the efective use of IS.

While several studies have considered efective IS use (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007; Burton-Jones & Grange, 2012; Burton-Jones & Volkof, 2017; Surbakti et al., 2020), most focused on its business and organisational contexts, while only few studies have focused on the efective use of IS at the individual level (Savoli et al., 2020; Serrano & Karahanna, 2016), and non have considered the efective use of consumer or personal IS. Previous research showed that the factors influencing consumer IS use can be significantly diferent from those influencing business IS use. For example, Venkatesh developed a theory for the efective use of business IS (Venkatesh et al., 2003) and later modified it to the context of consumer technology (Venkatesh et al.,

2012); these two theories are notably diferent in their explanation of IS use. Understanding the efective use of consumer IS is now of great importance given that consumer IS are often integrated with organisational and business IS, and the efective use of business IS depends on the efective use of consumer IS. Hence, in this study, we extended existing work on efective IS use to the domain of consumer/personal IS. This extension will improve our understanding of individual use behaviour and its impact on organisational/business IS and may lead to the development of new theories that focus on the efective use of consumer technology.

In this study, we focused on seniors’ use of wearable devices (WDs). We define a WD as a lightweight device that can be attached to the body to measure activity (such as steps and calories) and/or vital signs (such as heart rate and blood pressure). These devices are usually connected to an application to assess their measurements and provide feedback to users and often their care providers. We consider the efective use of WDs by seniors, which reflects how seniors use the features ofered by WDs to achieve their activity improvement goals. This definition of efective WD use is diferent than that of use behaviour often presented in the literature, which focuses on simply wearing WDs without considering its efect on achieving users’ goals.

Even with the growing body of research focusing on technology use, as a user group, seniors represent a small percentage of this research despite the importance of studying this user group and the abundance of technologies developed to improve seniors quality of life. This importance is now even more significant with the spread of the COVID-19 pandemic and with seniors identified as the most vulnerable population to this pandemic (Lee et al., 2020). WDs can be used to track seniors’ activity and vital signs and communicate these signs to their care providers (Kim et al., 2019). Care providers can then follow up on those seniors. Integrating WDs with healthcare systems can reduce pressure on healthcare systems (Popescu, 2014) and provide a better followup strategy for senior patients. Furthermore, research has shown that WDs can encourage activity and exercise and hence improve health outcomes (e.g., Bravata et al., 2007a). This improvement applies to both young and senior users(Abouzahra & Ghasemaghaei, 2020; Lin et al., 2018).

To design beneficial WDs for seniors and develop efective strategies, integrating WDs with seniors healthcare, we must improve our understanding of the contextual and technological factors that influence seniors’ use of WDs. Hence, we focus on seniors efective use of WDs.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Efective use of information systems

The IS literature has focused on IS adoption and use since its inception (Taylor & Todd, 1995). The logic behind this focus is that the eficacy of an information system does not automatically imply its adoption by users (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Hence, it is essential to study factors influencing IS user behaviour to design better information systems and to reap the benefits of these systems. The development of the technology adoption model (TAM (Davis, 1989) has resulted in hundreds of papers focusing on the concept of use and has paved the way for several other theories of IS use, such as TAM2 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000).

While the theory mentioned above and others have developed our understanding of IS user behaviour, they do not fully explain it. For example, in a review of TAM applications in healthcare IS, Holden and Karsh (2010) argued about the inclusion of contextual factors in IS use studies. The need to include contextual factors in IS use studies was advocated by other researchers who suggested that traditional IS use theories were developed in specific contexts and cannot be generalised to all IS contexts (Benbasat & Barki, 2007; Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006).

While some researchers (Benbasat & Barki, 2007) have called for the use of general behaviour theories, such as the theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen, 2005), to explain IS user behaviours, others (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007; Burton-Jones & Grange, 2012; Burton-

Jones & Volkof, 2017; LeRouge et al., 2007) have developed the concept of efective use. Efective use is the use of a system’s capabilities in a specific context to achieve the intended goals of the system. By definition, efective use strongly depends on the context of system use because the system’s goals are bound to this context. The inclusion of system goals and context diferentiates efective use from adoption. On the one hand, adoption focuses on using the system; it does not consider whether this use will lead to any improvement in the outcomes intended by using this system. This lack of focus on goals and context drew the attention of several researchers, who attempted to modify use to account for how it afects system goals. For example, Mishra et al. (2012) used the term “assimilation” to represent efective use. On the other hand, efective use considers the goals of the system as an essential part of system use studies. Systems are considered a means to reach specific goals, and hence system use should enable users to achieve these goals to be efective.

Burton-Jones and Volkof (2017) proposed a roadmap to develop theories for efective use. These theories are rooted in the context of IS use, and as such, they would provide better explanatory power compared to traditional IS use theories. This roadmap suggests using system afordances and afordance networks to develop context-specific theories of efective use.

Afordance is a concept that originally emerged to explain the interactions between animals and their environment (Chemero, 2003; Gibson, 1977); it represents what features the environment has and the possibilities of using these features through animals capabilities to achieve certain outcomes. Afordances are properties of the environment itself. The outcomes are obtained only when animals enable these afordances using their capabilities. The concept of afordances has been adopted in IS research as “the potential for behaviors associated with achieving an immediate concrete outcome and arising from the relation between an artifact and a goal-oriented actor or actors” (Strong et al., 2014, p. 69). IS then has a set of features or afordances that have the potential to allow users to achieve context-specific goals. These goals are realised only if the users engage in the behaviour required to realise them. For example, a WD ofers users the capability to engage in physical activity by providing alarms. However, realising the goal of these alarms (engaging in physical activity) depends on the users’ choice to acknowledge and act on the alarm.

The myriad of afordances ofered by IS can be classified into afordance networks, which explain the relationships among diferent afordances and simplify our understanding of the interactions between them as well as users’ interactions with them (Burton-Jones & Volkof, 2017; Pozzi et al., 2014). A system can be represented by describing its afordances and how these afordances can be used together (in relation to each other) to achieve system goals. For example, a smartphone may have many afordances, such as data connectivity and smart apps. However, to use smart apps, the data connectivity afordance must first be used. Hence, both these afordances are related network nodes. To achieve a system’s objectives using afordances, users must interact with the system in specific ways. These interactions are context- and user-dependent, and they actualise system afordances; that is, they help convert the features ofered by the system to actual outcomes (Strong et al., 2014). Hence, efective use becomes the actualisation of system afordances through interactions between users and the system.

The use of afordance networks to study efective use provides several benefits. Using afordance networks increases the flexibility of IS use study because the actualisation of afordances may vary from one user to another depending on their interactions with the system. The use of afordance networks also allows context to be integrated into the study of efective IS use.

Prior work on the efective use of information systems (e.g., Burton-Jones & Grange, 2012) focused on studying efective IS use in an organisational context, such as healthcare organisations. While this context is important because organisations can achieve their strategic objectives and justify the cost of using IS through efective use (Bhattacherjee, 2001), it is also vital to study efective use in a consumer context. This importance stems from the widespread use of consumer IS among individuals (e.g., smartphones and WDs) and the potential of these systems to help individuals realise their goals (e.g., improve their health). More importantly, in many cases, these systems are connected to organisational systems, and the success of the organisational systems may depend on the efective use of the consumer system. For example, many health networks manage specific chronic conditions that depend on patients using smartphone applications to capture or enter health-related data. These data are used to develop efective interventions and in analytics applications (Crandall et al., 2010). The “efective use” of these systems depends on the capability of patients to use their smartphones in this context efectively.

## 2.2. Wearable devices

For this study, we define WDs as devices that are physically attached to an individual to monitor their behaviour. WDs are most commonly used to monitor activity, such as steps and distance, as well as vital signs, such as heart rate. By monitoring activity, WD users can improve their activity and quality of life.

Modern WDs provide several features that are not only oriented to monitor activity or vital signs but to motivate behavioural changes. For example, most fitness WDs include software applications that provide users with an overview of their health and a comparison with other individuals of the same age and gender. These devices also use a system of rewards and social interactions to encourage users to engage in physical activity (Patel et al., 2015).

The efect of activity on improving overall health has long been acknowledged in healthcare research. However, with changes in lifestyles, there has been a decrease in the level of activity among individuals (Sallis et al., 2016), and this decrease has resulted in significant costs (Ding et al., 2016). WDs have been proposed as a means to improve activity for individuals. Findings show that using activity monitoring WDs can indeed boost the level of activity and has a myriad of health benefits related to blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol level (Bravata et al., 2007a). Moreover, healthcare providers encourage patients to use WDs and develop interventions based on these devices to manage their patients’ conditions. Hence, the efective use of WDs can be useful in improving health and decreasing healthcare costs.

Despite the eficacy of WDs, few studies have examined the adoption of these devices (e.g., Gao et al., 2015), and these studies used traditional acceptance theories, such as TAM, and did not include contextual factors. Moreover, these studies showed that in many cases, WDs are not efectively used (Lazar et al., 2015). For example, 50% of WD users stopped using their devices shortly after purchasing them (Ledger & McCafrey, 2014). Furthermore, WD users do not use all the features provided to improve their activity levels (Patel et al., 2015). Qualitative studies (e.g., Kaewkannate & Kim, 2016) have described some features that motivate WD use, such as price and accuracy. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have examined the factors or contexts that motivate the efective use of WDs. Given the potential benefits of WDs and the evidence indicating that users do not efectively use them, it is important to conceptualise efective WD use.

## 2.3. Use of technology and wearable devices by seniors

WD use has been growing recently, with the number of users in the US expected to grow from 45.8 million in 2018 to 67 million in 2022 (Wurmser, 2019). Despite the potential benefits of WDs to seniors (Cadmus-Bertram et al., 2015; Lyons et al., 2014; Mercer et al., 2016; O’Brien et al., 2015), 17% of US WD users are aged 25–34, while only 3.3% of users are 65+ (Wurmser, 2019). Seniors not only have a low adoption rate of WDs, but they also have a low adoption rate of WD-supportive technology, such as smartphones (Levine et al., 2016). This low adoption among seniors is problematic because activity can provide seniors with significant health improvements, and WDs can play a role in motivating activity. Despite the need to understand and explain seniors’ low adoption rate, there is scarce IS literature that focuses on seniors’ use of WDs and technology in general (Kapadia et al., 2015; Peek et al., 2014). This lack of research obstructs the ability to implement wearable technology for seniors and prevents healthcare providers from including these devices in their care plans.

Moreover, the limited literature that does exist in this area either implements established accepted theories, such as TAM (Niehaves & Plattfaut, 2014), or use qualitative studies to describe barriers and facilitating conditions for seniors’ use of technology (Abbey & Hyde, 2009). Both qualitative and quantitative studies have used constructs such as ease of use, usefulness, and complexity. However, to the best of our knowledge, none of these studies used constructs that are relevant to the contexts of seniors or wearable technology.

The diference in WD adoption between seniors and other age groups implies that contextual factors impact this adoption. Therefore, our goal is to develop a theory of seniors’ efective use of WDs. This theory aims to describe the afordances of WDs as a unique information technology artefact, how seniors interact with these devices to actualise their afordances, and what factors enable the actualisation of these afordances.

## 3. Methodology

To explain seniors’ efective use of WDs, we use grounded theory (GT) (Glaser et al., 1968) as an inductive method (Burton-Jones & Volkof, 2017). This research underwent several phases of planning, data collection, data analysis, and theoretical model synthesis based on the data.

## 3.1. Study context

This study utilised the “Fitbit Charge $2 ^ { \mathfrak { n } }$ WD. Fitbit Charge 2 was selected because it represents the next generation of WDs, which can track a myriad of activity measurements and vital signs. The device also provides a wide range of social support tools that enable users to interact with each other and compare their activity logs. Appendix A describes the major features of this device.

## 3.2. Grounded theory study planning

GT allows and encourages the use of multiple sources of data, including interviews, documents, and quantitative data to capture the depth and breadth of social processes (Charmaz, 2006). However, most GT studies use interviews as their primary data source. Interviews are usually employed because they ofer researchers the opportunity to interact with the study subjects and use the outcomes of one interview to develop questions for the next one and hence perform data collection and analysis in an iterative manner, which is a diferentiating characteristic of GT (Charmaz, 2014).

This study utilised data from interviews with seniors before and after using WDs. The data also include the actual activity data retrieved from the seniors’ WDs. Because the WD application stored historical activity, after one week of using the device (during the second interview) we accessed and downloaded the activity log for the week. The data collected included the number of steps, calories burnt, distance, heart rate, floors ascended, activity time, and sleeping patterns (if the seniors used the device during sleep). Because the activity data were collected at only one point in time (after using the device), we mainly used these data to verify the subjective responses of the participants. For example, if a participant said “I didn’t do much exercise”, we went back to the activity log and compared this participant’s objective activity to other participants objective activities to verify whether the subjective claim was correct. Based on our assessment, we followed up with questions to probe the participant’s responses. We asked why the participants thought their activity level was insuficient; this revealed the participants’ perceptions about their age and their physical and mental capabilities. We used nVivo for data collection and analysis and for tracking the concepts and categories discovered.

This study heavily depended on maintaining memos to keep track of the data collected, categories, and relationships among categories. The memos also helped in approaching GT reflexively (Charmaz, 2014).

## 3.3. Data collection

The participants were both female and male seniors aged 65+ living in California, USA and Ontario, Canada who had not used WDs before participating in this study. Participants had to own a smartphone to participate in the study. We recruited 44 participants using direct advertising in a local paper and from local senior centres. In total, we conducted 88 interviews, 44 before and 44 after the seniors used the WDs. Table 1 lists the demographics of the participants.

In this study, we collected data from diferent sources in two steps. In Step 1, semi-structured interviews were conducted. During these interviews, we introduced the WD to each participant. We set up the WD on the participant’s smartphone and we provided a 15 min tutorial explaining the diferent features of the WD and how to view and interpret the WD readings. The interview questions focused on the participants’ level of comfort with technology, current level of activity, exercise routine, and potential use of WDs. Appendix B outlines the main questions we asked in the interviews. After the first interview, each participant was provided with their WD (Fitbit) and was asked to keep it on at all times for one week. All participants used their WDs for one week except for the last eight participants, who used their WDs for three weeks (21 days). We increased the use duration for the last eight participants to capture categories and relationships that may appear after a longer use period. We compared participants’ responses to interview questions, especially the questions related to the efects of WD use on their activity level and the challenges they faced while using the WDs; we did not notice any change in the seniors’ use patterns for the longer use duration.

In Step 2, we conducted a second interview one week after the first one. In the second interview, we focused on examining whether the seniors’ perceptions of WDs had changed after using them, the changes in their levels of activity after using the WDs, and the value seniors perceived from using WDs. Furthermore, we collected actual activity data at the beginning of the second interview.

To enhance the validity and reliability of the qualitative study, verification methods (Morse et al., 2002) were applied as follows: (1) The interviews took place over four months, which allowed us to analyse and collect data concurrently and use the outcomes of the analysis to guide questions in the second wave of interviews. (2) We employed purposive sampling to ensure that the participants covered the whole range of WD users in terms of demographics and behaviour. Specifically, interviews covered a sample of seniors from 65 to 75 years old with diferent income levels and of both genders. We interviewed new participants until no new themes were discovered and the discovered themes were saturated. (3) An audit trail was maintained throughout data analysis and collection. The audit trail included interviewee information, the categories identified/reinforced during the interviews, and quotes to support these categories. Furthermore, as the analysis progressed, the audit trail tracked the relationships among categories and gaps that required filling by theoretical sampling.

One of the researchers reviewed the interview transcriptions to verify their accuracy, and two independent researchers coded them. Diferences between coders were resolved through discussions. After the four months, we ended up with a distinct set of categories that describe WD afordances and the relationships among afordances and immediate outcomes.

## 3.4. Data analysis

The goal of GT research is to create a theory grounded in data to explain the processes underlying the relationship between afordances and outcomes of WD use. We analysed interview data to create an initial set of concepts. Concepts were developed following Strauss and Corbin (1994) coding paradigm. After the first two waves of interviews, axial coding proceeded by synthesising related concepts into categories. Categories were created through joint analysis and the use of memos to record categories, properties, and relationships between categories. The coding process continued until no new categories were identified and category saturation was achieved. To identify relationships between categories, we conducted theoretical sampling to saturate the relationships among diferent categories. In the theoretical sampling, we identified categories for which we did not have suficient data to establish how they are related. To complete the development of our model, in subsequent interviews we focused on asking questions that revealed the relationships among these categories and included these relationships in our model. During data analysis, new coded interviews were continuously compared to existing interviews within the same category to gain more insights into the data and to guide our theory development (Charmaz, 2014).

## 4. Results

Below, we describe the findings on the elements of efective WD use. These findings include the goals that should be achieved for efective WD use, the WD features required to achieve these goals, and the interactions between the users and features to achieve WD goals.

## 4.1. Goals of using wearable devices

Despite the simplistic nature of WDs compared to the more complex systems discussed in the literature, we identified several goals and afordances associated with them. The main reason behind the multitude of goals and afordances is the software system and mobile applications supporting these devices, which transform WDs into structured IS systems with multiple goals and afordances. Therefore, in our discussion below we refer to the whole system associated with the WD, including the mobile application and software system. The goals identified from our interviews include activity monitoring, activity planning, and activity improvement.

## 4.1.1. Activity monitoring

The first goal of using a WD is activity monitoring. Monitoring is the primary function of a WD, which can be achieved by the device even if it is not connected to any application. The seniors used their WDs during and after exercising to monitor their heart rate, calories, and the number of steps and distance. All seniors considered this goal as a basic goal for their use of WDs. For example, when asked why use a WD, one of the participants said “I run every weekend, and it would be nice to know how long I ran and how many calories I burnt” (Interview $2 4 \mathrm { A } ^ { \mathrm { i } } )$

Based on our data, we identified two afordances associated with activity monitoring: (1) data recording and (2) data display and arrangement. Data is captured by the device either automatically through diferent sensors or manually by users. “My Fitbit counted everything I needed to know, including my heart rate” (Interview 2B<sup>2</sup>); the data is then displayed to the users in the format they expect to allow them to monitor their activities. “I am only interested to see the calories I burn and my heart rate, which is all I needed. I run the same path every week, so I know my distance” (Interview 14B).

## 4.1.2. Activity planning

Activity planning is not directly achieved by the WD but rather by the software and application associated with the WD. The WD software combines device readings with aggregated activity data of other users and evidencebased data to help seniors identify their activity levels compared to others and to plan their future activities. While activity analysis and activity planning may be diferent goals, they were viewed and discussed by seniors as a single goal. That is, the seniors viewed activity analysis as a step towards activity planning. “The Fitbit app showed me what I was missing to reach my age exercise level” (Interview 41B).

Afordances associated with this goal included the ability to provide a comparative analysis to seniors, providing recommendations for activity level and creating a strategy for exercising or engaging in activities. To achieve the activity planning goal, the WD application compares current activities with the recommended activity level at the senior age and allows the senior to observe this diference; the application then provides recommendations on how to close the gap between the current activity level and optimum activity level. Finally, the application will help seniors to schedule future activities to close this gap.

## 4.1.3. Activity improvement

The activity improvement goal is the ultimate goal of WD use. Seniors realise this goal when they enhance their activity routines to meet recommended activity levels for their age and gender. Four afordances were associated with activity improvement. The first afordance is the ability to alert seniors to engage in a planned activity, the second afordance is the capability to reward seniors for their activity improvement by providing accomplishment medals or trophies, the third afordance is to enable seniors to share their activities and trophies with others creating a sense of competition and further motivating seniors to engage in activities, and the fourth afordance is to actually improve seniors’ activity. Seniors shared their activities using Fitbit’s social capabilities, which allow seniors to engage with other Fitbit users (an afordance used by many users), or by sharing their achievements on Facebook from within the application. This afordance was popular among users. For example, one user called their Fitbit connection “My exercise body” (Interview 6B) and was very excited about competing against their newly acquired friend.

## 4.1.4. Dimensions of efective WD use

To actualise the afordances of WDs, we identified several dimensions that enable the efective use of WDs. These dimensions are enablers of efective use in the sense that system users utilise these dimensions to achieve the goals intended by the information system. Without utilising these dimensions, users’ goals will not be achieved despite the existence of afordances.

## 4.1.5. Perceived physical capability

As a dimension of efective use, perceived physical capability (PC) reflects how the seniors perceive their current capability to engage in physical activity compared to others and to their previous capabilities. While seniors with a low level of activity can benefit most from using a WD, such seniors were reluctant to use them. On the contrary, seniors who exercised regularly and perceived themselves as having a better PC compared to others were more willing to monitor their activities and use the WD to improve their future activities. Interestingly, perception and not actual PC influenced seniors’ use of WDs. That is, seniors are reluctant to use WDs because they believe their physical capabilities are lower than those of their peers even if their objective activity shows that they are exercising within the norm of their age group. This result was demonstrated by examining the activity logs of seniors with perceived disabilities. While those seniors perceived themselves as having low physical activity and being unable to exercise because of their disability compared to their friends of the same age, the activity log showed that they had a similar level of activity compared to their peers.

Two sub-dimensions of perceived PC were identified in the analysis. The first is related to how seniors compare their current PC to their prior PC. This dimension was the most salient in our findings. When seniors perceived themselves as “disabled” compared to themselves at a younger age, they were reluctant to interact with the WD; one of the seniors said, “I used to practice several days per week, now I only swim once per week and I can’t take the Fitbit with me into the pool” (Interview 4A).

The second sub-dimension of perceived PC is seniors comparing their activity capability to that of other seniors. When seniors felt that they were less capable of engaging in physical activity than others (e.g., their gym friends), they were not willing to use WDs to monitor their activity or share it with others.

The efects of perceived PC on monitoring activity and comparing activity with others agree with the theory of cognitive dissonance. When seniors believe that they will see data that is worse in comparison to others, they may decide not to see this data to avoid changing their view of themselves. One of our seniors expressed this in a post-use interview: “I told you my data wouldn’t be good, I only walked in my home this last week” (Interview 14B). On the contrary, seniors with good PC are willing to monitor and share their data to confirm their appearance as individuals with high activity levels.

## 4.1.6. Technology tendency

Technology tendency (TT) reflects the relationship between seniors and technology. This term goes beyond technology familiarity to include seniors’ perceptions of technical ability to improve their lives (technology appreciation). Seniors in our sample differed significantly in their ability to use technology and to adapt to WD technology. This diference ranged from complete control over the use of technology, e.g., “You young people think we know nothing about technology. You forgot that our generation invented the PC” (Interview 17A), to seniors who were not used to technology or did not believe technology would make a diference in their exercise needs, e.g., “When it comes to technology, I am a dinosaur” (Interview 42A). This relationship between seniors and technology controlled the seniors’ capability to achieve results with their WDs. Because we conducted pre- and post-use interviews, we were able to capture this relationship between TT and efective use of WDs by comparing seniors’ beliefs about technology in the pre-use interviews and their use of WDs in the post-use interviews.

We identified two sub-categories in this area. The first sub-category is technology capabilities. This reflects the senior’s skills related to understanding and using technology. Seniors who can manage technology were able to understand the purpose of WDs and use them to their full potential. Seniors who did not frequently interact with technology found dificulties in managing their devices and their use of the devices was restricted to observing their activity data. They did not use their devices to plan their activities or improve their exercise. This subcategory emerged from both interviews as well as the collected WD data. The WD data showed that seniors familiar with the technology used their devices much more than those who lacked technology skills.

The second sub-category is technology appreciation. Technology appreciation reflects seniors’ beliefs in the capability of technology to help them achieve their goals. This study found that when seniors believe in technology, they are more likely to use more features of this technology and are not frustrated by the nuisances they encounter from using their devices. Seniors who did not trust technology emphasised the shortcomings of their devices and were more often frustrated when they encountered any issues, e.g., “This was completely useless, it never captured my activity interview correctly” (Interview 31B).

## 4.1.7. Social influence

Social influence (SI) emerged as a dimension of efective use. While SI has been considered repeatedly in the literature, its efect on seniors’ use of WDs was multidimensional. We identified two sub-categories of SI.

Pre-Use SI: This sub-category represents the initial SI on seniors’ decision to use WDs. This influence has been studied in the literature before (Venkatesh et al., 2012) and was found to influence acceptance. There were two sources of SIs in this sub-category:

● Family influence: close family members of the seniors used these devices and their children encouraged the participants to use them.

● Strong ties influence: Seniors who exercised frequently in groups were impacted by friends recommendations or advice to use WDs. For example, a participant told us “Many of my friends use a Fitbit or similar wearables. They said Fitbit was very good in tracking their steps” (interview 7A).

Post-Use SI: This influence extended past the initial use of WDs. SI motivated the seniors to realise outcomes such as social competition and activity improvement. Again, there were two sources of SI in this sub-category.

● Strong ties influence: Seniors who had close friends using the same WD were motivated to participate in competitive social activities with those friends. This SI worked with the seniors perceived PC, as described above.

● Weak ties influence: Weak ties include online friends or application friends. Those friends use the same applications to monitor activity. Again, seniors were motivated to participate in social competition and to improve their performance to win this competition.

## 4.1.8. Adaptability

Adaptability (AD) refers to the ability to manage change to respond to new conditions (Sankar et al., 1995). AD has been studied in the IS literature to reflect the ability to customise an information system to the needs or expectations of the user. In the context of seniors’ use of WDs, AD refers to the customisation of a WD to meet its user’s needs and the AD of the senior to the WD functions.

Customisation of WDs to meet users’ needs was salient and covered many components of the WD. For example, the ability of a senior to select the colour of the WD band or the user interface created a sense of attachment and ownership towards the device. This feeling created trust in the device and motivated users to use it more efectively. To confirm this efect, in later interviews we did not provide some seniors with their desired colours or interfaces, and this sense of ownership decreased significantly.

Customisation extended to activity monitoring and alarms. When the indicators shown by the device were adapted to the seniors’ goals, seniors were likely to follow these alarms and use them to improve their health outcomes. On the other hand, if the device alarms were not adapted to seniors’ goals, they considered themselves to be “slaves” or “servants” to the device and refused to adhere to the activity alarms.

The second sub-category of AD was users’ AD to the device. The goal of the WD is to motivate users to engage in more physical activities and exercise. to do so, users may have to modify their activity level to match the WD recommendation. This ability of seniors to adapt their behaviour to device recommendations was necessary for the efective use of the WD. The AD of seniors to WDs was more dificult than expected. Seniors tended to have a comfort zone and established routines that they follow, and the WDs urged them to change their routines. This led seniors to have a negative attitude towards the devices and neglect their alarms. For example, one of the seniors said “I am sitting in my living room reading the paper and then this thing buzzes me to move. I want to read my paper in peace, so I turn it of and continue to read my paper” (Interview 13B).

## 4.1.9. Information quality

Information quality (IQ) was relevant in many phases of WD use. IQ had two main sub-categories: accuracy and timeliness (Ghasemaghaei & Hassanein, 2015, 2016). Accuracy is how the information collected and displayed by the device reflected the actual activity of the users and how recommendations of WDs reflected evidence-based information for the users. At the data monitoring phase, seniors paid close attention to whether the device was displaying what they actually did. This was especially true in the first two days of using the device. They showed discontent when the device failed to capture something they did (in most cases, the Fitbit missed some of the stairs they climbed). Accuracy in capturing the data and displaying it was essential to encourage seniors to use the device in activity planning.

For efective use, accuracy in entering data was also essential. The device used the data entered by the seniors, such as nutrition and water intake, to develop activity plans for them. Entering the wrong information led to many frustrations and discouraged seniors from using the WDs. This efect was especially true when they entered the wrong age or weight. For example, one of the seniors who entered the wrong age was anxious when the WD assessed their activity level as inadequate even though they exercised regularly.

The accuracy of the WD recommendations was important for the seniors to follow these recommendations. We were surprised during the interviews by the number of seniors who looked up their recommended activity level online and compared it to the recommendations provided by their devices. In the post-use interviews, they discussed their findings with the researchers and why in some cases they were diferent than what the device provided. For example, seniors searched the Internet and asked their family doctors about the time they should spend exercising, the maximum heart rate they could safely reach, and the frequency of exercise. One of these seniors said, “I wouldn’t just follow what the Fitbit app tells me to do. I need to make sure I’m doing the right thing for my health” (Interview 39B).

The second sub-category of IQ was timeliness, i.e., providing seniors with information at the right time. Timeliness in providing activity information for the seniors encouraged them to continue to monitor their activity and follow their activity alarms. On the contrary, delays in reporting activity information created distrust in the performance of the device and discouraged seniors from following its recommendations. These delays usually happened because the synchronisation between the WD and mobile application took a long time or was interrupted before completion.

Timeliness extended to the activity planning phase. Having information on time allowed the WD software to develop activity plans for the seniors better and encouraged them to adhere to these plans because they know the WD used their actual up-to-date activity information.

## 4.1.10. Efective use actualisation

As Table 2 shows, the dimensions of efective use difered from one afordance to another. The results of this study show that seniors’ use of technology is a complex process that goes beyond traditional technology acceptance literature. This process is rooted in seniors’ social relations and their perceptions of their mental and physical capabilities. As our results demonstrate, the interactions between seniors and WDs are impacted by how they compare their capabilities to their peers and the influence of those around them.

The afordance network of WD use represents this process and shows the relationships among system afordances and the competencies required to realise these afordances. This network shows that seniors can use WDs to achieve diferent goals and that use dimensions difer according to these goals. WD afordances are not suficient to realise seniors’ goals. Seniors must interact with their WDs and enable these afordances (through use dimensions) to realise their goals. To examine how afordances and dimensions work together to achieve seniors’ goals, we examine each goal and then examine how these goals relate to each other. Figure 1 shows the afordances associated with the activity monitoring goal and how these afordances are enabled to achieve this goal. For the seniors to achieve this goal, they must first record their activities, which creates the intermediate goal of activity recording. Hence, the data recording afordance of the WD must be activated. This afordance is enabled by the seniors’ TT, AD, and IQ. The SI role for this goal is that it develops an awareness of the importance of activity monitoring and hence motivates seniors to use WDs. These use dimensions, as discussed above, enhance seniors’ trust in WDs and encourage them to use these devices. Achieving this goal is similar to accepting WDs in traditional IS use models. After seniors wear WDs and the goal of activity recording is achieved, seniors use the next afordance (activity monitoring) to achieve their first goal of viewing and monitoring their exercise and activity levels. The dimensions of the activity monitoring afordance are similar to those of activity recording because seniors must trust the output of the devices to accept the values they see on the screen.

Some seniors stop at the first-level goal of monitoring their activities. Other seniors have a higher-level goal of creating a plan for their activities and exercise. They use more WD afordances to achieve this goal. For the activity planning goal (Figure 2), after the activities have been recorded, using the activity recording afordance, these activities are input to the comparative analysis afordance, which compares these activities with the activities of seniors of the same age and gender. Once this afordance is enabled by AD, IQ, PC, and TT, the users’ relative performance is analysed and passed to the next afordance of “recommending activity level”. When enabled by user interactions with the WD, this afordance provides recommendations of the activities the seniors can do to match their peers’ performance levels. Hence, these recommended activities are fed to the “activity planning” afordance, which after being enabled by AD, IQ, PC, and TT, puts these recommendations in a schedule or plan that seniors can follow to achieve their expected activity level. The activity planning goal requires more technical skills and interactions than the activity monitoring goal because in this case, seniors interact not only with the WD but with the mobile application associated with the WD. Hence, TT is essential to enable the afordances of this goal. Furthermore, the WD and application must be adapted to the activity level of the seniors as well as their expected activity level. For the planning to be efective, the information recorded by the WD must be accurate and reliable. SI is no longer relevant and is replaced by perceived PC because activity planning is a personal goal that compares an individual’s level of activity to their goals.

![](/api/attachments/MQTF93W7/fulltext/images/daa7673e4eb62252162ecd9c0fe3fad7d96c58998ed3ceff3c9c09f597363cc7.jpg)  
Figure 2. Activity Planning Goal.

Once seniors have created an activity plan, they may decide to move to the next goal of practically implementing this plan and improving their activity level (Figure 3). The plan created is fed to the activity alerts afordance to create alerts for seniors to practice to meet the schedule. This afordance is enabled by AD and PC to motivate seniors to exercise and engage in planned activities. Once seniors are motivated to exercise, the “rewarding activity” afordance sends them badges and rewards to their phones and emails. Hence, when this afordance is enabled, seniors are further encouraged to maintain their activities. Seniors can share their accomplishments using social media from within the application and compete with their peers or friends using the “social sharing” afordance of the WD. The alerts to exercise combined with the rewards and social competition activate the “activity improvement” afordance of the WD, motivating seniors to improve their performance and realise the ultimate goal of WD use. The activity improvement goa requires seniors to respond to alerts and exercise consistently. Thus, enabling this goal’s afordances depends on the ability of seniors to adapt to device recommendations and to perceive an improvement in their physical capabilities, which encourages them to continue with their activity improvement eforts. The WD adds a dimension of social interaction to activity improvement by allowing seniors to receive and share activity improvement trophies; hence, SI also becomes a motivator for activity improvement. Interacting with the online system and sharing activities online require users to be comfortable with using the WD application and social media. Hence, TT is required to enable social sharing afordance.

Figure 4 shows the overall afordance network explaining seniors’ efective use of WDs. The afordance network is non-linear and shows the dependence between goals and afordances. The levels shown in Figure 4 show that not all seniors have the same goals of using WDs. While some seniors focus on simply viewing their performance during exercise, others use WDs to achieve the higher goals of planning their activity levels or improving them. The figure also shows the dependence between diferent levels. For seniors to achieve higher-level goals, they must first achieve lower-level goals. For example, to plan their future activities, seniors must first record their current activity levels. The network also shows that improvement is a continuous process. Hence, after seniors’ activity has improved, the WDs record their improved activities, and the seniors can build on these new recorded activities to enhance their improvements.

## 5. Discussion

The use of WDs to improve health outcomes has been demonstrated in numerous studies (e.g., Bravata et al., 2007a). Several studies examined the adoption and use of WDs using a myriad of theoretical models, including TAM (Chuah et al., 2016), UTAUT (Reyes-Mercado, 2018) and UTAUT2 (Wang et al., 2015). These studies identified several factors that influence the use of WDs, including usefulness, ease of use, and SI. However, these studies focused on adoption and did not relate adoption to realising WD goals. Our results show that seniors goals of using WDs difer from one user to another. Traditional IS constructs, such as usefulness, may be suficient for device acceptance but not for achieving the goals of using WDs because this achievement requires interaction between adoption constructs and the afordances of WDs. Interestingly, another stream of research focused on WD capabilities and features that enable users to realise health improvement goals. These studies focused on WD behavioural interventions, such as alarms, rewards, and behaviour demonstration, and how these interventions motivate users to improve their activity level (Duncan et al., 2017; Lentferink et al., 2017; Mercer et al., 2016). These interventions are similar to our definitions of afordances in this study. These studies focused on WD behavioural intervention afordances but did not discuss how users interact with them to achieve activity goals. For the seniors in our study, SI further integrates both streams of research to explain how users interact with WD afordances to achieve their activity goals. Furthermore, we focused our attention on seniors as a distinct population with specific needs. Ghasemaghaei et al. (2019) proposed cognitive age, i.e., seniors’ perception of their age, as a predictor for use of online recommendation agents. Cognitive age is influenced by the mental capabilities of seniors and their relative competencies compared to others. This cognitive age is reflected in seniors perceptions of their physical capabilities and their capability to understand and use technology. Hence, this previous result confirms our findings related to the efects of seniors’ perceptions of their physical capabilities, TTs, and WD use.

An interesting result that emerged from this study is the diference between seniors’ perceptions of their activity levels and their actual (objective) activity levels. During the interviews, some seniors reported less activity than their actual activity measured by the WDs. This result may be explained by seniors’ beliefs that they “look older” or “are less fit” than their peers, and hence they expect that their activity level is less than that of their peers. Surprisingly, none of the seniors reported more activity than we saw in their activity logs.

SI has been shown to influence the use of technology by diferent user groups. For seniors, SI has a significant efect. Seniors tend to seek social interactions and engage in activities that provide them with these interactions (Conci et al., 2009). In this study, SI played two key roles. First, it created awareness of WDs and their uses, motivating seniors to use their devices. This role of SI is therefore crucial, especially for older seniors, to understand and to make a decision about using WDs. After seniors start using WDs, the SI role changes to motivate them to achieve higher goals using their devices by competing against others or engaging in collaborative exercises with other seniors. Hence, they can reap the benefits of long-term WD use. Given the salient efect of social interactions on seniors, we propose that the SI efect extends to seniors’ use of technology in general and that the social dimension of technology use should be considered when promoting these technologies to seniors.

Technology characteristics related to seniors’ use of WDs include the AD of technology and IQ. While these characteristics have been discussed before in the literature, they appear to be more salient for seniors in the case of WD use. The salient influence of AD is probably due to the nature of the technology itself. For example, WD users difer in their goals, and they expect WDs to accommodate these goals. Hence, the AD of WDs may play an important role in convincing users to use these devices for diferent goals. Another possible reason for the significant AD efect may be related to the cognitive capabilities of seniors and their use of WDs to communicate with others. The AD of WDs, coupled with seniors’ TTs, makes it easier for seniors to use these devices and to gain benefits from them. The AD of WDs also adds the dimension of self-representation, i.e., the ability of the user to use the WD to reflect how they desire others to perceive their identity (Yee & Bailenson, 2007), which has been shown to motivate the use of social technology.

While IQ has been found to influence IS use and satisfaction (Delone & McLean, 2003), in the context of seniors’ use of WDs, IQ is not only related to the benefits or outcomes of WD use but it extends to the social aspects of using WDs. Because seniors use these devices to compare their performance to others, they become uncomfortable if the metrics reported by the device are not accurate or do not reflect their actual activity. This social dimension of IQ is unique to the context of WDs and to the best of our knowledge, it has not been addressed in previous studies.

## 6. Contributions and future work

This study contributes to IS theory and to our understanding of seniors’ use of technology. It opens a window for future studies on consumer technology, seniors’ use of technology, and the use of WDs. The first contribution of this paper is demonstrating the complexities of studying the use of consumer technologies. In the extant literature, studying consumer technology is characterised by simplicity, especially in terms of theory, and limits the study of these technologies to a single dimension. On the contrary, studies on business IS has witnessed the use of more complex and developed theoretical frameworks. We strongly believe that this barrier between consumer technology and business technology is dissolving, or should dissolve, given the developments in consumer electronics and the integration between consumer technology and business technology, making the success of consumer technology essential to the success of business technology. In this study, we developed a model based on users’ perceptions of how users perceive technology diferently according to their goals and capabilities. Hence, we call for IS researchers to extend their study of business systems to include consumer technology and to create context-sensitive models to study these technologies.

Our second contribution follows from the first. We developed a model for seniors’ efective use of WDs. This model integrates two streams of research. One stream that focuses on the adoption of WDs and another stream that focuses on WD afordances and interventions and their efect on achieving users activity goals. By integrating these two streams, we achieved a better understanding of seniors’ use of WDs and what is necessary to motivate them to achieve specific goals by using these devices. For example, for activity improvement, SI by family and friends is not suficient; SI from competition and activity sharing is also required to motivate seniors to improve their activity level. Furthermore, there is a relationship between the perceived physical and mental capabilities of seniors and their willingness to use WDs efectively. We found these two points to be very important in guiding seniors’ decisions towards WD; hence, future research should focus on these two factors. However, it is not suficient to market WDs to seniors as simply being beneficial to their health. WD marketing should also consider the SI factor by encouraging group use of WDs, such as through community centres, and should address seniors’ perceived physical and mental capabilities. Seniors, especially those who perceive themselves as less active than their peers, may require psychological interventions to raise their self-confidence and encourage them to use WDs.

In this paper, we discussed WDs as an emerging technology and showed that despite the apparent simplicity of these devices, they are perceived and used by seniors as having several levels of afordances and are able to achieve various goals. We discussed the afordances of these devices and how they can be used efectively to realise the ultimate goal of activity and health improvement. In doing so, we showed that the efective use of similar technologies should be studied to further understand this technology and reap its benefits. We also propose that this hierarchy of afordances should be taken into consideration when developing care plans that employ WDs.

Finally, we restate the importance of considering the contextual factors when studying consumer technology and not to limit the use of contextual factors to more complex information systems.

## 7. Limitations

One of the issues with using the GT approach in this study is the generalisability of its results to other contexts. For seniors’ use of technology, we tried to include as many participants in this study as possible. Hence, we interviewed 44 participants across North America with diferent ethnic and demographic backgrounds. Hence, we are confident of the generalisability of our model in this context. Moreover, as discussed above, many of our findings agree with previous results covering seniors’ use of other technologies, such as the perceived physical and cognitive capabilities. Therefore, our findings related to actuators and seniors’ capabilities can be generalised to other technologies, but these factors must be considered in depth for those technologies. Additionally, the proposed afordance network itself is unique to the use of WDs and cannot be generalised to other contexts. Moreover, we did not ask participants’ whether they had chronic diseases, and we did not ask them to specify their ethnic group.

Another limitation of this study is the short duration of WD use considered. This short duration raises the question of whether seniors had time to efectively use their WDs. We believe that the one-week duration was suficient for several reasons. First, seniors were trained on the diferent features of the WDs and how to use them most efectively. This training covered all the device features, and given the simplicity of the WDs, we believe the training gave the seniors the necessary skills to use and benefit from the WD features. Second, WDs are diferent from business IS. While efective use studies in business contexts span several months (e.g., Burton-Jones & Volkof, 2017), WD devices are attached to users all the time, including during sleep. This attachment gives the user the ability to interact with their device much more than they would in a business context. Hence, users do not require months to realise the benefits of WDs. Finally, the last eight study participants used the device for three weeks. We did not identify any diferences in their use patterns compared to the participants who used the WDs for one week.

## 8. Conclusion

In this study, we developed a model of seniors’ efective WD use using afordance networks. This model can be used to drive future research in the area of seniors’ use of technology. This model shows that WDs, as an example of consumer technology, have several afordances that help seniors to achieve diferent goals. Seniors must use diferent capabilities to actualise the afordances of these devices. These capabilities include technology-related factors, such as AD and IQ, and senior-related factors, such as TT and perceived PC. We also found that SI plays a significant role in motivating the efective use of WDs. This role spans from creating awareness of these devices to encouraging activity improvement. We follow other researchers in highlighting the importance of using contextual factors in studying technology use and the need for a more in-depth study of consumer technologies.

## Appendix B- A sample of interview questions

## Pre-use interviews

(1) What is your education level? What is your current/last job?

(2) What do you think of technology? Are you comfortable in using technology? What other technologies/deviced do you use?

(3) What is your current activity level? How often do you exercise? Are you happy with your current level of activity?

(4) Why would you want to use a Fitbit? How will you use it?

(5) Do people around use use wearable devices? How?

(6) Do you have any concerns about using the Fitbit?

## Post-use interviews

(1) Tell us about your experience with using the Fitbit?

(2) What features did you use during this week? What features were the most useful?

(3) What features didn’t you use? Why?

(4) Did you face any challenges with using the Fitbit?

(5) How did using the Fitbit afect your activity level/exercise routine?

(6) Are you willing to continue to use your Fitbit?
