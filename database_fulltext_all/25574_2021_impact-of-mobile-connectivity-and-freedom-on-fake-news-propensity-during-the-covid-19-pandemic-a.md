# Impact of mobile connectivity and freedom on fake news propensity during the COVID-19 pandemic: a cross-country empirical examination

Anuragini Shirish <sup>a</sup>, Shirish C. Srivastava <sup>b</sup> and Shalini Chandra <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Université Paris-Saclay, Univ Evry, IMT-BS, LITEM, 91025, Evry-Courcouronnes, France; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems and Operations Management Department, HEC Paris, France; <sup>c</sup>S P Jain School of Global Management, Department of Information Systems, Singapore, Singapore

## ABSTRACT

COVID-19 pandemic has triggered a surge in the amount of fake news circulating on the Internet. However, despite the global bearing of the coronavirus pandemic, there is a significant variance in the propensity of COVID-19 related fake news instances across nations. To better understand the national-level factors contributing to the spread of fake news during the current pandemic, we theorise and examine the relationships of “mobile connectivity” and “freedom” (economic, political, and media) in a nation, with fake news propensity. We test the proposed model through a unique dataset comprising 72 countries generated by combining 6 independent cross-country data sources. We find that “mobile connectivity” and “political freedom” in a nation contribute to COVID-19 related fake news propensity, whereas “economic” and “media freedom” inhibit it. Our study provides preliminary insights into the mechanisms through which national-level systemic resources can be leveraged for battling the spread of fake news during the COVID-19 pandemic and beyond. In addition, our study ofers several important implications for governments and policymakers that we believe will be instrumental in stimulating future research on the subject.

KEYWORDS Fake news; COVID-19; pandemic; mobile connectivity; digital resilience; country’s freedom; dark side of IT; uncertainty management

## 1. Introduction

Unlike any other crisis in the recent decades, COVID-19 scripts a unique episode in the human history for two key reasons. First, the COVID-19 pandemic has touched almost all countries around the world. Second, the crisis marks an undefined period of uncertainty and fear among citizens around the globe (Kharasch & Jiang, 2020). Such prolonged conditions of uncertainty and fear amongst people is a fertile ground for the generation and spread of fake news, prompting individuals to share and consume misinformation related to health, economy, society, and other aspects of the environment (United Nations, 2020a). However, reports show that the propensity for generation and consumption of COVID-19 related fake news is not uniform across all regions of the world (Brennen et al., 2020). In fact, as highlighted by the Poynter Institute,<sup>1</sup> more than half of the COVID-19 related fake news until July 2020 originated from four countries, namely – Brazil, India, Spain, and the United States (Poynter, 2020). A recent survey found that the incidence of fake news is low in less polarised European nations such as Denmark, Germany and Netherlands, where citizens prefer consuming objective news and the Nordic countries, where media literacy is high (Newman et al., 2020). In contrast, fake news propensity is high in countries where the uncertainty about online information is high and national institutions are relatively weaker, such as Brazil, Kenya and South Africa (Newman et al., 2020). For better pandemic preparedness and control, it is necessary to mitigate fear among people, manage rumours, and dispel misinformation (Sakurai and Chughtai 2020; Islam et al., 2020). Hence, from a policy perspective, it is of utmost importance to appreciate the mechanisms propelling the production and consumption of fake news. Moreover, given the large variance in the COVID-19 related fake news volumes across nations and the potential damage that such misinformation can cause, it will be theoretically and practically interesting to understand the factors contributing to fake news propensity across countries. Such knowledge can help governments better understand the fake news phenomenon and design appropriate misinformation related policies to protect their citizens (Fleming, 2020; Laato et al., 2020).

In addition to augmenting the level of fear and uncertainty among individuals, COVID-19 related fake news have resulted in severe losses to human lives including deaths in several parts of the world (Islam et al., 2020). It is estimated that during the first three months of 2020, around 800 people died and 5800 hospitalised due to coronavirus-related misinformation (BBC, 2020). In Iran alone, there were over 700 deaths and 60 cases of complete blindness after drinking methanol, which was posed as a cure for coronavirus (Aljazeera, 2020; Delirrad & Mohammadi, 2020; Islam et al., 2020). Similar instances of false news causing serious health hazards or deaths due to consumption of toxic substances have been reported from Turkey, Qatar and India (Islam et al., 2020; Siddiqui et al., 2020). Moreover, rumours, stigma and conspiracy theories pertaining to COVID-19 are also having serious implications on the individuals and communities (Islam et al., 2020). For example, the spread of fake news has resulted in instigating tensions and violence against minority communities and healthcare workers in certain parts of the world, while 5 G conspiracy theories of coronavirus propagation have resulted in significant property losses to national telecommunication infrastructures in the UK (Islam et al., 2020; Newman et al., 2020). It has also been reported that during the COVID-19 pandemic, fake news phenomenon has been opportunistically capitalised by individuals to further their personal, political and business agendas (UN News, 2020). Clearly, in the current scenario of uncertainty and fear, fake news is emerging as a new form of digital control (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Aroles, 2020). This further precipitates the need for IS scholars to critically examine the potential mechanisms that facilitate and counteract the spread of fake news during a crisis (Rowe, 2018).

COVID-19 pandemic is a rather unique kind of crisis with no clear deadlines, alternatives, or solutions. The nature of uncertainties associated with COVID-19 are largely undefined but are impacting myriad issues related to the health, security, economics, politics, and social well-being of citizens around the globe (Drake et al., 2012; Qiu et al., 2017). The multifaceted and unpredictable nature of the crisis is aggravating the associated uncertainties and fears among citizens (Kharasch & Jiang, 2020). In fact, the World Uncertainty Index reports that COVID-19 related uncertainties are much higher compared to past virus outbreaks such as the severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) and Ebola (Ahir et al., 2020). A prolonged state of heightened uncertainty and fear is upsetting for citizens who want reassurance and predictability in times of crisis (Ågerfalk et al., 2020). To better understand the meaning of the unprecedented crisis situation, citizens resort to “sensemaking” through the diferent “resource lenses” which are at their disposal. We posit that the two key resources that citizens draw upon to better appreciate the current and future implications of the crisis are the informational and institutional resources in their nation (Schedler, 2013).

Informational resource is conceived as a reactive resource, which the citizens tend to use as a coping mechanism to counter the uncertainties and fears emanating from the pandemic situation. It comprises all the available information to the citizens through government and non-government channels. Internet, mobile phones, and other forms of technological connectivity have improved communication, thereby shortening business cycle times and enhancing productivity (Shirish et al., 2017). Countries with better technological connectivity are expected to perform more eficiently through improved information flows between citizens, businesses, and governments (Lin et al., 2011; Srivastava et al., 2016). However, better connectivity through the Internet can also serve as a medium for the generation and propagation of misinformation, especially during a period of uncertainty and fear. The literature on uncertainty reduction states that for situations where either the “process” or the “outcomes” are uncertain, individuals will be nudged to look for additional information to reduce their anxiety (Srivastava & Chandra, 2018). In the case of COVID-19 pandemic, this creates a paradoxical situation, where increased connectivity purported for positive impacts on organisations and societies, may contrarily result in negative outcomes through the production and consumption of fake news (Chandra et al., 2019). Hence, the first question that we address in this research is:

\# RQ1 – Is the level of technological connectivity in a nation related to the volume of COVID-19 related fake news propensity in that country?

The second key resource that the citizens draw upon to better assess the meaning of the crisis is the country’s underlying institutional and governance framework. Grounding our arguments in the institutional freedom and sensemaking literature, we posit that the citizens’ perceptions about the independent working of the country’s institutions could inhibit fake news propensity. In a country with greater institutional freedom, citizens will perceive less COVID-19 uncertainties related to healthcare, jobs, security, and the general economic condition. The protective resource of institutional freedom describes the country’s resilience to face any emergency (Liu et al., 2016; Ognyanova et al., 2020). In a scenario of stable and free national institutions, citizens would perceive greater predictability in times of crisis. Such an imbued citizen trust in national institutions will inhibit the citizens from looking for reassurance from alternative sources of information through informal channels. Hence, the second question that we seek to examine in our research is:

\# RQ2 – Is the level of institutional freedom in a nation related to the volume of COVID-19 related fake news propensity in that country?

To examine our research questions, we first theorise the citizen sensemaking mechanisms through the lens of informational and institutional resources in a nation for managing COVID-19 related uncertainties contributing to the fake news propensity. Next, we use country-level secondary data on mobile Internet connectivity as the informational resource and national institutional structures (economic, political, and media freedom) as the institutional resource, in combination with other national-level data from different datasets, to test the hypothesised relationships.

The findings from our study ofer preliminary insights into the national-level technological and institutional determinants of fake news propensity. Specifically, our results identify mobile connectivity in a nation as a potential playground for the spread of COVID-19 related fake news. Instead of providing an informational coping mechanism to the citizens during a crisis, mobile Internet access may aggravate the propagation of fake news. However, media and economic freedom guaranteed by the national institutional structures can prevent citizens from becoming the victims or perpetrators of fake news. Counterintuitively, we find that political freedom in a nation contributes to fake news propensity. Through our study, we address the calls to study the role of technological connectivity and institutional structures in the context of uncertainty management during a crisis (Ågerfalk et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2016). By identifying the salient role of mobile Internet connectivity in fake news propensity, we reiterate the need for governments and policymakers to assess the impact of infrastructural technologies in a holistic manner taking into account their possible negative efects. In addition, our research ofers several actionable implications for policymakers to augment their ongoing eforts towards crisis preparedness and management.

## 2. Background literature

## 2.1. Citizen uncertainty and fake news in times of COVID-19 crisis

Changes in the external environment have been identified as one of the principal sources of uncertainty (Venkatesh et al., 2016). The COVID-19 pandemic has ushered in multifarious uncertainties related to life, health, social relationships, jobs, and economic condition (Ågerfalk et al., 2020). The uncertainties related to the coronavirus pandemic are rather idiosyncratic, with no clear precedence, cure or alternatives. In such a state of “unknown uncertainty”, individuals look for ways and means to alleviate their multifaceted fears by gathering additional information to better understand their current and future pandemic impacted situation. It is for this reason, uncertainties during a crisis fuel the spread of misinformation and rumour-mongering (Guo and Vargo 2020; Oh et al., 2013). However, such instances of fake news were not limited to the time when scientists were struggling to find ways to counter the coronavirus but also when the news around COVID-19 vaccines started hitting the news headlines. For example, DW Fact Check recently debunked many fake scientists’ twitter accounts spreading misinformation about the coronavirus vaccine altering one’s DNA or the news about Peru forcing its people for compulsory COVID-19 vaccinations (Mudge & Weber, 2020). Consequently, we believe that fake news is an ongoing sociotechnical phenomenon. Uncertainties and fears related to the current pandemic or future crises will continue to spawn the propagation and consumption of fake news till concerted eforts are made to understand and arrest its proliferation.

Fake news is an umbrella term that refers to any intentionally deceptive digital misinformation, propaganda, or disinformation (Egelhofer & Lecheler, 2019; Wardle, 2018). It signifies fabricated information that has the format of news but is produced without following the practices of legitimate journalism and the necessary editorial checks (Lazer et al., 2018; Ognyanova et al., 2020). Fake news perpetrators are information producers willing to disregard journalistic norms to advance political agenda, make financial profits, gain psychological satisfaction, or any other form of social gains (Gradon, 2020; Ognyanova et al., 2020). The heightened uncertainty about the economy, employment, relationships, and physical and mental health during the current pandemic is resulting in the spread of misinformation about COVID-19 globally, especially across Internet-enabled social media platforms such as Twitter and Facebook (Brennen et al., 2020; Mahmud et al. 2020; Vinopal, 2020). IT infrastructure, digital connectivity, and social media services provide the necessary means for the fabrication and propagation of fake news. Hence, fake news signifies the “dark side of IT phenomenon” at the macro-level, capable of causing significant losses to an entire society.

However, there is a huge variance in the volume of COVID-19 related fake news propensity across nations. Hence, it is imperative to understand the country-level factors that influence the production and consumption of fake news. An in-depth understanding of such factors can help governments frame appropriate policies to tackle the currently ongoing “fake” news infodemic in addition to the “real” COVID-19 pandemic.

## 2.2. Uncertainty reduction through informational and institutional resources

In a scenario of information divide during a crisis, fake news propagation is fuelled by the people’s need for sensemaking<sup>2</sup> of the emergent, unpredictable and complex developments, which relate to their fears, frustrations, vulnerability, hopes, and identities (Christianson & Barton, 2020; Liu et al., 2016; Posetti & Bontcheva, 2020). Grounding our discussion in sensemaking and uncertainty management literature, we posit that citizens will use two key resources at their disposal for coping with the COVID-19 crisis, namely informational and institutional resources in a country.

Informational Resources. The available informational resources in a nation can be the citizens’ key to reassurance and predictability in times of crisis. Recent growth in mobile Internet connectivity has enabled fast and free flow of information among citizens through social media and mobile chat applications. Using a media ecology perspective, Strate (2004) describes how the informational dimension enabled through Internet connectivity is an important sensemaking mechanism for the society. The malleable and emerging nature of digital technologies in today’s world, ofering 24/7 connectivity among citizens, are seen as the source, message, method, and medium for uncertainty reduction and sensemaking by seeking additional information Dimmick et al. (2011); Gade and Lowrey (2011); Wikström, 2014). Though a reactionary information-seeking behaviour helps the individuals secure a sense of agency during a crisis (Van Ingen & Wright, 2016), it can also be misused to augment citizens’ uncertainty perceptions (Babrow & Kline, 2000). Thus, the technology-enabled informational resource, which on the one hand can support vulnerable citizens during a crisis, also facilitates the demands of cyber deviant actors for spreading misinformation during a crisis such as COVID-19 (Ågerfalk et al., 2020; Doyle & Conboy, 2020; Laato et al., 2020).

We posit that in a crisis when there is relative deprivation of formal trusted communication, or the problem itself is undefined, any form of informational resource may serve as a means for reassurance against crisis-related fears. Prior research has shown that under uncertain situations, citizens make extensive use of the available technology-mediated channels, such as social media, to gather additional information (Liu et al., 2016). Thus, the informational resource facilitated by the mobile Internet connectivity in a nation comprises the first resource that citizens may draw upon during an exceptional crisis such as COVID-19.

Institutional Resources. Governments around the world are grappling with the increasing threat of COVID-19 related fake news. A few countries are relying on diferent sources of electronic media to proactively involve citizens in their crisis management eforts. For example, in Québec, fact-checking services such as the Rumour Detector are available to the public to easily track down any misinformation. This service helps filter out false news and promote information from credible sources (Nafi et al., 2020). Singapore is using the contact tracing app in an extensive way and relying on many technical solutions to combat the infodemic (Ng, 2020). QSearch is using Big Data Analytics to create social intelligence from diverse social data available during this crisis to provide free viral news alerts to the governments of Singapore, Malaysia, and the Philippines (Ng, 2020). Other countries are attempting to reduce the spread of potential rumours by connecting with their citizens through a variety of channels to provide them with reliable information (Choudhury et al., 2020; Guo & Vargo, 2020). For example, the Taiwan government has launched chatbots that reside on the mobile social app LINE. The chatbots are designed to conduct daily health questionnaires, disprove fake news and ofer information on where to buy masks and the current inventory of masks with local pharmacies. Besides, it provides daily updates on local and global COVID-19 infections, educational materials about the epidemic, and latest news regarding vaccinations. The chatbot also ofers a dedicated hotline to call for pandemic related information and services (Ng, 2020). In a similar vein, the Singapore government is tackling the issue of fake news during the coronavirus crisis by communicating regularly with its citizens through popular mobile social apps such as WhatsApp, and promptly correcting misinformation whenever required (Choudhury, 2020).

Notwithstanding the short-term proactive communication measures that countries are adopting during the crisis, we believe that the pre-existing knowledge and beliefs of the citizens within a nation guide their response to any crisis (Couch and Coles, 2011; Ognyanova et al., 2020). During a crisis, the citizens are faced with multiple divergent and ambivalent aspects that are assessed in relation to the situating national context (Babrow, 2001). Citizens’ perceptions about similar uncertainties may vary across countries because of the diferences in the social and cultural construction of the uncertain situation (Bartscher et al., 2020), which to a large extent depends on the citizens’ perceptions of the national institutions in that country. Hence, the uncertainties and fears related to COVID-19 may be perceived diferently across nations depending on the institutional resources available to the citizens.

In the event of a crisis, institutions in a nation can ofer perceptions of crisis preparedness enabled by reliable governance structures that help in developing a resilient society (Liu et al., 2016; Ognyanova et al., 2020). Thus, the existing institutional structures within a country can reassure the citizens by ofering a sensemaking frame to guide their actions during a crisis. In fact, uncertainty perceptions stemming from the lack of adequate institutional structures tend to impact health as well as economic outcomes during times of crisis (Kelly, 2020).

The institutional resources in a nation can be measured as citizens’ institutional freedom perceptions indicated through the freedom of economic, political, and media institutions within a country (Srivastava et al., 2016). Such pre-existing citizen perceptions about the national institutions can reassure citizens about the predictable manner in which the institutions will perform in citizens’ interest during times of crisis (Liu et al., 2016; Ognyanova et al., 2020). The protective institutional resource signifying the citizens’ institutional freedom perceptions comprises the second essential resource that citizens may draw upon during an exceptional COVID-19 crisis.

Together, informational and institutional resources in a nation can impact the cognitive, emotional, and behavioural responses of citizens during a crisis (Hillen et al., 2017) by creating the perception of the present and future order/disorder in the economic and social spheres of the society (Choudhury et al., 2020; Frau-Meigs 2019; Gradon, 2020). The UN Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 16.10 specifying the “public access to information and fundamental freedoms” emphasises the vital role of the informational and institutional freedoms in alleviating citizens’ uncertainties and fears. It provides them with reassurance and predictability during a crisis through collective sensemaking mechanisms (Doyle & Conboy, 2020; Posetti & Bontcheva, 2020). Based on the discussion in the prior sections, we posit that both informational resource and institutional resource will influence the volume of fake news propensity in a nation (Figure 1).

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

COVID-19 related fake news propensity in a nation can be explained by the citizens’ need to make sense of the crisis by seeking more information about the pandemic situation. Armed with additional information, individuals expect to plan and cope better in the face of crisis, providing them with a sense of reassurance and predictability (Ågerfalk et al., 2020). On the other hand, the citizen perceptions about the free working of the pre-existing national institutions, namely – economic, political, and media institutions (Srivastava et al., 2016), will provide a collective sensemaking framework to help citizens construct the meaning of the COVID-19 pandemic for them. If the basic national institutions are free and eficient, citizens would perceive less pandemic related fears and anxieties despite the prevailing uncertainties in the environment. Hence, citizens in a nation with a predictable and free institutional environment may have a lesser need to look for alternative sources of additional information. Grounding our discussion on these basic assumptions, we present our research model in Figure 2 and develop the arguments for the hypothesised relationships in the subsequent sections.

Linking Technological Connectivity to Fake News Propensity – Information Seeking Mechanism. COVID-19 is a unique multifaceted crisis with many “unknowns” related to timeline, health, jobs, economy, and the overall well-being of the people. In the face of “unknown uncertainties”, individuals tend to speculate the outcomes as these could help them cope with the fear and uncertainties associated with the crisis (Daase & Kessler, 2007). To make such evaluative judgements, individuals in an uncertain pandemic situation will actively scout for additional information to help them plan their present and future actions.

Enhanced technological connectivity through the Internet has made it possible for an easy exchange of information among individuals, which could help them make better sense of a crisis (Yang et al., 2013). In fact, mobile Internet connectivity enables citizens to access personalised information conveniently through multiple platforms and social network channels in real-time. Prior research has shown that individuals attempt to reduce their uncertainty perceptions by searching and sharing information (Van Ingen & Wright, 2016; Venkatesh et al., 2016). In times of a crisis characterised by divergent, ambivalent and ambiguous uncertainties, such as COVID-19, technological connectivity can serve as a vital medium that provides the necessary informational infrastructure (Van Ingen & Wright, 2016). However, it is essential to note that it is the “quality” and not the “quantity” of the information shared that determines the final citizen outcomes in times of crisis (Andersen et al., 2010; Welch and Fulla, 2005).

Despite the imperative for quality information, citizens are generally less mindful during a crisis such as COVID-19, compared to normal times and are more likely to use the information resource potential ofered by mobile connectivity for maladaptive coping (Naidoo, 2020). In such situations of vulnerability, extractive coping behaviour may motivate the citizens to always be on the lookout for additional information, making them prone to fake news exposure and consumption (see Carr & Walther, 2014; Shirish, 2018). Moreover, mobile connectivity establishes a new kind of information contract that supersedes the institutional information contract with the public authorities of a country and shifts the users’ attention from “information quality” to “sharing quantity” (Frau-Meigs, 2019). Thus, the spread of misinformation becomes a standard unintentional citizen behaviour (Mirbabaie & Marx, 2020). Mobile connectivity acts a hotspot during a crisis for perceived sensemaking (Mirbabaie & Marx, 2020; Van Ingen & Wright, 2016) and also helps the cyber deviant actors to further their personal agenda of spreading misinformation (Ågerfalk et al., 2020; Doyle & Conboy, 2020; Laato et al., 2020). Recent evidence shows that cybercriminals are indeed taking advantage of the current pandemic situation to target the vulnerabilities of technologically connected end-users by feeding them with unverified information (Naidoo, 2020; Newman et al., 2020). It is for this reason, fake news, instead of alleviating citizen uncertainties and fears, may actually enhance them.

Notwithstanding, actual outcomes of misinformation, the psychologically stressed citizens during the pandemic will need more information to help them make evaluative judgements about the crisis (Godinic et al., 2020; Tull et al., 2020). Better technological connectivity in a nation through the mobile Internet can help in easy and eficient information dissemination and access. Hence, we hypothesise:

H1: The level of mobile connectivity in a country is positively associated with the volume of COVID-19 related fake news propensity in that nation.

## 3.1. National institutions and fake news propensity – cognitive constraints mechanism

Institutions define the framework for moral rules in a society, which through shared informational cognition, shape the citizens’ perceptions of reality to achieve collective prosperity. By making mutual interactions in a society predictable, national institutions define how citizens select, organise, and interpret information (Dequech 2004; Schedler, 2013; Srivastava et al., 2016). Prior studies have shown that collective sensemaking is undertaken through heuristics via an internalised cognitive constraints mechanism that guides the citizens to take appropriate actions in times of uncertainty (Weber & Glynn, 2006). Institutions are antecedents to action formation at the micro level and are therefore described as antecedents to sensemaking. Taken-for -granted ways of thinking make alternatives unimaginable, making actions automatically aligned with the situating institutions (Weber & Glynn, 2006). Such cognitive heuristics are the building blocks of collective sensemaking and provide the necessary templates for actions, scripts, schemas, roles and logic for individuals to act in a society. Though such institutional structures cognitively constrain the citizens’ individual sensemaking, they provide a framework for eficient and harmonised functioning (Weber & Glynn, 2006). Therefore, institutions provide the static and ongoing cognitive contextual mechanisms that guide the individual sensemaking and actions (Babrow, 2001). This is enacted by ofering predictability to the experienced identities (typified actors), expectations (typified behaviours) in diferent contextual frames (typified situations) even during uncertainty (Weber & Glynn, 2006). Thus, sound national institutions that guarantee citizen freedom within the specified framework ofer the much-needed social order, peace, and tranquillity in a predictable and protective manner.

Prior research has shown that institutional structures that guarantee economic, political, and media freedom are the building blocks of a healthy and resilient society (Dequech 2004; Ken Farr et al., 1998; Srivastava et al., 2016). During the COVID-19 pandemic, institutional freedom implies an assurance of governmental transparency, impartiality, honesty, and predictability in crisis management (Berger & Kellermann, 1994; Weber & Glynn, 2006). Citizen trust in the freedom of national institutions alleviates citizens’ uncertainties and fears in times of crisis, thereby cognitively constraining the citizens from individual reactive sensemaking by consuming and propagating misinformation. The cognitive constraints mechanism is like a vaccine that prevents citizens from falling prey to maladaptive coping behaviour in times of uncertainty. In sum, countries where citizens perceive higher institutional freedom have lesser uncertainty and fear perceptions and would rely less on additional Internet-based external sources of information for crisis sensemaking (Birkbeck & LaFree, 1993; Carlson, 2020).

Linking Economic, Political and Media Freedom to Fake News Propensity. The strength of a nation is said to depend upon the economic freedom that its citizens experience (Dunkelberg, 2018; Ken Farr et al., 1998). Though economic freedom is fundamental during normal times (Hanke & Walters, 1997; United Nations, 1976), its significance is amplified during the times of crisis. During a period of continued uncertainty brought about by COVID-19, economic freedom perceptions tend to restore the economic agency and control in the hands of citizens. The presence of free and fair economic institutions will enable the citizens to be confident about their economic welfare during and after the crisis. Prior research has shown that citizens’ economic freedom perceptions are positively associated with growth, investment, peace, and happiness (Hall & Lawson, 2014). Economic freedom perceptions will also enhance the generalised trust in the government agencies, which is instrumental in providing reassurance to the citizens about their welfare during times of crisis (Berggren & Jordahl, 2005). Because of the reassurance and predictability provided by economic freedom, citizens may neither be motivated to search for additional informational resources, nor will they share extraneous information. Hence, we hypothesise:

## H2a: The level of economic freedom in a country is negatively associated with the volume of COVID-19 related fake news propensity in that nation.

Political freedom in a nation has been shown to influence the economic and social well-being perceptions of the citizens in a country (Ken Farr et al., 1998; Schedler, 2013). In times of crisis, such as COVID-19, political freedom will allow citizens to seek information freely and be involved in transparent discussions directly or through political representatives to chart the best possible course of action (Schedler, 2013). A fair chance to add an alternative proposal to any political agenda about the pandemic is an important reassurance that can cognitively constrain the citizens’ actions (Weber & Glynn, 2006) through their anticipated sense of fairness in political governance and welfare (Edmundson, 2020). Because of a sense of political agency and reassurance of their participation in the political process in a free and fair manner, citizens will not be on the lookout for additional information from non-institutional channels. Moreover, the sense of political agency will also instil a sense of responsibility, which will constrain them from consuming or propagating unverified information during the current pandemic. Hence, we hypothesise:

## H2b: The level of political freedom in a country is negatively associated with the volume of COVID-19 related fake news propensity in that nation.

Media is often regarded as the informal, but the most important pillar of national institutions (Srivastava et al., 2016). It is the watchdog national institution, which can highlight any aberrations in the fair application of the specified institutional framework. Freedom in media ensures communication of impartial, free, comprehensive, and factbased information within a country. By ofering pluralism in participation, media freedom guarantees responsibility for reporting truthful information to citizens in times of crisis (Powers & Russell, 2020). In the context of COVID-19, highlighting the importance of free media, the UN Secretary-General commented – “The antidote to this pandemic of misinformation is fact-based news and analysis. It depends on media freedom and independent reporting” (United Nations, 2020b). In a country where media freedom is upheld, there would be less government interference in news reporting. This would ensure that any discrepant information provided by the national agencies could be challenged or refuted by the free media. Such a situation would guarantee citizens of having access to free and fair information related to the ongoing pandemic.

Moreover, media as an independent institution ensures checks and balances on the actions taken by other national agencies and institutions. These checks provide reassurance to the citizens that all other national institutions are working in the best interests of the citizens in times of crisis, and citizens can trust the media to report freely on the developments related to the pandemic (see Ringen, 2007).

Media freedom thus reduces the informational and institutional uncertainty for the citizens. The independent checks on the information and actions of the government agencies by the free press and media reassure the citizens of having access to the right information. Besides, citizens can also be certain that the government agencies are acting in their best interests in times of the COVID-19 pandemic. Hence, the citizens in such countries would not be motivated to look for or propagate additional unverified information. Hence, we hypothesise:

H2c: The level of media freedom in a country is negatively associated with the volume of COVID-19 related fake news propensity in that nation.

The Poynter website hosted 7100 fact-checks from more than 70 countries in over 40 languages when this research was conducted. Control Variables. COVID-19 related fake news propensity in a nation may be related to variables other than the ones hypothesised in our research model. To ensure robustness of our results, we carefully selected suitable country-level control variables by (1) consulting prior country-level studies, and (2) considering factors that might influence citizen “uncertainties” and “fears” related to the COVID-19 pandemic and thus may be related to fake news propensity. Four categories of control variables at the country-level of analysis were identified for our study, namely variables related to economy, population, health, and education. Please see details on all variables in Appendix A.

(1) Economy: Three economy-related country-level factors used as controls in our analysis are (a) gross domestic product (GDP), (b) level of income, and (c) unemployment rate, as these might be related to the citizens’ resilience to crises.

(2) Population: The population of the country in millions was used as a control as this might influence the quality of resource access by citizens.

(3) Health: Two national-level health factors were used as these would determine the level of health-related uncertainties and fear among citizens (a) public spending on healthcare, and (b) quality of healthcare.

(4) Education: The adult literacy rate in the country might determine the access as well as the ability to discern fake news. Hence, it was considered as a suitable control variable in our research model.

## 4.3. Data analysis and results

Appendix B and C present the descriptive statistics and correlations for all the variables used in our study. Because our dependent variable is a count variable, we conducted negative binomial regression to test our hypotheses. Though Poisson regression is often used to model count data, negative binomial regression, which is a generalisation of Poisson regression, loosens the restrictive assumption made by the Poisson model that the variance is equal to the mean (Hilbe, 2011). Appendix B clearly shows the dependent variable “fake news propensity” as an overdispersed variable with variation greater than the mean. Hence, the negative binomial model, as compared to other count models (such as Poisson model), is assumed to be the appropriate model to conduct the analysis (Choudhury et al., 2020; Medappa & Srivastava, 2019; Medappa & Srivastava, 2020). We also performed a variance inflation factor (VIF) test to identify the influence of multicollinearity confounding our results. Because all the computed VIFs were lower than the recommended threshold of ten (Hair et al., 2006; James et al., 2019; Davis et al., 2009), we conclude that multicollinearity is not a confounding issue in our research model.

## 5. Discussion and implications

Overall, we found good support for our proposed model. After controlling for several salient nationallevel factors, the results show significant relationships (in the hypothesised directions) between “informational resource” signified through the extent of mobile connectivity, and the “institutional resource” represented by extent of economic and media freedom perceptions, with the volume of COVID-19 related fake news propensity.

Although we had expected political freedom to aford the reassurance of citizen participation in the political process in a free and fair manner, we found that political freedom in a nation has a significant positive relationship with fake news propensity. This surprising result, however, is in consonance with past instances of political parties using political freedom to manipulate public opinion by spreading misinformation (Press Trust of India, 2018). Prior research has also shown that, in a scenario of political freedom, political parties and politicians can influence and shape public opinion by spreading fake news (Carr et al., 2020). A recent fact file on COVID-19 related fake news reported that a substantial percentage of such instances of misinformation originated from the top echelons, and the spread has generally been in a top-down fashion (Brennen et al., 2020). In sum, our results establish the key role of mobile connectivity and political freedom in increasing fake news spread in a nation, whereas the economic and media freedom is shown to curb its spread.

## 5.1. Limitations and future research directions

Though our study makes several important contributions, we acknowledge a few limitations in our work that can help pave way for future research in this important domain. First, in our study, we considered only mobile connectivity in a nation as the informational resource. But there are other sources of Internet connectivity that may contribute to the informational contagion in a nation. In the current day scenario approximately 3.8 billion individuals, including individuals from the low- and middle-income countries, have access to mobile Internet connectivity out of a total of 4.13 billion people in the world with Internet access (Clement, 2020; GSMA Intelligence, 2020). Nonetheless, it may be worthwhile to investigate the influence of other sources of citizen Internet connectivity such as fibre optics and broad band on fake news contagion. Future research can examine these nuanced distinctions across the diferent informational channels to have a more granular understanding of the fake news phenomenon. Second, in our research we found the salient role of media freedom in inhibiting the spread of fake news in a nation. However, nations may difer in the extent of their citizen media literacy. In our research model, we do control for the level of educational literacy in a nation, but it may be worthwhile for future researchers to examine the role of citizen media literacy in discerning fake news from real news. Similarly, the level of media professionalism in a country may interact with the extent of media freedom to have a significant influence on fake news propensity. For example, it is possible that countries with a high level of media freedom but a low level of media professionalism, might actually witness an increased fake news propensity in times of crisis. Clearly, examining the role of media freedom coupled with media professionalism can be one of the rich avenues for future fake news research. Third, in our research we found that political freedom contributes to the fake news propensity. We did not consider the influence of political professionalism in our research model. However, political professionalism in a country can play an important role in the spread of fake news and can be examined by future research.

## 5.2. Implications for research

Our large-scale cross-country research has several implications that can help us better understand fake news propensity during the times of COVID-19 pandemic and perhaps counterbalance its impact on society and nations.

First, we propose and test an initial theory that conceptualises the key national resources (informational and institutional) required by the citizens – both for reactive and anticipatory coping with fear and uncertainty during the COVID-19 crisis. Our theoretical development moves beyond the curren body of knowledge to ofer a systematic interpretation of the citizens’ sensemaking mechanisms during the current pandemic. It explains how citizens counter their fears and anxieties through reassurance and predictability. Specifically, we contribute to the uncertainty management literature by presenting the key role of national informational and institutional resources as mechanisms for making citizens less or more reliant on the fake news and misinformation. Our cross-country study contributes to the scant literature that links the resources available within national systems to fake news propensity during COVID-19 (Abu Arqoub et al., 2020). In addition, our study incrementally contributes to other similar studies that have investigated societal factors augmenting or attenuating COVID-19 related fake news propensity (Humprecht, 2020; Brennen et al., 2020). Our research also validates the role of media neutrality and independence for efectively building resilience against fake news perpetrated by malicious actors or political parties trying to extend their control over digital societies through polarisation tactics (Humprecht, 2020; Brennen et al., 2020), thereby contributing to recent call to engage in new forms of policy enactments termed – counter-surveillance – to keep a check on deviant institutional actors (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Aroles, 2020; Monahan, 2006). Future research can examine the positive role of technologies and other social, educational, and even religious institutions in combating the growing menace of fake news. In addition, our study can serve as a theoretical guide to inspire future research to understand other types of crises, such as organisational, natural, technological, and financial.

Second, prior research has generally focused on the positive influence of technological connectivity and Internet usage in a nation. For example, the role of e-government in increasing national economic performance (S. C. Srivastava & Teo, 2010), reducing corruption (Srivastava et al., 2016) and increasing government transparency and citizen trust (Venkatesh et al., 2016) or the role of technology in bridging the service divide (Shirish et al., 2020; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015) and decreasing poverty levels (Jha et al., 2016). In fact, technology is considered as the panacea for economic growth and development, and governments are making concerted eforts to increase Internet penetration and mobile phone usage (Haftu, 2019; Niebel, 2018; Venkatesh et al., 2020). Contrary to this dominant perspective, our research highlights the possible negative side of technological connectivity through mobile Internet in a country by serving as a conduit for the spread of fake news and misinformation during a crisis.

Our study exhorts researchers to recognise this dark side of technology and plan for suitable checks and balances to mitigate the negative influence of technological development in a nation. Notwithstanding prior IS literature on the dark side of IT phenomenon with clear implications for individuals (Turel & Serenko, 2012; Turel et al., 2011) and organisations (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018; Srivastava and Shainesh, 2015); Tarafdar et al., 2019), countrylevel of analysis on the subject is rather sporadic (Sahay et al., 2017; Unwin & Unwin, 2017). Our study is one of the few studies that highlights the unintended negative consequences of national technological infrastructure and motivates future research to examine the dark side of IT phenomenon at the country level of analysis.

In addition, the findings from our research extend other studies that have examined the negative impacts of technology induced phenomenon, especially in times of the COVID-19 crisis such as – cyberchrodria; telework related stress, and IS control in society during the current pandemic (Latto et al., 2020; Carillo et al., 2020; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte and Aroles, 2020; Doyle & Conboy, 2020). Concurrently, our study lays emphasis on critically analysing sociotechnical systems to understand their broad policy implications (King & Kraemer, 2019; Rowe, 2018).

Third, our study highlights that national technological and informational resource of mobile connectivity does more damage when used indiscriminately by citizens for uncertainty reduction in times of a crisis. The ubiquity and convenience ofered by mobile connectivity can downplay the role of default institutional sensemaking in citizens, thereby propagating deviant behaviours (Doyle & Conboy, 2020; Godinic et al., 2020). Our work adds to prior research highlighting the prevalence of maladaptive coping behaviours and deviance due to the excessive use of technology during a crisis (Oh et al., 2013; Van Ingen & Wright, 2016). This deviant behaviour raises significant questions about the use of technological resources compared to institutional resources as efective tools for crisis management. In addition, the intensity and kind of crisis may also determine the need for citizen sensemaking by seeking additional information and possibly becoming a victim of “disinfodemic” (Mirbabaie & Marx, 2020; Naidoo, 2020). We believe that such questions can have significant implications for future research that examines the role of technology during crisis management.

Finally, our research highlights the vital role of economic and media freedom in a country in mitigating the fake news propensity during the COVID-19 crisis. To develop long term national resilience against unforeseen crisis situations, governments must focus on developing institutions that ensure economic and media freedom. However, the negative role of political freedom in contributing to fake news propensity underlines the importance of appropriate checks and balances to ensure the sustainability of such systems. Future research can examine the optimal level of political freedom that can ensure all-round, healthy, balanced, and sustainable growth of nations without transgressing the tenets of a free state. Perhaps technology can be a useful tool to attain this fine balance by increasing citizen transparency and public accountability without mandating coercive measures of control (Srivastava et al., 2016).

## 5.3. Implications for practice and policy

Governments across the world consider fake news as a sociotechnical phenomenon requiring contextualised policy and advice tools to combat its spread (Capano et al., 2020; King & Kraemer, 2019). In addition to the aforementioned research implications, our study ofers several implications for practice, especially for government agencies and policymakers looking for ways to combat fake news especially during times of an exceptional crisis such as the COVID-19.

First, our study highlights that citizens at the time of crisis are vulnerable and may seek solace in unverified information circulating on the social media. In the last few years, because of the enhanced mobile Internet connectivity, social media tools have become easily accessible to everyone. This has dramatically increased the chances of citizens falling prey to fake news consumption and propagation. Moreover, prior studies have shown that social media use for information gathering is more prevalent in uncertain environments, such as a health crisis (Latto et al., 2020) or polarised environments, which can push people to look for validation in social media (Ross et al., 2019). Therefore, it is imperative for governments to understand and develop an efective mobile Internet policy that is able to provide the right information to the citizens yet arrest the spread of misinformation. The issue has become even more pertinent in the recent COVID-19 times, which have seen an unprecedented spread of pandemic related fake news. To arrest the spread of fake news, in some situations, governments have resorted to extreme measures such as shutting down the mobile Internet (Human Rights Watch, 2020). But this goes against UN’s sustainable development goals and the basic tenets of freedom. Moreover, mobile applications such as contact tracing apps have proved to be efective in arresting the spread of virus (Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020). Hence, governments need to carefully plan countermeasures for arresting fake news contagion without discontinuing mobile Internet services – perhaps by designing proactive communication strategies to provide the right pandemic related information to the citizens through mobile phones and apps. This would allow governments to leverage mobile connectivity not only for maintaining public health and citizen protection services, but also for fighting against fake news propensity.

Our findings prompt governmental agencies and policymakers to make directed eforts for providing accurate and trustworthy information to the citizens during times of exceptional crisis situations such as COVID-19 pandemic. Easy and timely access to accurate information can protect citizens from misinformation and fake news. As a crisis management measure, we recommend increased government presence on social media platforms, such as Twitter, Facebook, WhatsApp, and Instagram, as this could help in providing credible local content about the pandemic and information on related governmental actions, in real-time to the citizens. Moreover, because most misinformation contagion happens through the social media channels, provision of trustworthy government communication through the same channels may help in addressing the informational needs of the citizens, thereby mitigating the extent of fake news propensity. However, cyber security measures should be in place to ensure that suficient monitoring is maintained in these communication channels so that social bots are not used by malicious agencies to polarise and manipulate opinions amongst collectives in the wrong direction (Ross et al., 2019). Clear national and international regulatory framework to handle cyber deviances and measures to cut down on corruption should act as a complementary policy step in the right direction (Srivastava et al., 2016).

Second, our empirical analysis demonstrates the significant role of media and economic freedom in curbing the spread of fake news propensity in a nation during times of crisis. Governments and policymakers should prioritise their institutional resources to ensure media and economic freedom to their citizens. This will help in developing a resilient society, which is not on the lookout for additional information during times of crisis for alleviating their fears and anxieties. As a crisis preparedness measure, we recommend bolstering government communication strategies that increase media and economic freedom perceptions to arrest uncertainty perceptions from crippling the national peace and prosperity when an exceptional crisis such as COVID-19 strikes. These measures can be seen as protective policy-level capabilities that can come to the rescue of the citizens in crisis situations.

We have seen that prior experience with the SARS outbreak and legitimacy perceptions in governmental agencies acted as a bufering resource to several Asian countries such as Singapore, Japan and China, which could quickly mobilise their agencies to plan and execute coherent, multipronged policies to combat COVID-19 pandemic compared to many countries in North America and Europe (Capano et al., 2020; Sakurai and Chughtai (2020). Similarly, the already existing strong media literacy polices in the Nordic countries such as Finland bufered the citizens from falling prey to fake news consumption and propagation. Much before the COVID-19 crisis, since 2016, the school curriculum of Finland included media literacy and critical thinking, which perhaps served as a protective resource to fight against the menace of fake news during the COVID-19 pandemic (Henley, 2020). We thus believe that along with media freedom, governments should make eforts to boost media literacy among its citizens, which should act as a counter surveillance measure against fake news perpetrators in times of crisis.

Another aspect that impacted the citizens significantly during the COVID-19 crisis was the looming economic uncertainty because of the multiple pandemic triggered restrictions and lockdowns. Most government initiatives to alleviate economic uncertainty among citizens were reactive in nature (Capano et al., 2020). However, our findings reiterate the importance of economic freedom as the fundamental need for enhancing public trust (Woo et al., 2015). Preexisting economic freedom perceptions are the building blocks for stability in a society, which countries can mobilise to maintain citizen trust when a crisis strikes. Results from our study indicate that economic freedom as an institutional resource acts as a protective national-level capability that can ofer collective resilience against fake news propensity. Hence, from a policy perspective, governments should undertake proactive eforts to build economic freedom perceptions among its citizens as a crisis preparedness measure (Capano et al., 2020; Woo et al., 2015).

Third, our findings show the negative role of poli tical freedom as it enhances fake news propensity during time of a crisis. The results inform the policymakers that fake news propensity, especially in times of crisis, can be a challenge for democratic systems. For instance, as reported by Freedom House, the Trump administration repeatedly downplayed the severity of the coronavirus and created the misinformation mist around the pandemic (Repucci & Slipowitz, 2020). Prior research has also shown that in environments imbued with political freedom, there have been instances of political parties using fake news for gaining in elections or furthering their agenda (Carr et al., 2020). During the COVID-19 crisis poli tical freedom was misused by some authorities to propagate and apply lockdown measures in an openly discriminatory manner by targeting marginalised populations as scapegoats (Freedom House, 2020). Such lack of professionalism by some politicians led to greater fake news propensity during the coronavirus crisis. Hence, there is a pressing need for democratic countries to ensure suitable checks and balances on the spread of fake news by political parties. Countries need to develop politically neutral and federated systems to restrain the menace of fake news. While poli tical freedom has been shown to enhance fake news propensity, a distorted image of the national political institutions can also have a disempowering impact on a society during a crisis. This enhanced uncertainty would further push people to resort to informationseeking through the unverified mobile Internet in the hope to reconcile any information asymmetry percep tions about the crisis. Such reliance on technology can lead to enhanced consumption of misinformation (Celliers & Hattingh, 2020). This poses an interesting challenge for governments and policymakers to design the optimal mix of institutional political freedom with the necessary checks and balances.

## 6. Conclusion

Our research highlights the urgent need to arrest the growing infodemic of fake news, which has precipitated significantly during the current COVID-19 pandemic. There is a clear need for governments to plan and invest in tools for identifying misinformation and improving online accountability especially during times of a crisis. Few nations such as France and Germany are combatting the spread of fake news through targeted legislation, while others prefer administrative responses to specific instances (Hartley & Vu, 2020). Countries such as Singapore are using a combination of both – targeted legislations along with coherent government responses grounded in the principles of public transparency (Han, 2020). But we cannot draw conclusions about the eficacy of legislation on the fake news propensity at this stage because very few nations have adopted such measures during the COVID-19 times. However, the findings from our study do underline the need for governments to reflect and reorient their strategies related to mobile Internet connectivity by taking preventive measures to avoid misuse of this potent medium for the spread of misinformation during a crisis. Perhaps governments should plan for more proactive communication with its citizens through the mobile Internet using popular apps and social media tools, which misinformation mongers also tend to use. Another strategy could be to strengthen media and economic freedom in their countries, which have been shown to contain fake news propensity during the current pandemic. Besides, educational institutions and the governments should prioritise media literacy so that people become educated to follow authentic news and exercise discretion in what they watch and read. This is extremely important as general educational literacy did not have a significant relationship with fake news propensity in our sample. A multi-pronged governmental approach integrating Internet security, proactive citizen communication, citizen digital and media literacy training along with the necessary institutional trust building eforts (Machete & Turpin, 2020), could perhaps curb fake news from further fuelling fear and uncertainty during unprecedented crises situations such as COVID-19.

## Appendices

Appendix A.

Description of research constructs.

## Dependent Variable

Fake News Propensity refers to the volume of COVID-19 related fact-checks on misinformation originating from each country in the global database of International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN), Poynter Institute as of June 9 2020. The volume of fake news is measured using the numeric “count data” available from the Poynter website. Source link: https://www.poynter.org/ifcn-covid-19-misinformation

## Independent Variables

Mobile Connectivity refers to the level of mobile internet connectivity in a country. It is indicated by the Mobile Connectivity Index for each country from the Global System for Mobile Communications Association (GSMA Intelligence) Foundation Intelligence Report 2020. The index assesses the performance of countries on the key enablers of mobile internet adoption – infrastructure, afordability, consumer readiness, content, and services. The value is scored on a numeric scale in the range “0” to “100”, where a higher score represents stronger performance in delivering mobile internet connectivity. Source link: https://www.mobileconnectivityindex.com/?source=post\_page #year=2019&dataSet=indexScor

“Economic Freedom” is modelled as a single indicator index comprising twelve quantitative and qualitative factors grouped into four broad categories related to the level of economic freedom in a country, namely -(1) rule of law, which includes the property rights, government integrity, and judicial efectiveness (2) government size, which includes the government spending, tax burden, and fiscal health (3) regulatory eficiency, which includes the business freedom, labour freedom and monetary freedom (4) open markets, which includes the trade freedom, investment freedom, and financial freedom. The values have been taken from the Heritage Foundation Report 2020 where countries based on a composite score are grouped on an economic freedom scale ranging from “1” to “5”, with “1” indicating “repressed” and “5” indicating “free.” Source link: https://www.heritage org/index/about

Political Freedom refers to the freedom related to citizen’s political choice and participation in a country. The construct “Political Freedom” is modelled as a composite country-level index comprising three indicators related to the level of freedom in (1) electoral process (2) political pluralism, and (3) participation in the functioning of the government. The values have been taken from Freedom in the World Index Report 2020. The level o political freedom is scored on a numeric scale ranging from “1” to “7” and reverse coded, with “1” indicating “the lowest degree of political freedom” and “7” indicating “the highest degree of political freedom.” Source link: https://freedomhouse.org/report/freedom-world/2020/leaderless-struggle democracy

Media Freedom refers to the degree of freedom that journalists, news organisations, and netizens have in each country, and the eforts made by authorities to ensure this freedom. The construct “Media Freedom” is measured using Press Freedom Index, the values for which are taken from Reporters Without Borders Report 2020. The index comprises media pluralism, independence, legislative quality related to media and safety of journalists. The level of media freedom is based on scale ranging from “1” to “5” and reverse coded, with “1” indicating “no media freedom” and “5” indicating “complete media freedom”. Source link: https://rsf.org/en/ranking

## Control Variables

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) represents the standard of living in a country and is related to its productivity. The values of GDP in US Dollar (in billions) are taken from Global Health Security Index 2019 Report, which provides these figures compiled from the Economic Intelligence Unit, World Bank, and CIA World Factbook 2019. Source link: https://www.ghsindex.org/

Level of Income represents the earning capacity of a country’s population. The values have been taken from the United Nations Report on the Classification of Government Survey 2018, which divides the economies according to GNI per capita, calculated using the World Bank Atlas method. The four groups as per the report are (1) low income with US\$1,005 or less, (2) lower middle income with US\$1,006 – \$3,955, (3) upper middle income with US\$3,956 – \$12,235, and (4) high income with US\$12,236 or more. The level of income is scored on a numeric scale ranging from 1 to 4, with “1” indicating “lowest income” and “4” indicating “highest income”. Source Link: https://publicadministration.un.org/egovkb/Portals/egovkb/ Documents/un/2018-Survey/E-Government%20Survey%202018\_FINAL%20for%20web.pdf

Unemployment Rate represents the number of unemployed persons measured as a percentage of the total labour force. The data has been taken from the Heritage Foundation Report 2020, which has compiled the most recent unemployment rate data from International labour Organization and World Employment Social Outlook : Trends 2019. Source link: https://www.heritage.org/index/about

Population of a country represents the total number of people in the country (in millions). The values of national population have been taken from Global Health Security Index 2019 Report, which has compiled these figures from the Economic Intelligence Unit, World Bank, and CIA World Factbook 2019. Source link: https://www.ghsindex.org/

Public Spending on Healthcare is indicated by the per capita government health expenditure in US dollars. The values have been taken from the Global Health Security Index 2019 Report which has sourced these figures from the World Health Organization (WHO) expenditure database, Source link: https://www.ghsindex.org

Quality of Healthcare is indicated using the quality of the healthcare index from the Global Health Security Index 2019 Report. It is a composite of three indicators (1) life expectancy measured in years, (2) healthcare access, and (3) quality index frontier score. The construct is measured on a numeric scale ranging from 0 to 100, with “0” indicating “lowest quality of healthcare” and “100” indicating “highest quality of healthcare”. Source link: https://www.ghsindex.org

Literacy Rate is indicated by the literacy levels index from the United Nations Development Programme and the UNESCO Institute for Statistics (UIS) Reports 2019. It is measured as the percentage of literate adults (above 15 years) in a country. Source link: http://hdr.undp.org/en/data
