# Small business digital transformation in the context of the pandemic

Munir Mandviwalla and Richard Flanagan

Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, United States

## ABSTRACT

The pandemic is a major crisis for small businesses. Digital transformation (DT) ofers a lifeline for some small businesses. In this paper, we outline a rapidly conceived and implemented research project to digitalise small business. We apply action design research using the DT lens to intervene and impact 42 diferent small businesses over an intense six-month period. Three detailed case studies illustrate the problem, drivers, mechanisms, and challenges of DT. The results illustrate how small businesses can engage, sell, and deliver using technology, and the factors that influence the transformation process. We also discuss limitations and identify research opportunities in relation to the DT, platform, and small business literature. The paper demonstrates how IS can have an immediate impact on the livelihoods of the businesses in our community during the pandemic.

## 1. Introduction

The pandemic is a major crisis for small businesses, which represent more than half of the world’s GDP (Arnold, 2019). These businesses lack the economic, social, human, and organisational capital to respond to the challenges posed by the pandemic. Retail spaces no longer have foot trafic, markets are closed, service providers cannot directly engage with customers, key employees are no longer available, and the customer is buying less than before. It is not surprising that many small businesses are expected to fail soon or in the near-term (Parilla et al., 2020).

Digital transformation (DT) is rapidly changing industry and society (Lucas et al., 2013; R.W. Gregory et al., 2019), ofering scale, scope, and speed to transform organisations (Bharadwaj et al., 2013). However, research on small business digitalisation is limited. Therefore, in this paper, we ask How can digitalisation transform small business?

The research context is a project to transform small businesses during the pandemic. We apply action design research (ADR) (Sein et al., 2011) to intervene and directly impact 42 diferent small businesses over an intense six-month period. Three detailed case studies illustrate the 42 cases, followed by evaluation, results, and implications. We conclude the paper with discussion, limitations, and future research opportunities.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Small business

Small and medium-sized organisations represent more than 90% of all businesses, about 60–70% of employees, and 55% of GDP worldwide (Arnold, 2019). In the United States, there are 30.7 million small businesses compared to about 20,000 large businesses. These smaller businesses comprise 99.9% of all firms, and 47.3% of private-sector employees (United States Small Business Administration, 2019). The United States Small Business Administration Ofice of Advocacy considers a small business to include fewer than 500 employees (United States Small Business Administration, 2019). However, the specific definition varies by industry incorporating annual receipts of up to 41.5 USD million and the number of employees up to 1500 for industries such as Parking Lots and Garages and Silver Mining (Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, Ofice of the Federal Register and the Government Publishing Ofice, 2020). The majority, 81%, have no employees (e.g., sole proprietorship), start-ups average 5.9 employees, and the overall average for small business is 24 (United States Small Business Administration, 2019). Most small businesses are microbusinesses of 1–9 employees, which consist of about 75% of all firms (Headd, 2017).

Small businesses face diferent competitive challenges (Zimmerman et al., 2002), including lack of scale, which brings challenges that are diferent from larger businesses. Lenders view small business as risky, and the median business typically has 27 days of cash (Hornstein, 2020). During economic downturns, they are disproportionately afected when consumer demand and available credit are limited (Liu & Parilla, 2020). Small businesses are often familyowned, bringing the challenge of mixing family with work. Even though owners have a fiduciary obligation to maximise the company’s economic value, they must also maintain culture and values that are priorities for family businesses. Small businesses often have an advantage in personalisation (Litz & Stewart, 2000), and in ofering products that are physically and culturally close to the customer (Zimmerman et al., 2002).

## 2.2. The pandemic and small business

The pandemic is a major crisis for small businesses. According to Parilla et al. (2020), about 2 million US small businesses employing about 27.5 million people are in the immediate risk category (26%), while another 28% of small businesses fall into the nearterm risk category with additional 20.3 million more vulnerable jobs.<sup>1</sup> Combined, these represent 54% of small businesses (4.2 million total) and 47.8 million jobs, of which about 70% of small businesses in the immediate or near-term risk categories are microbusinesses representing 2.9 million establishments and 8.7 million jobs (Parilla et al., 2020).<sup>2</sup> The City and State Covid-19 stay-at-home orders had a major impact on the above businesses. Especially hard hit are businesses that rely on physical spaces:

● Wine store in which the customer walks in, browses, and then completes the purchase.

● Bridal store that ofers planning, browsing, and a changing room.

● Hair salon that ofers unique services in a social atmosphere.

● Pet store in which animals are dropped of for some service.

● Boutique personal care product manufacturer that sells in flea markets.

● Therapist or music instructor that requires private one on one intense interaction.

## 2.3. Digital transformation

DT is rapidly changing industry and society (Lucas et al., 2013; R.W. Gregory et al., 2019), ofering unprecedented scale, scope, and speed to transform organisations (Bharadwaj et al., 2013). DT definitions and associated research focus on novel products (e.g., Keeley et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010), nature of change and innovation (e.g., Fichman et al., 2014; Nambisan et al., 2017; Westerman et al., 2014), and business models and strategy (e.g., Bharadwaj et al., 2013).

Extant IS DT research tends to focus on larger incumbent organisations (Legner et al., 2017; R.W. Gregory et al., 2019). There are exceptions including Chan et al. (2020) – small business IT-enabled innovation and Mohd Salleh et al. (2017) – small and medium-sized businesses IS adoption. Like larger businesses, small businesses are also incumbent organisations with specific boundaries and fiduciary forprofit responsibilities. Yet, their boundaries are also porous, linked to individual and family capabilities and trends. Porous boundaries could lead to a more open and agile response to the societal trends of digitalisation (Balsmeier & Woerter, 2019), entrepreneurship (Nambisan et al., 2019), and consumerization (R. W. Gregory et al., 2018). The diference in boundary, scale, scope, capabilities, agility, and structure of small business provides a diferent yet important area for expanding DT research.

Vial (2019) defines DT broadly as “a process that aims to improve an entity by triggering significant changes to its properties through combinations of information, computing, communication, and connectivity technologies”. (p. 118). We adopt this definition because it can go beyond large incumbent organisations, is open to the improvements ofered by digitalisation, as well as the more traditional topics of automation and integration (Legner et al., 2017). The definition also encompasses the radical changes aforded by novel products or services (R.W. Gregory et al., 2019; Westerman et al., 2014).

Given the above, our research question is How can digitalisation transform small business? Our focus is on incumbent microbusinesses impacted by the pandemic. The research question is important given the current economic and social environment. It is also important as to-date we have not found formal theoretical or empirical research focusing on small businesses DT. We follow a socio-technical approach (Sarker et al., 2019) recognising that DT is both a human issue involving family and local relationships, as well as economic and digital. Next, we describe the research context and approach.

## 3. Research context and approach

The authors created the research project to ofer digitalisation services to small business in response to the pandemic. Supported by corporate members of a university institute, the services are available at no cost. The research approach is action design research (ADR) (Sein et al., 2011). ADR applies design science (Hevner et al., 2004; Mandviwalla, 2015; Nunamaker et al., 1991; Walls et al., 1992) in the context of iterative design and assessment in an organisational setting. ADR involves three key phases: problem formulation; building, intervention; and evaluation (BIE), and reflection and learning (see Appendix A for details on ADR application).

ADR is relevant because (a) we are intervening in an organisation and (b) developing an ensemble artefact (Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001, Sein et al., 2011), and (c) more generally seek to produce new knowledge about DT. Diferent from prior applications of ADR is our parallel intervention in forty-two diferent organisations in an intense overlapping period of about 6 months. Each organisation initiated the intervention by contacting the team. The research team includes a design science academic and a practitioner with dual experience of close to 40 years in industry and higher education. Twenty-four faculty, alums, executives and 41 students from information systems, marketing, advertising, graphic arts, and design served on implementation teams – the “practitioners” – and were diferent for each project. The students and many of the advisors received compensation for each project. The above combination of intense engagement in diferent organisations with a varied complementary team afords an interesting opportunity to conduct multiple ADR cycles of problematisation, intervention, theory inscription, evaluation, and reshaping across and within diferent organisational contexts.

## 4. Cases

To date, we have received 105 inquiries, 23 were rejected or presented an alternative (e.g., an intern), 17 are inactive, and 64 are in-progress or completed. In this study, we focus on 42 completed cases. Ninety per cent of the cases represent microbusinesses with less than 10 employees, of which 48% provide a service (e.g., yoga), and the rest sell products (e.g., clothing). Table 1 provides a summary; Appendix B details the specific cases reflecting a diverse mix of product and service-centric business, sizes, industries/markets, and their specific primary and second DT goals.

We observed that in the majority of cases: 1. The business owners tend to be domain experts (e.g., knows wine, knows how to cut hair), so knowledge of business functions is not always internal. 2. A few day-to-day operations are outsourced (e.g., accounting, payments) while contractors take care of other tasks (e.g., setting up a website) (McCrea, 2006). 3. Most businesses did not have an ongoing relationship with an IT vendor, with the exception of their Internet provider, so access to digital know-how was limited. A friend or small contractor on a one-time basis set up the initial website or POS. 4. Most had some type of digital or Internet presence, only two – a legal firm (case no. 15) and discount store (case no. 26) had no digital presence. Next, we analyse three specific cases to illustrate the DT process.

BBB (case no. 27) is a sole proprietor boutique cosmetics product business with a unique channel; Pure Eco (case no. 13) has eight employees, larger in scope and size and operates in the mainstream wellness industry, while Uncorked (case no. 35) has two employees, and is a neighbourhood liquor store. We selected these cases from the sample of 42 because they were willing to participate and are diferent in size, market, and product, reflecting diferent value propositions, structures, and processes. Following the ADR cycle approach, next, we describe the problem, building, intervention, and results for each to illustrate the cycles of all the cases.

## 4.1. Brie’s body boutique

Context and Problem: Brie’s Body Boutique (BBB), a single owner proprietorship in Philadelphia, sells all natural products that promote healthy skin and hair. In March 2018, the founder, Sabrina – “Brie” – sufering from dry skin and diabetes started investigating commercially available lotions. Unable to find relief, Brie started making her own body butter. Today, the BBB ecommerce site ofers a range of personal care products including body butter, beard balm, hair cream, lip balm, and shampoo. BBB has about 60–70 customers per month for about 10 total products. BBB is vertically integrated including handcrafting each product, package design, marketing, and sales. Brie understands the value of social media so BBB started a Facebook page and an Instagram account. More recently, BBB started a YouTube channel featuring two educational videos.

However, BBB’s most important marketing channel is the owner – Brie – who is an expert at explaining and promoting the products at open-air “flea” markets in the Philadelphia area. In these markets, BBB purchases table space to display, promote, and sell the merchandise. These markets are BBB’s primary marketing, sales, and distribution channels, with social media and ecommerce as supplements. At the markets, the prospects are out and about interested in browsing products and might stop by given the colourful table and the power of Brie’s personality. Now with the pandemic market closure, it is unclear how to identify and reach these customers. Currently, Brie is delivering phone orders to regulars.

Given the market closure, BBB is interested in improving digital marketing. Brie is unhappy with the ecommerce site, which she set up on her own on GoDaddy. The colour is poor, the product pictures could be better; overall the site does not “pop”. The site does not reflect the image she wants to convey. In addition, to drive people to her website, she wants to create more educational videos and ads on YouTube.

Results and challenges: The pandemic shut down markets, so the main DT focus for BBB is to improve sales. However, the extant website had a problem and new channels were needed. We could not implement the desired look and feel on the website since the

GoDaddy platform provides basic colours and designs. As a workaround, we found a freeware tool to identify the HEX codes from a picture that contained the desired design and were able to edit the backend HTML.

On the other hand, other aspects of the GoDaddy platform were more capable than previously understood. For example, the sizes of each product were listed as separate items in the ecommerce shop (e.g., Body Butter 4 oz., Body Butter 8 oz., Body Butter 16 oz.). We found that the platform has the option to display one product with a dropdown menu to select size. Consolidating the product sizes further improved the look and feel. Brie also purchased a new backdrop to retake pictures of the products, and a friend redesigned her logo.

To identify new channels and customers we trained Brie on the basics of market research. Based on the above, we added Etsy as a new channel and selling platform, this is especially compelling since there is a direct connection from Etsy to GoDaddy ecommerce. To manage costs, only a few popular products are listed on Etsy, and prospects are redirected to the GoDaddy store for products and descriptions.

Brie is eager to learn, especially items that provide direct control over the business. We provided training on the website generator, how to leverage other platforms (YouTube, Etsy, Pinterest), and on market research to identify new channels (e.g., analysing competitors, getting feedback). Overall, BBB now has a second digital channel, a more compelling website, and a simpler ecommerce store to sell more.

## 4.2. Pure eco-wellness salon & spa

Context and Problem: Susan founded Pure Eco-Wellness Salon & Spa in Yardley, Pennsylvania in 2011 by acquiring a hair salon and merging it with an existing facial, waxing, and massage business. Today, Pure Eco also ofers beauty products and classes taking a holistic eco-health and well-being approach. Pure Eco has eight full-time employees with annual sales of about 750,000 USD. Prior to the pandemic, Susan was expecting to grow the business by about 10%.

Pure Eco has an existing website, another (now abandoned) site started by consultants, and a third site started by the owner on Wix. An in-store point of sale (POS) system that is part of a salon specific platform runs the bulk of the business while a diferent POS is used for in-store and online gift cards. Pure Eco also has an active presence on Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram.

Pure Eco’s goals include completing the Wix site, reducing the costs of two POS, closer digital relationships with customers perhaps through a blog, an ecommerce store to sell products, and improved website SEO. Finally, they want to improve their online consult so that complex service requests (e.g., colouring) are matched to relevant materials, stylist, and time slot.

Results and challenges: The pandemic shut down in-person services, so the main DT focus for Pure Eco is maintaining engagement with regular customers. To that end, completing the Wix site and associated blog was simple, since Susan had already organised relevant content from the existing and abandoned sites. Shifting gift cards to the new platform proved problematic since they did not ofer customisation. We learnt that in response to the pandemic the vendor was rewriting the gift card code. For now, it made sense to keep the old POS for gift cards. The new consultation form was also straightforward though it required an upgrade to the Wix subscription to allow clients to upload photographs.

Building the e-commerce store was more challenging. Susan’s preferred salon specific platform does not integrate sales and inventory data with other platforms such as Wix. Given the modest sales volume, we trained Susan on a manual integration approach using Wix for ecommerce. Each new order in Wix generates an email, which serves as a prompt to ring up the sale on the salon platform. Overall, Pure Eco is now able to engage with regular customers, generates revenue during the pandemic, and significantly improves operational and marketing capability using digital tools.

## 4.3. Uncorked by the sea

Context and Problem: Established in Southport, North Carolina in 2011, uncorked by the Sea is a wine and beer retail store with two employees. Uncorked serves a small community of about 20,000 local residents in the winter but several times that in the summer. The husband and wife team of Jef and Meredith became sole owners in 2018.

Prior to the pandemic, Uncorked’s business model was that customers come into the store, engage with the staf about preferences, taste a wine or two, select the wine(s), and complete the transaction. Uncorked sponsored weekly wine tastings with the proceeds going to local charities put out an electronic newsletter with about 2,000 subscribers, and ofers two clubs that send subscribers wines every month. Uncorked’s typical in-store inventory is about 400 bottles. The top 50 SKUs reflect about 80% of sales and about 70% of the sales are walk-in-trafic. Even though the store stayed open during the pandemic, many customers stopped coming. Regulars switched to call-in orders, with curbside pickup and deliveries within a limited area. In-store sales went down, with only the wine clubs producing a steady monthly revenue.

Uncorked has an existing website that is currently being overhauled by a local vendor. However, this vendor does not ofer ecommerce services, specialising on static sites. Given wine industry price sensitivity, they prefer that the ecommerce store is restricted to existing customers. A major concern is managing the in-store Clover POS system in parallel with the ecommerce store. Given the number of SKUs and the amount of data stored about each one, duplicating the information on a second system is challenging.

Results and challenges: The pandemic slowed down in-person sales, so the main DT focus for Uncorked is improving sales. The first challenge at Uncorked was waiting for the local vendor to complete the website, since the owners and vendor saw ecommerce as a separate activity. We found a restaurant plug-in that integrates WordPress, ecommerce, and POS, which was seen as “magic” by Uncorked. Configuring the e-commerce capability and integrating the restaurant centric plugin into the in-store POS was challenging. Further, given the vast options available in WordPress, it was dificult for Uncorked to identify what they wanted. We explored diferent designs, look and feel options, and configuration settings with the client. Still, appropriating a restaurant centric design necessitates compromises. For example, discovering an undocumented 100-item category limit, which made the “All Wines” category impossible. In contrast, the owners were pleasantly surprised to find an excellent order history function. Over time, the goal to restrict access was abandoned after the owner understood the challenge of managing over 2,000 accounts.

Even with the above challenges, customers responded positively. The first 15 digital customers generated more than 1,000 USD of revenue. Further, since e-commerce orders are averaging about 300 USD each, rather than the typical in-store sale of one or two bottles, Jef is now thinking about adjusting his business model to leverage the new digital capabilities.

## 5. Evaluation and results

The individual examples of BBB, Pure Eco, and Uncorked illustrate the DT of the 42 cases. In this section, we evaluate the results. Per ADR, we followed a broad exploratory evaluation approach including 1. “Practitioner” team members wrote a reflection about their experience at the end of each case. 2. The research team reviewed each result including followup questions for the practitioner team and client. 3. The client, typically the business owner or responsible individual evaluated the results. The evaluation consisted of an email with questions about business problem, delivery of the statement of work, results and impact on business, and concerns or suggestions for improvement. We focus on the client evaluations next (see Appendix C for details and excerpts of responses).

Overall, the evaluation across cases is positive with comments such as raise my profile on . . . platforms leading to increased trafic (case no. 17), enable us to attract more customers and capture more revenue (case no. 25), new website with a re-brand is a HUGE win. new year will start out strong (case no. 36). Each business responded positively to the DT. The enthusiasm, speed, and comprehensiveness are encouraging, suggesting that the businesses in our study are confident about and willing to invest in DT. This is important in itself, as we know very little about how small firms approach DT. We believe two factors are driving this trend. First, clearly, the pandemic drove the initial impetus, though the follow-through to completion and use is likely a reflection of enduring interest, which leads to the second factor. The businesses in our sample were only generally aware of the power, low cost, ease of use, malleability, and extensibility of modern digital platforms. The DT project provided a context-specific demonstration, which likely drove the follow-through. We explore these issues further below.

Two of the businesses, however, fell behind in applying the DT. The first is a music school (case no. 38) that we transformed to ofer online instruction, scheduling, and ecommerce. The owner was positive about the outcome; however, soon after going live, the site went ofline. According to the owners, they needed more time to get “comfortable” with what it ofered to generate new customers. The comfort theme repeats for a discount dollar store (case no. 26), who like the music store was enthusiastic about DT, but ultimately (we believe) found the highly structured process of ecommerce less comfortable than operating a cash register. Discussions with the practitioner team who worked closely with these businesses suggest that “comfort” is a combination of computer literacy, familiarity, and the sense that technology will make day-to-day operations more challenging, than doubts about DT value. However, fully identifying these barriers requires additional research. To the best of our knowledge, in the remaining cases, the owners are still applying the DT results.

Though encouraging, the above analysis relies on summary perceptions. To gain additional insights on DT value we considered standard eficiency (e. g., reduce cost) and efectiveness (e.g., increase profit) measures. Yet, we observed in interaction with the owners that these measures may be insuficient or irrelevant for such businesses, whose raison d’être for DT is often mixed. For some, it is about generating suficient additional sales to sustain operations, and less about increasing profit (e.g., case no. 9 – childcare). For others, the business is just as much avocation (e.g., case no. 38 – music instruction) than vocation, so DT is also about increasing interest in the avocation. For yet others, it is about lifestyle (e.g., case no. 19 – yoga), DT is seen as a path to better identify customers with similar interests. Disentangling all of these potential measurement dimensions is challenging.

Therefore, we further explored the value proposition of DT for all the cases. Vial (2019) lists four value proposition paths: which include: (a) new or altered value propositions (e.g., shifting from physical good to services), (b) new or altered value networks (e.g., disintermediation, co-creation), (c) use of digital channels for distribution and sales (e.g., new customer-facing channels), and (d) improving organisational agility and ambidexterity (e.g., optimise process to identify opportunities). Even though larger firms are the basis for the above paths, they provide a starting point. We observed that the primary initial focus of the businesses in our sample was on the potential aforded by digital channels to reach new or existing customers. It was only later after some experience that the businesses considered changing their value proposition (e.g., Uncorked) or exploring new value networks (e.g., BBB). We saw little to no interest in increasing agility.

Given the above, we further explore the digital channel transformation path by categorising and investigating the digital transformation focus of each case, which following Vial’s (2019) definition is the overall thrust of the process that the entity is interested in improving by triggering change. We found three DT foci centred on digital channels: Engaging – connecting with the customer, through marketing, advertising, and relationship management describes 55% of the cases, selling – transacting or facilitating the sale and associated inventory and management activities is 33%, while delivering – getting the product or service to the customer is 12% (see Appendix A).<sup>3</sup> The results further support the intuition that the primary initial transformation path of small business in our sample is interest in digital channels. We chose basic well-known terms because (a) they fit the scale of the businesses in our sample, for whom complex omnichannel, smart procurement, and other digita channel strategies discussed by Vial (2019) are likely out of reach, and (b) provide a simple framework to logically group DT insights and learnings from the BIE cycles (Sein et al., 2011) of the 42 cases. Table 2 summarises the insights relevant to engaging, selling, and delivering. The table also lists issues related to the overall DT process. Next, we discuss these insights.

## 5.1. Engaging

## 5.1.1. Digitalising engagement can augment physical space

Loss of engagement is the most obvious challenge facing businesses due to the pandemic. We found that engagement goes beyond seeing physical space as a platform for completing a sale. Engagement involves social, contextual, and relevant space elements. For example, for Pure Eco, the salon provides a social forum and friendly atmosphere aforded by the ambience of the space, which in turn leads to a better self-image as well as sharing of styles, and eventually transactions. Therefore, we added a blog to remind customers of Pure Eco’s and its ambience. This in turn encouraged regulars to purchase discounted gift cards to relive the experience post-pandemic. Similarly, in an open-air market, customers are in the mindset to purchase and engage in friendly conversation, which works well for BBB to diagnose skin problems and provide solutions. Therefore, we created the “right” look and feel for BBB’s site and store and identified a new “market” (Etsy) to generate sales. Similarly, for Uncorked we focused on “look and feel” – because it was important that their ecommerce site reflected their unique social, contextual, and space identity.

In the case of a boxing gym (case no. 3), we created a new digital process to engage the customer. Prior to the pandemic, potential customers would drop into the retail space, check out the facility, and do a trial workout, which resulted in 80% acquisition. We transformed the process by (a) setting up a social media marketing campaign and (b) importantly, connecting the campaign to an online scheduling process, so that prospects instantly receive confirmation of their choice of date and time for the free trial. We found that digitally conveying ambience, look and feel, and new digital processes represent DT engagement mechanisms. Additional research is needed to identify the full range of mechanisms that engage the physical with the digital. An important question is the relationship of DT and engagement, i.e., does DT ofer a new “engagement enabled” value creation path for small business.

## 5.1.2. Getting to know the customer digitally is a barrier

Small businesses are experts in engaging customers, assessing needs, providing relevant services, and creating a positive experience (Litz & Stewart, 2000; Zimmerman et al., 2002). However, we found that most of this knowledge is tacit, specifically location and demographics. Small businesses rely on customers connecting to a physical space as they go by or actively look for locations convenient to them. Self-selection drives customers not demographic targeting (e.g., flea markets for BBB, neighbourhood or word of mouth for Pure Eco). They find it dificult to specify the demographics needed for cost cost-efective digital marketing. For example, for BBB, the key demographic is females of ages of 30–40 who self-identify by visiting open-air markets and stopping at the booth. Yet, this specification is too broad for digital tools, resulting in prohibitive costs, even when focused on specific areas (e.g., local city). Even with training on how to place digital ads, and the support of loyal customers who were willing to participate in market research, it is unclear if BBB can overcome this challenge. We saw the same challenge in other cases such as an event venue business (case no. 22) and an art gallery (case no. 2). Further, most small businesses ignore digital communities since the physical space provides the default focal customers (e.g., BBB – flea markets, Uncorked – weekly wine tasting). After our assistance, BBB is now targeting online forums and communities (e.g., eczema support group). Overall, to engage customers digitally, small businesses will need fine-grained structured demographic and location data.

In extant DT research, the challenges of tacit knowledge, and specifying demographics and location are ignored. This is not surprising, since for larger businesses, investment in analytics can address these challenges, or as an outcome of changing the value creation path (e.g., building networks). Acquiring, collecting, and maintaining demographic data to apply sophisticated analytics is likely out of the reach for smaller businesses. Therefore, an interesting future research question is whether the digital trace data (Østerlund et al., 2020) generated by the platforms such as the ones described below can facilitate small business engagement with customers.

## 5.2. Selling

## 5.2.1. Exposure to digital platforms accelerates transformation

In digitalising the 42 cases, we observed that digital platforms that enable business today are powerful, low cost, easy to use, malleable, and extensible. They have realised much of the generative, modular, layered, service-dominant future envisioned by IS scholars (Faulkner & Runde, 2019; Nambisan et al., 2017; R. W. Gregory et al., 2018; Yoo et al., 2010). These platforms significantly reduce the need for financial, human, and knowledge capital (Dean & Kretschmer, 2007), and may allow smaller firms to compete on an equal footing with larger firms. The most common platforms relevant to small business include:

● GoDaddy provides web page building, marketing, scheduling, and ecommerce with about 19 million users.<sup>4</sup>

● WordPress with its plug-in architecture evolved from blogging to web sites and online stores to more than 60 million websites today.<sup>5</sup>

● Shopify provides e-commerce and point-of-sale, and hosts more than 1 million businesses.<sup>6</sup>

● Wix ofers a drag and drop website builder, and marketing and e-commerce plug-ins for about 150 million users.<sup>7</sup>

● Clover Network started as a point of sale (POS) device platform, now has 1 million customers<sup>8</sup> ofering “ERP like” capabilities including inventory, customer management, and an APP marketplace for their API and SDK.

We found that businesses in our study were willing and able to invest in these and other related platforms, yet perceived that they lacked the skills to get started. However, just as consistently, we saw this perception disappear by the end of the DT process (see comments in Appendix C). Therefore, one important benefit of our DT process is exposure to the accessibility of modern digital platforms, which can motivate continued transformation.

Still one major challenge remains. The majority were already using a point of sale system (POS). These devices, which most businesses have become comfortable using for many years, are challenging to integrate with current digital platforms, often requiring significant technical expertise. Barring this expertise, the business will have to manage two parallel accounts receivables and continually reconcile inventory, which will inhibit the DT process. We saw this challenge in other cases including a retail-clothing boutique (case no. 34) whose mobile payment system enabled selling at festivals but does not connect with the main POS. As discussed earlier, Pure Eco addressed this issue with a manual process, while Uncorked was able to integrate their POS with WordPress with our assistance. Overall, our observations are good news for platform providers, if they can overcome the initial apprehension, then use will likely increase. However, providers will have to address the

POS integration challenge to accelerate the DT process. There is also an opportunity for design research to investigate automated solutions.

## 5.2.2. Multiple platforms with multiple roles

Related to the above, we observed that if there is sales potential, the businesses in our study were very willing to switch platforms and/or use multiple platforms simultaneously. BBB ended up using both GoDaddy – regular sales channel and Etsy – tap into Etsy community as a channel; Pure Eco continued with the two POS – regular transactions and gift card sales, as well as Wix – consultations; and Uncorked, evolved their WordPress site into an ecommerce channel integrated with the in-store Clover POS.

In contrast to above, extant digital platform research tends to focus on monolithic and singular platforms such as Apple or Blackberry (Blaschke et al., 2019; Brunswicker et al., 2019; Tiwana, 2015). Further, unlike prototypical hardware-centric platforms such as the Apple iPad, the focal platforms in our study are software-centric; they are more servicedominant than goods-dominant (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015). There is a need to extend platform research to (a) consider diferent usage contexts such as small business, and (b) investigate the interesting architectural, design, and economic questions that arise in switching, combining, and integrating across software-centric platforms such as Wix and Shopify.

## 5.3. Delivering

## 5.3.1. Fulfilment can be digitally addressed but there are tradeofs

Fulfilment involves “generating, filling, delivering, and servicing customer orders” (Croxton, 2003, p. 19). At the onset of the pandemic, the cases in our sample did not have a supply chain strategy or logistics capability to fall back on. Some businesses, like Uncorked and BBB, shifted to pick-up or personal delivery, others such as bakeries ofered both (case no. 25 and 30). Still, delivering product in a personal vehicle is likely not sustainable given time, scheduling, cost, and personnel challenges. For Uncorked, the repurposed restaurant plugin fortuitously enabled robust scheduling and pickup with defined delivery areas and add-on fees. Pure Eco, which sells personal care products such as shampoos as part of the salon experience, started investigating drop shipping.

Drop shipping refers to fulfiling retail orders directly by suppliers that are further up the supply chain stream (Cheong et al., 2015), which on the surface seems like a game changer for certain products. However, going upstream can be problematic. In the case of Pure Eco, we found that not all suppliers support drop shipping, while the ones who do, require API codes of varying complexity to integrate with the ecommerce store. Given the complex multi-layered architecture of modern platforms (Yoo et al., 2010), the few lines of API code are beyond the expertise of most small businesses. For example, Pure Eco tried to experiment with drop shipping personal care products but quickly ran into insurmountable technical challenges. Moreover, drop-shipping platforms typically ofer thousands of products such as Modalyst – a Wix partner,<sup>9</sup> most small business owners lack the training and time to formalise procurement.

Service centric businesses such as tutoring (case no. 42), yoga (case no. 19), music (case no. 38), training (case no. 7), and therapy (case no. 8) face a diferent delivery challenge. In responding to the pandemic, such businesses face fear of close contact, social distancing, and the lockdown. We helped such servicecentric businesses adapt available conferencing tools (e.g., Zoom) to fulfil their service, and integrated digital booking and calendaring tools for appointments. For others, we helped move content online behind a paywall. Still, the DT requires major changes in practice such as learning how to demonstrate a yoga move (case no. 19) on a small two-dimensional screen. As we discuss next, it is unclear if all such changes are sustainable.

Small businesses face a fulfilment conundrum: To what extent do they adapt their business logics (R.W. Gregory et al., 2019; Vargo & Lusch, 2016) to leverage available digital delivery options? How do they transform their operations and still preserve their unique identity? Finally, it is unlikely that all digitalised fulfilment options will make sense post-pandemic (e.g., delivery of product in personal vehicle), while others may negatively change the identity of the business (e.g., Pure Eco ofering thousands of products), and still others may fail (e.g., yoga ofered remotely). Yet, these experiments may also lead to new digital business models, i.e., extensive transformation (e.g., successfully ofering music instruction could dramatically increase scale and reach). In the next section, we reflect on the overall challenges and opportunities of DT.

## 5.4. Digital transformation process

The 42 businesses in our study were able to trigger DT relatively quickly. Not surprisingly, the owners led the changes, so the transformation was top-down rather than bottom-up. However, compared to larger businesses (Sabherwal et al., 2001), the triggers were primarily external (e.g., loss of income, Covid-19, new technology) rather than internal (e.g., strategy, business logics, employees). However, we also observed that the small business DT process faces several unique challenges and opportunities.

## 5.4.1. Fear of missing out

FOMO – is fear of missing out – in which the business is fearful of making a DT choice since it may mean missing some desirable benefit. We observed that several of the owners had started or previously considered projects but did not see them through. For example, a vendor left Pure Eco with the impression that optimal SEO is a challenge with Wix so that project was put on hold. BBB had experimented with Facebook, Instagram, and YouTube, yet was unsure which one to focus on. In most cases, the owners were willing to abandon a platform if there was “something better”. FOMO manifests even after a platform is selected, in the owner wanting to know and fully utilise the benefit of every capability. Finally, after the DT is complete, many of our cases were impatient to see results, which would fuel a new round of FOMO. Yet, FOMO also suggests that the businesses in our study are open to change.

## 5.4.2. Many problems many solutions

In contrast to managers in large firms, small business owners have a comprehensive 360-degree understanding of their operation and associated problems. This means that requirements analysis and adoption are relatively frictionless which can accelerate DT. However, this also brings challenges, as small businesses assume that almost every problem has a digital solution. Foucalt’s notion of the double movement can frame this issue – “in which one tries to see how the diferent solutions to a problem have been constructed; but also how these solutions result from a specific form of problematization”. (Barnett, 2015). The business is very aware of what others have called convergence of SMAC – social, mobile, analytics, and cloud (Legner et al., 2017) and consumerization of these and related technologies (R.W. Gregory et al., 2018). However, unlike managers in larger businesses, they see the promise, not the challenges. Most owners thus seamlessly go forward from articulating a problem to identifying a desirable general digital solution, and then back from that solution to another but related problem, i.e., many problems/solutions seeking many solutions/problem.

We saw the double movement enacted at high speed in most of the cases. For instance, Pure Eco was simultaneously interested in SEO, Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, website, adding a call to action, ecommerce capability, digital drop shipping, online consultations, customer engagement through newsletters, email, and blogging, POS, and digital gift cards. The challenge with each was less about identifying a legitimate problem or even understanding the how and why of it, but matching an important problem to a doable solution. The businesses in our study were swift, flexible, and willing to crisscross the double movement – shifting back and forth from major innovation to radical performance improvement to operational changes to considering new digital business models. Therefore, even though the double movement can inhibit the process, it can also expand opportunities. For instance, the pandemic restricts events, so a “wedding party favors” business (case no. 36) lost customers. The owners explored multiple value creation paths with the practitioner team centred on their core competency in printing. The exploration simultaneously and rapidly in 2 weeks considered background, interests, problems, needs, and the myriad solutions that DT ofers. A digital business model emerged that focuses on selling European and Gold Leaf stationery online. Similarly, a custom large scale catering business (case no. 28) changed their model to digitally enabled consumer takeout, for which many there are many accessible digital options. This is interesting since swiftly matching problems to DT options, and willingness to experiment is markedly diferent from larger firms. There is an opportunity to build on DT research to identify the pros and cons of the levers associated with particular value creation paths (Nambisan et al., 2017; Westerman et al., 2014) to guide the double movement. However, the process currently involves trial and error and guesswork, which can easily lose momentum as discussed next.

## 5.4.3. Trees in the forest

We found that small business search cost for relevant solutions to technical problems is high with no obvious constraints (Simon, 1996), i.e., the nonmaterial objects and bearers embedded in platforms facilitate the use of some but not all the relevant material bearers (bitstrings) (Faulkner & Runde, 2019). It is only through fortuitousness that a small business finds cost-efective and timely solutions. This cannot see the trees in the forest problem is about finding the relevant type of tree, not the conventional zooming out to see the forest. Pure Eco, Uncorked, and BBB, and almost every other case faced this issue.

In the case of BBB, the underlying platform does provide easy to use site creation tools and source code access. However, conveying image is challenging, involving advanced HTML, graphic design, typography, layout and accessibility, emotional, and cultural considerations. Therefore, small businesses like BBB tend to ignore the problem leading to pausing or abandoning the process, or seeking friends/contractors for help. This can be problematic. For example, a friend may try to solve the problem by changing the code, end up taking a long time to fix unintended consequences, and create a long-term downstream problem. Alternatively, a contractor, specialising in design (only) may recommend a pre-built theme, which requires a small but critical software modification to operate correctly. Uncorked experienced a similar but diferent challenge. They had recently contracted a small local firm that specialises in web design not POS systems or ecommerce. When we started the DT, for Uncorked, the website (WordPress), their POS (Clover), and a new ecommerce capability were three separate problems requiring three diferent solutions, bringing with it the challenge of duplicate inventory and accounts.

The forest of many problems-many solutions is relatively easy to see for the small business owner, but the relevant tree is elusive. The modular and layered architectures of today’s platforms greatly expand capabilities by separating and arranging internal components in concert with external components. However, they come with a cost. Knowing the “ideal” arrangement of components is dificult to master. The environmental scanning skills needed to harness the most relevant solution for a particular small business niche are scarce. In contrast, larger businesses have access to a more expert and more diverse ecosystem, and their scale provides a search time cushion. Unlike the traditional make vs. buy problem, where the users embarks on a largely independent journey after making the initial buy, the reprogrammability of today’s platforms is available at any stage, and constantly providing new opportunities and obstacles (Yoo et al., 2010). Therefore, it is important to research if small business ecosystems can ofer ongoing support for the above challenges.

## 5.4.4. Ecosystems are critical

We were able to address “trees in the forest” challenges in our cases because of access to a large university ecosystem of experts, alums, and corporate partners (e.g., BBB – web design expert – freeware colour tool, Uncorked – WordPress – plugin to integrate three problems). The ecosystem also provided the credibility and authority to address FOMO and to focus discussion of many problems and many solutions. So how do smaller business access such ecosystems? Perhaps service exchanges such as industry or regional cooperatives can fill this need. Further, the notion of service platforms that co-create value in a service ecosystem (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015) is close in spirit to the needs of small business. The service ecosystem literature provides relevant concepts such as density and integration that focus on mobilising, optimising, combining, and bundling resources (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015). However, in the above, resource availability is a given, perhaps reflecting the reach of larger businesses, suggesting the need for additional research on conceptualising the role of ecosystems for small business.

## 5.4.5. DT punctuation process is sharper

In the 42 cases, we observed that FOMO, many problems and many solutions, trees in the forest, and ecosystems can inhibit or accelerate DT, a process of punctuated equilibrium (Romanelli & Tushman, 1994). Following R.W. Gregory et al. (2019), we posit that the small business transformation process will reflect an interplay of evolutionary and punctuated change with periods of complete stagnation. After the initial trigger, such as the pandemic, recurring issues (e.g., reduction in customers), new or newly discovered platform capabilities, or other environmental events, the business will seek out resources to start reaping value from digital capabilities, eliciting a revolutionary response, the results of which will fuel use during an evolutionary period of fine-tuning and adjustment. In the forty-two cases, the DT was completed rapidly in 2–3 weeks. However, it is unclear if the speed is a function of the pandemic or the inherent agility of smaller businesses, or a combination. Eventually, the transformation will become stagnant or generate a new trigger given FOMO, many problems many solutions, trees in the forest, and the criticality of ecosystems. Unlike larger firms (Sabherwal et al., 2001), we expect that the punctuation will be sharper in both the highs and lows. For example, even within the short time period of our cases, we found that digital tool usage could become completely stagnant (e.g., case no. 38 – music school, case no. 26 – discount store). Further research on the punctuation process is important to disentangle the above DT inhibitors and accelerants in the context of factors such as inertia, resistance, culture, and values (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Svahn et al., 2017).

## 6. Discussion

In this study, we applied ADR (Sein et al., 2011) to explore DT of 42 diferent small businesses – primarily microbusinesses impacted by the pandemic. The simultaneous intervention in a large number of organisations aforded the opportunity to rapidly problematise, learn, and reflect which we believe leads to more generalisable learning and outcomes. Overall, we observe that across a variety of businesses, DT is welcome and has the ability to generate value.

More specifically, the study shows that focusing on “digital transformation” is an important research question for small businesses, definitely in the context of the pandemic, but also potentially post-pandemic. Applying Vial’s (2019) building blocks, there is a disruption, the businesses want to change, there is interest in digital technologies, and it is possible to influence the value creation path. Further, the digital technologies, especially platforms that target small business have matured to a level that can accelerate the transformation. However, the value creation path of smaller businesses is unique, focusing initially on digital channel basics such as engaging, selling, and delivering, and over time expanding to explore new digital business models. Still, there are challenges, which if managed can also be opportunities such as digitalising engagement to augment physical space, getting to know the customer digitally, use of multiple platforms, and digitally addressing fulfilment. Further, the DT process is sharply punctuated with various inhibitors and accelerants such as FOMO, many problems many solutions, trees in the forest, and access to ecosystems. Compared to the hierarchical layers, processes, and structural constraints of larger organisations, smaller businesses can quickly trigger DT, and more easily transform using multiple platforms for multiple roles but find it dificult to identify the relevant trees – solutions – in the forest of digital options.

The research has several limitations. First, our results are limited to microbusiness, which represents about 75% of all firms (Headd, 2017), instead of “small and medium size enterprises (SME)” which can include up to 500 employees. Second, given that our sample of businesses is diverse, it is possible that the insights are too generic or only applicable to a subset. Third, to our knowledge, this is the first application of ADR to large numbers of simultaneous projects. The process was successful; however, it is unclear if multiple simultaneous projects fit the original notion of BIE cycles (Sein et al., 2011). Fourth, according to experts, the pandemic has accelerated the ongoing trend of digitalisation (Nadella, 2020). Therefore, though we believe our work will provide insights post-pandemic, this will require further research.

## 7. Future research

IS has generally ignored research on small business and especially microbusiness digitalisation with a few notable exceptions (e.g., Chan et al., 2020). Yet, such businesses represent more than half of the world’s GDP (Arnold, 2019). Beyond the economic importance and social obligation to study small business, we also found that they are remarkably open to research. We identified several future research opportunities above. In this section, we identify a more general research agenda.

## 7.1. Digital transformation value

DT provides new value propositions, networks, and channels and increases agility, which in turn positively influence operational eficiency and organisational performance (Vial, 2019). We observed three interrelated issues unique to small business DT. 1. Eficiency and efectiveness measures may be insuficient or irrelevant for small business, whose raison d’être is often mixed. 2. Small improvements can generate major impacts such as digitalising a process to reduce labour requirements (e.g., reduce the need for one part-timer). This creates a measurement challenge as even small changes can have outsize impacts. 3. Given FOMO, many problems many solutions, importance of ecosystems, and the ongoing expansion of multiple platforms taking on multiple roles, small businesses are uncertain how to value all the options. Therefore, small business DT needs frameworks and tools that can assess value, trade-ofs, and guide the process.

## 7.2. McDonaldization of small business

According to Ritzer (1992) eficiency, calculability, predictability, and control are the key elements of McDonaldization, i.e., the process in which the principles of the fast food restaurant rationalise other businesses and society. DT generates value by making operations more eficient, calculable, predictable, and controllable, i.e., by McDonadlizing. If small businesses shift engagement, selling, and delivering to an all-purpose platform, how will they retain their identity? What is the “cost” of retaining the identity? Alternatively, will small business DT lead to the emergence of platforms modelled on Grubhub? Grubhub and similar platforms provide benefits to consumers, yet they also homogenise products and shift supplierpricing power – a form of McDonaldization – that is increasingly causing dissatisfaction (Kelly, 2020).

## 7.3. The duality of smallness

Consistent with prior literature, we observe that smallness has a duality, a positive as well as negative impact. However, DT changes the calculus. Prior literature details how smallness influences mission and culture, customers and competition (e.g., Zimmerman et al., 2002), outsourcing (e.g., McCrea, 2006), and personalisation (e.g., Litz & Stewart, 2000). Consistent with the literature we also found that smallness begets agility but limits personnel and expertise, which in turn influences DT issues such as access to ecosystems and ability to see the trees in the forest. Further, vendors that service smaller businesses also tend to be small with similar limitations. However, the rapid expansion of sophisticated, easy to use, relatively low-cost digital platforms is changing the smallness calculus allowing smaller firms to access the scale of larger firms. This phenomenon is already starting; we did not see a substantive diference in DT in our sample, which includes sole proprietorships, firms of 2–10 employees, and a mix of product and servicecentric business operating in diferent markets. Further probing the smallness duality is an important research question.

## 8. Conclusion

The pandemic is a worldwide crisis. DT ofers a lifeline for many small businesses. In this paper, we outline a rapidly conceived and implemented research project to digitalise small business. This paper is one of the first to focus on small business DT across a range of diferent businesses. We analyse the results of 42 cases to identify new insights and suggest future research. The paper demonstrates how IS can have an immediate impact during the pandemic on the livelihoods of the businesses in our community. We hope that our exploratory work will inspire colleagues to impact their neighbourhoods while conducting impactful research on small business digitalisation.

## Appendix A ADR

## Problem formulation

The researchers engaged with each organisation to describe an overall problem space. The initial perceived problems by the organisations varied from loss of retail access to customers to a choir that is unable to practice or perform. We played a major role in framing the problem on a per project basis and in connecting the specific problem to a class of problems and the relevant technology. For each specific small business instance, some problems have obvious solutions such as setting up an ecommerce store for a retail store, while other problems are more challenging (e.g., vegan bakery that no longer has access to their facility).

## Building, Intervention, and Evaluation

We followed an iterative and interwoven organisation dominant building, intervention, and evaluation approach. The implementation teams who are diferent from the researchers “build” the solution, comprise of one project advisor and one or two student(s). Each team starts by working with the organisation on narrowing down the specific problems and solution options identified initially by the researchers. This process continues until we reach tentative agreement on the problem(s) and solution(s). The researchers then participated in finalising the agreement. Next, the implementation team conducted a two-week agile sprint to solve the agreed upon problem(s). The sprint involved rapid reciprocal shaping in which each iteration of the artefact led to refinements in the problem and solution spaces. In parallel, each team posted short status updates after each meeting on a collaboration tool shared across all projects. The status updates are important because (a) they provide evaluation, (b) an opportunity for the researchers to share generalised problem or solution suggestions with each team, (c) to intervene if needed, (d) for teams to directly share with each other.

The resulting artefacts varied including search engine optimisation and website update, ecommerce, online gift cards, POS integration, and a transformed digital business model implemented on a new platform. The roles were always separate and clear, the research team was interested in reflection and learning and project success, while each implementation team was focused on achieving the agreed upon organisational goal. Yet, through the collaboration tool and direct engagement, we mutually influenced each other. In other words, the mutual influence and learning occurred both between the researchers and implementers and between the diferent implementation teams. The evaluation included (a) rapid cycles of building and evaluation between the implementation team and the client, (b) informal evaluation of progress between one of the researchers and the implementation team, and (c) a formal evaluation questionnaire sent to the client post-completion.

## Reflection and Learning

Reflection and learning occurred at multiple junctions. As we ramped up the number of projects, we abstracted each problem and its associated solution into broader classes of problems and ensemble solutions. For example, at the onset, we assumed that the general DT problem was adoption and use, i.e., transitioning from physical to online. However, it turned out that most clients already had some type of online presence. Therefore, we evolved our thinking to describe diferent types of problems and solutions. Finally, we lightly tried to guide each project based on the rapid evolution of our understanding of the problem domain.

Given the unique context of working on a myriad multiple sets of problems, and varied solutions, we formalise the learning as a series of insights presented in the results section. The results map into the problem, design, and kernel theory aspects of ADR.
