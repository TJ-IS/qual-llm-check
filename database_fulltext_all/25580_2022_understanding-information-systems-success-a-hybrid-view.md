# Understanding information systems success: a hybrid view

Jennifer Jewer and Deborah R Compeau

Faculty of Business, Memorial University of Newfoundland, St. John’s, Canada; Department of Management, Information Systems and Entrepreneurship, Washington State University College of Business, Pullman, USA

## ABSTRACT

The information systems (IS) success model, introduced in 1992, provided IS research with a comprehensive set of dependent variables for project success. While the model addresses both process and variance considerations, the latter has dominated the research. Concurrently, the benefits of hybrid theories have been discussed in the literature, and there have been calls for an integrated view of IS success that includes the process perspective. We build on this momentum by presenting a hybrid model based on a longitudinal case study of the development and implementation of a patient-flow decision-support system at a large not-for-profit hospital. Our model remains true to the DeLone and McLean framing but elaborates on the process elements. The hybrid model expands our ability to analyse multiple dimensions of IS success and integrates diverse research findings into the IS success model, providing a revised version for future research to extend.

## 1. Introduction

Organisations rely on information systems (IS) to support their operations and enable growth. However, realising the intended outcomes of these systems can be dificult, so there is ongoing interest in understanding the factors for success. Success of a new information system can be understood in terms of the project (e.g., on-time, on budget), the system (e.g., high quality system that produces expected benefits), and user responses (e. g., satisfaction or resistance) (Wilson & Howcroft, 2002). DeLone and McLean (1992, 2003)) model is dominant in the literature (hereafter referred to as the D&M IS success model), having been examined by over 180 studies (Petter et al., 2008). It focuses on the system and user and identifies six success factors: a) system quality (SQ), b) information quality (IQ), c) use, d) user satisfaction, e) individual impact, and f) organisational impact. We conceptualise IS success, then, as the positive net benefit generated from using the system; it includes user satisfaction, and the individual and organisational level outcomes resulting from system use (i.e., Seddon’s (1997) process interpretation of IS success).

The IS success model was created based on both variance and process considerations (DeLone & McLean, 1992, 2003), but the variance view has dominated the literature (Petter et al., 2008). Burton-Jones et al. (2015) highlight the lack of a well-developed process model which could “add a layer of meaning and insight to IS success” (p. 673). Developing a process view of IS success is important because while a variance perspective focuses on the what (i.e., net benefits), a process perspective focuses on the how and why. A process model shows activities and events that occur over time to generate the variables that we consider as denoting “success”. For example, although IQ and SQ variables have been shown to influence use and ultimately IS success, these variables and their relationships may depend on activities and external influences that occur before the system is implemented or during implementation and use (e.g., management turnover, communication during the implementation process, and so on). Without a clearer picture of these activities and events, a variance view of IS success provides an incomplete and potentially misleading view of how IS success is achieved.

This paper develops a hybrid process/variance view of the IS success model by building on models proposed by Burton-Jones et al. (2015). Our hybrid model incorporates process and variance views in the same model, following the recommendations of Ortiz de Guinea and Webster (2017). It explicitly includes events that make up the process of IS success and the variables that reflect success. While researchers have argued historically that you cannot combine diferent views in a single model (e. g., Mohr, 1982; Newman & Robey, 1992; Seddon, 1997), this notion is increasingly challenged (e.g., Burton-Jones et al., 2015; Langley, 1999; Ortiz de Guinea & Webster, 2017; Peterson, 1998; Sabherwal & Robey, 1995).

Developing our hybrid model answers the question: “How does IS success unfold over time?” We approached this question by: a) elaborating the process of success – necessary since this view has received scant attention in the literature, and b) integrating this process view with the more established variance view to produce a hybrid view of IS success, to better reflect the original intent of the D&M IS success model (DeLone & McLean, 1992, 2003). Through an indepth case study of the development and implementation of a patient-flow decision-support system at a large not-for-profit hospital, we analyse the process of IS success and construct a hybrid model of success that remains true to the original D&M framing but elaborates on its process elements. Our model incorporates an updated interpretation of the model concepts (i.e., events, variables, contextual influences) along with their relationships, and integrates these with findings from the literature to provide a generalised hybrid view of IS success.

## 2. Theoretical development

## 2.1. Process and variance theorising

There are significant diferences between process and variance views of theory (Mohr, 1982) that scholars have identified and discussed in relation to IS research (Markus & Robey, 1988; Newman & Robey, 1992; Sabherwal & Robey, 1995). The primary emphasis in a variance view is how variation in one or more dependent variables can be explained by a set of independent variables, while a process view seeks to understand the sequence of events that make up a phenomenon. The variance perspective examines the relationships between the dependent and independent variables; however, it “often neglects to ‘explain’ exactly how or why the predictors and outcomes are related,” (Newman & Robey, 1992, p. 250). A process view helps explain why variables are related by describing the events that connect them (Pentland, 1999). It enables the researcher to examine how the elements emerge, evolve, and interact with other events over time to produce outcomes (Langley & Truax, 1994). Preserving the sequential structure of the data enables researchers to take context into consideration (Langley, 1999; Pettigrew, 1992).

Nearly 80% of the articles on IS impact in leading IS journals employ a variance view (Paré et al., 2008). Many of these have called for further study of process models (Burton-Jones et al., 2015; Markus & Robey, 1988; Mohr, 1982; Newman & Robey, 1992; Paré et al., 2008; Sabherwal & Robey, 1995; Shaw & Jarvenpaa, 1997). We address that call in this research.

## 2.2. Process and variance views of is success

The IS success model (DeLone & McLean, 1992), as shown in Figure 1, “recognizes success as a process construct which must include both temporal [process] and causal [variance] influences in determining I/S success” (p. 83). DeLone and McLean (2003) reiterated the process considerations, which have “important implications for the measurement, analysis, and reporting of IS success in empirical studies,” (p. 11).

The variance view (Figure 2) has been used in 90 empirical studies. Yet several areas require more research as the “science of measuring information success or performance in empirical studies has seen little improvement over the last decade” (Petter et al., 2008, p. 258).

Notwithstanding the broader support for the variance model in the literature, and consistent with Petter et al.’s observations, Burton-Jones et al. (2015) claim that concepts and relationships in the variance model are insuficiently specified and this restricts how the theory can be used to analyse a situation. They propose a process model with a sequence of three events – create system, use system, and consequences of system use (Figure 3) to clarify these elements. This simple understanding, while appropriate for the illustrative purposes of Burton-Jones et al. (2015), however does little to explain the process and how it unfolds.

The incompleteness of the process view of IS success both stems from and reinforces the dominance of the variance view. With attention focused on the variance elements, the process view has not been developed further since its inception, but the simplistic nature of the current model fails to provide the theoretical richness needed to support a dialogue between the views.

The value of true hybrid models, combining process and variance in a single model, has recently been acknowledged. Ortiz de Guinea and Webster (2017) challenged the assumptions underlying typical process and variance distinctions, specifically addressing the examination of IS use. They propose that it contains both process (i.e., a pattern that occurs over time) and variance (i.e., a variable captures the breadth or frequency of use) characteristics. They suggest that “hybrids are particularly suited for addressing research questions that tackle notions of change (Van de Ven & Poole, 2005) – a property of process – and notions of outcomes or impacts – – a property of variance approaches” (pp. 40–1). This logic also applies to IS success involving changes in the success dimensions like IQ, SQ, use, and user satisfaction over time as the system is created and used, and the consequences associated with system use. Ortiz de Guinea and Webster (2017) also suggest that hybrids are suited to the examination of “events that trigger certain changes in states or outcomes” (p. 41), and to “research questions that include time, as well as elements of antecedents, impacts, events, and processes” (p. 41). All these elements are important to understanding IS success and are part of an integrated process/ variance hybrid model.

## 3. Research methods

We used a longitudinal case study to examine IS success as a hybrid model. Our case study took place at a hospital over an 18-month period during the development and implementation of a new patient flow decision support system (Dashboard). A single case study provides the depth to capture contextual detail in the processes, but sacrifices some generalisability, relying on “the reader to judge the transferability of the ideas to other situations” (Langley, 1999, p. 695). Through iterative analysis of this case, and integration with existing literature, we develop an empirically grounded conceptual model to serve as a foundation for future research.

## 3.1. Research context

The setting is a 400-bed community teaching hospital in Canada. It operates a busy emergency room (ER) with almost 98,000 annual visits, and has an occupancy rate of over 90%, making efective and eficient patient flow management a critical success factor.

Dashboard integrates several systems to track patient movements and displays key metrics including length of stay, unit occupancy, and organisational census, on a centralised dashboard in real time. Its goal is to create a holistic view of flow by allowing staf in diferent departments to see the same information at the same time. Dashboard was implemented using an iterative approach. Our study took place during the first five phases as users and functionality were added incrementally over 18 months.

Management identified three key user groups for the system-personal care managers (PCM), patient flow team, and senior executive team. PCMs coordinate and approve inter-hospital discharges and admissions. The patient flow team manages patient flow by liaising with various departments and agencies to avoid delays in patient admission, transfer, and discharge. The senior executive team uses patient flow data to support tactical and strategic decision making to manage the development and provide oversight of integrated hospital programmes.

## 3.2. Data Collection

We used a mixed-methods approach for data collection (Table 1) to examine system success. The total potential user group was approximately 50 people, all of whom were invited to participate. Twelve formal interviews and three focus group sessions were conducted with 18 system users at diferent implementation phases of the system. The interviews, lasting on average 30 to 45 minutes, followed a semi-structured interview guide. Interview sessions were recorded, transcribed (\~80 pages single-spaced) and anonymised. All qualitative data were stored using QSR’s NVivo 10. The focus group participants consisted of senior leadership, clinical directors and managers, and patient flow specialists. After the focus groups, participants completed an open-ended questionnaire about the advantages and disadvantages of using the system, barriers to use, and impacts of the system. We also reviewed project documents and engaged in ongoing discussions with the project team, the steering committee, and the vendor. During Phase 3, we conducted an online survey of users and potential users of Dashboard. The surveys were based on validated measurement scales from prior research and new measures incorporated from the hospital context, as determined by the focus groups and interviews. Though the sample size is small (n = 20), it represents 41% of the population of potential users and we are confident of its representativeness.

We presented findings from the data collection to project management and the vendor at two points in time, after Phases 2 and 3 of the implementation. The data collection was an iterative process of collection, analysis, and then more data collection based on responses, until further data collection and analysis yielded no new explication of concepts (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Discussions with the management and vendor revealed that it was not necessary to conduct further interviews with users of the system through subsequent phases of the project because system use did not increase.

## 3.3. Data analysis

We first constructed a detailed narrative of the activities and events (Langley, 1999), and then developed a more abstract model from our analysis of the narrative. We used the narrative strategy to understand the ways the process elements of “create” and “use” interact and generate consequences. This approach is suited to our hybrid analysis, since the focus is on how the system is created and used, not just on outcomes. This kind of qualitative inquiry is particularly suited for process analysis because it requires descriptions of how people engage with each other and a description of the process from diferent viewpoints. It is appropriate in situations where the process cannot be easily summarised on a rating scale, and participants perceptions are key (Patton, 1990).

We used visual graphical representations to capture the narrative. This allows for the presentation of large amounts of information and efectively shows precedence, parallel processes, and the passage of time (Langley, 1999). We also incorporated the idea of discrete versus continuous events from Ortiz de Guinea and Webster (2017) toolbox for modelling hybrid approaches.

Using the simple process model of IS success proposed by Burton-Jones et al. (2015) as a sensitising device (Charmaz, 2003), we started by coding the data into the broad categories of the IS success model – Create, Use, and Consequences. All data were independently coded by the authors and discrepancies were discussed until agreement was reached. We adapted the process and rules of Sabherwal and Robey (1995) for identifying and extracting event and activity descriptions from our interview text. Our coding included a broad range of quotes, including past and future events, both generic (i.e., “we sought input”), and specific (i.e., “we had a kick-of meeting last September”) activities. It also included quotes that described aspects of these elements even if they were not activities per se.

Next, we created a table for each interview, focus group, and questionnaire that listed the coded data. Appendix A shows an excerpt from the coding for Interview 1. After verifying the coding, we created summary tables for each element (i.e., Create, Use, and Consequences) and each data source.

We then created visual representations of the create and use processes (our approach to handling consequences was diferent and will be explained below). The diagrams were completed by one of the authors and verified by the other. We found that when participants described the process by which the system was developed, they focused on the collective action of the development team (e.g., we held early scoping discussions), rather than on individual actions. Thus, we modelled the create process in terms of the role it plays in IS success as existing at the level of the collective (the development team) rather than as a collection of individual processes. While such a collection of individual processes might be relevant for other research purposes, for the purpose of understanding the IS Success model, treating “create” as a collectivelevel process is adequate.

On the other hand, we found that individual-level use processes were partly dependent on user roles. The PCMs tended to use Dashboard to coordinate the movement of patients to and from their units. Members of the patient flow team used it to assist in managing patient flow within the hospital, and the senior executive team focused on high-level oversight.

Top management and the vendor did not use the system, so these five interviews did not require a use diagram. Therefore, we created a separate use diagram for each of the six users interviewed. The diagram for Interview 1 is shown in Figure 4.

We analysed the individual use diagrams and developed a general model of a typical use process. As Newman and Robey (1992) explained, every activity “cannot be considered of equal theoretical importance, so (like all theories) the model is a simplification of reality and treats diferent events in diferent ways” (p. 253). Using this approach, we were able to compare individual use diagrams to look for common sequences of activities and consequences and integrate them into a typical usage view at the individual level of analysis.

As we analysed our data, consistent with Burton-Jones et al. (2015), we found that consequences were best conceptualised as variables reflecting states of various entities, for instance, feelings of mistrust in the data experienced by some users. As a result, consequences were integrated into our overall hybrid model as outcomes. We coded each of our interviews as reflecting high, moderate, or low levels of the variables based on how participants discussed outcomes. Having this coding of individual and organisational consequences allowed us to analyse how the activities that made up the create and use processes influenced the level of the outcomes represented by these variables.

We created codes for each phase of system implementation to capture diferences between phases. We supplemented the information from the interviews, focus groups, and the associated questionnaires with data from the online survey, ongoing communications with the vendor and management, and project materials. To cope with the statistical limitations of the small survey sample size, we confine our quantitative analyses to descriptive statistics. This analysis provides a validation check on the qualitative data, by showing that interview participants’ views were reflected in the survey data.

The final step was to create an integrated representation of the entire process throughout the diferent phases (Appendix B). This analysis shows the changes in the process and events throughout the phases, and how the actions and outcomes in a phase influenced the actions and outcomes in subsequent phases. We also noted antecedent conditions and external events that shaped the context of the system implementation.

## 4.1. Create system

Illuminated through the process view, we found that specific activities during the create process influenced the use of the system and vice-versa. Our model shows the create process as having two efects, one on the development of the IT artefact (Link 1 in Figure 5), and the second on the process of use (Link 2). The efects are due both to technical and to social activities in the create process. Formally, we defined create activities as actions undertaken by a person/people that contribute to the construction of the system. This broad definition encompasses technical activities, such as iterative development of Dashboard, and social activities, such as “project information communicated” or “hold user engagement sessions”. The impact of the social activities during the create process is not captured in the variance-based IS success model.

The first efect of the create process is the creation or update of the Dashboard system with IQ and SQ properties. These variables<sup>1</sup> can be considered as both actual properties of the system and perceptions that form when the user experiences the system. The second efect of the create process was generated by the social activities. These activities contribute to the development of the system by influencing the decision to use the system. Our detailed model (Appendix B) shows specific activities in the organisation, but as these activities are likely to difer across organisations and software development methodologies, our conceptual model does not include them. Development team activities exerted a positive influence on system use in the first phase of implementation. The implementation team held user engagement sessions to gather system requirements, and this communication helped increase system use. Participants knew that the software was being introduced in a phased manner and they appreciated it:

## 4.2. Use System

Analysis of the system use process revealed three main themes: a) the importance of distinguishing humancomputer interaction (HCI) processes like how to access the system and its features, from task-related decisions or actions related to work practice; b) the role of SQ and IQ in changing the use process or the specific behaviours and characteristics of use, rather than simply degree of use as theorised in the variance view, and; c) recognition of collective use and its importance, especially given the nature of Dashboard. Previous research has focused on individual aspects of these themes but has not done so holistically, addressing the components in terms of the use process rather than the process of IS success. Our study synthesises them and integrates them into the IS success model.

## 4.2.1. HCI versus work practices

We found that the two diferent aspects of use were influenced by diferent properties of the system and had distinct impacts. Whereas HCI was influenced by SQ, the integration of Dashboard into work practices was influenced by both SQ (directly and through HCI)

and IQ. This is consistent with the findings of Burton-Jones and Grange (2013) who diferentiated between physical and surface structures, like how a user accesses the system, and the deep structure or how well the information represents the work domain. User experiences of the HCI process influenced their incorporation of Dashboard into work practices, and both aspects of use influenced their satisfaction with Dashboard.

Accessibility issues like SQ afected how users could interact with the system in Phase 1 in terms of HCI (Link 3). Participants found it dificult to access the system in Phase 1 because they had to log in with a password. Also, there was no native mobile version of the system, so its usability on a mobile device was limited.

These limitations resulted in a lack of satisfaction with the interface, shown in our conceptual model in Figure 5 as part of individual consequences, which in turn influenced how well Dashboard was incorporated into work practices, resulting in low use in the Phase 1 system.

The password restrictions were removed in Phase 2 and the perception of SQ increased. With the barrier to use removed, people increased system use for work practices and became aware of other IQ and SQ dimensions, as reflected in the detailed model in Appendix B. Some issues with accessibility remained, however, as a mobile version was not created.

## 4.2.2. Impact of SQ and IQ on use

Examining the variance concepts SQ and IQ with the process view enabled us to see not only the efects of these dimensions on use, but also how those efects were created. The dominant sentiment of the interview and focus group participants was echoed in the survey data (Table 2). For each of the SQ and IQ questions we asked, respondents indicated moderately positive views. These variables facilitated use of the system when the SQ and IQ met their needs and slowed down use of the system when these variables inhibited their ability to manage patient flow. Moreover, the users changed not only the extent of use, but also the use process (Link 3).

There were some positive aspects of SQ, but overall it was problematic primarily because of accessibility issues in Phase 1 and deficient system functionality. As discussed, accessibility issues afected the HCI aspec of use and the functionality dimension of SQ afected the work practices use. Users appreciated that Dashboard provided an overview of the entire organisation. They also found it easy to use, with colourcoding to flag important information (see Table 2). Several users also reported intuitive system navigation:

You click on the area you want to see, and you can drill down a little bit more. So, the interface is pretty simple to use. I don’t find it dificult (I-1).

These things helped increase use of the system for patient flow, but although users saw the potential benefits of the system, its lack of functionality made it less relevant to the work and therefore less attractive to use. It provided a good overall picture and was easy to use, but it did not provide the capability to initiate patient moves, and the lack of advanced decision support capabilities made it less useful for most participants. Even though it was called a decision support tool, it did not include any real decision support features:

With Dashboard it’s what is happening right now, we can’t do anything about it, we have to be really reactive about it . . . I just have to deal with it as opposed to proactive about any decisions I need to make. (I-1)

Furthermore, users indicated that they could get the information they needed to perform their jobs from sources other than Dashboard (Table 2). The relative dificulty of accessing the application, as compared to the thrice-daily email on bed status that participants received, made usage an issue.

This illuminates the impact on use of having competing systems, an issue insuficiently addressed in the literature. Most prior literature assumes that systems exist in isolation. Even in models that include relative advantage, this has largely been treated as identical to perceived usefulness (Wang et al., 2011). Our case study shows that a system’s value in the context of other systems in the organisation is a key issue. Attempting to understand the value of a system in isolation limits our understanding of use whenever multiple systems are available.

In terms of IQ, the dimensions relating to content and availability of information had a large impact on system use for work practices. IQ was especially important for our users since Dashboard’s aim was to provide summary information to various groups. Despite some positive IQ dimensions, such as an easyto-view format of the data with colour coding to show status and real-time access to information from multiple sources, several IQ issues negatively impacted use. Not all the abbreviations used were clear to users, usage was less intuitive, and data quality problems resulted in users mistrusting the data. Mistrust is a particularly interesting influence in our model because of the way it evolved over time, something a variance view of IS success does not easily uncover. As they tried the initial prototype, users expressed concerns about information validity; we learned that data problems arose in part due to inaccuracies in feeds from the existing systems and these in turn arose from process issues at the hospital. By Phase 2 the data issues and feeds from other systems had been addressed and the data were accurate, but mistrust of the data persisted despite the absence of actual data quality issues (see Table 2 and Appendix B).

## 4.2.3. Collective-level Use

Our discussions with users about their interactions with Dashboard quickly surfaced that use included individual and collective activities. Thus, we added collective use to the model. This addition parallels the separation of individual and organisational consequences and reflects more recent thinking about the nature of IS use. We use the multilevel lens adapted from Morgeson and Hofmann (1999), terminology from Klein and Kozlowski (2000), and guidance provided by Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007), Zhang and Gable (2017), and Negoita et al. (2018) to analyse use at the collective level. Following these guides, we assert that collective use of the system emerges from instances of individual use (Link 4), and we acknowledge the role of interdependencies-in-use as a precondition to collective use.

The emergence of collective use “is shaped by different configurations of task, user, and system interdependence between instances of individual-level IS use” (Negoita et al., 2018. p. 1282). The hospital intended to use Dashboard to provide tight system coupling to support high task interdependencies between those involved in patient flow management. We discuss each of these types of interdependence in the following paragraphs.

Task interdependence was high. In the hospital setting, especially with high capacity and frequent situations of overcapacity, issues of patient flow cannot be managed within one floor or unit, so team task interdependence is needed. Patient flow must be managed holistically and often involves decision makers working collaboratively in multiple areas to optimise bed usage. Users with diferent roles used Dashboard diferently, with each group’s tasks dependent on those of the other groups. PCMs focused on using Dashboard to manage flow in a particular unit, whereas the patient flow team took a wider view. Dashboard provided the structure for the emergence of the collective use from lower-level interactions. The users’ tasks required them to interact with other users. Users would call other employees and discuss how to manage patient flow or check the validity of the information presented in the system by discussing with others.

User interdependence in patient flow was also high as it involved sharing information and mutual adjustment of bed assignments among staf in different areas of the hospital. The intention of Dashboard is to help the management of patient flow move from siloed tasks – ways of working that result in limited coordination – to having all the staf involved in patient flow working as a tightly coupled team. Examination of the work practice activities revealed how system use as part of work practice is connected to broader tasks of communicating, such as “Initiate Conversation” and working with others to manage patient flow like “Take Action”:

[After checking Dashboard, I] call someone to validate the info, [for example] do we look like we’re not getting any discharges at all from who you’ve talked to, and have you talked to the in-charge nurses because this is what the system is telling me. (I-1)

Working with others to manage patient flow is a form of collective use. Although the hospital implemented Dashboard to create tight system interdependence and networked use, there was loose coupling at the system level because of Dashboard’s limited functionality.

From these high levels of team task and user interdependence, coupled with the not-yet tight system interdependence, there emerged a pattern of collective use over time. In Phase 1 collective use was limited by system accessibility and data quality issues, so few people used Dashboard to manage patient flow. Since the system had little impact on anyone’s actual job until Phase 2, it did not result in any meaningful collective use. However, once these issues were fixed, the positive individual consequences from satisfaction with Dashboard and the holistic picture of patient flow led to more individuals using the system to manage patient flow, and collective-level use emerged from this individual-level use. This collective use of the system led to further discussions on how to resolve issues that were identified:

[Dashboard] really triggers a lot of my conversations across the organization, depending on the director, the manager, depending where the pressures are it outlines it, or it kind of gives me a sense of direction where to put my attention for that time period. (I-5)

However, the lack of tight system interdependence restricted the emergence of substantial collective use. The data in Dashboard was viewable, but changes to patient flow still needed to be entered in the bed management system; this decreased individual use and slowed down the emergence of collective use. For example:

I don’t like that Dashboard seems to be sort of a oneway communication, meaning that it doesn’t seem to integrate with the current systems that we use very well . . . the fact that it doesn’t communicate with [the bed management system], I think it gets disregarded because there isn’t a real purpose right now. (I-4)

Collective use of the system also influenced individual use (Link 5). As more people used Dashboard together to manage patient flow, this encouraged more individual use of the system. For example:

If get a call from emerg that there are too many patients down here . . . . Prompts me to see what is happening overall in the organization, is there any additiona capacity [I2]

In summary, collective use emerged from the individual to the collective level through networked use (Negoita et al., 2018). The identification of collective use is a primary contribution of our study.

## 4.3. Consequences of systems use

Consequences of systems use are conceptualised as individual and organisational-level variables and occur because of the create and use processes. Viewed this way, they include the psychological outcome of satisfaction from the D&M model as well as individual and organisational benefits and other outcomes, both positive and negative, depending on the purpose of the system. Create and use activities link to the variance view through consequences (Burton-Jones et al., 2015). This is consistent with Beaudry and Pinsonneault’s (2005) Coping Model of User Adaptation in which individual eficiency and efectiveness or states of the individual are outcomes in the process model.

## 4.3.1. Individual-level consequences

We found several individual-level consequences of using Dashboard. As discussed in the previous section, the SQ and IQ variables of Dashboard afected use of the system, and this ultimately afected user satisfaction with the system. Negative influences persisted throughout the implementation, resulting in low to neutral feelings of satisfaction with the system. Data quality issues also resulted in mistrust of the data that persisted throughout the implementation, even after the data issues were fixed.

Only the holistic picture of flow represents an individual performance improvement consequence. This is not surprising, considering how little the system was used, primarily attributable to Dashboard’s lack of functionality, since its view-only capability did not allow users to make improvements in managing patient flow. Having the data “all in one place” was nonetheless a recurring positive theme throughout the implementation of the system, particularly after the password restriction was removed in Phase 2. This relates to the IQ dimension of completeness and was a major goal of Dashboard. The strength of the tool was in taking data from multiple internal sources and using it to create an overall picture of the hospital’s status:

## I like that [Dashboard] gives you an immediate glimpse of what the entire hospital looks like. (I-4)

We have represented user satisfaction as an individual consequence resulting from system use, which represents not only goals like individual and organisational benefits, but any outcome associated with use of the system (Link 6). This is consistent with Seddon’s (1997) process interpretation of IS success where user satisfaction and individual and organisational impacts are outcomes of a process that begins with use of the system. While the variance model of ISS includes satisfaction separate from other impacts, there is support in the literature for not separating it from other dimension of IS impact (Gable et al., 2008).

## 4.3.2. Collective-level consequences

Our model also captures how collective use emerges from individual use, similar to Nan’s (2011) complex adaptive systems model of IT use. While D&M acknowledged the existence of benefits at the individua and organisational levels, they only considered use at the individual level. But our analysis, consistent with prior theorising on use (e.g., Negoita et al., 2018), highlights the multilevel character of both use and consequences. One collective consequence, continuity of patient care, emerged from the team’s use of Dashboard, which is collective use. Collective-level consequences emerged from collective-level use (Link 7), rather than from individual-level consequences. The consequences were diferent at the individual and collective levels, and thus we did not find the direct relationship between individual and collective consequences that Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007) proposed. More individual-level consequences did not necessarily mean more collective-level consequences. This fits with the team task interdependence (Negoita et al., 2018) that we found in the case study, where collective performance is not reducible to the sum of the individual performances. Interestingly, in the survey, the participants rated the organisational benefits or collective level consequences from use higher than they did individual benefits (Table 2). This supports our finding that collective-level consequences emerged from collective use, rather than from individual-level consequences, and reinforces the importance of including collective-level use in our conceptual model. As the system was used jointly to manage patient flow, this collective use resulted in an increase in the ability to coordinate continuity of patient care (see Table 2, Co4. to Co6.).

The connection to positive collective-level consequences for the hospital was tenuous. There was some mention of improving continuity of patient care, but the degree of change was minor. When asked about the overall impact on patient care, this quote represents the sentiment of most users:

I think that one of the benefits is that we may be able to get faster transfer times from the ED to the inpatient units . . . . With respect to improving patient care, you’re getting them in the right spot in a faster time in a nicer location or nicer setting. (I-4)

The lack of consequences for the hospital may be attributed to the fact that there was limited collective-level use of Dashboard, thus limited collectivelevel consequences were felt.

The goal of system use for management was to improve the flow of patients; however, at the time of our data collection this consequence had not yet been realised:

It’s not yet a decision support tool. And I think in the end it will be. Once all the data is correct then I can use it to support decision making. Right now, it’s supporting theories of what might be an issue. (I-6)

## 4.4. Feedback efects

Our case study demonstrated the continuous nature of the Create→Use→Consequences process at both the individual and collective levels. Feedback loops from the resulting consequence variables impacted the create and use processes. The variance view showed the relationship between the system’s IQ and SQ properties and use and consequences, while the process view illuminated these relationships and helped us to see how actors drive or respond to activities. It showed how feedback was initiated, how consequences afected the create and use processes in the current and future phases of the implementation, and how consequences could have lingering efects on future use.

At the individual level, we saw how consequences influenced create (Link 8). Users enacted feedback by reporting issues to the development team, which resulted in updates to the system in subsequent phases. Some issues slowed down the development of the system in subsequent phases, whereas others reoriented the process (Langley & Truax, 1994) by changing the development priorities. Each phase of development started with planned functionalities; however, as issues were reported by users, further modifications were added to the development plans, which extended the implementation time. For example, the data issues in the Phase 1 system resulted in much rework by the development team. This was noted as one of the biggest challenges for the project, especially as the data problems revealed underlying process problems in the hospital:

Then [the vendor] highlighted process errors (the accuracy problems), the environment had a high resistance to change. The system highlighted problems and people were afraid of losing their jobs. [The project lead] didn’t want to implement a change because it highlighted a problem in [their] area. (I-12)

Commenting on the speed of the development process, one of the people on the implementation team said:

. . . the most challenging . . . [issue] is that data quality [is]not within our control . . . . So, I would say that’s a challenge, and also just, the speed at which we . . . are rolling out the tool to actually have it adopted by users. I think it could be a lot quicker. (I-9)

The reported issues also reoriented the create process, as initially the system was to be rolled out in three phases, but this was changed to five phases as users reported issues on data quality and lack of functionality to the development team.

We also saw that individual-level consequences afected use (Link 9) as users reflected on their usage and adjusted their actions over time. The consequence “holistic picture of flow” facilitated continued use of Dashboard. For example, this user called it a “trigger point”:

So, the system . . . gives me a quick overview, it kind of gives me a sense of direction where to put my attention for that time period. If there’s great pressure from one programme, then I divert my attention to that programme. So, it’s good in that system, I guess trigger point. (I-5)

The data also indicated that consequences slowed down use. Low feelings of satisfaction, constrained by the limited functionality of the system, reduced future use of the system in the current and subsequent phases. As the capabilities of the system like the ability to drill down on an area of the organisation in Dashboard in Phase 4 expanded throughout the phased implementation, satisfaction increased, resulting in increased use.

As noted earlier, the consequence “mistrust of data” had unexpected and lasting consequences throughout the implementation of the system. As one user reported:

I’m still not sure of the data, so I look at it, it gives me information, but there have been enough times that the data has been wrong that I can’t really rely on it at this stage in the game. (I-6)

The lingering sense of mistrust was exacerbated by the lack of communication on the changes from the development team to the users. User perceptions that communication about implementation of the phases and the lack of ongoing issue support seemed to slow down their use of the system:

I never really hear back that [something] was fixed or wasn’t fixed. I don’t know if that’s outside, whoever is managing it on our side, or whether it’s maybe [the vendor] isn’t telling them if they’ve fixed it. I don’t even know does [the vendor] get the information. (I-6)

At the collective level we saw feedback efects from collective consequences to the create system process, to individual use, and to collective use. In terms of the feedback efects on the create process (Link 10), when management recognised they were not achieving the desired impact on the coordination of patient care, they requested additional functionality for the system. For example, they attributed some of the low level of collective consequences to the lack of system use due to emails sent directly to users three times a day. To fix this issue, management requested that manual email be replaced by automated emails from Dashboard with a link to Dashboard to alert users when certain levels of capacity were reached (Phase 5).

The data also indicated that collective-level consequences influenced individual and collective-level use (Links 11 and 12). As users saw the benefits to the hospital, they felt encouraged to use the system for their own understanding and to support the collective activities of patient flow, even if they had not traditionally thought they were responsible for patient flow:

There’s been a bit of a culture change on the importance of using data to make decision-making, and I think there’s a culture change that access and flow is the responsibility of many people in the organization not just one department that may have that title. (I-1)

## 4.5. The context: antecedent conditions and external influences

Unlike the traditional IS success model which focuses on the IS artefact itself (IQ and SQ) and the outcomes of use, our model extends IS success theory to include the social context. The context has been identified as being under-studied and representing an important gap in our knowledge in understanding the determinants of IS success (Petter et al., 2013). The context throughout the project, including antecedent conditions (Link 13) and external influences (Links 14 and 15), impacted the creation and use of Dashboard. This is another advantage of using the hybrid view, as these contextual influences would not be captured in an examination of the IS success model from a pure variance or pure process view.

Antecedent conditions reflect the history before the project (Newman & Robey, 1992). The specific antecedent conditions, such as the presence of existing systems and processes, would depend on the context in which the system is developed and used. In the hospital we identified the hospital’s strained capacity, the existing systems that fed Dashboard, and the concurrent development of the computerised patient order entry (CPOE) system as among these conditions. The antecedent conditions provided the impetus for the creation of the Dashboard system. At the time when management was deciding to implement this system, they were under increased pressure to manage patient flow, the hospital was operating over capacity and had been for several years, and there were multiple systems used to manage patient flow, resulting in information silos that made it dificult to gain a holistic view of flow. However, other systems, such as the CPOE system, were being implemented at the same time and this also slowed down the implementation of the system:

There are so many things going on that it just isn’t as important . . . . There are many competing priorities . . . so perhaps leaning to the slower implementation as well. (I-2)

The antecedent conditions also slowed down the use of Dashboard. For example, the capacity issues that triggered the leadership team’s interest in implementing this patient flow system, interestingly also limited the usefulness of Dashboard for some users since they already knew that the beds were over capacity (I-2 reports that they had been “in escalation” 80% of the last few months) and there was nothing that they could do about it. Moreover, the existing systems that fed Dashboard did not capture the current situation in real time, which presented problems when using this information to populate the Dashboard, and thus slowed down use.

In addition to impacting individual use, the antecedent conditions also impacted collective use and thus collective consequences. We looked for evidence of synergistic efects in the collective consequences (Negoita et al., 2018); however, we found that they were constrained by the same organisational context that limited the usefulness of Dashboard. This reinforces the importance of considering the antecedent conditions in the IS success model.

Beyond antecedent conditions, we observed several external influences (Langley & Truax, 1994; Ortiz de Guinea & Webster, 2017). These were defined as either discrete or continuous events within the organisation and outside the Dashboard project that shaped the context within which the system was created and used. Human resource changes, for example, were discrete events that impacted the create activities. A new CEO was hired within months of the contract being awarded to the Dashboard vendor. This exerted a positive influence on the process followed to adopt the new system because the new CEO recognised the need for a new system to improve the patient flow process. Other human resource changes at the beginning of this project (i.e., CFO left, a new project sponsor was appointed) slowed down the create process because the CFO had been a champion of the project, and the new project sponsor needed time to learn about the system. The Dashboard implementation proceeded without further major external influences until Phase 5 when the project lead resigned. This had a reorienting efect on the development of Dashboard. When hearing about the resignation, the vendor commented that “we lost a sponsor” (I-12) and would need to “hav[e] a meeting with [the hospital’s development team] to try and reset” (I-12).

The continuous events, including the need to manage patient flow, the existing bed management system, the daily bed management meeting, and the thricedaily email reporting of patient volumes afected the create and use processes throughout the implementation phases. Use was influenced by the users’ need to manage patient flow. This need triggered staf to use Dashboard but also to use other systems when the need outweighed the performance of Dashboard and the availability of other alternatives. Most users expressed a limitation to usefulness, in that the data were typically also available in other systems, some of which better supported their ability to carry out their tasks. This was particularly true for the front-line staf:

At this stage it’s just a visual tool. For my purposes, I can’t do anything with it. Whereas with [the bed management system] or any other sort of electronic board, that we move patients back and forth in the system. That is just a “this is what is happening at this time” (I-3).

Many users used the thrice daily email rather than the new system to manage patient flow, and the bed management meeting did not incorporate Dashboard into its processes, continuing to use the emails:

We also receive an email three times a day from our patient access and flow group letting us know what our census is at (if we are in escalation etc.) so maybe it’s a habit. That is there in my hand, I don’t have to go to a website or bookmark or whatever, so I probably read that email more than I go to [Dashboard]. (I-2)

Overall, these conditions set the context for the development and use of the system in an environment where Dashboard did not appear to be a priority for the hospital; this slowed down the development and use of the system throughout all phases of implementation.

## 5. Discussion

Our longitudinal case study of the iterative development and implementation of a hospital patient flow system examines how IS success unfolds over time. Our hybrid model emerged from the case; it expands our understanding of multiple dimensions of IS success and integrates diverse research findings into the IS success model. Our conceptual model shows IS success unfolding over time through parallel and interlocking processes – Create and Use – that are intertwined with variables (IQ, SQ, and consequences), and influenced by a dynamic set of contextual elements (antecedent conditions and external influences). The processes are interlocking because information flows between them, and consequences from one process may impact other processes. The processes are described as parallel because systems were not created once and for all but changed over time. The evolution was particularly pronounced in our case study because it followed an iterative development approach with more and faster cycles of feedback, but experiences of using any system would be expected to influence its future evolution through this feedback loop.

Our research provides an empirically grounded conceptual model combining process and variance views of IS success. The incorporation of a process perspective provides a greater understanding of IS success, as it enables the retention of more information in the model, and the sequential structure shows relationships more clearly. The variance and process views thus reveal diferent but related information about a theory. The variance view shows the relationships between the variables, and the inclusion of the process involved in creating IS success helps to explain why the variables are related (Pentland, 1999). Our findings support Ortiz de Guinea and Webster (2017) proposition that the hybrid view is appropriate for situations that tackle the notion of change and events that trigger changes in state or outcomes and antecedents. Such an approach enabled us to increase the explanatory capability of the IS success model.

While the activities we observed over the case study are somewhat unique to the specific setting and system we studied, more generally we found that a certain combination of activities in a sequence resulted in certain consequences and influenced subsequent activities in the create or the use process. We acknowledge the limitations on the generalisability of our findings due to the reliance on one case study; in the following discussion, we present the contributions of our study and identify some areas for future research to highlight novel theoretical issues raised by our hybrid model of IS success. We hope this will inspire researchers to investigate these questions and serve as a reference to frame future research.

## 5.1. Contributions for theory and future research

## 5.1.1. Clarified conceptualisation of create, use and consequences

Our examination of IS success as a hybrid model enabled us to clarify Create System, Use System, and Consequences of system use. IS success literature treats IT development as a black box, so connecting consequences to the create process, in addition to the use process, shows how specific consequences emerged from diferent parts of the process. Identification of the social create activities, in addition to the technical activities, enabled us to see how both types of actions influence system use, and ultimately how those activities afected the consequences. This contributes to the IS success literature by clarifying: a) the relationship between social characteristics during the system implementation process and IS success (Petter et al., 2013), and b) the scope of the create process that Burton-Jones et al. (2015) included in their simplified process model of IS success. An extensive body of literature discusses the impact of the implementation process on the system that is created; we contribute additionally by integrating this understanding into the create process in the IS success model. Since the specific steps in any create process will be influenced by the methodology chosen, we recommend that the first step in applying our hybrid model should be to represent the create process through the activities defined in the formal methodology. Future studies could examine other development methodologies to help explicate the events in the create process and explore the nature of feedback within projects and across projects.

We clarify the conceptualisation of use, which has focused on time spent or features used, by looking at three dimensions of use that emerge from our hybrid model – HCI versus work practices, the nature of use, and individual versus collective levels of use. First, we extend the measurement of use in the IS success model beyond the use of the system’s information (DeLone & McLean, 1992) to incorporate how users interact with the system. Both the HCI and work practice views are consistent with Seddon’s (1997) definition of IS use as “an event in a process leading to individual or organisational impact” (p. 243). We provide empirical support for Burton-Jones and Grange (2013) proposed influence of HCI on users’ abilities to conduct work practices with the system, and the influence of these two aspects of use on eficiency and efectiveness. Second, diferences in use revealed from examining the system properties in combination with the process view showed how the SQ and IQ variables afected not just the extent of use, but also how the system was used For example, password restriction afected user interactions with the system (HCI) and reduced their usage for work practices, and when users perceived IQ issues like data quality, they needed to check the accuracy of the data before they used it. Third, our model captures the multi-level nature of IS use. Prior research tells us that the benefits construct in the IS success model is inherently multi-level; however, until now the IS success model has not examined usage at multiple levels. Adding collective use exploits the multi-level nature of the D&M model. In this way, we provide empirical support for Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007) model of multi-level IS use and integrate it with the IS success model. We thus provide a foundation for future IS success studies to incorporate multi-level use.

Our findings also help to clarify the meaning of consequences, an issue in both variance and process views of IS success (Burton-Jones et al., 2015). Viewing consequences as variables provides a clear link between the processes and their outcomes (Sabherwal & Robey, 1995). This enables us to understand how sequences of events and activities influence outcomes; understanding which activities and events lead to success is the primary objective of the IS success model. Our analysis shows consequences as a part of an iterative sequence with create and use rather than the termination of a linear process. Consistent with the process perspective, our model enables entities to change over time and shows how they influence and are influenced by activities and events. We also saw how efects change over time. The IS success model does not theorise how soon events occur after one another. However, over the 18 months of our case study we saw diferences in the timing of consequences. We found that the adverse impact of events or activities can persist even when an issue is resolved.

We also found support for D&M’s conceptualisation of consequences at multiple levels, something missing from Burton-Jones et al.’s (2015) process model. Our data indicate that impacts emerge at the collective level from collective use. This contradicts Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007), who proposed that collective-level consequences would emerge from individual consequences. We assert that this is because the dependent variables or consequences were diferent at the individual and collective levels; thus, the individual level consequences did not aggregate to the collective level. Key questions concern whether a situation having the same individual and collectivelevel dependent variables would support Burton-Jones and Gallivan’s model, or whether other relationships would be found. Furthermore, we saw that Dashboard used team task interdependence to manage patient flow and relied on this networked type of collective use, so future research could examine the effect of other types of collective use whether siloed, processual, or coalesced, on the multi-level relationships (Negoita et al., 2018). We saw a time diferential for the emergence of individual versus collective-level consequences (Negoita et al., 2018). Klein and Kozlowski (2000) referred to this as time scale diferences and proposed that individual level, or top-down efects, tend to manifest more quickly than collective, or bottom-up emergent efects, and that research designs must be sensitive to this temporal requirement of theory. An examination of the system over 18 months with the process view enabled us to see some of these delayed collective consequences, but further studies over a longer period would be useful in this area.

## 5.1.2. Clarified evolutionary nature and feedback efects

Our model captures the evolutionary character of systems as they are being implemented, something that would be missed in a pure variance model. Frameworks that are based on “system characteristics” tend to assume a stable (fixed, predetermined) set of characteristics. Even work that looks longitudinally at changes in IS adoption or success over time (e.g., Karahanna et al., 1999; Kim & Malhotra, 2005) seems to see characteristics as stable while their importance to users changes. Phased approaches to implementation are frequently based on bringing in new functionality over time to diferent groups of people. We observed this evolution in our data, and our hybrid model acknowledges the evolutionary character of both the system and the context. Trying to take a snapshot view of an organisation during a change poses limitations. Perceptions of the system are changing, but perhaps more importantly, the system itself is changing, as is the organisation, and this suggests that the meaning of the concepts (e.g., the relative importance of a diferent system or IQ sub-dimensions to the overall view of SQ or IQ) we use may also be changing. The hybrid view allows us to capture the iterative nature of the influences that directly afect create, use, and consequences. It enables us to see how software developed iteratively influences use, which in turn influences how the software is created and its subsequent consequences.

Furthermore, the phased rollout of Dashboard created a situation where its value was limited in the early phases. This more clearly illustrates how the independent variables in the IS success model influence use and user satisfaction, and ultimately consequences. As additional functionalities were added (i.e., improved SQ), there were more opportunities to achieve the benefits.

The feedback efects reveal the process interdependencies between dimensions of IS success as D&M intended. Our data showed the feedback efects from individual and collective level consequences to the create and use processes. This has not been empirically examined in the IS success literature. The continuous nature of feedback is based on the premise that the creation process (including updating the system) is ongoing and, although changes stabilise over time, there is an ongoing process of adapting or using the system to achieve the desired consequences. Our model reflects this process of change and how the SQ, IQ, and consequence variables change over time through the Create-Use-Consequences process. The feedback from consequences to use and consequences to create demonstrates adaptation actions (Burton-

Jones & Grange, 2013), where users adjust based on feedback or give feedback that impacts the creation of the system. Future research could investigate further why and how feedback is enacted through these adaptation actions and what influences these actions.

We also saw how consequences can have lingering feedback efects on IS success. If we had not studied feedback efects, we would not have seen the influence of mistrust on use of the system. Future research could examine how the degree of system success influences feedback loops. The system in our study was not overall successful, so our analysis highlights the negative feedback loop but does not capture positive feedback loops. Future research could employ a systems view to further examine feedback efects by considering the Create-Use-Consequences system as a whole and focusing on the emergence and interactions among the parts and properties of the system. Few studies have taken this view of IS success. Burton-Jones et al. (2015) proposed a simplified system view and acknowledged the value that could be gained from examining IS success from this view further.

Because the system employed an iterative version of a development process, the efect of feedback loops may have been heightened in our case. Key questions remain regarding feedback efects in the IS success model using other development methodologies. An extension of our work is the idea that the feedback loops in the model can work for phases of a system (as we saw) and for replacement of existing systems. In other words, the same logic should apply to a system that is built in one phase and then used over time. The feedback to “create” is really feedforward into the next system and the way we represent this in our conceptual model is as an advance in knowledge because it recognises that all new systems developed today are extensions of existing systems so can be looked at as an extended iterative process.

## 5.1.3. Captured context

We observed that context, composed of antecedent conditions and external influences, plays an important role in IS success. Context is not included as part of the original IS success model, but its importance as an influence on model constructs has been examined in the broader systems development literature, and more recently the relationship between contextual factors and IS success has been discussed (Petter et al., 2013). The sequential structure of the process view allowed us to consider context and see how antecedent conditions and external influences (both discrete and continuous) changed the process and the variables. This provides support for the importance of context in understanding a phenomenon (Ortiz de Guinea & Webster, 2017), and in particular with respect to IS success (Burton-Jones et al., 2015). Key questions that remain in this area concern how diferent types of external influences afect the process: what are the implications of diferent types of events?

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Our results reafirm the importance of both social and technical elements of systems development processes. The enduring nature of negative perceptions, even when the underlying issues have been addressed, suggests a need for better communication of problem resolution to help users realign their views as the system evolves. Consequences are not the end of the process, but a part of an iterative sequence of events. Thus, managers should be aware of the ongoing impacts of consequences on the creation and use of the system and manage these consequences appropriately. In addition, our finding that the integration of Dashboard into work practices was not possible until a minimally acceptable HCI was in place, while not surprising from a development standpoint, has implications for the assessment of IS success. Measuring outcomes too early, before users have had a chance to efectively change their work practices, risks underestimating the potential of the system. As a result, we suggest that measurement of the system’s impacts may be best completed after all HCI issues are addressed.

Our hybrid approach also provides a potentially useful way of understanding the context in which IS success occurs. The antecedent conditions and distinct and continuous external influences that can trigger changes in the process must be examined and their influence managed. The multilevel aspect of the model also has the potential to provide additional insights about IS success. As Negoita et al. (2018) propose, the type of collective use (i.e., siloed vs networked) will influence the consequences, so managers should be mindful of the level of collective use that can be attained. Furthermore, collective use emerges based on task, user, and system interdependencies. We saw how the lack of system interdependencies constrained collective use and thus the expected benefits of the system were not achieved. Managers could use this information to set realistic expectations around collective use and consequences.

## 6. Conclusion

Our hybrid model of IS success builds on the work of Burton-Jones et al. (2015) and DeLone and McLean (1992, 2003)) by significantly elaborating the process elements and integrating them with variance elements in the same model. We found this to be a powerful approach for understanding how IS success unfolds over time. For decades, researchers have noted the potential benefits of process theories, and there have been recent calls for a process view of IS success. As well, there have been calls to integrate process and variance perspectives into hybrid approaches, particularly for research questions dealing with change over time, antecedent conditions, events, and outcomes. We build on this momentum and provide a hybrid IS success model for future research to extend.
