# Extending the story of IS success: A meta-analytic investigation of contingency factors at individual and organisational levels

Nikhil Mehta<sup>a</sup>, Sumedha Chauhan<sup>b</sup> and Inderjeet Kaur<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Systems and Supply Chain Management, University of North Carolina Greensboro, Greensboro, NC, U.S.A; <sup>b</sup>Jindal Global Business School, O.P. Jindal Global University, Sonipat, Haryana, India; <sup>c</sup>Management Information Systems, Indian Institute of Management (IIM) Rohtak, Management City, Rohtak, Haryana, India

## ABSTRACT

In the last decade, a diverse array of information systems (IS) has emerged to help organisations achieve their performance goals. As a result, global spending on information technology (IT) has increased manifold. These developments have reopened the fundamental question of how IS success is shaped at the organisational level. Scholars and practitioners would also like to understand if, compared to the individual level, IS success patterns difer at the organisational level. The last meta-analysis of IS success was conducted nearly a decade ago and it could not examine IS success at the organisational level. There is also a need to gain a deeper understanding of key contingencies influencing success at the organisational level. Based on a comprehensive meta-analysis of research studies conducted in the last decade, we not only report how IS success patterns are shaped at the organisational level but also show how these patterns compare to success configurations at the individual level. Additionally, we conduct detailed multi-level meta-analyses of system complexity and user type as two key contingencies influencing success at both individual as well as organisational levels. Findings of our moderation analyses reveal distinct constructions of IS success across both levels.

KEYWORDS DeLone and mclean model; information systems success; is success model; meta analysis

## 1. Introduction

In the coming years, global organisational spending on information technology (IT) is expected to reach 5 trillion USD, and organisations would like to evaluate the success of their information systems (IS) (IDC global ICT spending forecast 2019). Over the years, substantial progress has been made towards improving the understanding of IS success. However, in the last few years, a wide variety of systems have emerged to serve diverse user groups. This has made the measurement and prediction of IS success more complex and perplexing (Larsen & Myers, 1999).

Researchers have profered various taxonomies and interpretations of IS success (c.f., DeLone & McLean, 1992; Larsen & Myers, 1999; Seddon, 1997), of which DeLone and McLean's (1992) IS success model is probably the oldest and most widely cited. The authors updated this model a decade after it was proposed (see DeLone & McLean, 2003). Key modifications were made to the model, which further improved its inclusiveness and robustness. The updated model proposed interrelationships among six dimensions of IS success: system quality, information quality, service quality, intention to use, use, user satisfaction, and net benefits (DeLone & McLean, 2003). Since then, several researchers have empirically tested the interrelationships among these dimensions, and others have conducted meta-analyses and literature reviews of one or more relationships (e.g., Petter et al., 2008; Petter & McLean, 2009; Sabherwal et al., 2006). This body of literature has contributed significantly to our understanding of IS success. Still, for two reasons, the fundamental question of how to measure and predict IS success remains open.

First, IS success needs to be evaluated at a specific stakeholder-level – whether individual or organisational (Seddon et al., 1999), and the last meta-analysis of the IS success model<sup>1</sup>, done more than a decade ago, reported results only at the individual level of analysis (see Petter & McLean, 2009). The last theoretical review of IS success model also could not report conclusive insights about system success at the organisational level due to insuficient data (see Petter et al., 2008). As a result, both studies reiterated DeLone and McLean's (1992, 2003) calls for more empirical research to map IS success patterns at the organisational level. We answer these calls by conducting a comprehensive meta-analysis of IS success at the organisational level. We also conduct a comparative examination of IS success at both individual and organisational levels. In doing so, we inform the literature whether IS success patterns difer across these two levels.

Second, prior meta-analyses reported large unexplainable credibility intervals for many relationships in the IS success model, suggesting that contingency conditions could be moderating these relationships (Petter & McLean, 2009). Previous theoretical reviews of IS success have also suggested that various user and system-related contingencies could afect IS success at both individual and organisational levels (Petter et al., 2008). Petter and McLean (2009) suggested that the ‘user population”, such as students, could potentially moderate IS success at the individual level, and its moderating influence should be examined. Studies have also observed diferences in the usage behaviours of managers and non-managers (Liao et al., 2009; Stadin et al., 2020). For example, while nonmanagers regard user satisfaction as a key success criterion, managers typically focus more on performance benefits as a success marker (Seddon, 1997). Despite previous evidence suggesting potential moderating efects of user population on IS success at both individual and organisational levels, such an examination is missing in the literature. Our study conducts this examination.

System complexity is another key contingency that could elicit diferential patterns of IS success across individual and organisational levels. System complexity is a vital dimension of any IS (Hanseth & Ciborra, 2007; Sun & Zhang, 2006). Simple systems typically ofer a moderate set of functionalities to achieve modest goals (Rai et al., 2002; Sun & Zhang, 2006). Given their ease of use, these systems may elicit keen intentions to use, making “intentions” a key predictor of their success. Complex systems, on the other hand, ofer a comprehensive portfolio of features and capabilities, which may trigger stronger perceptions of net benefits, making these “benefit perceptions” a key indicator of success for complex systems (Fitzgerald & Russo, 2005). An examination of these contingency efects of system complexity at both individual and organisational levels would add more precision to the theory on IS success. To the best of our knowledge, scholars are yet to conduct such an examination, leaving another theoretical gap in the IS success literature.

Overall, conducting a comprehensive examination of system complexity and user population contingencies would help create a more accurate and nuanced understanding of the IS success model. To develop this understanding, we extend our examination to conduct sub-group meta-analyses for the following contingencies: (i) simple and complex systems at both individual and organisational levels; (ii) students and nonstudents as user types at the individual level; and (iii) managers and non-managers as user types at the organisational level. Thus, the overall purpose of this study is to use rigorous meta-analytic procedures to address the following research questions:

(1) How do the relationships in IS success model explain system success at the organisational level, and how do these relationships difer across individual and organisational levels?

(2) How do contingencies related to system complexity and user type moderate the relationships in IS success model at both individual and organisational levels?

In addressing these research questions, we contribute to both the breadth and depth of the theory on IS success. We expand the breadth of theory by informing how success is shaped at the under-explored organisational level. Results of this examination will inform us of the explanatory and predictive capabilities of IS success model at the organisational level of analysis. It will also help us better understand how IS success patterns at the organisational level compare with IS success patterns at the individual level – something prior studies were unable to accomplish. Our analysis of contingencies also helps improve the depth of IS success theory. Results of eight sub-group metaanalyses conducted to examine these moderating efects depict diferent configurations of IS success shaped by the two contingencies at both individual and organisational levels. Finally, this study also extends the timeline of the previous meta-analysis that was done more than a decade ago. Given the quick pace of technology change, this study reports the most recent sentiment of IS success literature. Table 1 summarises the breadth, depth, and timeline of our examination compared to the previous metaanalyses.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses development

The pursuit for the dependent variable in IS resulted in multiple conceptualizations of what makes IS successful. While Davis' (1989) Technology Acceptance Model explained why some systems are more readily accepted by users than other systems, it was realized that ‘acceptance’ is more of a precursor to success than its representation (Petter & McLean, 2009). Other researchers focused on ‘user satisfaction’ as a surrogate for IS success, and developed tools and relational models to conceptualize satisfaction (Bailey & Pearson, 1983). These eforts helped researchers realize that IS success is a complex and multidimensional construct. DeLone and McLean (1992) also profered an IS success model that encompassed six interdependent dimensions: system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact. The D&M model is possibly the most widely cited model of IS success, and it has gone through a major cycle of literature review– based modification (see DeLone & McLean, 2003).

## 2.1. IS success model: background and current understanding

DeLone and McLean (1992) based their IS success model on the communications research of Shannon and Weaver (1949), the information influence theory by Mason (1978), and the relevant IS literature from 1981-1987. They proposed that IS success could be measured at three levels – technical, semantic, and efectiveness. Success at these levels could be assessed using six dimensions of IS – system quality assessed technical success, information quality assessed semantic success, and use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organisational impact assessed the efectiveness aspect (see Appendix A, Figure A1).

Subsequent years saw widespread adaptation of and modifications to the model by researchers to suit specific requirements. Increased use of the model also led to calls and recommendations to extend it with improvements (Pitt et al., 1995; Seddon, 1997). To address these calls, DeLone and McLean (2003) proposed an updated model with three key modifications (see Appendix A, Figure A2). First, service quality was added as the third quality dimension to account for the increasing interaction between IS users and service providers (Pitt et al., 1995), and the impact of this interaction on system use and user satisfaction. Second, the updated IS model included “intention to use” as another dimension, to reconcile the debate regarding the multidimensional and causal aspects of the “use” dimension (see Seddon, 1997). It was suggested that system use would precede “user satisfaction” in the process sense, and a favourable experience with system use would cause user satisfaction (Petter et al., 2008). Further, improved user satisfaction would increase intention to use, which would also impact system use (DeLone & McLean, 2003). Finally, the third addition to the model entailed the replacement of “individual impact” and “organizational impact” dimensions with “net benefits”. In making this addition, DeLone and McLean (2003)

acknowledged that benefits may accrue at multiple levels. Appendix A (Table A1) provides definitions, measures, and sources of all IS success dimensions.

Researchers have conducted theoretical reviews and quantitative meta-analyses of one or more relationships embedded in the IS success model. Sabherwal et al. (2006) validated 5 of the 15 relationships at the individual level. Petter and McLean (2009) conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of all the relationships, also at the individual level. In their theoretical review, Petter et al. (2008) examined IS success literature at both the individual and the organisational levels but could not report conclusive insights for organisational level due to insuficient data. Our meta-analysis extends this stream of research by examining how IS success model captures success at the organisational level.

In addition, we also examine IS success at the individual level for comparative analysis. Social systems, such as organisations, have characteristics that may not be observed in individual-level phenomenon (Gully et al., 2002). As a result, statistical results observed at one level of analysis are not always generalisable to another level (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). Scholars have suggested that IS success be examined separately at individual and organisational levels (Petter & McLean, 2009). In their updated model, DeLone and McLean (2003) also suggested that IS success can be examined at diferent levels. In their review of IS success literature, Petter et al. (2008) also investigated diferences in strength of relationships “based on whether the studies focused on an individual or organizational level when measuring and evaluating the various success constructs and relationships” (p. 240). Our examination of how IS success compares across individual and organisational levels is guided by these recommendations.

## 2.2. Moderating efects

Guided by prior calls to examine various contingencies afecting IS success relationships, we reviewed the IS success literature, as well as relevant studies in the broader IS literature, to identify key moderating variables. Based on this review, we identified system and user as two possible causes of contingencies of IS success (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006; Devaraj & Kohli, 2003; Grifith, 1999; Rai et al., 2002). This was followed by a more detailed review of the relevant literature, which helped us identify system complexity and user type as two key moderating variables. We discuss below our rationale for selecting these variables.

A system is defined as an artefact that embeds features and capabilities to achieve individual or organisational goals (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Prior reviews of IS success literature have called for the examination of moderating efects of system-related contingencies, such as system complexity. Systems can be classified as simple or complex (Petter & McLean, 2009; Sun & Zhang, 2006). Simple systems, such as web portals or employee portals, are defined by their moderate set of features and capabilities that are typically used to support modest goals (Sun & Zhang, 2006). Simple systems are also relatively easier to use and may not require significant changes in workflow activities or processes (Ilie et al., 2009; Venkatesh, 2006). On the other hand, complex systems, such as customer relationship management (CRM) systems, knowledge management (KM) systems, or group decision support systems (GDSS) are defined by their multi-faceted cluster of functionalities and capabilities to achieve myriad goals (Newell et al., 2000; Po-An Hsieh & Wang, 2007). Compared to simple systems, complex systems typically require users to perform additional processes or workflow activities (Ilie et al., 2009; Sun & Zhang, 2006).

As the system component plays a central role in any IS success scenario (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006), it is highly probable that attributes embedded within complex and simple systems diferentially afect their success patterns. In other words, compared to simple systems, complex systems may exhibit a diferent presentation of relationships in the IS success model. Scholars have not yet conducted meta-analytic examinations of system complexity as a moderator of IS success, but studies in technology acceptance literature have reported that acceptance patterns difer for simple and complex systems (Sun & Zhang, 2006). These studies strongly suggest that findings related to simple system are not generalisable to complex systems (and vice-versa). Our meta-analysis extends this stream of research by examining how IS success patterns difer between simple and complex systems.

Scholars also agree that “user” is another important constituent of an IS success scenario (Petter & McLean, 2009). Studies suggest that given their unique characteristics and needs, users in diferent roles interact with systems diferently (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). This would influence how they understand and perceive a system, and how their perceptions shape IS success. As a result, IS success patterns would emerge diferently for users in diferent roles (Petter et al., 2013). For example, it has been observed that regardless of available system features, satisfaction levels vary among diferent users, even if these users had similar performance outcomes (Au et al., 2002).

Classical IS studies also propose that the “sensemaking” processes of interpreting and understanding a technology vary across diferent users (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 1992). Sensemaking processes difer for diferent user roles, which, based on the unique characteristics and preferences of each role, lead to diferent “use” intentions and behaviours (Grifith, 1999). As a result, the same technologies may “come to have diferent meaning and efects for diferent users (Orlikowski, 1992, p. 408).” This unique process of users in diferent roles appropriating varied meanings to IS, leads to diferent users manipulating and shaping systems in myriad ways (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). We propose that these variations would influence IS success diferently, and we conduct additional meta-analyses to examine how IS success patterns difer across user types. In the ensuing sections, we hypothesise that system complexity and user type contingencies will influence various relationships in the IS success model (see Table 2).

## 2.2.1. Moderating efects of system complexity

This study examines the moderating efects of system complexity at both individual and organisational levels. Given the limited functionalities of simple systems, these systems are typically more user-friendly than complex systems (Rai et al., 2002). As a result, users across both individual and organisational levels may develop stronger perceptions regarding the convenience, ease of use, functionality, and usability of simple systems. These perceptions of system quality for simple systems may shape use intentions and behaviours diferently for these systems compared to complex systems (Sun & Zhang, 2006). Strong perceptions of system quality for simple systems may also shape more compelling beliefs about 'user satisfaction, i.e., systems meeting user requirements successfully. In other words, system quality would shape 'intention to use,' 'use,' and 'user satisfaction' diferently for simple systems compared to complex systems (H1a, H2a, H1b, H2b, H1g, H2g).

The influence of information quality on various use- and user-related dimensions of IS success model may also vary between simple and complex systems. Complex systems such as e-learning systems (individual level) and KM systems (organisational level) generate significant amounts of contextually rich information, making information quality a stronger determinant of success for complex systems compared to simple systems, at both levels (Ali et al., 2017). As a result, it is expected that information characteristics such as understandability, conciseness, and usability would influence 'intention to use,' 'use,' and 'user satisfaction' diferently for complex systems compared to simple systems (Halawi et al., 2007) (H1c, H2c, H1d, H2d, H1h, H2h).

Modest features and functionalities of simple systems may also make it relatively easier for the IT service providers to fulfil the users’ service expectations for these systems at both individual and organisational levels. This would typically create more favourable perceptions of service quality for simple systems, which may shape more favourable perceptions of 'intention to use' and 'user satisfaction' for these systems (Chen et al., 2012). Studies typically report a positive influence of service quality on 'intention to use' and 'user satisfaction' (Chen & Chengalur-Smith, 2015; Sørum et al., 2012), and this influence would exist for complex systems too, but the relationship is expected to be stronger for simple systems (H1e, H2e, H1i, H2i).

The influence of service quality on actual use behaviours may be guided by a diferent dynamic across simple and complex systems. Users of complex systems often face challenges using the myriad functionalities embedded in these systems to achieve their goals and objectives (Po-An Hsieh & Wang, 2007). The responsiveness and empathy shown by the IT department to help users better utilise complex systems would create stronger perceptions of service quality for these systems. These perceptions may then have a stronger influence on various 'use' metrics for complex systems (H1f, H2f).

Complexity may also shape diferent use-related attitudes and behaviours towards systems (Fitzgerald & Russo, 2005). As mentioned earlier, the use of complex systems typically requires more efort as individual and organisational users may have to apply multiple features to successfully achieve their goals (Po-An Hsieh & Wang, 2007). As a result, more cognitive resources are typically required for using complex systems to achieve individual and organisational goals (Ilie et al., 2009; Newell et al., 2000). More cognitive investment in using complex systems may lead to deeper perceptions of 'user satisfaction' and 'net benefits' derived from the 'use' of these systems. On the other hand, simple systems permit relatively singular patterns of use, requiring less cognitive efort to support individual and organisational goals. Low cognitive investment while using simple systems may contribute to less strong perceptions of 'user satisfaction' and 'net benefits' for these systems (H1j, H2j, H1k, H2k).

Diferences between simple and complex systems would create variability among other relationships as well. Complex systems, with their cluster of functionalities, elicit value expectations that are typically less well defined compared to simple systems (Newell et al., 2000). This would shape less convincing beliefs among individuals and organisations alike, regarding the ability of complex systems to meet information requirements satisfactorily. While simple systems ofer relatively clearer value expectations of meeting users needs, which would help form more clear-cut 'user satisfaction' beliefs for these systems. In other words, 'user satisfaction' beliefs would form diferently for simple and complex systems, which would lead to diferences in how these beliefs impact the perceptions of 'net benefits' and 'intentions to use' for both systems (H1l, H2l, H1m, H2m).

The lack of clarity in value expectations of complex systems may also create less clear perceptions of 'net benefits' for complex systems, making the assessment of their benefits more challenging (Fitzgerald & Russo, 2005). The relatively less evident perceptions of 'net benefits' for complex systems would contribute to generating less clear 'intentions to use' for these systems. On the other hand, the clarity of value expectations for simple systems would help shape clearer perceptions of 'net benefits“ for these systems. The relative clarity of benefits’ perceptions for simple systems may have a stronger influence on 'intention to use' for simple systems (Al-Debei et al., 2013). Diferences in relative clarity of 'net benefits' perceptions for simple and complex systems would also influence 'user satisfaction' beliefs diferently for these two systems. Simple systems with their clearer statement of 'net benefits' would elicit diferent perceptions of 'user satisfaction' compared to complex systems. Thus, the respective perceptions of 'net benefits' for complex and simple systems would influence both 'user satisfaction' and 'intentions to use' diferently for the two systems (H1n, H2n, H1o, H2o). Overall, system complexity is expected to moderate relationships between various dimensions of the IS success model at both individual and organisational levels.

## 2.2.2. Moderating efects of user type: organisational level

A user at the organisational level is defined as a subject who utilises system functionalities to primarily achieve organisational goals (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003; Sabherwal & Jeyaraj, 2015). Prior literature on IS success has observed that user type could impact multiple dimensions of IS success at the organisational level (Petter et al., 2008; Seddon et al., 1999). These studies have suggested that future research should examine IS success patterns distinctly for diferent user types.

Our categorisation of users at the organisational level is based on managerial and non-managerial roles (Liao et al., 2009; Stadin et al., 2020). Managerial roles are typically defined as roles with more supervisory, decision-making, and goal-setting responsibilities, whereas non-managerial roles typically involve more operational responsibilities (Isabella, 1990; Zyphur et al., 2016). Prior studies suggest that managerial roles are more interpretational with more complex information processing needs compared to non-managerial roles (Isabella, 1990). Managerial roles typically entail higher cognitive demands, as managers need to interpret a wide variety of information inflows for decision-making. Managerial roles typically also have more political power to influence key organisational issues (Siu & Lam, 2009). Studies suggest that the psychological processes through which people perceive and interpret organisational reality also difer between managerial and non-managerial roles, and managers develop differing perceptions of organisational reality compared to non-managers (Liao et al., 2009). As a result, the process of experiencing and appropriating meaning to technology may also difer between these roles, and managers are expected to exhibit diferent IS-related expectations and behaviours compared to nonmanagers.

Studies in organisation science support these observations and suggest that managers also experience more uncertainty in their roles compared to non-managers, who typically face lesser uncertainty, as their jobs typically have more predictable outcomes (Zyphur et al., 2016). These key diferences between managerial and non-managerial roles would frame the IS-related attitudes, expectations, and behaviours of these two user groups diferently. Specifically, managers, given the higher cognitive responsibilities combined with higher levels of uncertainty they face in their roles, would expect more convenience, ease of use, and a quicker response time from their systems (Zyphur et al., 2016). As a result, 'system quality would be a stronger criterion for 'intention to use and 'use' of IS for managers than for non-managers (H3a, H3b). Similarly, managerial roles rely more on complete, concise, accurate, and actionable information. As a result, managers may also assign more weight to information quality, compared to non-managers, when making their 'intention to use and 'use' decisions (H3c, H3d). Additionally, given the increasing dependence of managers on various systems, managers would also expect a more reliable and responsive service experience, making service quality a key determinant of IS success at the organisational level (Petter et al., 2008) (H3e, H3f). Overall, it is expected that the three quality dimensions, namely, system, information, and service quality would be stronger determinants of 'intention to use' and 'use' for managers compared to non-managers.

Given their more interpretational roles, managers may also have higher expectations of system functionality, adaptability, and reliability (Hwang et al., 2015). These high demands of system quality are also expected to strongly shape managers’ expectations of the systems meeting their needs satisfactorily, i.e., their 'user satisfaction' (H3g). A similar pattern is expected in the relationships between information quality and user satisfaction. Managerial roles entail strong information processing requirements (Hwang et al., 2015). To ease the complex cognitive load of their decision-making activities, managers employ heuristics that are supported by contextual, concise, and usable information, and they would rely heavily on systems to provide such information. Thus, information quality would strongly shape managers’ satisfaction beliefs (H3h).

Comparatively, given the more operational nature of non-managers’ roles, information quality would play a relatively moderate role in shaping their satisfaction beliefs, suggesting that the influence of information quality on “user satisfaction” would difer for managers and non-managers. Additionally, given the more complex demands of their roles, managers are expected to experiment more with systems and deploy them diferently compared to non-managers (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). As a result, managers would have higher expectations of service quality compared to their non-manager counterparts. These higher service expectations are expected to play a stronger role in shaping satisfaction beliefs between the two user groups (H3i).

Prior studies have also reported that managerial and non-managerial roles have diferent dominant organisational realities (Isabella, 1990; Zyphur et al., 2016). In other words, managers and nonmanagers assign diferent interpretations of organisational events which leads them to assign difering meanings to these events. Managers are more likely to develop a reality based on an organisation’s vision and strategy, while non-managers’ view of reality is typically grounded in the day-to-day realities of their work (Corley, 2004). As a result, the process of experiencing and making sense of IS is also expected to difer between managers and non-managers. Given their focus on day-to-day realities of work, users in nonmanagerial roles experience IS more as their operational assistants, and their perceptions of 'net benefits and 'satisfaction' from IS would emerge primarily from their everyday 'use' of the IS (H3j, H3k).

On the other hand, managers’ sensemaking is influenced more by organisational vision and strategy, and they would experience IS more as interpretational aides to fulfill their complex cognitive demands of achieving larger organisational goals (Hwang et al., 2015; Zyphur et al., 2016). As a result, their perceptions of 'net benefits' may be shaped more strongly by their satisfaction with the system’s ability to fulfil their complex needs, than just 'use' of IS (H3l). Stronger satisfaction derived from IS fulfiling their complex cognitive demands is also expected to promote stronger intentions among managers to use the system in the future (H3m).

Managers’ perceptions of success are also more closely derived from organisational goals (Zyphur et al., 2016). Given the more complex nature of these goals, managers are expected to experience stronger 'intentions to use' for IS that accrue clear 'net benefits of improved decision-making or predictability of future trends. In other words, the influence of 'net benefits' on 'intentions to use' would be stronger for managers than for non-managers (H3n). Given the uncertainty and complexity attached to managerial roles, realising 'net benefits' from IS is also expected to generate stronger perceptions of 'user satisfaction for managers (Zyphur et al., 2016) (H3o). Overall then, IS-related perceptions, behaviours, and outcomes may difer considerably between managers and non-managers.

## 2.2.3. Moderating efects of user type: individual level

Drawing on prior streams of research, we define a “user” at the individual level as a person who utilises one or more system features to achieve personal goals (Grifith, 1999; Heijden, 2004). As mentioned earlier, our examination of “user” as a contingency is based on the understanding that users’ sensemaking processes would be guided by their role requirements and preferences. As a result, users in diferent roles interact with systems diferently. At the individual level, we selected college students as one of the main user groups. Student respondents are frequently used as a “convenience sample” in most social science disciplines (Belot et al., 2015; Peterson, 2001). There is a tendency to use college students even as surrogates to nonstudents, that is, adult non-college going users, with an expectation that the two user groups show extensive similarities. This pattern of examining college students as surrogates of nonstudents is also visible in the IS success literature (c f Chen et al. 2013: Valaei & Baroto, 2017). But studies show that students difer from nonstudents in their behaviours (Belot et al., 2015). In their meta-analytic examination, Peterson (2001) reported significant diferences in effect sizes derived from student versus nonstudent samples. King and He (2005) also observed that student users showed distinct IT acceptance patterns compared to nonstudent users. Their study concluded that future findings related to technology acceptance behaviours of students should not be generalised to the nonstudent population. Later, Schepers and Wetzels (2007) also examined the moderating efects of student and nonstudent respondents on technology acceptance patterns and reported clear diferences between the two groups. As a result, prior researchers have called for studies to examine if IS success configurations difer for students and nonstudents (Petter & McLean, 2009). Examining these diferences is critical to improving our understanding of user contingency at the individual level. It would help researchers employ these two user groups appropriately as respondents in IS research.

Studies report that IS adoption patterns difer between students and non-students (Šumak et al., 2011). These patterns difer even for e-learning systems that are specifically aimed at achieving learning objectives, suggesting that the sensemaking process adopted by users in student role to understand and interact with systems, difers from that of users in the nonstudent role. Studies show that students have less crystallised attitudes, a less-formulated sense of self, and strong cognitive skills (Sears, 1986). Students are also relatively less risk-averse and more open to experimenting compared to nonstudents (Belot et al., 2105). Also, their sensemaking is influenced by their beliefs that abilities are dynamic and can be improved with efort (Chadwick & Raver, 2015). As a result, students display relatively more experimentative and proactive learning behaviours aimed at continuous growth and expansion of their competencies (Hollenbeck et al., 2011). These behaviours are indicative of a more exploratory mindset, which is characterised by the pursuit of learning, constant experimentation, creativity, risk-taking, and openness to learning from mistakes (Gupta et al., 2006; March, 1991). Accordingly, we propose that users in a student role will actively perceive new patterns and insights in ways that characterise a more exploratory mindset (Chadwick & Raver, 2015).

On the other hand, the sensemaking processes of users in a nonstudent role are guided by characteristics and beliefs unique to their role. Nonstudents have relatively more crystallised attitudes and a more defined sense of self, which afects their belief that abilities are already formed and predetermined (VandeWalle, 2003). As a result, they would focus their eforts more on reusing and refining their existing competencies, which characterises a more exploitative mindset (Baum et al., 2000; March, 1991). Compared to students, nonstudents are also relatively less experimentative, more risk-averse, and seek quicker results (Belot et al., 2015; Carpenter et al., 2005). These characteristics may also elicit more exploitative behaviours among nonstudents aimed at utilising their existing routines, technologies, and resources more for goal achievement than for exploratory learning (Benner & Tushman, 2003). In other words, these exploitative behaviours would motivate nonstudents to interact with systems more for goal achievement than for learning satisfaction or knowledge exploration (Chadwick & Raver, 2015).

Given the relative diferences between the mindsets of students and nonstudents, their system-related attitudes and behaviours would also shape diferently. For example, more proactive learning behaviours and experimentative attitudes among students would motivate them to embrace a variety of systems with difering levels of quality (Hollenbeck et al., 2011). Thus, students may not pay too much attention to system quality, information quality, and service quality while forming their 'intentions to use,' or in their 'use' behaviours, and the three quality dimensions may not strongly influence 'intention to use' and 'use' for students. On the other hand, given their risk aversion and a keen focus on goal achievement, nonstudents would pay close attention to the three quality dimensions of IS while making their 'use' decision. In other words, the three quality dimensions are expected to have a stronger influence on 'intention to use' and 'use' for nonstudents than for students (H4a-H4f).

But the three quality dimensions may strongly shape students’ perceptions of 'user satisfaction. Given their more exploratory mindset, students believe that their abilities are dynamic and that IS could help them improve their abilities (VandeWalle, 2003). As a result, the quality of IS and its capabilities would strongly influence the satisfaction beliefs of students. Students also typically expect that IS would help them expand their learning boundaries, and the quality of the system’s information output would strongly influence their perceptions of whether these expectations are satisfactorily fulfilled or not (Freeze et al., 2019). In other words, information quality would have a strong influence on 'user satisfaction for students. Students also like to experiment with the systems and would expect the IS service providers to help them derive creative benefits from the systems (Chen et al., 2013). As a result, service quality would also strongly influence 'user satisfaction' for students.

omparatively, users in a nonstudent role have more crystallised attitudes and a more defined sense of self, which results in beliefs that their capabilities are predetermined (Peterson, 2001; VandeWalle, 2003). Thus, expanding their abilities and competencies may not be as strong a motivation for nonstudents to interact with IS, as for students. In the same vein, nonstudents may not primarily have strong expectations of IS helping them expand their learning boundaries. As a result, their user satisfaction beliefs may not depend strongly on system quality and information quality. Additionally, compared to students, nonstudents are usually more risk-averse, and they may experiment less with IS (Belot et al., 2015). This would shape their service quality expectations diferently from students, and the responsiveness and empathy of service provided may not influence their 'user satisfaction' beliefs as strongly as for students. Overall, the relationships of system quality, information quality, and service quality with 'user satisfaction would shape diferently for non-students and students (H4g-H4i).

Key diferences between the mindsets of users in student and nonstudent roles would influence other IS success relationships as well. For example, IS use patterns and behaviours of nonstudents would be driven by their more exploitative mindset (Chadwick & Raver, 2015). This would aford nonstudents clarity and directedness in their 'use' decision and behaviours, contributing to clearer and stronger perceptions of 'user satisfaction' and 'net benefits' that are derived from IS use. On the other hand, users in student roles exhibit a more exploratory mindset, and they may exhibit more experimentative system use patterns. As a result, 'use itself may not elicit very strong perceptions of satisfaction and benefits for students. To summarise, the respective influence of IS use on 'user satisfaction' and 'net benefits' would difer between student and nonstudent roles (H4j, H4k).

Nonstudents, with their relatively stronger exploitative mindset, would also have more definitive and clear expectations of a system’s net benefits. This clarity would strongly define their satisfaction beliefs, that is, how satisfied they feel with the system meeting their benefits expectations. This clarity is also expected to strongly influence future intentions to use a system, if the expected benefits are attained (Chen et al., 2015).

Comparatively, a student role is typically associated with a more exploratory mindset, and students’ expectations of the 'net benefits' of a system may remain relatively fluid. The more fluid nature of these benefits expectations may have a less strong influence on how satisfied students believe they are with an IS if it meets their expectations. This would also influence their future 'intentions to use' less strongly compared to non-students. In other words, the influence of 'net benefits' on both 'user satisfaction' and 'intention to use' would difer between the students and nonstudents (H4n, H4o). In fact, given that satisfaction beliefs are more aligned with the students’ exploratory mindset, we expect that the influence of 'user satisfaction' on 'intention to use' will also difer between students and nonstudents (H4m). To that extent, the diferences in 'user satisfaction' beliefs of students and nonstudents would diferently influence their respective perceptions of 'net benefits' as well (H4l). Overall, users in nonstudent and student roles are expected to display distinct portfolios of system-related behaviours and expectations.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Meta-analysis

There are several qualitative and quantitative methods to summarise existing findings (King & He, 2005). For our study, we used meta-analysis techniques, which involve describing the results of several empirical research studies by typically using an estimate of efect size, such as correlation coeficient, and then combining these estimates into a summary (Hedges & Vevea, 1998). We adopted meta-analytic techniques for three reasons. First, meta-analysis is a statistical method that allows us to synthesise the existing literature on multiple relationships. In doing so, it “provides the opportunity to view the ‘whole picture’ in a research context by combining and analysing the quantitative results of many empirical studies” (King & He, 2005, p. 668). Second, meta-analysis ofers higher statistical power by integrating the quantitative results of multiple individual studies and by eluding the statistical shortcomings of a specific study (Borenstein et al., 2011). Finally, meta-analysis assists in examining probable moderating variables derived from logical reasoning and existing theory (King & He, 2005).

According to the “fit” perspective, relationships between variables can be conceptualised and tested in multiple ways such as fit as moderation, fit as mediation, fit as covariation, fit as matching, fit as gestalts, and fit as profile deviation (Venkatraman, 1989). In this study, we apply the 'fit as moderation perspective, which is used when the underlying theory proposes that the direction or the strength of the relationship between the predictor and the criterion would vary across diferent levels or types of moderator variable (Venkatraman, 1989). Drawing upon prior literature, we hypothesise similarly that the predictive ability of variables in the IS success model varies across two levels of system complexity (simple and complex) and two user types, at both individual and organisational levels (Petter et al., 2008). This theorising fits with a moderation perspective – specifically relating to the strength of relationships. From a statistical perspective, moderation analysis is recommended when precise, criterion-specific relationships are theorised (Venkatraman, 1989). This condition is also fulfiled in this study as specific predictor–criterion relationships are tested across two contingencies at a time. Previous research also confirms that the inclusion of moderating factors enhances a model’s explanatory power (Sun & Zhang, 2006) and can account for inconsistencies in the previous findings.

Thus, we use meta-analysis to examine moderating efects of user type and system complexity in the context of IS success model. The three typical steps of meta-analysis – identifying studies, coding research studies, and statistical analysis, are described below.

The Boolean operator “OR” was used. We also identified peer–reviewed conference papers from Association for Information

To ensure the independence of samples for the meta-analysis, one study was excluded (Rana, Dwivedi, Williams, & Lal, 2015) as it used the same sample used by another study in our dataset. Finally, backward and forward searches of relevant cited articles added 17 more publications to this dataset, resulting in a final sample of 125 studies. To ensure conceptual homogeneity across our sample (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001), we re-examined the shortlisted studies to ensure that they were consistent in terms of definitions and measurements of the IS success model dimensions as listed in table A1 (Appendix A). We examined the construct definitions and scale items, wherever available, to ensure that the overall sentiments captured by the definitions and the measurement instruments were largely comparable.

Meta-analysis could be carried out on a total of 15 or more studies (Field, 2001), and our sample of 125 studies satisfies this condition. ## 3.1.2. Coding studies for meta-analysis

Two authors independently coded the extracted studies based on whether they examined IS success at the individual-level or the organisational-level (Petter & McLean, 2009). A total of 74 relevant publications were obtained for IS success at the individual-level and 51 relevant articles for IS success at the organisational-level. If a study had multiple sub-studies, each sub-study was treated as a single study. As a result, the number of individual-level studies increased from 74 to 78, and the number of organisational-level studies increased from 51 to 53. IRR for this categorisation was excellent (93.60 % agreement, with Cohen’s kappa of 0.87). Disagreements were resolved through discussion between the two authors. Examples of organisational-level studies include Stefanovic et al. (2016), who examine the success of e-governance IS, and Gharib et al. (2017), who examine the success of business-to-business online communities. Examples of individual-level studies include Chiu et al. (2016), who examine the success of mobile e-books among students, and Tam and Oliveira (2017), who examine mobile-banking success at the individual level. These studies are listed in Appendix B.

Further, the same two authors also coded each paper for the contingency analysis. Individual-level studies were labelled as either 'student' or 'non-student' for user type contingency, and as 'simple' or 'complex' for system complexity contingency. Examples of simple systems at the individual level include social media websites and web portals, while examples of complex systems at the individual level include e-governance systems, e-learning systems, and online tax filing systems. In the student/nonstudent sample of studies, 36 studies used students as respondents. Out of these, 28 studies were in the educational context. We examined if there were significant diferences in the results of educational and non-educational studies using students as respondents. Most examined relationships showed non-significant diferences between the two groups of studies. As a result, we combined both educational and non-educational studies for a student sample for our moderator analysis of user type.

We also conducted additional analysis to examine if the presentation of IS success relationships difered significantly when studies were coded based on student/nonstudent user classification from when they were coded as educational/non-educational context. We recoded the studies with student users in noneducational context as “non-educational context”, leaving remaining studies with student users as a purely “educational context.” As per this new coding scheme, the two groups of studies represented: (i) studies with student users purely in the context of educational systems $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ educational context); and (ii) studies with both student and nonstudent users in the context of non-educational systems (i.e., noneducational context). Summarily, this coding scheme represented the “educational/non-educational context.” The results of our moderation meta-analysis using this coding nomenclature suggested no significant diferences from the results of our original classification of “student/non-student users.”

The organisational-level studies were coded as either 'manager' or 'non-manager' for user type contingency, and as 'simple' or 'complex' for system complexity contingency. Examples of simple systems at the organisational level include employee portals and picture archiving systems, while examples of complex systems at the organisational level include CRM systems, clinical information support systems, KM systems, and business intelligence (BI) systems. ## 3.1.3. Statistical analysis

From each study, information such as names of authors, title, publication source, publication year, the context of the study, sample size, pair-wise relationships examined, and correlation coeficients was recorded. Also, if a study had multiple sub-studies, each sub-study was treated as a single study. In all, 15 pair-wise relationships were examined based on the IS success model. Kirca et al. (2005) suggested that meta-analysis should be conducted with at least three studies. Therefore, relationships examined by less than three studies were not included for analysis.

The correlation coeficient was used as the efect size metric for the meta-analysis. Studies yielded 703 correlation coeficients (individual level = 425; organisational level = 278). Efect size is the estimation of the strength of a phenomenon in a population (Cohen, 1988). A larger value of efect size is generally representative of the greater degree of a particular phenomenon (Petter & McLean, 2009). The random-efects model was considered suitable for the present study (Hedges & Vevea, 1998), as the fixed-efects model assumes that all the studies included in the sample share a common efect size, and it is typically more appropriate for studies carried out in a controlled environment in a comparable way. The randomefects model allows the assumption that the sample of studies incorporated in the meta-analysis has enough in common, but may not be completely identical in terms of their true efect size. In other words, the random-efects model assumes that the sample studies were drawn from populations with diferent efect sizes (Borenstein et al., 2011).

Guided by the recommendations of Hedges and Vevea (1998), the meta-analysis was conducted using the following steps:

Step 1: Q-statistic was used for examining the homogeneity among the studies. It specifies the variability in the efect sizes due to sample heterogeneity rather than sampling error. Q-statistic was calculated as follows:

$$
Q = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} W _ {i} * (T _ {i} - \bar {T}) ^ {2}
$$

where

$$
\overline {{{{\mathrm{T}}}}} = \sum (\mathrm {W_ {i} * T_ {i}}) / \sum \mathrm {W_ {i}}
$$

$$
\mathrm {T_ {i} = Effect size for the i^ {th} study}
$$

$$
\mathrm{W} _ {\mathrm{i}} = \text {   Sample   size   for   the   i } ^ {\text { th }} \text {   study   }
$$

Step 2: Next, combined efect sizes were calculated using the following equation:

$$
\text { Overall   Effect   Size } = \sum_ {\mathrm{i} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{n}} \mathrm{W} _ {\mathrm{i}} ^ {\prime} * \mathrm{T} _ {\mathrm{i}}
$$

where

$$
1 / \mathrm{W} _ {\mathrm{i}} ^ {\prime} = 1 / \mathrm{Wi} + \tau^ {2}
$$

$$
\tau^ {2} = \mathrm{Max} [ 0, (\mathrm{Q-df}) / \mathrm{C} ]
$$

$$
\mathrm{df} = \text {   Degrees   of   Freedom   }
$$

$$
C = \sum W _ {i} - \frac {\sum W _ {i} ^ {2}}{\sum W _ {i}}
$$

Step 3: Significance of the combined efect size was assessed through the p-value associated with it. We also calculated 95% confidence intervals of the metaanalysis efect size using the standard error. Confidence intervals that do not include zero suggest a significant relationship.

Further, quantitative reviews like meta-analysis may be influenced by publication bias. The most common publication bias is the journals’ propensity to publish studies with only statistically significant findings. Therefore, we tested for the existence of publication bias by calculating the failsafe N values for each relationship. As shown in, all values exceeded the recommended value of 5 N + 10, where N is the total number of studies for a relationship (Rosenthal, 1979). These values provide confidence that the overall efect sizes are robust despite the lack of availability of studies with nonsignificant results (Sabherwal et al., 2006).

To obtain meaningful results from a metaanalysis, assessing study quality is important (Conn & Rantz, 2003). Study quality is the extent to which a study’s design and implementation support the types of inferences that the researchers want to make (Cooper et al., 2019). In this study, we assessed study quality in two ways. First, we used explicit inclusion criteria based on our research objectives to assess study quality during the study selection process, and studies that did not meet these criteria were excluded. Second, based on prior literature, we developed quality assessment criteria (sample size, measurement, study design, and publication impact factor) to categorise selected studies into high and low-quality studies (Conn & Rantz, 2003). For example, if a study had a sample size below 300, was published in a journal with a low impact factor, and lacked measurement rigour, it was categorised as low quality . Quality was then examined as a moderator (Conn & Rantz,

2003). The results showed that study quality moderated some relationships, as shown in Tables C1 and C2 (Appendix C). In such cases, the efect sizes of high-quality studies are considered valid (Conn & Rantz, 2003). In our sample, the efect sizes of high-quality studies matched closely with the combined results; therefore, all the included studies were retained.

Prior studies have followed heuristics to assess the magnitude of efect size as strong (0.50), moderate (0.30), and weak (0.10) (Petter & McLean, 2009). We grouped our meta-analysis results using these heuristics to interpret variability in efect sizes. Results reveal diferent patterns of relationships at individual and organisational levels.

Our results show that at the organisational level, all three system dimensions (system quality, information quality, and service quality) play an important role in shaping IS success by strongly influencing 'user satisfaction' and 'intention to use.' However, these system characteristics afect the actual 'use' behaviour only moderately, suggesting that system use at the organisational level may be strongly influenced by other factors. Specifically, system quality and information quality are strongly associated with both 'user satisfaction' and 'intention to use,' and service quality has a nearly strong association with 'user satisfaction. Also, all quality dimensions influence 'use,' albeit moderately.

Strong relationships at the organisational level were also observed among the outcome variables (use, user satisfaction, intention to use, and net benefits). For example, 'use' strongly influenced 'user satisfaction and 'net benefits,' and both of these influenced usage intentions, in turn. In fact, the 'net benefits → intention to use' relationship reported the strongest efect size (0.723), suggesting that the realisation of IS-related benefits, such as improved decision-making and resource savings, is a very strong predictor of future IS use at the organisational level.

At the individual level, the three system dimensions (system quality, information quality, and service quality) show a relatively modest influence on IS success compared to the organisational level. Specifically, system quality has a moderate relationship with all use-related dimensions (use, user satisfaction, and intention to use), establishing it as a relatively more important dimension, compared to information and service quality, at the individual level. Information and service quality moderately impact usage intentions and satisfaction, and both show only a weak influence on actual system use. 'User satisfaction' strongly influenced 'use,' net benefits,' and 'intentions to use' making it one of the key variables for IS success at the individual level (Table 4). Also, 'user satisfaction' was in turn, strongly influenced by 'use.

## 4.3.1. Moderation results: system complexity at the organisational level

Moderating efects of system complexity were examined at the organisational level. As presented in Appendix D (Table D1), all except two relationships were significant. Some relationships could not be examined due to insuficient data. These include the respective relationships of system quality, service quality, user satisfaction, and net benefits with 'intention to use' (H1a, H1e, H1m, and H1n) and the relationship between ‘net benefits' and 'user satisfaction' (H1o). Q-between values suggest that system complexity moderated five out of the remaining examined relationships (Table D1).

Results suggest that both system quality and service quality influence 'user satisfaction' more for simple systems, confirming H1g and H1i, but their respective influence on 'use' does not vary across systems, refuting H1b and H1f. The influence of information quality on 'user satisfaction' does not vary for both systems, refuting H1h, but it afects 'use' more strongly for complex systems, confirming H1d. Information quality also influences ntention to use' more strongly for simple systems (H1c).

There is less variability across simple and complex systems at the organisational level. System complexity moderates only the 'user satisfaction → net benefits' relationship (H1l). Results show that 'user satisfaction' is much more critical in eliciting benefits perceptions for simple systems compared to complex systems. The relationships between 'use' and 'user satisfaction' and 'use' and 'net benefits' were equally strong for both simple and complex systems, disproving H1j and H1k. ## 5. Discussion

We conducted multi-level meta-analysis of IS success model at individual and organisational levels, and examined contingency efects of system complexity and user type at both levels. Significant contingency efects were observed for most examined relationships, as we found distinct patterns of these relationships at both individual and organisational levels. Appendix E (Table E1) summarises the key implications of our metaanalyses results.

## 5.1. Organisational and individual levels

Previous studies have reported inconclusive evidence regarding the respective impacts of the three quality dimensions on outcomes such as 'user satisfaction' at the organisational level (Petter et al., 2008). Our study fills this gap and shows how each quality dimension afects outcomes uniquely across simple and complex systems. For example, prior meta-analyses did not examine information quality in detail at the organisational level (see Petter & McLean, 2009; Sabherwal et al., 2006). Our results establish information quality as an important element of IS success at the organisational level. Our results on service quality address another gap in our understanding of the pivotal role of this dimension . The results demonstrate a nearly strong association of service quality with 'user satisfaction.' These results signify that compared to the use patterns and behaviours, 'user satisfaction' may be driven more strongly by the quality of service provided by the IS department.

Comparatively, at the individual level, the quality dimensions have a moderate influence on both 'intention to use' and 'user satisfaction.' This variability between individual and organisational levels is understandable. At the organisational level, IS that provide accurate, timely, useful, and actionable information would elicit stronger satisfaction beliefs and use intentions (Petter & McLean, 2009). These implications are supported by our finding that the three quality dimensions also have a stronger influence on IS 'use' at the organisational level, compared to the individual level. These results help create a better understanding of the importance of these quality dimensions across both levels. The overall notable finding regarding the three quality dimensions is that they have, at best, a moderate influence on system use across both levels, suggesting the presence of other, possibly stronger determinants of 'use.' These results also suggest that issues that are not being captured by the IS success model, such as organisational policies for IS use (mandatory vs. discretionary use), may predict 'use' more strongly than the three quality dimensions (Heijden, 2004).

Organizational level results for 'intention to use show that it is strongly predicted by multiple dimensions such as system and information quality, user satisfaction, and net benefits. Comparatively, 'intention to use' at the individual level is shaped strongly by 'user satisfaction' and moderately by 'net benefits. These findings imply that 'intention to use' is a complex construct and a reliable measure of IS success, as it is capable of capturing unique diferences between individual and organisational levels. Another interesting observation at the organisational level is the strong influence of system use in shaping 'user satisfaction.' Petter et al. (2008) had mentioned, “. . . there is insuficient empirical evidence to know whether more use will lead to greater user satisfaction” (p. 256). Our findings show that more system use would lead to stronger perceptions of user satisfaction. Our findings conclusively show that more 'use' would also shape stronger perceptions of net benefits. Petter et al. (2008) also reported insuficient findings regarding the influence of user satisfaction on net benefits. Our findings fill this gap too, suggesting that at the organisational level, 'user satisfaction' is strongly associated with users’ productivity, job performance, efectiveness, and decision-making (McGill et al., 2003; Rai et al., 2002).

## 5.2. Moderation analyses

Results of the four sub-group meta-analyses conducted to examine the contingency efects of system complexity and user type at individual and organisational levels also show interesting patterns of IS success. At the organisation level, results reveal distinct variability between simple and complex systems. Overall results imply that while information quality is a key antecedent to 'user satisfaction' for both systems, system quality and service quality are more important in eliciting 'user satisfaction' specifically for simple systems. Thus, information quality could be a critical success determinant for both simple and complex systems at the organisational level. Results also imply that while information quality is a key determinant of 'use' for complex systems, system quality is equally important as a determinant of 'use' for both systems. A strong influence of 'use' on 'user satisfaction' and 'net benefits' for both simple and complex systems implies that system use is a key success-defining metric at the organisational level. In addition, 'user satisfaction' has a stronger impact on 'net benefits' for simple systems. Although the efect sizes are strong for both systems, which suggests that organisations could improve perceptions of 'net benefits' for both simple and complex systems by ensuring higher levels of 'user satisfaction' with these systems.

At the individual level also, we observed diferent patterns of results based on moderation of system complexity. For simple systems, 'intention to use seems to be an important indicator of success. It shares moderate-strong relationships with the three quality dimensions and also shares strong relationships with 'user satisfaction' and 'net benefits.' Results suggest that comparatively, 'use' may not elicit as strong perceptions of satisfaction and realisation of benefits for simple systems as intention to use. On the other hand, 'use' would be a better measure of success for complex systems. Individuals derive stronger benefits perceptions from using complex systems, implying that the degree to which various capabilities of complex systems are used strongly defines the net benefits for these systems.

We also found that at the individual level, information quality has a significantly stronger role in shaping use patterns and behaviours for complex systems than for simple systems. Results support our proposition that complex systems such as learning management systems typically generate more contextually rich information than simple systems. As a result, information quality would influence the 'use' patterns of complex systems more strongly than the 'use' patterns of simple systems. Results imply that the 'use' of simple systems may be more strongly influenced by other factors, such as their ease of use.

Results of the individual level have two interesting implications for the IS success model . First, service quality is instrumental in shaping 'use intentions' and 'user satisfaction' more strongly for simple systems. Additionally, for complex systems, service quality has a stronger influence on system use. These findings fill a key theoretical gap by highlighting an improved role of service quality compared to its influence as reported by the findings of the last meta-analysis (Petter & McLean, 2009). Second, there is an ongoing debate in the literature about the respective eficacy of 'use versus 'user satisfaction' as an accurate measure of IS success. DeLone and McLean (2003) had envisaged that given the potential dificulties of measuring actual 'use,' perceptual metrics such as 'user satisfaction' may continue as the variable of choice for researchers examining IS success. Our meta-analysis suggests that, at the individual level, 'user satisfaction' may serve as a better measure of success for simple systems, while actual 'use' may be a better measure for complex systems.

Results for the moderation of user type at the organisational level also suggest disparities and similarities between managers and non-managers. Both system quality and information quality influenced 'use' more strongly for non-managers. On the other hand, information quality and service quality shaped 'user satisfaction' more strongly for managers.

In terms of similarities, results suggest that the convenience, functionality, reliability, and other characteristics of the system play an equally important role in shaping satisfaction perceptions for both user groups. A similar conclusion can be drawn regarding the influence of 'user satisfaction' on 'net benefits. Results suggest that despite diferences between managers and non-managers regarding how they experience IS, that is, as interpretational and strategic aides for managers versus operational aides for nonmanagers, the perceptions of 'net benefits' for both groups are strongly shaped by their satisfaction with IS. Additionally, for managers, relationships involving 'user satisfaction' are stronger than relationships involving 'use,' while for non-managers, it is the opposite. These findings indicate the possibility that 'system use' for managers is more strongly influenced by factors beyond the scope of IS success model. Under such conditions, 'user satisfaction' emerged as a better indicator of IS success for managers. On the other hand, 'use' emerged as a key indicator of IS success for nonmanagers.

Distinct patterns also emerged for user-type contingency at the individual level. A noteworthy pattern is the higher efect sizes of relationships between the three quality dimensions and IS use for nonstudent users (Table 5). These results suggest that compared to student users, the use patterns and behaviours of nonstudents are influenced more by quality aspects of IS. These results confirm our proposition that given their more exploitative mindset, nonstudents would typically use high-quality IS to achieve their objectives. On the other hand, given their more explorative mindset, students show more openness to using IS with acceptable quality standards.

A diferent dynamic is visible in relationships between the quality dimensions and 'user satisfaction. Moderation is not significant for the 'system quality → user satisfaction' and 'information quality → user satisfaction' relationships. These findings imply that both information quality and system quality are important drivers of satisfaction in both user groups.

Also, looking at the results within the student user group, it is observed that the three quality dimensions influence students’ user satisfaction beliefs more strongly than their 'use' patterns. Results also show that their 'user satisfaction' beliefs strongly define students' perceptions of 'net benefits' (Table 5), which also aligns with their more explorative mindset. To summarise, our results suggest that student users value 'satisfaction' derived from the exploration of and experimentation with systems more than just using the system. These results suggest that compared to system use, 'user satisfaction' is a better success metric for students.

Finally, the efect sizes of most relationships for students ranged from weak to moderate (Table 5), while multiple strong relationships emerged for nonstudents. This variability between students and nonstudents suggests a key theoretical caveat – despite a plethora of studies in IS success literature using student population, the D&M model only moderately explains the IS success profile of student users. On the other hand, the emergence of multiple strong relationships for nonstudents highlights the robustness of IS success model for examining the nonstudent population. We suggest that researchers remain cognisant of these diferences when adopting IS success model in the future for student users. We also recommend that future studies explore stronger determinants of students’ IS use outside the D&M model.

## 6. Implications

## 6.1. Implications for research and future directions

Previous meta-analyses and theoretical reviews of IS success literature either did not examine IS success at the organisational level or could also not report conclusive findings due to insuficient data (see Petter et al., 2008; Petter & McLean, 2009). Our metaanalysis of IS success at both individual and organisational levels addresses this issue. Our results reveal more strong relationships among IS success dimensions at the organisational level, than at the individual level, suggesting a better applicability of the updated DeLone and McLean (2003) model at the organisational level.

Our findings at the organisational level fill some key gaps in the IS success literature. For example, prior studies did not examine the nuanced diferences in the respective roles of the three quality dimensions at the organisational level (Petter et al., 2008). Our metaanalytic findings suggest that the three quality dimensions are critical to improving both 'user satisfaction and 'intention to use' in organisations. Information quality has also been an ignored area of research at the organisational level (Petter et al., 2008). Information quality is a key dimension of IS success, as it highlights the system’s ability to provide users with accurate, timely, and usable information. Our results underscore the importance of this dimension by reporting that information quality plays a strong or moderate role in all IS success outcomes at the organisational level.

Another key contribution of our study is to highlight the importance of service quality as an important dimension of IS success. This dimension was included by DeLone and McLean (2003) in their updated model, yet prior reviews and meta-analyses either did not examine the influence of service quality at the organisational level or did not find conclusive results (Petter et al., 2008; Petter & McLean, 2009). Our results show that, in addition to its strong role in shaping 'user satisfaction,' service quality also has a moderate role in shaping system use behaviours at the organisational level.

Organizational level results also show that 'intention to use' shares strong relationships with most success dimensions. This result also has key implications for IS success research. Findings related to 'intention to use' establish it as a reliable indicator of IS success at the organisational level, validating its inclusion in the updated IS success model (DeLone &

McLean, 2003). Organizational level findings regarding 'net benefits' also contribute to the IS success literature. Organizational investments in technology have continued to rise. Yet, the absence of conclusive results about the influence of 'use' and 'user satisfaction' on 'net benefits' at the organisational level forced IS researchers to find other key determinants of 'net benefits' (Petter et al., 2013). Our results suggest that while researchers continue to search outside the IS success model for predictors of 'net benefits' at the organisational level (e.g., system-task fit or top management support), constructs within the model, such as 'use' and 'user satisfaction' are also strong and conclusive determinants of benefits.

Another notable contribution of our study is the analyses of user type and system complexity contingencies for both individual and organisational levels. The variability in relationships across the two levels and sub-contexts within these levels presents an overwhelming evidence supporting the contextual nature of IS success. Although prior researchers made similar suggestions, they did not examine any contingency efects (Petter et al., 2008). Some compelling illustrations of this capability relate to the role of information quality and service quality at the organisational level. For example, the overall results of our meta-analysis at the organisational level suggested a moderate but significant influence of information quality on system use. But results of moderation analysis show that the influence of information quality on 'use' varies significantly across two user groups – it is significant for non-managers and not significant for managers. Similarly, another noteworthy implication of our moderation analysis pertains to service quality. At the organisational level, service quality shares a strong relationship with 'user satisfaction.' Results of comparative analysis (Table 5) show variability across user types, such that service quality plays a strong role in improving 'user satisfaction' for managers but has only moderate influence for nonmanagers. The results of contingency analyses also suggest that both these quality dimensions are key determinants of 'user satisfaction' for managers, but not for non-managers.

The moderation analysis also supports DeLone and McLean (2003) caution of treating satisfaction and usage measures as alternatives to performance measures, and they called for more research to better understand the relationships between these three success dimensions. Our meta-analysis answered this call. Supported by quantitative evidence, our results suggest that use, user satisfaction, and net benefits are not surrogates. Results clearly show that these dimensions share relationships such that 'use' afects 'user satisfaction,' and both 'use' and 'user satisfaction' afect 'net benefits.'

Finally, results also suggest certain boundaries of the IS success model. For example, results of the organisational level analysis imply that system use is only moderately influenced by various dimensions of IS success model. In other words, strong determinants of system use at the organisational level may exist beyond the scope of IS success model. Contingency analysis also defines some boundaries for the applicability of the IS success model. For example, future research should remain cognisant of a unique attitudinal and behavioural profile of student users, and understand that using the D&M model to examine success among student users may stretch the model’s applicability. Similarly, analysis shows that system use among managers may be shaped more strongly by factors outside the model. Based on these findings, it is recommended that future studies in IS success consider these boundary conditions.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

This study has potential implications for two groups of practitioners – firms using IS and firms developing IS. For organisations using IS or planning to implement new IS, eliciting strong intentions to use IS is a key objective. Organizational level results suggest that 'user satisfaction' strongly impacts 'intention to use.' Thus, to improve use intentions of IS, organisations should actively adopt methods to enhance user satisfaction, such as by increasing user participation in systems development and implementation processes (Petter et al., 2013). Results also suggest that perceptions of 'net benefits' strongly influence 'intention to use.' In fact, the 'net benefits → intention' to use relationship had the strongest efect size at the organisational level. Given the largely intangible nature of IS-related benefits, organisations could significantly improve 'intention to use systems by frequently communicating their benefits statement.

For organizations already using IS, maximising 'net benefits' for their IS investments is a key objective. Results suggest that the IS success model may help organizations identify key determinants of 'net benefits.' User satisfaction is the strongest predictor of 'net benefits' among both managers and nonmanagers, and organizations should strive to improve the satisfaction beliefs of both managers and nonmanagers in order to elicit strong perceptions of net benefits. Results suggest that 'user satisfaction' among managers is strongly afected by all three quality parameters. Thus, system developers should ensure that systems meant for managers are highly functional, and reliable, and also provide timely, accurate, actionable, and complete information. Our findings also have interesting implications for organisations trying to maximise the success of their simple systems. Simple systems such as employee portals and e-learning systems are typically accessible to a large majority of employees. Organizations could ensure high levels of 'user satisfaction' for simple systems also, by improving the convenience, functionality, and reliability of these systems.

Organizations should also emphasise service quality as a key dimension of their IS implementation strategy. IT function should be able to provide desirable support in the form of individualised attention, and convey trust and confidence among their nonmanagerial users and for simple systems. This is a key issue, given the increasing trend of IT outsourcing, where ofshore service providers replace the organisational IS function.

Results of the individual level suggest that system developers should be aware of the diferences between students and nonstudents. Student users would 'use systems for trial and exploration, and their perceptions of net benefits are contingent more strongly on their satisfaction with the systems. On the other hand, nonstudents make significant investments in selecting highquality systems for their use. Developers should especially ensure high information quality as it strongly afects both 'use' and 'user satisfaction' for nonstudents.

## 7. Limitations and conclusions

This study has some limitations, which need consideration while interpreting the results. First, although our study included 125 papers related to IS success model, we may have missed a few relevant papers. Second, metaanalysis procedures typically favour studies with significant findings compared to studies with non-significant results as there are more chances of research with significant findings to get published (Cooper et al., 2019). Although we tested for this publication bias and found no significant evidence of it, studies with insignificant results are missing from the final dataset. Third, some relationships could not be examined due to an insignificant number of studies examining these relationships. Finally, although this study captured the contingency efects of system complexity and user type, other possible contextual factors could also be contributing to the variability observed in the relationships. For example, a valid contingency at the individual level would be the “use context”, such as educational vs. non-educational use of IS.<sup>2</sup> Such an examination would add more precision to our examination of student versus nonstudents users.

In conclusion, researchers have been emphasising that context matters in the examination of IS success. Answering multiple calls for a deeper investigation of context in IS success (DeLone & McLean, 2003; Petter et al., 2008), we conducted a multi-contingency examination of the relationships between IS success dimensions at both individual and organisational levels. Based on the extant literature, we examined how user type and system complexity contingencies influence the nature of IS success at both individual as well as organisational levels. A multi-level meta-analysis revealed a vividly multidimensional and contextual nature of IS success. Results of this investigation advance knowledge of IS success beyond the findings of prior studies. In doing so, the study contributes to both breadth and depth of literature and reopens avenues for future discussion on IS success.
