# The efects of cyber regulations and security policies on organizational outcomes: a knowledge management perspective

Anat Hovav<sup>a</sup>, Itzhak Gnizy<sup>b</sup> and Jinyoung Han<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Center for Information Systems and Technology, Claremont Graduate University, Claremont, California, USA; <sup>b</sup>Ono Academic College, Kiryat Ono, Israel; <sup>c</sup>Da Vinci College of General Education, Chung-Ang University, Seoul, Korea

## ABSTRACT

Knowledge management (KM) has emerged as an operational and strategic organisational tool. However, the influence of recent cyber regulations (CR) and security policies (SP) on the usefulness of KM, are absent from the literature. This study focuses on the intersection of knowledge management and knowledge fortification. The study employs survey data from a sample of firms and utilises partial least squares (PLS) with extensive post hoc analyses, to examine the influence of CR and SP on KM core processes (acquisition, sharing, and utilisation) and subsequently on the operational efectiveness and strategic performance of firms. Contrary to prior research, we found that external knowledge sharing negatively afected performance. These findings support our assertion that KM processes are tied to contingencies that change the relationship between KM outcomes.

## KEYWORDS

Knowledge management; knowledge acquisition; knowledge sharing; knowledge utilisation; cyber regulations; security policies; efectiveness; performance

## 1. Introduction

With the emergence of knowledge-based economy, knowledge has become an essential component of the modern firm (Huang et al., 2018). Knowledge represents an important key asset of organisations and a main source of sustainability and business success (Perez-Soltero & Soto, 2017; Riege, 2007). A hallmark of the new economy is the organisation’s ability to realise the value from the collection and utilisation of knowledge assets (Dulipovici & Robey, 2013; Gold et al., 2001; Xiaojun, 2017). In our increasingly globalised world, businesses’ survival depends on intelligence derived from knowledge systems (Durst & Edvardsson, 2012). Organizational knowledge management (KM) is defined as a systemic process used to acquire and communicate knowledge (Huang et al., 2018). With its multidisciplinary nature, KM ofers a strategy to achieve superior competitive advantage (Ceci et al., 2019; Perez-Soltero & Soto, 2017).

Whereas KM and its outcomes have been studied extensively in the literature, prior research reports inconsistent consequences of positive, negative, or no relationships between KM processes (i.e., knowledge management acquisition, knowledge management sharing, and knowledge management utilisation) and organisational outcomes (Novak, 2017; Zaim et al., 2019). For example, KM benefits managers that use large volumes of information and knowledge. However, these benefits diminish for managers in rapidly changing information and knowledge environments (Kim et al., 2016). Some research found that managing knowledge positively increases sustained competitive positioning and growth, contributes to the success of the company (Ferreira et al., 2018), and facilitates organisational strategic benefits (Perez-Soltero & Soto, 2017).

The above inconsistencies may be due to the diferential influence of KM processes on organisational operational and strategic outcomes. For example, while some research found that knowledge acquisition and knowledge sharing enhance efectiveness directly (Boh, 2014) and indirectly (Gold et al., 2001), other research found that knowledge acquisition is associated with high risk levels for desired benefits and may lead to uncertain performance and strategic outcomes (Chen & Edgington, 2005). Furthermore, internal knowledge sharing was found to enhance organisational performance indirectly (Iyengar et al., 2015) while external knowledge sharing that result in information leakage and knowledge spillover was found to hinder organisational returns from innovation (Elliott et al., 2019). Finally, knowledge utilisation was found to increase organisational performance (Boh & Wong, 2015). We view organisational efectiveness as the firm’s ability to streamline internal processes and adapt to change (Gold et al., 200) and performance as the firm financial growth and strategic competitiveness (Zou et al., 1998). While efectiveness represents operational outcomes, performance measures strategic outcomes.

In addition to the diferential efects of KM processes, these contradictory findings may be due to emergent exogenous factors that have not been considered in the extant literature. Moreover, with the advent of disruptive technologies such as cloud computing, the Internet of Things, and big data, secure KM has emerged as critical research domains (Elliot et al., 2019; Okere, 2017). As the number of information leakage incidents increase, firms are expected to establish organisational information security policies (SP) and conform to national and international cyber regulations (CR). While there is an inherent continuous need for organisations to evaluate the efectiveness of their KM (Okere, 2017), the understanding of the influence of these security and privacy issues on KM is absent from the literature. This study responds to calls for the need to continually identify and evaluate the conditions that facilitate KM success (Huang et al., 2018) and suggests that further examination of the efects of contextual factors may help explain some of the inconsistencies regarding the efect of KM on firms’ outcomes. In this study, we investigate firms internal (e.g., SP) and external (e.g., CR) factors that may play a role in their efect on the dimensions of KM. Specifically, we explore the relationships between SP and CR, and KM’s core processes, namely knowledge management acquisition (KMA),<sup>1</sup> knowledge management sharing (KMS), and knowledge manage ment utilisation (KMU) at the organisational level and their efect on firms’ efectiveness and performance. Furthermore, research suggests that external sharing of knowledge carries more risk than internal sharing of knowledge (Elliott et al., 2019), since organisations can exert more control over internal KMS than over external KMS. By exploring both internal and external aspects of KMS, our study empirically assesses the above assertions. Such examinations also provide a practical foundation for determining the relative contribution of KM to organisational efectiveness and performance.

Finally, information security research has focused on internal and external organisational rules and guidelines that govern (and often restrict) the use and sharing of information assets, which refers to organisational data, information, knowledge and systems (Whitman & Mattord, 2013). Conversely, KM research has focused on processes, procedures, and systems that facilitate the flow of organisational information and knowledge (Nissen, 2002). In this work, we examine the two in tandem.

Our study answers the following research questions:

RQ1: How do information security policies afect the relationships between knowledge acquisition, internal knowledge sharing, and organisational efectiveness?

RQ2: How do cyber regulations afect the relationships between knowledge utilisation, external knowledge sharing and organisational performance?

The remaining of the paper is organised as follow. We first present the study’s background and hypotheses. Next, we outline the methodology, analysis, and findings. We conclude by discussing the implications for theory and practice, and identifying the study’s limitations and directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background and construct definitions

## 2.1. Knowledge management

Organisational knowledge is embedded in systems, routines, procedures, policies, documents, and individual employees (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). As such, managing knowledge is multifaceted and complex (Binney, 2001). Yet, proper management of organisational knowledge is essential to the survival and success of firms (Durst & Edvardsson, 2012). Due to the above complexity, the current literature conceptualises KM in various ways depending on the underlying assumptions and the expected outcome of managing knowledge. For example, one conceptualisation treats the organisation as a system where people, processes, and resources are integrated to achieve sustainable outcomes by improving performance through learning (Seleim & Khalil, 2011; Wang, 2011). Others conceptualise KM as a set of procedures, infrastructures, and technical and administrative tools designed for the creation and efective use of information and knowledge within and across organisations (Perez-Soltero & Soto, 2017). Finally, KM is often defined as a process involving a range of activities used to identify and evaluate attainable knowledge to accomplish organisational, operational and strategic objectives (Chawla & Joshi, 2010). While the literature introduces slight variations in the conceptualisation of KM and uses diferent labelling interchangeably (e.g., knowledge sharing and knowledge transfer), the underlying concepts of KM are fundamentally based on the framework first introduced by Alavi and Leidner (2001).

In this study, we adopt an integrated view of the diferent approaches discussed above. Moreover, the implied KM focus is on knowledge flow as defined by Nissen (2002). The extended model of knowledge flow dynamics suggests that KM operates in two dimensions: the life-cycle of knowledge and its reach (Nissen, 2002, 2005). We specifically focus on KMA, KMS and KMU as the core elements of KM as they are critical to organisational outcomes (Iyengar et al., 2015). This view is consistent with prior studies that identified three phases in the life-cycle of KM, namely knowledge management creation, knowledge management sharing, and knowledge management application (Hayton & Cacciotti, 2014). We define KMA as a process by which knowledge is obtained or produced for the firm’s use. KMS refers to organisational processes, routines, and systems that facilitate the dissemination and synthesis of knowledge difused in individuals and organisational units (Kale & Singh, 2007). KMS also reflects the intensity and willingness of knowledge exchange between internal and external organisational entities (Bock et al., 2005). While it is important for organisations to manage knowledge internally, it is equally important to efectively manage knowledge externally (Gold et al., 2001; Kim et al., 2016). The prevalence of inter-organisational relationships, strategic alliances, outsourcing, and globalisation has led to an increase in the transfer of knowledge between organisations (Lin et al., 2005). Thus, we define internal KMS as the managing and sharing of knowledge within organisational entities and external KMS as the management of interorganisational sharing of knowledge. Finally, KMU refers to the application, utilisation, and leverage of knowledge (Gold et al., 2001). The extended model of knowledge flow dynamics suggests the existence of reach as a dimension of knowledge flow (Nissen, 2002, 2005). Reach is defined as who has access to the knowledge and ranges from the individual to intra- and inter-organisational entities. Our study distinguishes between intra-organisational reach of KM (KMA and internal KMS) and interorganisational reach of KM (external KMS and KMU). This delineation of KM by reach enables us to examine the efect of internal conditions (security policies) on intra-organisational reach of KM and internal outcomes (efectiveness) and external conditions (cyber regulations) on extraorganisational reach of KM and external outcomes (strategic performance).

## 2.2. Cyber security regulations and policies

Organisations often follow both privacy laws in the form of cyber regulations and information security policies. While laws/regulations are external to the firm and are established and enforced by institutions such as governments, policies are internal and are developed and enforced by the organisation. CR are usually involuntary and uncontrolled by firms whereas SP are often voluntary and designed to protect the organisation’s information assets (Morin & Hovav, 2012). However, SP are occasionally implemented to comply with governmental laws (Whitman & Mattord, 2013). SP are mostly driven by standardisation organisations such as the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), Department of Commerce in the U.S., the International Standards Organization (ISO) in Europe and Asia, and by industry consortia such as the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) (Hovav & Gray, 2014; Kang & Hovav, 2020; Whitman & Mattord, 2013).

Information Security Policies. Organizational SP define the rules and guidelines for the use of information assets (D’Arcy et al., 2009). The literature describes three types of organisational SP (Whitman & Mattord, 2013). Enterprise policies are general and set the tone for the entire organisation. These policies prescribe the development of more specific SP. Issue specific policies provide general guidelines regarding specific issues such as actions related to the management of knowledge and information. System specific policies address specific architecture, platforms, and technologies, and guide the configuration rules for such systems. Top level managers are more likely to be familiar with the broadly defined enterprise level policies than with issue or system specific policies since those are often developed by middle management and departmental users. Furthermore, in the context of this work, information assets are defined as data, information, knowledge, and systems, including people and processes as suggested by Whitman and Mattord (2013). SP specify what are the acceptable behaviour given a particular asset and a particular user’s role (Jeon & Hovav, 2015) and the repercussion for non-compliance. For example, email related SP provide guidelines regarding what data, information or knowledge maybe shared via email, when, and with whom. While SP address a range of organisational computing and information resources, in this study we focus on SP related to intra-organisational information and knowledge transfer as we seek to understand the interplay between users ability to share knowledge while conforming to organisational SP.

Specifically, most organisations have established access control SP. These SP determine who is allowed to access what information assets, when, and for what purpose (Whitman & Mattord, 2013). Access control SP are often implemented using a variety of hardware and software solutions to ensure the integrity and confidentiality of the information assets they protect (Hovav & Berger, 2009). Despite the existence of such systems, users tend to circumvent SP for a number of reasons (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). The most notable reason for this circumventing behaviour is that access control SP are often rigid and interfere with the users daily tasks (Jeon & Hovav, 2018). Although SP are revised periodically (Whitman & Mattord, 2013), the need for rapid decision-making increased competition, and the rapid change of the organisation’s environment demand flexible work patterns, leading users to engage in workarounds (Jeon et al., 2018; Siponen, 2006). Thus, managers often advocate KMS as a tool to increase productivity and performance, while SP compartmentalise knowledge based on rigid “need to know” based policies (Elliott et al., 2019). The influence of this juxtaposition on organisational efectiveness and performance has yet to be examined in academic settings.

Furthermore, information leakage is a major issue for organisations (Kane, 2017; Siponen & Vance, 2010). Information leakage refers to the ability of users to share confidential information and knowledge with unauthorised entities. Although most organisations have established SP in regard to the sharing of information and knowledge, enforcement of these SP is dificult for two reasons. First, once the information has leaked, it is practically impossible to control the leaked information using traditional security mechanisms such as access control systems (Morin & Hovav, 2012). Second, some implicit organisational knowledge exists in peoples’ heads and can be verbally leaked intentionally or accidentally (Elliott et al., 2019). For example, an engineer might leak the details of a new design for monetary benefits (intentional leakage) or a group of collaborators from various firms might discuss the market potential of a new technology and inadvertently reveal the launch of a new service based on that technology (accidental leakage). While the latter is hard to control using technical measures, organisations occasionally implement enterprise rights management (ERM) systems (Morin & Hovav, 2012). Such systems provide persistent controls for information assets. ERM systems may increase confidentially but reduce flexibility and KMS capabilities (Jeon & Hovav, 2015). Thus, while technologies such as big data and data analytics provide new opportunities for value creation, gathering, organising, and disseminating knowledge in a secure manner might encumber these opportunities.

Cyber Security and Privacy Regulations. We define CR to be government laws and regulations that determine what organisations are allowed (or not allowed) to do with data, information, and knowledge. CR often instruct organisations in the security of information. That is, government regulations often describe what information must be secured, in what manner, for how long, and the penalties for non-compliant behaviour.

Early cyber security laws mostly addressed the infrastructure used to manage raw data and basic information. The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996 in the U.S. is the first known regulation to address information privacy. HIPAA was passed by the United States Congress and addresses medical records and the privacy of patients across the healthcare industry ecosystem (i.e., healthcare providers, labs, insurance companies, intermediaries). As of 2017, most developed countries have adopted similar laws that aim to protect medical records and patient privacy. In 1999, the United States Congress passed the

Financial Services Modernisation Act also known as the Gramm-Leach-Bliley (GLB) Act, which sanctioned extensive regulations regarding financial information. GLB calls for financial institutions “to insure the security and confidentiality of customer records and information.”<sup>2</sup> Similar regulations now exist in most developed countries and are often driven by the Basel Accord.<sup>3</sup> The most sweeping privacy regulation to date was passed by the European Union Commissioner (EUC) in April 2017 and has been in efect since May 25 2018.<sup>4</sup> The General Data Privacy Regulation (GDPR) is not limited to any one industry and applies to any organisation that stores, processes, transmits or manages data, and information or knowledge about a European Union (EU) citizen regardless of location. In June 2018, California passed a new data privacy law namely, the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)<sup>5</sup> that mimics the core tenets of the EU’s GDPR. While the above laws refer to “data privacy”, they also encompass information (most notably organisations should not include any identifiable information when sharing data) and knowledge (most often derived from big data and predictive data analytics) such as consumers buying patterns, social preferences, and political influences.<sup>6</sup> Although most developed countries have established some security and privacy regulations, their scope and reach vary. Appendix C summarises some of these fundamental diferences.

Compliance with CR is known to create institutional pressure that drives companies to implement pertinent SP (Hu et al., 2007). Early research suggests that although organisations view CR as inconsistent with the free flow of information and knowledge, the implementation of information security could increase organisational competitive advantage and provide new marketing opportunities (Wood, 1991). However, to date, there is no empirical evidence to support Wood’s assertions. Rather, research suggests that the market reacts negatively to privacy-related CR (Hovav et al., 2017; Al Mamun et al., 2004).<sup>7</sup>

At the time of this writing, little is known about the expected impact of recent privacy CR on information intensive organisations (i.e., social media sites) and processing (i.e., big data and data analytics). Moreover, little is known of how these new CR influence organisational ability to share and leverage knowledge for increased productivity and improved performance. Notwithstanding, it has been reported that Facebook lost 20% of its value while attempting to overhaul its security and privacy.<sup>8</sup> The report also mentions a significant drop in Facebook’s advertising revenue and active users in Europe after the implementation of GDPR.

## 3. Research model

Our conceptual model appears in Figure 1. We propose a model that examines the direct efects of the KM elements on the operational and strategic organisational outcomes (i.e., efectiveness and performance) and the moderating efects of SP and CR on these relationships. While KMA, KMS, and KMU improve organisational operational and strategic performance (Otondo, 2019), the influence of these discrete processes on organisational outcomes may vary. For example, while KMA and KMS were found to enhance efectiveness, KMU was found to increase performance (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). These deferential influences are reflected in our proposed model.

Research examining the relationship between KM and its organisational consequences has used efectiveness and performance as such outcomes. Notwithstanding, the promise of efective KM as a key organisational competency is still in its infancy (Kane, 2017). Knowledge is a manageable asset that enhances the organisation’s capability for efective actions (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). In addition, the fundamental aim of KM is not only to improve efectiveness but also to ultimately enhance performance (Schultze & Leidner, 2002). However, as stated above, the influence of the discrete processes of KM on organisational efectiveness and performance may vary. For example, while KMA and KMS are expected to enhanced efectiveness under some conditions, KMU was found to increase performance (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). The KM life cycle starts with the firms’ operational activities of knowledge acquisition and ends with the strategic utilisation of the acquired knowledge (Hwang et al., 2015; Kettinger et al., 2015). The sharing of pertinent knowledge is the conduit between acquisition and utilisation. Therefore, we posit that KMA and internal KMS influence operational outcomes (i.e., efectiveness), whereas external KMS and KMU influence strategic outcomes (i.e., performance).

We define organisational efectiveness as the firm’s ability to stream-line internal processes and adapt, respond, and react to changes (Gold et al., 2001). In the context of KM, efectiveness reflects the organisation’s ability to leverage knowledge-based resources to achieve the above goals (Khan & Vorley, 2017). Performance is defined as the outcomes of a firm’s activities in its markets and reflects the extent of the firm’s financial (e.g., profitability), and strategic (e.g., goal achievement) success (Zou et al., 1998).

As for the moderation efects, we expect SP to moderate the KMA-Efectiveness and the internal KMS-Efectiveness relationships, and CR to moderate the external KMS-Performance and the KMU-Performance relationships. Such separation is motivated by the diferential efects of the KM processes on the organisation’s operational and strategic outcomes discussed above.

## 4. Hypotheses development

## 4.1. Knowledge management acquisition and sharing and organizational efectiveness

KMA refers to the organisation’s ability to acquire information on customers, competitors, markets, and industry. While most research on KM assumes that KMA has positive implications for organisations, we contend that this is not always the case, especially in the today’s disruptive digital era. A substantial knowledge creation challenge lies in the trade-of between future benefits and current tasks, and the need to balance essential near-term goals without compromising long-term competitiveness. However, the firm’s ability to create and leverage knowledge for competitive advantage comes with a price. As markets continue to evolve, the decision-making required to evaluate knowledge creation has become highly complex (Chen & Edgington, 2005). Businesses today are surrounded by vast pools of data characterised by large volumes and multiple unstructured formats. Consequently, managers operate in information-rich environments that generate massive amounts of available data sources (George et al., 2016). Information overload may increase coordination eforts and the redundancy of information and knowledge, thereby impairing the firm’s ability to efectively adapt and respond to market changes. The constant flow of data sourced from digital technologies may also lead to knowledge depreciation due to decay or obsolescence of existing information, resulting in degraded knowledge integrity and complicated internal processes. Knowledge may also lose its value as a source of sustained competitive advantage when it becomes commonplace (Ceci et al., 2019; Chen & Edgington, 2005). In summary, conditions such as information overload and decay and integration complexity may reduce the value of knowledge acquisition by hindering the firm’s ability to efectively enact on the acquired knowledge. Therefore, we posit that KMA reduces organisational efectiveness.

H1a: Knowledge acquisition is negatively related to organisational efectiveness.

Internal KMS refers to the timely transfer of knowledge among employees and business units within the organisation. It improves the general efectiveness of organisational processes such as in product development (Kumar & Ganesh, 2011). Internal KMS can be informal (e.g., unscheduled meeting) or formal (e.g., training session). Both formats contribute to efectiveness in terms of saving time and resources (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Boh, 2014). Internal KMS allows individuals and units to work together and collaborate, thereby eliminating impediments that may have prevented such interaction (Gold et al., 2001). In addition, the interactions between individuals or between business units promote learning. By accessing the knowledge of other individuals or units with specialised experiences, organisational entities can reduce their learning curve (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). Furthermore, knowledge about a particular subject may reside in various systems or processes. Sharing and integrating this knowledge may enhance consistent representation, and subsequently improve efectiveness (Gold et al., 2001). Internal KMS also increases the knowledge overlap between employees and thus enhances the employees’ ability to generate associations between new and existing knowledge, thereby increasing the firm’s ability to recognise, assimilate, and apply new knowledge (Iyengar et al., 2015). Such improved integration and learning reduce the cycle time for decision-making and thus enhance efectiveness.

H1b: Internal knowledge sharing is positively related to organisational efectiveness.

Moderating Efects. Organizational policies, processes, and systems can determine access to and flow of knowledge. Such systems can lower or increase barriers to efective KM activities (Gold et al., 2001). Grant (1996) identified three primary mechanisms for the integration of knowledge to create organisational capability: directives, organisational routines, and selfcontained task teams. Directives refer to the specific set of rules, standards, procedures, and instructions. Directives enable the dissemination of tacit knowledge (Demsetz, 1991). Organizational routines refer to the development of protocols and processes that enable the integration of an individual’s specialised knowledge without the need to articulate and communicate that knowledge to others. SP include rules, guidelines, procedures and instructions regarding the use of computing and information assets (Whitman & Mattord, 2013). Such policies often contain guidelines and procedures to facilitate the secure acquisition of knowledge. We posit that the efect of SP on KMA is similar to the efect of directives and organisational routine on KMA as depicted by Grant (1996). Thus, we expect SP to facilitate the integration of acquired knowledge into organisational capabilities, thereby mitigating the negative efect of KMA on organisational efectiveness.

H2a: Security policies mitigate the negative relationship between knowledge acquisition and organisational efectiveness.

SP that address internal sharing of information assets are often rigid and err on the side of limited accessibility (Jeon et al., 2018; Kang & Hovav, 2020). Compliance with such SP has been associated with work impediment (Bulgurcu et al., 2010), response cost (Herath & Rao, 2009) and reactance (Hovav & Putri, 2016), which afect users’ task-performance. When employees have to comply with SP that restrict their access to information assets, they either engage in circumventing behaviour or they do not complete their task as prescribed (Jeon et al., 2018). Therefore, we posit that SP regarding access to information and internal KMS may diminish the positive efect of internal KMS on organisational efectiveness.

H2b: Security policies decrease the positive relationship between internal knowledge sharing and organisational efectiveness.

## 4.2. Organisational efectiveness and performance

Theory- and practical-based evidence in prior research support the positive relationship between organisational efectiveness and performance. In the context of this study, efectiveness (e.g., efective KM) in different levels of the organisation leads to unique capabilities, which contribute to superior performance (Gold et al., 2001). For example, efectiveness achieved through KMS, contributes to creativity and innovation, which are known to increase performance. KM also support organisational decision-making subsequently improving performance (Ferreira et al., 2018; Warrier, 2009).

H3: Organizational efectiveness is positively related to organisational performance.

## 4.3. Knowledge management sharing and utilisation and organizational performance

Performance may not necessarily be driven by the existence of knowledge per se but by the firm’s ability to efectively utilise that knowledge and take actions that form the basis for achieving competitive advantage from knowledge-based assets (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). Organizations that efectively facilitate the management of knowledge utilisation perform better (Boh & Wong, 2015). Thus, KMU plays a role in attaining higher performance. Companies that struggle to sustain competitiveness need to invest in managing their knowledge resources since these eforts are imperative to improve growth and market share. Hence, firms that employ appropriate KMU practices strengthen their capabilities and thus improve business performance (Seba & Rowley, 2010; Zaim et al., 2019).

H4a: Knowledge utilisation is positively related to organisational performance.

As for external KMS, inter-organisational sharing of information and knowledge promotes organisational outcomes (Iyengar et al., 2015; Jeung et al., 2017). It ofers opportunities for companies to achieve competitive advantage and performance (e.g., financial benefits) (Boh & Wong, 2015). External KMS facilitates organisation’s interaction with business partners and thus improves the organisation’s outcomes (Gold et al., 2001; Kankanhalli et al., 2005). External KMS is also an efective way to obtain market knowledge about competitors and the industry. This knowledge is used to adjust strategic positions and adapt to changes (Gold et al., 2001). Hence, external KMS improves business performance.

H4b: External knowledge sharing is positively related to organisational performance.

Moderating Efects. Extensive data sharing between organisations increasingly poses concerns of consumer privacy (Schneider et al., 2017). While the moderating efect of CR on the relationships between KMS and KMU on organisational performance has yet to be addressed in the extant cyber security and privacy literature, several studies have investigated the efect of other regulatory activities on the market value and financial performance of firms. Early studies suggest that the market reaction to a drug recall was significantly higher for cases when new or stricter regulations were anticipated. The market reacts to the potential added cost to comply with the new regulations, which in turn is expected to lower the regulated firms’ financial performance (Dranove & Olsen, 1994). Research also indicates that imposing regulations on firms may actually have a positive market reaction for the regulated industry (Li et al., 2008). However, these same regulations had a negative efect on firms that exhibited information technology (IT) related risk. That is, the market reacted more severely to IT risks after the implementation of regulations such as Sarbanes–Oxley (SOX) in the U.S. (Benaroch et al., 2012), privacy laws in South Korea (Hovav et al., 2017) and GDPR in the EU.<sup>9</sup>

Furthermore, the incorporation of privacy-related components in a given law was found to have a significant negative efect on the performance of regulated firms (Al Mamun et al., 2004). For example, the introduction of the GLB Act resulted in a positive market reaction. However, the market reacted negatively first when President Clinton suggested that a privacy provision be included in the bill and subsequently when the House of Representatives’ version was introduced on July 1 1999. The second negative reaction was because the consumer privacy provision in the House of Representatives’ version was much more pronounced than in previous versions (Al Mamun et al., 2004). Therefore, we posit that CR will reduce the positive efect of KMU and external KMS on organisational performance.

H5a: Cyber regulations decrease the positive relationships between knowledge utilisation and organisational performance.

H5b: Cyber regulations decrease the positive relationships between external knowledge sharing and organisational performance.

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Sample

We chose to collect data in Israel for the following reasons. First, Israel’s economic growth relies on technological advances, especially in hi-tech and cyber security. In 2016, Israel founded the National Cyber

Security Authority (NCSA), a governmental entity responsible for protecting Israel’s civilian cyber space. The NCSA encourages Israeli firms to engage in cyber operations via educational programs, incentives, and policies. While not oficially part of the EU, Israeli firms operate under the EU’s regulatory frameworks. This interplay between technological advancements and privacy regulations provides an interesting context for our study. Second, Israel is at a constant conflict and thus has been sensitive to cyber terrorism and information manipulation for decades. Yet, it is unclear how Israel’s heighten national sensitivity to security may afect the behaviour of companies. We chose senior managers as our target population since senior managers have a broad overview of their organisation’s operational and strategic outcomes. Senior managers are also better positioned to respond to questions regarding cyber regulations and their influence on the organisation’s financial and strategic performance.

## 5.2. Development of measures

The unit of analysis is the organisation. After developing the research model and hypotheses, we developed a survey questionnaire. We adapted the questions for KMA, KMS, KMU, and organisational efectiveness from Gold et al. (2001). The survey items for organisational performance were adapted from Zou et al. (1998). The questions for SP and CR were loosely based on D’Arcy et al. (2009). However, while D’Arcy et al. (2009) surveyed organisational users, our target respondents were senior executives. Accordingly, the questions were phrased to address regulations and policies that afect the organisation by using phrases such as “our organisation operates under specific guidelines that . . . .” The SP survey items were designed to ascertain the existence of internal policies and guidelines regarding the use of information assets. The CR survey questions were design to ascertain the existence of cyber regulations, and external guidelines regarding the use and sharing of information assets as mandated by governing entities. Finally, we included control variables to account for extraneous sources of variation in the terminal dependent variable (Figure 1). The control variables were adapted from Gnizy (2019).

All constructs except for the control variables were measured on a seven-point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree, ” as suggested by Preston and Colman (2000). The survey was translated to Hebrew. Backward translation (with the material translated from English into Hebrew, and back into English; versions compared; discrepancies resolved)

was used to ensure consistency between Hebrew and the original English version of the instrument (Singh, 1995). To assess the face validity, clarity, and relevance of the items, an initial version of the survey instrument was subsequently refined through extensive pretesting with a panel of academic and industry experts. In addition, pilot tests were conducted with a preliminary sample of managers (n = 50). The respondents were asked to complete the survey and provide feedback and suggestions for improvement when instructions or questions were not clear. We used the pilot sample to refine some questions, and to test and confirm the initial reliability and validity of the scales (Appendix A).

## 5.3. Data collection

Data were collected from firms through a professional panel company using an online survey. As such, the sample is a convenience sample. The market research company approached firms and managers according to a pre-defined profile that best fits this study’s context. The research company filtered any respondent that was not a C-level executive and removed respondents from small size companies and self-employed contractors. To guarantee a high level of participants suitability and reduce potential bias, the questionnaire included six 7-point items that assessed respondents familiarity with and confidence in addressing KM, cyber security, and performance issues covered in this research (Gnizy, 2019). The three items for familiarity averaged 4.11, 4.36 and 5.11, respectively. The three items for confidence averaged 4.66, 5.13, and 5.19, respectively. These steps ensured that the survey respondents were well positioned and qualified to provide insights about the study’s subject matters. Our sample reflected a cross-section of executive rank and function, and of firms’ sizes and industries (Table B1). This multi-characteristic design allows greater variability, reduces the likelihood of sampling bias, and enables greater generalisability.

## 5.4. Common method and non-response bias assessment

As with all self-reported data, there is a potential for common method bias (CMB) resulting from multiple sources such as consistency motif and social desirability (Podsakof et al., 2003; Podsakof & Organ, 1986). We followed ex-ante and ex-post procedures recommended by Podsakof et al. (2003) to minimise CMB concerns.

Ex-ante, we applied a systematic design of the questionnaire and development of the measures (e.g., division into sections and multiple scale formats) to ensure clarity and consistency. Respondents were guaranteed anonymity and confidentially. The respondents were advised that the questions had no right or wrong answers and should be answered honestly. Using Qualtrics survey software ensured that the respondents could not skip survey questions before completing answers to all current displayed questions. Finally, our model entails complex relationships among the constructs and thus it is unlikely for respondents to anticipate the relationships under investigation based on the survey questions.

Ex-post strategies included the following (Podsakof et al., 2003). First, we conducted a single-factor test. In an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) that included all indicators of the study’s constructs, the first factor accounted for only 34.44% of the variance, suggesting no single factor is responsible for the majority of the variance in the measures. Second, we used the ex-ante marker variable approach that requires a priori use of a study variable that should theoretically not relate to the model’s latent variables (Lindell & Whitney, 2001). The correlations between the item (i.e., respondents readership of academic journals) and the model’s constructs were unrelated. Collectively, these tests reinforce the absence of CMB evidence.

Additionally, a series of t-tests that compared early (i.e., pilot data collected several months before the main data collection) and late (i.e., actual main survey) respondents with regard to the study’s constructs, found no significant diferences at the 1% level (Armstrong & Overton, 1977). Therefore, there is no evidence of a non-response bias.

## 6. Data analysis and results

## 6.1. Analysis method

SPSS 23.0 and SmartPLS M2.0 were used to analyse the measurement and structural models and WarpPLS 5.0 was used for the post hoc analysis. Partial least squares (PLS) was chosen to examine the proposed model for the following reasons. First, PLS is suitable for assessing theories in the early stages of development. Second, PLS requires minimal demands on sample size to validate a model (Chin et al., 2003). Third, PLS has been shown to be an efective analytical tool for testing product-term interaction efects (Rollins et al., 2012) that were used to test our moderation hypotheses.

## 6.2. Measurement model

We analysed the content validity, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the instruments. The content validity of the instruments was established by ensuring that the measurement items are consistent with the extant literature, pre-testing of the instruments, and receiving professional advice from a panel of experts. Convergent validity was assessed by extracting composite reliability and average variance value of the constructs (J. Hair et al., 1995). The composite reliability values for all constructs are larger than 0.5 and the average variance extracted (AVE) estimates exceeded 0.5 (Table B2). These demonstrate appropriate reliability and convergent validity for all measures (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

The loadings of the items used to measure the constructs. All measures are significant on their path loadings at the 0.001 level (Table B3). Therefore, the eight constructs used in our study may be judged as being appropriate for analysing the structural model.

Table 1 shows that the square root of the AVE for each construct was larger than the correlations between the average variance and all other constructs. Furthermore, the results of the inter-construct correlations showed that each construct shared greater variance with its own measures than with other measures. In further support of reliability and validity of the measurement model, constructs in a research model should have relatively low inter-correlations (i.e., <0.6, Voorhees et al., 2016). The vast majority of the model’s constructs were not highly correlated (Table 1).

To verify the scales structure, an EFA was conducted. The results show that the measures were unidimensional where all items loaded onto their respective factors with considerable item loadings (J. F. Hair et al., 2010). In addition, a visual inspection of the matrix of loadings and cross-loadings results through the SmartPLS run showed that items loaded on the intended constructs, thus confirming appropriate discriminant validity for the latent variables of the model (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Gefen & Straub, 2005).

## 6.4. Additional and post-hoc analyses

To better understand the non-significant relationships found for the internal KMS-SP-efectiveness (H2b) and external KMS-CR-performance (H5b) relationships, we conducted post-hoc analyses. We analysed the research model using a nonlinear algorithm provided in WarpPLS 5.0 that allows for nonlinear relationships between variables (Kock, 2015). Specifically, we employed the Warp3 algorithm, which tries to identify S-curve relationships among linked latent variables.

## 6.4.1. The efect of policies on internal KMS and organisational efectiveness (H2b)

Figure 3 illustrates the moderating efect of low and high SP levels on the relationship between internal KMS and efectiveness. For the high SP group (right graph), the positive relationship is almost linear. This provides further support for our hypothesis regarding the positive efect of internal KMS on efectiveness (H1b). However, for the low SP group (left graph), the relationship between internal KMS and efectiveness has an S-curve shape. This graph suggests that without SP, initially internal KMS improves efectiveness. However, as KMS increases, the efectiveness remains the same. A plausible explanation is that without clear policies, the cost to share knowledge might diminish its benefits. This contradicts prior research that suggests that SP increases work impediment (Bulgurcu et al., 2010) and reduces task-performance (Jeon et al., 2018). The contradiction may be due to the oversimplification of the level and type of policies used in the above studies. Furthermore, prior studies examined individual employees’ perceptions of the influence of SP on work-task performance. In this study, we examine organisational efectiveness. It is possible that while individuals feel that SP impedes their work, organisations view policies as a facilitator. This is also evident by our finding that SP mitigates the negative relationships between KMA and organisational efectiveness (H2a).

## 7. Discussion

Our findings provide insight into the dynamics of the interplay between managing knowledge and securing information and its contribution to organisational efectiveness and performance.

## 7.1. Organisational efectiveness

We found that KMA impeded organisational efectiveness as proposed by Ceci et al. (2019) and Chen and Edgington (2005). We also found that internal KMS was positively related to efectiveness. This finding is consistent with empirical studies showing that internal sharing and transfer of knowledge contribute to organisational efectiveness (Boh, 2014; Kumar & Ganesh, 2011; Lin et al., 2005).

However, SP mitigated the negative efect of KMA on efectiveness. It is possible that while knowledge sharing improves organisational processes, the cost to acquire, manage, and store knowledge complicates these processes and thus reduces organisational efectiveness. The availability of rules and guidelines regarding what information the organisation is allowed to collect and how this information should be managed, reduces that cost. While SP had no statistically significant moderating efect on the relationships between internal KMS and efectiveness, our post-hoc analysis (Figure 3) suggests that the existence of policies enables organisations to utilise internal knowledge-sharing to improve efectiveness, while lack of policies diminishes the value of internal knowledge-sharing.

## 7.2. Organisational performance

Our findings indicate that KMU is positively related to performance as suggested by the extant literature. However, CR decreased the positive efect of KMU on performance. These findings suggest that regulations regarding the use of knowledge might limit the benefits that organisations may gain from utilising collected and shared knowledge. Given the increase in these two conflicting trends (i.e., regulations such as the GDPR versus increase in the use of KM), the negative efect of CR on knowledge-based economies requires further investigation.

Contrary to our assumption and the literature on KM, external KMS was negatively related to performance. Often, knowledge begins to lose value as a source of sustaining competitive advantage once it is codified (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995), becomes common knowledge (Ceci et al., 2019), or shared, transferred, or sold, thus diluting its novelty, scarcity, and competitive value (Chen & Edgington, 2005). Knowledge-sharing with external entities requires coordination mechanisms and carries a cost. It is possible that the increased cost and overhead are likely to produce less-than-desirable performance outcomes. Furthermore, in highly regulated environments, external knowledge-sharing may require a costly compliance mechanism. This assertion is supported by our post-hoc analysis (Figure 4). It is also important to note that prior research examined knowledge-sharing as a single construct. In this study, we found that internal knowledge-sharing and external knowledge-sharing have a conflicting efect on the organisation. These findings might explain some of the inconsistencies found in the extant literature regarding the efect of KM on organisational performance.

In conclusion, our study suggests that the existence of KM does not guarantee an increase in organisational efectiveness and performance. Rather, organisational internal (i.e., SP) and external (i.e., CR) conditions can determine the influence of KM on organisational efectiveness and performance.

## 7.3. Theoretical implications

Organisations are turning to contemporary KM (e.g., big data and data analytics) to leverage their knowledge resources internally (i.e., efectiveness) and externally (i.e., performance). Both industry and academic research tout the organisational ability to leverage their knowledge as a key strategic management factor that can help organisations to sustain a competitive advantage in volatile environments. Concomitantly, privacy advocates call for an increase in information governance and regulations. Furthermore, while some research on KM assumes that knowledge has positive implications for organisations (Chen & Edgington, 2005; Schultze & Leidner, 2002), other studies identified a number of inconsistencies regarding the relationships between KM and performance (Novak, 2017). Yet, these studies did not attempt to investigate the root of the inconsistencies. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that examines recent institutional cybersecurity developments such as CR and SP that either facilitate or impede the efects of KM on firms’ outcomes.

Additionally, while knowledge spans many levels of analysis, this study has approached KM from the perspective of the organisation. By disentangling the KM concept, our study examines the efect of cybersecurity on and relating to each of the KM processes. This approach enabled us to explore how the KM processes relate to specific organisational outcomes. For example, the diferential influence of external versus internal knowledge sharing could explain the mixed results found in the extant literature.

## 7.4. Managerial implications

The continuing velocity and dynamic nature of the marketplace creates a competitive incentive for firms to efectively manage and maintain their knowledge assets as a means of creating sustainable value. However, top management should pay attention to the KM and KM elements to further enhance organisational outcomes.

According to the results of the study, KM elements could afect organisational efectiveness and performance diferently. Furthermore, the rapid change of information technologies such as pervasive social media or big data is changing the organisational cybersecurity environment and thus the need for detailed SP. Firms should implement segmented security policies for KMA and KMS to achieve better efectiveness. This is because clear security policies could improve the organisation’s ability to respond to market demands or to react to new information.

As mentioned above, while the use of knowledge assets is expected to improve organisational performance, the increase in cyber regulations reduces the positive efects of knowledge utilisation on performance. Top management should include tangible and intangible costs of complying with cyber regulations in their strategic plans. Since compliance with these regulations is mandatory, incorporating cyber risk in a firm’s strategic planning is prudent. These findings are applicable across industry regardless of the industry’s regulatory environment.

## 7.5. Limitations and directions for future research

By highlighting this work’s limitations, we also draw attention to potentially future research avenues.

Knowledge spans many levels of analysis that inform the design of KM, a complex and multi-faceted phenomenon. Our study did not distinguish between the various types of knowledge. Future research can account for the characteristics of knowledge such as tacit or explicit, knowledge that refers to an object or a capability, and knowledge that resides in individuals, groups, documents, processes, policies, physical settings, or computer repositories.

The data were collected from organisations in one country, which could limit the generalisability of the findings due to contextual diferences and localised regulations. Diferent contexts, regulations, and expectations of security policies might yield diferent outcomes. Institutional diferences among countries such as regulations and practices related to security may also influence organisational performance. National cultural diferences such as collectivism, risk aversion, reliance on the government, and privacy index may also influence organisational efectiveness or performance. Future research can examine our proposed research model in various contexts to account for the above diferential factors.

Additional contextual factors might influence the relationships between KM, organisational outcomes, and secure data management. For example, future research can examine the influence of organisational data protection and business continuity plans (e.g., backup and recovery, system redundancy) on the relationships between KM and performance.

Although we investigated the unsupported hypotheses using post-hoc analyses and provided plausible explanations for our empirical findings, further research is needed to better understand the intricacies of internal and external KMS, their influence on organisational efectiveness and performance, and the moderating efect of internal (SP) and external (CR) factors on these relationships.

## 8. Conclusions

In a future characterised by volatile environments, the efective application of organisational knowledge is a diferentiating factor. Firms need to consider the influence of contextual factors such as cyber regulations and policies on knowledge acquisition, internal and external sharing, and utilisation. While industry touts the strategic benefits of contemporary KM applications, privacy advocates emphasise the need for information security policies and regulations. This study adds to our current body of knowledge by highlighting the juxtaposition between the need to share knowledge within and across organisations and the need to secure and protect information. Furthermore, by examining the various processes of KM, we underline the contradictory influences that these processes have on organisational efectiveness and performance.

## Appendix C - Fundamental diferences in CR across countries/regions

Opt-in versus Opt-out: in some countries, such as the U.S., individuals have to opt-out (notify any company they do business with if they do not want their data used or shared). Other countries such as all members of the EU have adopted the opt-in option where customers have to deliberately agree before their data can be shared or used. Global organisations need to comply with local rules. From a business perspective, the opt-out framework enables companies to provide personalised products and services, accurate recommendation systems and targeted marketing.

Localised versus universal laws: Early data protection regulations developed by the U.S. government (i.e., HIPAA and GLB), focused on particular industries. This model was followed by most governments. The EU’s GDPR is the first overarching CR and aims to address privacy of information globally. Neither model is perfect as both fail to address new technology, new types of information and new types of breaches in a timely manner. From a business perspective, localised laws are economically beneficial to companies that are not regulated. Such companies have less incentive to invest in advanced security solutions than companies in regulated industries (Hovav & Gray, 2014).

Who owns the information: Web 2.0 created novel business models, where information is a tradable commodity. Firms such as Google or Facebook use information that they collect as a means to increase their profits. At present, these companies own the data that they collect and with a few exceptions, can use it for their benefit or share it (as long as the PII data is scrubbed). In May 2014, the European Court of Justice ruled against Google in a case brought by a Spanish man, who requested the removal of a link to an article. The “right to be forgotten” began and various countries ruled regarding digital data stored in the likes of Facebook and Google data warehouses. Furthermore, GDPR imposes new information privacy laws regarding the collection, storage, dissemination, and use of information about EU citizens. These laws apply to all relevant organisations regardless of their physical location or lega jurisdiction. From a business prospective, information intensive companies whose business models rely on the value of the information they collect and their ability to leverage that information (e.g., selling, sharing, or marketing), will have to adapt their business models. As of the writing of this paper, it is unclear to what extent GDPR will afect organisations’ ability to leverage the knowledge that they collect to gain competitive advantage.
