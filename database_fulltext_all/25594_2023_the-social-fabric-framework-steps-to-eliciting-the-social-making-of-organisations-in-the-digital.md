# The social fabric framework: steps to eliciting the social making of organisations in the digital age

Michel Avital , Tina Blegind Jensen and Signe Dyrby

Department of Digitalization , Copenhagen Business School, Copenhagen, Denmark

## ABSTRACT

The proliferation of enterprise social media generates an ever-growing record of digital traces that provides ample opportunities to study the social making of organisations. Subsequently, we present the social fabric framework, which comprises a structured five-step approach for eliciting, interpreting, and representing the situated social idiosyncrasies and underlying patterns of the social making of organisations. The paper focuses on the application of the social fabric framework as a research method. However, the framework also lends itself to practice as a diagnostic tool that can detect emergent changes in the social fabric of an organisation as well as support organisational development and change. Moreover, by providing a vocabulary for articulating the social making of organisations, the framework can help organisation members reify their dispositions, make sense of the social dynamics, and enable a constructive discussion at the grassroots level about any controversy or aspiration.

## 1. Introduction

It has long been acknowledged in the social sciences that discourse and narratives mirror the milieu in which they were conceived and can cast light on the social making of the world (Fairclough, 1992). The proliferation of social media in organisations, and particularly enterprise social media platforms, generates an ever-growing record that provides ample opportunities to study organisations through the increased visibility of the interactions between people and work practices, as reflected in the digital traces that are left in the course of everyday activities (Cheney et al., 2011; Lindberg, 2020; Marres, 2015; McKenna et al., 2017; Whelan et al., 2016).

The open and perpetual display of messages exchanged via social media in organisations sheds light on the transparent and ad-hoc nature of knowledge sharing, as well as the relationships that form the foundations of organisational practices (Richter & Riemer, 2009; Riemer & Scifleet, 2012). While conversations on more traditional media such as email, chat, and telephone are confined to the interacting individuals, interactions on social media are typically shared across large groups or the entire organisation, allowing a majority of members to see, follow, and engage in the interaction (Leonardi et al., 2013; Treem & Leonardi, 2013). A growing body of research demonstrates that social media platforms are places not only for congregation but also for aggregation of social life. Studies highlight how the algorithmic foundations of social media platforms bring about a sociality mediated by computational means and instrumented through social media technologies (e.g., Dittes et al., 2019; Kallinikos & Tempini, 2014; Leidner et al., 2018; Pentland et al., 2020; Van Osch & Steinfield, 2018).

Building on this premise, this paper presents and illustrates a research method for studying the social making of organisations as it is reflected in the pervasive layers of its digital traces. Specifically, we propose the social fabric framework as a means to reveal and describe patterns of the social making of organisations. We use the term social making to denote the dynamics between individual practice, communicative interactions, and the formation of relationships between organisational actors. While “social” emphasises the relationships among individuals in a collective, “making” refers to the evolving interactions that form and shape these relationships. The sociological roots of the term social making underscore how studying the social can rely on connecting (digital) traces of individuals’ activities to identify overall patterns or structures of social life (Fairhurst & Putnam, 2004; Vass, 2010). Hence, studying the social making of an organisation calls for a structured analysis of social activity in search of patterns that highlight distinct social formations and demonstrate how the organisation comes to life (McKenna et al., 2017).

The social fabric framework is an additional research method for scholars studying the social making of organisations. In particular, the framework serves as a means to elicit, interpret, and represent the distinct underlying patterns of the social making of organisations. The inductive nature of the framework makes it inherently sensitive to the emergent, situated, and dynamic characteristics of the social making. The basic application of the framework elicits and renders the dominant dimensions of the social fabric. However, the structured nature of the analysis, combined with the inherent temporal depth of the data, makes it conducive to longitudinal studies that aim to examine how the social making of an organisation changes over time. Furthermore, the social fabric framework is well-suited to comparative studies that aim to juxtapose the social making of several organisations or parts thereof. All in all, the framework can prove useful in a wide spectrum of research that can benefit from explicit characterisation of the social making of organisations and how it evolves over time. Naturally, the application of the social fabric framework presumes that the discourse of the underlying organisation is mirrored reliably on social media. Consequently, the framework is efective only if the social media platform in use provides a valid representation of the discourse in the organisation of interest.

Besides serving as a research method for studying the social making, the social fabric framework lends itself to practice as a diagnostic tool in the context of organisational development. The framework can be used to sense emergent changes in the social making of an organisation, as well as to monitor the efect of organisational development eforts. Subsequently, the social fabric framework can be used as a springboard for a collective dialogue among involved organisation members about the prevailing work practices and the organisational culture. By providing a language for articulating the social making, the framework can help organisation members reify their dispositions, make sense of the social dynamics, and enable a constructive discussion at the grassroots level about any controversy or aspiration.

In the following section, we present the underpinning premises that form the foundation for the social fabric framework. Subsequently, we provide a step-bystep description of the framework development and demonstrate its application. We conclude with a discussion of the implications of the framework and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Conceptual underpinnings

The social fabric framework is a research method for studying the social making of organisations. At its core, the framework is grounded in the presumption that knowledge of reality, and in particular of human action, is a social construction by human actors who create their own subjective and intersubjective meanings as they interact with the world around them (Burr, 2015; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). Accordingly, the framework is designed to serve scholars in a quest to understand the social making of organisations through the eyes of those generating it and ascribing meanings to the way they experience it.

The proposed social fabric framework builds on generally known approaches to data collection and analysis that fit well with the social media milieu. The framework provides a systematic data-driven method to articulating the social making of organisations with an emphasis on rigour while retaining an open-ended and generative potential for revealing new insights (Gioia et al., 2013). The framework builds on a few established conceptual insights about the relationship between social phenomena and data. In particular, and as discussed next, the development of the framework relies on the following threefold conceptual underpinnings: (a) data traces on social media platforms mirror interactions and dispositions of people, (b) the analysis of discursive exchange in social media about disagreements or controversies in an organisation reveals the situated beliefs and practices that reflect its social making, and (c) the social fabric metaphor provides a literary device for delineating the social making of organisations.

## 2.1. Traces of data as a mirror of social making

Organisational life can be understood in the nexus between individual practices (Nicolini, 2009), communicative interactions (Cheney et al., 2011), and the relationships that form between organisational actors (Orlikowski, 2010). In this paper, we treat organising as a reiterated, continuous, evolutionary process of interaction (Weick, 1979a). Furthermore, we focus on organisational activities and behaviours that are formed as part of an integrated network of intersubjective meanings, common language, and social interactions (Weick et al., 2005). In addition, we build on the observation that distinct patterns of social structures surface through the aggregation of accumulated traces of interactions among individuals and similar social activity (Lazar et al., 2009). Consequently, studying the social making of organisations entails a structured observation of traces of social activity in search of patterns that highlight the unique relational constellations and demonstrate how the organisation comes to life.

The digital traces of human conduct on social media, and particularly on enterprise social media, presents an opportunity for observing and studying work practices over time. In turn, scholars have called for new ways of analysing and interpreting online practices and interactions (e.g., Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2020; Gasson & Waters, 2013; Leonardi & Vaast, 2017). Subsequently, research on social media has revealed a mix of methods for data collection and analysis, which allows for consideration of the online display of communication and interaction (McKenna et al., 2017). The most common approach to data collection has been a mix of interviews and surveys together with online observations and the logging of posts and comments from social media platforms. The increased visibility of interactions on social media in the form of online posts, comments, “likes,” and “shares” gives an insight into the actions and activities of the networked members (Majchrzak et al., 2013). In particular, the logging properties of social media allow for the retrieval and subsequent analysis of all previous activity on the platform at a level of granularity that previously was not possible (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014). In addition to logs of metadata and use patterns of interest, the continuous flow of online posts and comments can provide new insights into the communicative interactions between individuals on the platforms as they take place (Gasson & Waters, 2013; Gibbs et al., 2014).

In sum, the proliferation of social media in organisations, and particularly enterprise social media platforms, generates an ever-growing record that provides ample opportunities for studying the social making of organisations as it is reflected in layers of digital traces that are left by the organisation members in the course of everyday activities. Uncovering and describing the social making of an organisation requires that we examine instances in which the “social” becomes explicit as described next.

## 2.2. Making situated social practices explicit

Studies of organisations using social media data have relied on a variety of analytical strategies ranging from thematic content analysis (Gibbs et al., 2014; Wasko & Faraj, 2005) to regression analysis (Ellison et al., 2007), sentiment analysis (Gu et al., 2014), and network analysis (Kane et al., 2014). However, the apparent amorphous nature of the social making of organisations calls for a more definitive analytical strategy that can cater to its intangible, situated, and dynamic properties.

The study of organisations and human activity systems has a long tradition of setting up procedures for the systematic collection of data on observable meaningful events that reflect latent organisational characteristics and their tacit dynamics. For example, the critical incident technique (Flanagan, 1954) laid the foundation for similar event-driven approaches such as the punctuated socio-technical change model (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). The main challenge with applying event-driven research methodologies is identifying the situated meaningful events in vivo. This challenge has been addressed in the study of organisations and human activity systems through the analysis of crisis or conflict that sheds light on latent organisational characteristics and their tacit dynamics. For example, the analysis of environmental jolts to study organisational change (Meyer et al., 1990) and the analysis of fire disasters to study organisational sensemaking and resilience (Weick, 1993).

In this paper, we build on the analysis of controversies that has been used in conjunction with digital methods to map issues of public concern in social media in the context of science, technology, and society (STS) studies (Burgess & Matamoros-Fernández, 2016; Marres, 2015). Controversy analysis presumes that the discourse around debates and disagreements in a social group provides an opportunity to examine the group’s social making through analysis of the nature of the controversy, the subsequent discussion, and the resulting settlement (Latour, 2005). Controversies are normal in collective life and inevitable part of the social world. They “become empirical resources that allow us to address questions like, is this topic an issue? where is it happening, and what forms does it take?” (Marres, 2015, p. 677). It is through disagreements and debates that opinions become evident and meaning is socially constructed (Latour, 1988, 2005). Hence, we can investigate controversies by exploring situations in which organisation members disagree about something. The disagreements are often subtle and do not reach a state of open fights; rather, they are expressions of mundane diferences of opinion among colleagues who come together in the creation of a shared universe (Venturini, 2010). Therefore, controversy analysis is not only an opportunity to render visible the range of dispositions about a situated topic of interest among organisation members, it also provides an “empirical occasion” for further inquiry into the social making of the organisation (Latour, 2005; Marres, 2015).

Subsequently, inspired by Venturini’s (2010) cartography of controversies, we formed the controversy analysis cycle to portray the life cycle of a controversy in collective life. Accordingly, the life cycle of a controversy starts when organisation members realise that they cannot ignore an emerging disagreement, and it ends when some form of agreement has been reached among the involved parties. Figure 1 portrays the circular process of a controversy analysis in which a “local disagreement” evokes a “public debate” that reaches a “settlement” and eventually becomes manifested in the “performed practice” of the organisation. The cyclical nature of the model underscores that a given performed practice prevails only until it is questioned and evokes a new disagreement.

The analysis of controversies reveals situated beliefs and practices that can shed light on the social making of the underlying organisation. However, while controversy analysis helps to surgically identify discrete instances, each attesting to one particular aspect of social making, it falls short of describing the interrelationships among these instances. Next, we introduce the social fabric metaphor as a literary device to describe and analyse the social making of the organisation as a whole.

## 2.3. The social fabric metaphor

Metaphors are common in the discourse on organisations and organisational life: “Metaphors supply language with flexibility, expressibility and a way to expand the language” (Weick, 1979b, p. 47). Building on the notion of fabric<sup>1</sup> as a figure of speech, the social fabric portrays social making as “threads” of interaction between people that are woven together to form a whole. In line with previous conceptualisations of the social fabric metaphor (see Table 1), we define “social fabric” as a metaphor (Lakof & Johnson, 2003) that describes the social attributes which underlie the threads of relationships between people in a collective. The social fabric is shaped and reshaped by the prevailing beliefs and practices in a collective as well as its technological milieu.

Social fabric has been applied in other disciplines, including computer science, economics, sociology, human ecology and urban studies, as well as organisation and management studies, as a metaphor to describe a system of relationships between individuals, groups, and institutions forming a coherent whole. As highlighted in Table 1, the social fabric metaphor has been utilised to explain the nature of human interactions (Alario and Freudenburg 2003; Broadbent, 1986; Short, 1984; Teymoori et al., 2017) that is reflected in the prevailing beliefs and practices of a collective (Moolenaar & Sleegers, 2012; Van Maanen & Schein, 1979; Wenger, 1996; Zammuto et al., 2007) shaped by the environment (Corcoran et al., 2008; Henderson, 1995; Huijbens, 2012; Netto, 2017; Pflieger et al., 2008)

and embedded in interlacing social networks (Cheng et al., 2005; Reynolds & Ali, 2008).

As already shown in the related studies, we can observe the social making in the flow of behaviours and narratives expressed and experienced by individuals in a collective. Building on a rich tradition of using metaphors in organisational research (Cornelissen et al., 2008), the social fabric metaphor can be leveraged to examine the relationship between distinctive threads of the social making that emerge from the controversy analysis of social media in a target organisation and, subsequently, to render a holistic description of that organisation’s social making. The social fabric metaphor can be instrumental in guiding the perceived images of the social making and helping to formulate these perceptions as intelligible accounts. In the next section, we describe and illustrate the social fabric framework.

## 3. The social fabric framework

Building on the above-mentioned premise that (a) data traces mirror social making, (b) the application of controversy analysis can reveal situated beliefs and practices that reflect social making, and (c) the social fabric metaphor provides a literary device for delineating the social making of organisations, we next describe a five-step analytical framework that can reveal and portray the social making of organisations.

The first step elicits social making as “threads” of interactions between people that are woven together to form a whole. We identify the applicable threads that are prevalent in the organisational discourse through controversy analysis. The second step estimates the degree of association between all possible pairs of social fabric threads by developing a co-occurrence matrix. The third step clusters the highly-related threads to reveal specific patterns of the social fabric. The fourth step reveals the social fabric and validates its distinct dimensions. Finally, the fifth step renders the pattern of the social making of the target organisation on the backdrop of its social fabric. Together, the resulting representation reflects the social fabric of the organisation and renders its unique social making. Table 2 summarises the outline of the framework in terms of the analysis process and its outcome. Each step is described and illustrated in the remainder of this section.

To demonstrate the application of the social fabric framework, we draw on data from a case study on the use of the enterprise social media platform Yammer in an IT consultancy company, which in this paper we will call TopConsulting. TopConsulting had 43 employees when the data collection began and had grown by almost 500% when the data collection ended two years later. TopConsulting specialises in providing software solutions and consultancy services within the Microsoft ecosystem to a range of local and international companies. The company operates from a home-base headquarters that hosts the management team and the administrative staf; however, most of the employees are consultants who work from client ofices on a daily basis.

Inspired by its afiliation with Microsoft, the CEO decided to adopt Yammer for internal communication. As part of the adoption strategy, the company turned Yammer into a mandatory and central communication channel for all operational information in the organisation and encouraged all employees to use the platform to engage with one another on all matters as part of their daily work. Overall, the data consist of a mix of 1,997 Yammer posts and 2,696 comments, 363 hours of onsite and ofsite field observations, 24 in-depth interviews with key stakeholders across the board, and countless hours of informal interactions at TopConsulting.

Next, we describe the five-step approach of the social fabric framework and illustrate its application to studying the social making of organisations.

## 3.1. Step 1: eliciting the prevalent social fabric threads

The first step of the framework utilises the controversy analysis cycle to identify and describe specific instances in which the “social” becomes explicit.

Using the fabric metaphor, we regard the outcome of this analysis as the threads that make up the social fabric.

The operational objective of the first step in the analysis is to identify and scrutinise instances of disagreement and the resulting negotiation among the members of the organisation. Each instance of disagreement provides an opportunity to reveal an aspect of the prevailing beliefs and practices that sheds light on and furthers our understanding of the social making of the organisation. In other words, the instances of disagreement reflect the threads of the social fabric of the organisation. Naturally, we are not looking for ephemeral instances of disagreement but, rather, for the more prevalent disagreements that get the attention of a substantial number of people.

In the case of TopConsulting, the data included field notes, interview transcripts, and extracted Yammer posts collected over a two-year period. We used MAXQDA software to perform a detailed thematic analysis of the data that aimed to reveal specific prevalent disagreements regarding Yammer among the organisation members. The objective of this step of the analysis is to classify the data into a certain number of distinct common controversies.

Overall, the analysis revealed five prevalent controversies concerning the use of Yammer at TopConsulting: 1) Disagreeing on the appropriateness of social content on Yammer, 2) Disagreeing on the imposed requirement to use Yammer, 3) Disagreeing on the inclination to share everything with everyone on Yammer, 4) Disagreeing on the relevance of certain content on Yammer, and 5) Disagreeing on the appropriateness of negative feedback on Yammer. Given that qualitative data reduction involves an intersubjective process of systematic and disciplined analysis (Miles & Huberman, 1994), the number of revealed controversies is dependent on the properties of the data and the granularity level of the analysis.

Next, we illustrate the nature of each controversy by providing examples from TopConsulting. An overview of the five controversies is shown in Table 8.

## 3.1.1. Professional/social content thread

The Professional/Social Content thread stems from a disagreement about the appropriateness of social content on Yammer. At TopConsulting, some organisation members believe that Yammer is suited for sharing socially-oriented content such as jokes and pictures from social events, while others prefer to limit Yammer to professionally-oriented content such as oficial documents, memoranda, and other work-related posts. We elicited the Professional/ Social Content thread through a controversy analysis, as illustrated in Table 3.

The local disagreement was spurred by senior consultant ${ \mathrm { R S } } ^ { 2 }$ and sales employee YE, who expressed opposing views on what kind of content would be appropriate on the Yammer platform. The confusion about what kind of content to share was underscored by the tension between the perception of Yammer as a “social” platform, in which all kinds of content is welcome, and its design as a professional knowledge base for sharing information that can benefit coworkers (Yammer, 2019). During a lunch break, the diferent views evoked a public debate about the appropriateness of mixing professional and social content and whether the social content should be moved to a dedicated Yammer group. The discussion was settled with a shared agreement that despite the professional orientation of the platform, isolating the social content would “take away what’s good about the platform.”

Subsequently, the consensus was reflected in the performed practice that endorsed the legitimacy of both types of content on the Yammer platform and the approval of socially-oriented content despite the earlier objections. The analysis of this controversy revealed the Professional/Social Content thread, which highlights how the interactions on the platform are dependent on striking a balance between professionally-oriented and socially-oriented content.

## 3.1.2. Necessity/frill evaluation thread

The Necessity/Frill Evaluation thread stems from a disagreement about the imposed requirement to use Yammer. At TopConsulting, the management team positions Yammer as the primary communication channel and expects employees to engage with it on a daily basis. However, some employees disregard this expectation and rarely use Yammer because they consider it a non-essential add-on technology, among many other technologies in the company. We elicited the Necessity/Frill Evaluation thread through a controversy analysis, as illustrated in Table 4.

The local disagreement emerged from divergent perceptions about the need for Yammer. Whereas the HR manager expected that everyone would read the messages shared on Yammer, the consultants actual behaviour revealed that not everybody in the company adopted Yammer and checked in on a regular basis.

The diferent dispositions towards the use of Yammer evoked a public debate fuelled by the management’s decision to use Yammer as the primary communication channel even though some organisation members preferred receiving information via email. The debate was not settled with new formal rules, and it was left to the individual to decide whether and how he or she wanted to engage on the platform. However, the performed practice reflected a status quo in which Yammer remained the de facto channel for some important communication from the management team, and individuals had to engage with Yammer in order to stay informed. The analysis of this controversy uncovered the Necessity/Frill Evaluation thread, which highlights how interactions on the platform are dependent on the evaluation of the Yammer platform as a necessity or as a frill.

## 3.1.3. General/specific context thread

The General/Specific Context thread stems from a disagreement about the inclination to share everything with everyone on the Yammer platform. While some organisation members share much information indiscriminately with the entire organisation via the platform, others question whether the information shared should be addressed to everyone or be targeted to specific recipients. We elicited the General/Specific Context thread through a controversy analysis, as illustrated in Table 5.

The local disagreement arose from the posts and comments concerning buying and selling items on the Yammer platform, which made some less-interested individuals question the appropriateness of these specific conversations in the general forum. The amplifi cation and ongoing dissemination of posts on the Yammer platform ignited a public debate between those who found it appropriate to address communication of that nature to everyone in the organisation and those who thought it should be placed in a designated group. A settlement was reached by moving conversations of limited interest from the general forum to specific groups on Yammer. Subsequently, the performed practice prescribed using Yammer as a public space as well as a place to form special interest groups. The analysis of this controversy revealed the General/Specific Context thread, which highlights how the interactions on the platform are dependent on whether the exchange addresses the general audience or a particular interest group on the platform.

Specifically, building on the cooccurrence matrix, we form clusters by matching each thread manifestation to the two other thread manifestations that have the highest level of cooccurrence.

The procedure is explained and illustrated in Figures 3a-3e with the case of TopConsulting. ## 3.4. Step 4: revealing the dimensions of the social fabric

The fourth step of the framework is designed to make sense of the emerging triangular clusters (Figure 3e) in the context of the data and to reify them as distinct dimensions of the social fabric of the target organisation. Specifically, the operational objective of this step in the analysis is to ratify each of the triangular clusters with data and to solidify its face validity as a distinct dimension of the social fabric.

Each triangular cluster reveals three highly–related thread manifestations that together reflect a unique facet of the underlying social fabric. For example, the professional content (1a), necessity evaluation (2a), and general context (3a) thread manifestations create a clear triangular cluster. Naturally, the total number of triangular clusters is driven by the number of social fabric threads that predominate the data. As demonstrated in Step 1 of the analysis, we revealed five social fabric threads in the case of TopConsulting, and, therefore, we also expected five triangular clusters. Of course, a caveat should be noted here: given the inherent nature of the data analysis process, the ultimate number of revealed social fabric threads, and thereby the number of triangular clusters, is dependent on the properties of the data and the granularity level of the analysis.

## 3.4.1. The directive dimension

A cluster consisting of professional content, necessity evaluation, and general context creates relationships of a directive nature, which are characterised by the necessity of engaging with the Yammer platform to stay informed about professional content disseminated in a general context. In directive relationships, the focus is on the dissemination of professional content on the platform, and it is expected that this content will reach the entire group of participants. Furthermore, the message underscores the necessity of checking Yammer to stay updated with professional content. Directing messages to the entire group is enabled by Yammer acting as an intermediary receiver and transmitter of the information. The delivery of professional information in the general context, and the necessity of using Yammer to stay updated, create a directive relationship among the organisation members.

## 3.5. Step 5: rendering the pattern of the social making of the organisation

The fifth and final step of the framework is designed to render the situated social making pattern of the target organisation on the backdrop of the social fabric. Specifically, the operational objective of this step in the analysis is to provide a holistic representation of the social making of the organisation as it is reflected by the given data.

The application of the framework is explained and illustrated using the case of TopConsulting. We used the raw counts of the social fabric thread manifestations (see Table 10) to calculate the relative prevalence of each complementary thread manifestation as shown in Table 11. For example, the professional/social content in the case company is expressed as the ratio between the raw count of the professional content manifestations (401) and the raw count of the social content manifestations (233). We can see that 37% of the organisation members regard social content on Yammer as acceptable, and the other 63% regard only professional content as acceptable. This ratio indicates that about two thirds would not tolerate social content on Yammer.

This step requires further explanation. As discussed earlier, each social fabric thread represents a continuum of potential settlements of a controversy between two opposing positions on a given subject matter. For example, the professional/social content in the case company represents the controversy regarding the appropriateness of posting social content in addition to professional content. The two manifestations of each thread represent the two opposing positions, or anchor points, of that continuum. In this case, one position regards social content on Yammer as acceptable, and the other regards it as unacceptable. The ratio of each complementary thread manifestation indicates the prevalent disposition and practices at a given time. The relative prevalence of each complementary thread manifestation is summarised in Table 11, which shows the raw counts of thread manifestations and the respective ratio of each complementary thread manifestation at TopConsulting.

Subsequently, building on the data in Table 11, we render the situated social making pattern of TopConsulting against the backdrop of the social fabric dimensions, as illustrated in Figure 5, which provides a top-level visual representation in a spider map format.

In summary, grounded in the abundance of digital traces of organisational life and human conduct, the social fabric framework provides a structured approach for studying, characterising, and representing the social making of organisations. Using the case of TopConsulting, we have illustrated the five steps of the framework as follows: (1) eliciting the prevalent social fabric threads through controversy analysis, (2) estimating the extent of relationship between the social fabric threads, (3) clustering highly-related social fabric threads, (4) revealing and validating the dimensions of the social fabric, and finally (5) rendering the pattern of the social making of the target organisation. Next, we extend the framework one step further to describe and illustrate how the crosssectional rendering of the social making that was described in this section can be used in longitudinal analysis, and by analogy, also in comparative studies.

## 4. Exploring change with the social fabric framework

Overall, the application of the framework as a research method elicits the dominant dimensions of the social fabric of a case organisation and renders the situated social making pattern thereof against the backdrop of these dimensions. However, the structured nature of the analysis combined with the inherent temporal depth of the data also makes the framework well suited for examining how the social making of an organisation develops over time.

## 5. Discussion: the social fabric framework as a research method

In this paper, we have introduced the social fabric framework as an additional research method for studying the social making of organisations. The framework builds on the premise that the social making of organisations is reflected in distinct patterns that can be distilled from data traces that mirror the interactions and dispositions of people in organisations. Accordingly, we introduce and define the social fabric metaphor, which describes the social attributes that underlie the threads of relationships between people in a collective. The social fabric is shaped and reshaped by the prevailing beliefs and practices in a collective as well as its technological milieu. Subsequently, the social fabric framework provides a way to systematically identify and characterise the underlying patterns of social making within and across organisations.

The application of the social fabric framework results in a situated representation of the social making that is embedded in the social and historical background of the organisation of interest. Furthermore, the application of the framework can benefit from interaction with the organisation members and critical reflection on how the controversies relate to the predominant dimensions of the social fabric. While the framework comprises a computation phase to estimate the degree of association between all possible pairs of social fabric threads, and to cluster the highly-related threads to identify social fabric patterns, revealing the distinct dimensions of the clusters and rendering the pattern of the social making of the target organisation requires careful attention to the inherent tensions between the multiple emerging patterns. All in all, the social fabric framework provides an intersubjective representation that reflects the situated array of dispositions on key dimensions of the social making of a particular organisation.

## 5.1. Contributions and practical implications

The social fabric framework ofers five contributions. First, the social fabric framework provides a structured approach to eliciting, interpreting, and producing distinct representations of the social making of organisations. The explicit representation of social making ofers insights into the interactions between individuals as they take place on social media and beyond (Gasson & Waters, 2013; Gibbs et al., 2014). Such representation mitigates some of the current challenges to depicting and explaining the “social” in the context of the organisation (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2020) by providing a vocabulary for articulating it in a systematic fashion.

Second, the social fabric framework ofers a bottom-up approach that is inherently sensitive to the emergent, situated, and dynamic characteristics of the social making of organisations. Consequently, the social fabric framework is highly receptive to temporal change and adapts accordingly. This contrasts with other top-down frameworks that ofer a one-size-fits-all approach to delineate the social fabric of organisations, such as the Competing Values Framework (Cameron & Quinn, 2011) and Hofstede’s Cultural Dimensions Theory (Hofstede, 1998).

Third, the social fabric framework can shed light on some of the blind spots in social data analysis. The analytical strategy of the social fabric framework caters to further understanding of the intangible, situated, and dynamic conversations and debates that take place among organisation members about everyday practices (Berente et al., 2010; Kane, 2015; Riemer & Hafermalz, 2019). While we would argue that, in principle, the social fabric framework can be used with a wide range of social trace data, it is highly conducive to social media data and can complement other research methods that are used in this milieu. For example, with social network analysis, it is possible to outline the complex links between the diferent nodes in an organisation and determine how some nodes bridge and bond together to form a network structure (Riemer et al., 2015). However, social network analysis provides limited insights into the social nature of the links between the actors in the network. Consequently, the application of the social fabric framework can go beyond an overall representation of the interactions between actors and instead move towards eliciting, analysing, and interpreting the nature of these relationships and how they form the basis of the organisation.

Fourth, the social fabric metaphor provides a useful apparatus (Østerlund et al., 2020) for reflective exploration and inquiry into the social making of organisations. An apparatus in this context refers to a literal device that “has in some way the capacity to capture, orient, determine, intercept, model, control, or secure the gestures, behaviors, opinions, or discourses of living beings” (Agamben, 2009, p. 14). The social fabric metaphor highlights the performative traits of the social fabric threads that do not merely mirror the image of the organisation but also contribute to constituting the reality that they represent (Callon, 1998). Accordingly, the application of the social fabric framework helps to reify the social attributes that shape and reshape the threads of relationships between people in a collective.

Fifth, the structured nature of the social fabric framework combined with the inherent temporal depth of the data makes the framework conducive to longitudinal and comparative studies. While in a longitudinal study, we aim to look for changes in the social fabric of the same organisation over time, in a comparative study we aim to look for diferences between the social fabrics of diferent organisations or parts thereof at the same time.

Regarding its practical implications, the social fabric framework can be used as a valuable diagnostic tool, especially in the context of organisational development and change. For example, the framework can be used to track the efect of interventions or general organisational development eforts. The framework can also be used to sense emergent changes in the social fabric of an organisation, which in turn can be nurtured or discontinued as needed. Subsequently, the social fabric framework can be used as a springboard for a collective dialogue about the prevailing work practices and the organisational culture. By providing a vocabulary for articulating the social making of the organisation, the framework can help organisation members to reify their dispositions, to make sense of the social dynamics, and to enable a constructive discussion at the grassroots level about any controversy or aspiration.

Furthermore, in the milieu of the IT organisation, the framework can be used to monitor the difusion and adoption of new systems and services. Whereas decisionmakers in the IT organisation adopt new technology due to its technical capabilities and expected contribution to the bottom line, they are often less aware of the efect that the adopted technology has on the dynamics of work and the prevailing conventions in the organisation. In the same vein, system developers should find the social fabric framework to be a valuable tool in their arsenal. The framework can extend interaction design practices from their current individual-level focus to organisationallevel reach. Moreover, the application of the social fabric framework goes beyond providing a unidimensional description of opportunities and constraints of the technology, towards revealing the social environments and specific pattern of relationship among individuals, as well as between individuals and technology.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research

The social fabric framework was developed as a research method in the course of a two-year longitudinal study of the social making of three case organisations as it is reflected on their Yammer platforms, of which TopConsulting was one of them. Any further testing, especially applications that stretch the boundary conditions of the initial study, would contribute to strengthening the validity and generalisability of the framework. Further applications of the framework in additional organisations of diferent types, over longer periods, and using additional sources of data would be beneficial.

The social fabric framework is grounded in data that originates in social media. Therefore, the framework is adequate only if the available data mirrors reliably a valid representation of the discourse in the organisation of interest. Threats to validity may stem from prevalent digital illiteracy and inequitable access to social media in the organisation. Prevalent ofline discourse and the use of underground social media to discuss sensitive issues may also engender threats to validity. Using data from mixed sources, including observations and interviews, to supplement the social media data is a good way to mitigate and control for such representation biases.

Given the generative nature of the framework, further applications of the framework to study various facets of organisational life are likely to uncover additional types of distinct dimensions of the social fabric depending on the situated factors of the underlying organisations. Furthermore, studies in other contexts are likely to detect the emergence and development of some new dimensions as well as the dissolution of others.

Further research could explore ways to mechanise the social fabric framework, building on computational digital method that were developed in earlier STS studies (e.g., Marres & Moats, 2015) as well as advances in sentiment analysis, semantic analysis, and machine learning as proposed by Berente et al. (2018). The current operationalisation of the framework is labour-intensive and requires solid research aptitude, which makes it acceptable in an academic context but less palatable in an everyday organisational context. Given the proliferation of social data that organisations generate, developing algorithms that can automate the application of the social fabric framework to assess social making in work units and organisations would be a natural step forward.

## 6. Concluding remarks

In this paper, we presented the social fabric framework as an additional research method for studying the social making of organisations. Building on the premise that the social making of organisations can be elicited from data traces that mirror the interactions and dispositions of people in organisations, the framework underscores the inherent dialectic relationship between the technological and social facets of work systems. Furthermore, the framework highlights that accounting for the situated social idiosyncrasies of the people who use digital technologies should be the point of departure for embedding these systems in organisations.

Further applications of the social fabric framework may prove useful in the study of other IS-related phenomena, such as system design and development, user involvement and systems implementation, business-IT alignment, IT-enabled organisational change, mergers and acquisitions, and other research areas that deal with the social aspects of organisations. We hope that the application of the social fabric framework as a research method will provide a springboard to rejuvenate our ontological view on the nature of the social making of technology-mediated organisational work.
