# Privacy concerns and digital government: exploring citizen willingness to adopt the COVIDSafe app

Jiesen Lin, Lemuria Carter and Dapeng Liu

Information Systems and Technology Management, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia

## ABSTRACT

Contact tracing is a key public health intervention during the coronavirus pandemic. While government contact tracing apps (e.g., COVIDSafe) may enforce personal information protection, privacy concerns remain among citizens. To date, few studies have investigated the adoption of contact tracing technology and corresponding citizen information privacy concerns. To address this gap, we propose a research model to explore the impact of individual privacy concerns, trust, and risk perceptions on citizen’s willingness to download a federal contact tracing app. To test the model, we administer a survey to Australian citizens to assess their perceptions of the government’s “COVIDSafe” app. The results of this study indicate that relative advantage, compatibility and trusting beliefs increase adoption intentions. The study provides recommendations for governments tackling COVID-19 and guidance for contact tracing strategies in preparation for future pandemics.

## 1. Introduction

The public perception of smartphones is evolving from a cutting-edge gadget to a necessity in human life (Lee et al., 2014). Most developed countries surpass 100% mobile penetration (Data.gov.sg., 2020). The boom in data is giving rise to privacy debates concerning the boundaries of acceptable information collection. Information privacy concerns afect individual intention to use online services, with higher internet privacy concerns leading to lower intention to use the service and reducing willingness to share personal information (Bélanger et al., 2002; Dinev & Hart, 2006).

In 2020, governments used technology to communicate coronavirus information and undertake contact tracing. In the case of COVID-19, the high penetration of smartphones in some countries allows smartphones to play a pivotal role in helping citizens cope with coronavirus lockdowns. Digital contact tracing is efective for epidemic prevention and reducing infections (Farrahi et al., 2014). Danquah et al. (2019) find that a contact tracing app is quicker and more accurate than a paper-based system. To date, few studies explore the impact of citizens’ privacy concerns on government mobile tracing app utilisation (Cho et al., 2020; O’Callaghan et al., 2020; Trang et al., 2020). Cho et al. (2020) call for research on the privacy trade-ofs citizens are willing to endure for the sake of public health. Similarly, Oaic (2020) highlight citizens’ concerns about security weaknesses of government contact tracing apps and the potential for misuse by malicious actors. Calvo et al. (2020) state contact tracing measures are a form of enduring health surveillance which societies accept as a temporary, exceptional means for exceptional time. Surveillance reduces feelings of autonomy, negatively afecting wellbeing and thus imploring an ethical measure of health surveillance which is chosen freely by constituents. Culnan (1993) calls for more research of information privacy within varying contexts, an important directive to which this study responds. In this study, we address the gap in the literature by integrating the Difusion of Innovation theory (Rogers, 1983) and Internet Users’ Information Privacy Concerns model (Malhotra et al., 2004) to explore citizen adoption of a digital government initiative: the Australian government’s COVIDSafe contact tracing app. In particular, we explore the following research question “how do privacy concerns, trusting beliefs and difusion of innovation factors afect citizens’ intention to adopt a contact tracing application?”

## 2. Background literature

## 2.1. Information privacy

Information privacy has been explored extensively in the literature (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Bélanger & James, 2020; Smith et al., 2011). Information privacy refers to “the ability of individuals to control the terms under which their personal information is acquired and used” (Culnan & Bies, 2003). Information privacy concerns refer to an individual’s subjective views of fairness within the context of information privacy (Campbell, 1997). Individual privacy concerns vary by external conditions such as their industry sector, culture, and regulatory jurisdictions, as well as their own internal characteristics and experiences (Malhotra et al., 2004), thus generating an entire spectrum of user willingness to share information. The two eminent research models for information privacy concerns are Concern for Information Privacy (CFIP) and Internet User’s Information Privacy Concerns (IUIPC) (Xu et al., 2011). CFIP intends to reliably capture individuals’ concerns about organisational information privacy practices within the context of ofline direct marketing (Smith et al., 1996). CFIP includes four factors: Collection, Unauthorised Secondary Use, Improper Access and Error. Concern for collection stems from the collection and storage of voluminous amounts of personal data in databases (Smith et al., 1996). Internal and external unauthorised secondary use refer to the use of an individual’s personal information by organisational actors for secondary internal purposes or disclosure to external parties for unauthorised purposes (Smith et al., 1996). Improper access refers to the concern that “data about individuals are readily available to people not authorised to view or work with this data” (Smith et al., 1996). Error refers to the concern that “protections against deliberate and accidental errors in personal data are inadequate” (Smith et al., 1996).

The development from traditional media to online marketing has seen corresponding privacy concerns evolve and emerge between traditional media consumers and those who are more active on the internet. Stewart and Segars (2002) validate the dimensions of Smith et al. (1996) and expose a need to “reinvestigate” underlying theoretical and operational assumptions of the instrument in light of emerging technology, practice and research. In response to this new technology environment, Malhotra et al. (2004) draw on social contract theory and develop a multidimensional model of online users’ information privacy concerns (IUIPC). IUIPC empirically demonstrates itself as a more useful tool to analyse internet users’ reactions to online privacy threats when compared to CFIP (Malhotra et al., 2004; Xu et al., 2011). As technology evolves, organisations continue to face the challenge of securing user data. IUIPC consists of three dimensions – namely, collection, control, and awareness (Malhotra et al., 2004). Collection depicts how an individual’s level of concern of personal data possessed by others is relative to the value of benefits reciprocated (Malhotra et al., 2004). Control represents how an individual’s concern for information privacy centres on whether they have control over personal information by the power to approve, modify or opt out of the service (Malhotra et al., 2004). Awareness is a passive dimension of information privacy, referring to the extent to which a user is concerned about their awareness of organisational information privacy practices (Culnan, 1995; Foxman & Kilcoyne, 1993; Malhotra et al., 2004). IUIPC influences individual beliefs, such as trusting beliefs and risk beliefs (Malhotra et al., 2004; Ozturk et al., 2017).

The very act of data collection originates information privacy concerns. Collection features the extent to which one is “concerned about the amount of individual-specific data possessed by others relative to the value of benefits received” (Malhotra et al., 2004). This construct is congruent with the fundamental “costbenefit” assessment an individual undertakes in the exchange of personal information, also known as the “privacy calculus” (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Laufer & Wolfe, 1977). Thus, exchange of personal information between people occurs when doing so produces benefits to the relationship which usurps the inherent risk of privacy breach. The pivotal role of technology in our society today reinforces the risk in sharing personal information, however the imperative is for parties to ensure mutual benefit whilst taking cautious measures to secure information. In the private sector, studies elucidate the balancing struggle between profit and justice in the tension between the economic interests of businesses and individuals’ privacy concerns (Culnan & Bies, 2003).

The importance of users being in control of their information is indisputable. Malhotra et al. (2004) suggest that marketers provide visibility over collected information, its accuracy and use to help internet users feel in control over their personal information. Similarly, Phelps et al. (2000) espouse that individuals will be less concerned about data collection when they explicitly allow firms access, or have the ability to optout, even when in practice only a small fraction decide to. Boyd (2008) explains that privacy is a “sense of control over information”, underscoring the ambiguity between the factual state of information privacy control and perceived information control (Hoadley et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2012). For example, various manipulations of the user experience will result in diferences in perceived information control, even if the actual information privacy of the user remains unchanged. In a study responding to Facebook’s Newsfeed controversy, Hoadley et al. (2010) conceptualise control as a psychological perception as new features which improve the ease of access to user’s personal data induce lower levels of perceived control as a result of easier information access.

Awareness is composed of interactional justice such as organisational transparency and informational justice, which relates to the disclosure of information which positively influences perceptions of fairness. Ironically, Facebook users who joined in spite of their recognition of the inherent privacy concerns, proceeded to reveal great amounts of personal information after convincing themselves of their personal ability to control the information they share, as well as external access to it (Acquisti & Gross, 2006). This exposes the paradoxical elements of privacy awareness where users’ knowledge and perception of their information privacy contradict the reality of the situation, alluding to the privacy paradox (Awad & Krishnan, 2006).

In the context of COVID-19 information systems, contact tracing has proven beneficial in fighting epidemics, while raising information privacy concerns (Rowe et al., 2020). The technologies fighting COVID-19 (e.g., Alipay Health Code App used in China, COVIDSafe in Australia, Smittestop in Denmark, and TraceTogether in Singapore) collect real-time data on the location of people and may act as “control machines that support instant communication and enable continuous forms of control” (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Aroles, 2020, p. 750).

## 2.2. Technology adoption

Difusion of Innovation (DOI) theory is a social science theory that explains how an idea or product gains traction and “difuses” through a specific population to achieve adoption, by inheriting a new way of doing something (Rogers, 1995). DOI underscores that the adoption of a new idea or product does not occur instantaneously in a social system, rather it reflects how some people, industries or organisations are more apt to adopt the innovation than others. Rogers (1983) posits the five innovation attributes of trialability, observability, relative advantage, complexity and compatibility. Moore and Benbasat (1991) extend these attributes to include image, visibility, result demonstrability, and voluntariness of use. The expanded Perceived Characteristics of Innovating model accounts for further factors influencing the acceptance and usage of a technology innovation.

The literature suggests relative advantage, compatibility and complexity are the most salient predictors of adoption (Lin & Lee, 2006; Low et al., 2011; Thong, 1999). Hence, we include those three constructs in our proposed model. Relative advantage refers to the extent to which an innovation is considered as being superior to the item it replaced (Lee et al., 2011). Compatibility refers to the extent to which an innovation is consistent with existing processes, practices and value systems (Zhu et al., 2006). Complexity refers to “the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively dificult to understand and use” (Rogers, 2003). Venkatesh (2000) posits complexity as synonymous with the TAM construct of perceived ease of use. In this study, we refer to complexity as perceived ease of use (Lee, 2013; Sun & Zhang, 2006; Venkatesh, 2000). Perceived ease of use is defined as the degree to which an innovation is perceived to be easy to use (Moore & Benbasat, 1996).

## 2.3. The COVIDSafe app

The Australian Government released the COVIDSafe app for Android and iOS on April 26 2020, 92 days since the first case of COVID-19 in Australia. The COVIDSafe app, also referred to as COVIDSafe, is developed by the Australian Department of Health, the Department of Home Afairs and the Digital Transformation Agency. It is based on the same protocol used in the Singaporean app, where an exchange of Bluetooth tokens occurs with nearby devices that also have the app installed (Australian Government, 2020). COVIDSafe takes a note of a contact when it occurs, through a digital handshake. The tokens are random time-varying strings, linked with the individual (Cho et al., 2020), whilst the user is not aware of information about their tokens. “The app securely logs the other user’s encrypted reference code and the date, time, Bluetooth® signal strength and proximity of the contact on the user’s phone and notes the phone model. This information is then securely encrypted and stored on the phone”(health.gov.au., 2020).

COVIDSafe is voluntary to adopt and the Biosecurity Determination Legislation mandate that state or territory government health authority employees must gain the consent of the device owner to access COVIDSafe information. The COVIDSafe app “will only record the following contact data:the encrypted user ID, date and time of contact, Bluetooth signal strength, and make and model of the phone of other COVIDSafe users with which you come into contact”(health.gov.au., 2020). COVIDSafe users have the option to update their COVID-positive status in the app to inform the Australian government. Health oficials can only access app information if someone tests positive and agrees to the information in their phone being uploaded” (health.gov.au., 2020). If the user consents to information sharing, her data is uploaded to the centralised National COVIDSafe Data Store. The shared information will assist health authorities with contact tracing. It can only be accessed by health authorities with prior system training. A key aspect of the Australian government’s messaging is that whilst the app was voluntary to download, it is instrumental to “slow the spread” of COVID-19. The government widely encouraged downloading COVIDSafe, spending \$7 m AUD in the first two months of app release in marketing costs. The Australian government wants 40% of the population to download the app to ensure ideal efectiveness (Grifth, 2021), conveying there is limited risk of personally identifiable information (PII) exposure.

This study examines inherent perceived privacy concerns associated with COVIDSafe. Its functionality on iPhones remains questionable due to the security limitations of the operating system (Welch & Besser, 2020). As of June 9<sup>th</sup>, 2020, there have been more than 6 million downloads of the app with data accessed less than 10 times in NSW and 18 times in Victoria. South Australia, Western Australia, Queensland, the ACT and the Northern Territory have not used app data (McIlroy, 2020). Prominent privacy fears for the app include the potential for bugs and loopholes in the code as a result of the quick rollout, the security vulnerability of storing data on Amazon Web Services’ servers and the attraction for hackers as a result of the anticipated widespread adoption by the population (Welch & Besser, 2020). At the time of publication, Australia’s contact tracing app records the third highest rate of adoption in the world at 21.6% of the population (Rivero, 2020). There is no precursor to the COVIDSafe app, emphasising the unknown of adoption attitudes and thus necessitating this study.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

In this study, we utilise an integrated IUIPC and DOI model to test the impact of privacy concerns, trusting beliefs and risk beliefs on users’ behavioural intention to download the COVIDSafe app. According to Moore and Benbasat (1996), relative advantage is an important predictor of adoption. In a period of high growth of the internet and emergence of online marketplaces, De Ruyter et al. (2001) find that value adding interactive e-services with high relative advantage increases customer quality perceptions and thus increases usage intentions. Lin (2011) investigates mobile banking adoption and discover that relative advantage has significant efects on adoption attitudes. The relative advantage of an online e-government tax filing system over manual processes significantly increases adoption intentions (Liang & Lu, 2013). More recently, Senarathna et al. (2018) find that the relative advantage of cloud computing significantly influences adoption by Australian small and medium enterprises.

H1: Relative advantage will have a positive efect on intention to use the COVIDSafe App.

Carter and Bélanger (2004) find citizen intentions to use state e-government services increase if the service has a perception of being easy to use through intuitive navigation and organisation of information based on citizen needs. Hung et al. (2013) find that ease of use significantly increases the intention to adopt mobile government services. Investigating e-commerce adoption, Gefen et al. (2000) find perceived ease of use can significantly increase online visitors’ intention to adopt a book-selling website for inquiry about and purchase of books. Abu-Shanab (2014) find perceived ease of use of e-government websites to positively influence intention to use e-government services.

H2: Perceived ease of use will have a positive efect on intention to use the COVIDSafe App.

Compatibility significantly influences citizen intention to use state e-government services (Carter & Bélanger, 2004). Wu and Wang (2005) discover that the most important determinant for behavioural intention to use mobile commerce is the compatibility of user requirements, past experience, lifestyle and beliefs. Compatibility positively and directly influences behavioural intention to use. In the organisational IT context, Sun et al. (2009) confirm that perceived work compatibility between organisational tasks and a given IT positively relates to their intention to use the technology. Al-Jabri and Sohail (2012) find compatibility to be the most significant determinant of mobile banking adoption in Saudi Arabia. Jaklič et al. (2018) examine the interrelated role of compatibility in the adoption of business intelligence and analytics (BI&A) technology and find that compatibility has a positive impact on usage intentions of BI&A in medium and large Slovenian businesses.

## H3: Compatibility will have a positive efect on intention to use the COVIDSafe App.

Privacy violations, such as the breach of sensitive health information, are undesirable (Malhotra et al., 2004). In a study of personal dispositions in disclosing health information online, Bansal and Gefen (2010) find that privacy concerns decrease users’ intention to disclose health information. Dinev and Hart (2006) build a privacy calculus model to investigate e-commerce adoption and find that higher levels of perceived internet privacy risks reduce willingness to share personal information online. Increased privacy risks negatively afect trusting beliefs in e-government services (Mutimukwe et al., 2020). Few studies have examined the impact of internet users’ information privacy concerns on trusting beliefs in a government contact tracing app.

H4. Internet users’ information privacy concerns will have a negative efect on trusting beliefs.

Risk beliefs refer to an expectation that a high potential for loss is associated with the release of personal information to the firm (Malhotra et al., 2004). Growth in IT increases privacy risk beliefs associated with personal information disclosure (Dinev & Hart, 2006). Dinev et al. (2008) discover that government intrusion concerns increase privacy concerns, which increase risk beliefs and thus decrease willingness to provide personal information. In a study of Facebook users, Kusyanti et al. (2017) investigate the impact of personal dispositions on information privacy concern and trust in online health information disclosure and find a positive efect of IUIPC on risk beliefs.

H5. Internet users’ information privacy concerns will have a positive efect on risk beliefs.

Xu et al. (2005) study consumer attitudes towards personalised location-based service providers in light of the increase in mobile positioning technologies. They find that consumers’ trusting beliefs, which are enhanced when a service provider joins a third party privacy seal and introduces device-based privacy features, help mitigate privacy risk beliefs and increases intentions to disclose personal information through location-based services. Nicolaou and McKnight (2006) examine interorganisational data exchanges and find support for trusting beliefs to negatively influence risk beliefs. Bélanger and Carter (2008) test the role of trust in e-government adoption and find trust of the US government to reduce perceived risk of using e-government services. Yang et al. (2015) find that trust in mobile shopping services negatively afects perceived risk of these services. Meanwhile, Park et al. (2019) conduct a study of mobile payment technology adoption and identify a negative relationship between risk and trust.

## H6. Trusting beliefs will have a negative efect on risk beliefs.

Trust-risk literature establishes the expectations that trusting beliefs significantly influence behavioural intention (Liu & Weistrofer, 2020; Malhotra et al., 2004). For example, in a study of strategic information flows between buyers and suppliers within logistics supply chains, Klein and Rai (2009) establish a significant direct efect for buyer and supplier trusting beliefs on corresponding strategic information flows. McKnight et al. (2002) investigate initial consumer trust on intentions to transact with an e-commerce website and discover that trusting beliefs through the form of trust in the online vendor predict intention to share personal information. Bélanger and Carter (2008) find trust of government positively influences intention to use e-government services. Trust of e-Government services through cybersecurity significantly increases intention to use e-Government systems (Sang et al., 2009). Ho et al. (2017) also find trust intentions among corporate technology users to have a positive efect on intention to adopt cloud computing services.

H7. Trusting beliefs will have a positive efect on intention to use the COVIDSafe App.

Bansal and Gefen (2010) identify that individuals with poor health status perceive their information with more sensitivity and thus have greater privacy concerns over health information disclosure and risk beliefs. Dinev and Hart (2006) conclude that higher levels of privacy risk beliefs suggest greater resistance to personal information disclosure. Higher levels of perceived risk decrease intention to use a U.S. government e-file system (Schaupp et al., 2010). Groß (2016) finds perceived risk of mobile shopping to negatively influence consumers’ continued usage intention. Recently, a study of Indian consumers intention to adopt internet banking identify perceived risk to negatively influence intention to use (Chauhan et al., 2019). As risk beliefs have shown to have a negative efect on intention to reveal personal information in fields such as healthcare and e-commerce, we believe it will also be the case in the context of the COVIDSafe app.

## H8. Risk beliefs will have a negative efect on intention to use the COVIDSafe App.

Malhotra et al. (2004)’s IUIPC model is grounded in the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Ajzen, 1991; Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). The TRA posits beliefs influence intentions, and intentions influence one’s actions. While intention has been recognised as a proxy for behaviour, researchers have called for more studies that examine actual behaviour (Bélanger & Crossler, 2019; Bélanger & Xu, 2015; Smith et al., 2011). Our study responds to this call by examining both intention and behaviour. We posit:

H9. Intention to use will have a positive efect on downloading the COVIDSafe App.

## 4.2. Data collection

To test the proposed research model, we used Qualtrics to administer an online survey to Australian residents. COVIDSafe was initially released on April 26 2020. We collected data between 8th October and and October 13 2020. Prior to launching the survey, we conducted a pre-test to validate the instrument. Based on the pretest recommendations, we corrected a number of typos. Qualtrics respondents received an incentive based on the length of the survey, their specific panellist profile and target acquisition dificulty. The specific type of rewards vary and may include cash, airline miles, gift cards, redeemable points, sweepstakes entrance and vouchers. In this study, Qualtrics provided survey participants non monetary compensation (less than \$7.50 AUD in total value) in the form of gift cards. Participants were required to meet the following screening criteria: be at least 18 years old, be English speaking, reside in Australia, and own and use a smart phone. Qualtrics managed the data cleaning of responses and ensured response validation, by removing incomplete, non-genuine and straight-line response surveys. Qualtrics also conducted bot checks, double opt-in confirmations, GeoIP and postcode risk scoring and manual reviews of respondents (Qualtrics, 2019). The survey items were presented in order by construct, and all participants received the identical sequence.

Based on the rule of ten (Westland, 2010), Structural Equation Modelling should have at least “ten times as many subjects as variables” (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1967). A total of 209 valid responses were recorded. Among the respondents, 39.7% were male and 60.3% were female. 56.94% of respondents had downloaded the COVIDSafe app. Table 1 summarises the respondents’ demographics.

## 5. Data analysis

The minimums, maximums, means and standard deviations for each construct are displayed in Table 2.We used the confirmatory factor analysis approach (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988) to assess the measurement quality of constructs in the measurement model. We used structural equation modelling (SEM) to test the model. Analysis of the measurement and structural models was performed with Smart PLS (Version 3.0).

Variable inflation factors (VIF) help detect multicollinearity, which occurs when two or more dependent variables are strongly correlated with each other in a regression model. All VIF values were between 1.664 and 6.531 and thus were lower than the acceptable threshold of 10, avoiding multicollinearity issues (Hair, 1995; Hair et al., 2010; Shih et al., 2017). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is a statistical approach that scrutinises interrelationships between a host of variables and explains common underlying dimensions (Gefen et al., 2000). Due to low factor loadings in the confirmatory factor analysis (in Table 3), RISK5, PEOU1 and CONT3 were removed.

Table 4 displays composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). Composite reliability, a measure of internal consistency of scale items, range from 0.802 to 0.953, well exceeding the baseline index of 0.700, implying there is a robust shared variance between latent constructs and its measure than error variance (Bagozzi et al., 1998; Nunnally, 1978). Fornell and Larcker (1981) suggest the use of Average Variance Extracted (AVE) to assess convergent validity. AVE indicates the amount of variance explained by the given latent construct and its measures. An AVE exceeding 0.500 is suggested to evidence convergent validity given the corresponding latent variable explains more than half of the variance in the belonging indicators and consequently, all other latent variables explain less than half (Bagozzi & Yi, 2012; Fornell & Larcker, 1981). All factor loadings exceed the minimum threshold of 0.7, concluding convergent validity. Our model demonstrates discriminant validity, as the square roots of the AVE, represented in bold, is greater than the correlation coeficients beneath (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). This indicates that the latent construct best explains the variance of its own indicators, over the variance with other latent constructs, verifying adequate discriminant validity (Ab Hamid et al., 2017).

## 7. Discussion

Integrating DOI and IUIPC theories, we present a model that can be used in future research to predict the adoption intention of technologies perceived to have a personal privacy cost. The unique context of a global pandemic, and its impact on privacy and adoption attitudes are certainly felt, particularly as risk beliefs about the COVIDSafe app did not reduce usage intentions. The model explains 75.4% of the variance in citizen intention to use the Australian government’s COVIDSafe application and 43.9% of the variance in actual use..

## 7.1. Implications for Research

In addition to contributing to research on mobile contact tracing, privacy and adoption, this study also reinforces the prevalence of the privacy paradox, challenging the assertation that higher information privacy concerns reduce intention to use an online service (Barnes, 2006; Bélanger et al., 2002; Dinev & Hart, 2006). The multi-faceted considerations of mobile contact tracing, transcend not only adoption and privacy factors, but also the societal, cultural and sociopolitical factors declared by Riemer et al. (2020), reinforcing the need for further research into this topic area.

It is important for future research to expand on this study and explore privacy attitudes for contact tracing in diferent countries to document global perspectives. Studies on countries that successfully managed the virus without a lockdown including Taiwan, and others that took diferent approaches with varying success, such as the United States, Indonesia and Sweden may yield interesting results, as will research on autocratic political systems such as Russia and China. Analysis of cultural diferences and their impact on governments’ ability to experiment with new technologies will develop the literature in this research area. Researchers should explore the diferences in citizen attitudes towards government and private-sector developed contact tracing, including decentralised storage setups by individual businesses. Comparison of international contact tracing app specifications and their respective adoption, privacy measures and efectiveness can incite intergovernmental, public-private, and other arrangements of contact tracing which balance privacy and practicality. Longitudinal studies of actual usage could incorporate other DOI constructs. Studies of contact tracing adoption intentions by coronavirus survivors or people with poor health status, known to heighten information sensitivity and thus privacy concerns (Bansal & Gefen, 2010) are valuable to determine the extent to which health conditions afect adoption.

## 7.2. Implications for Practice

Support for relative advantage increasing intention to use the COVIDSafe app reveals Australians are willing to embrace mobile contact tracing initiatives due to its relative advantage over manual paper-based contact tracing. It may also indicate a belief that COVIDSafe is superior to these state specific QR code sign-in mechanisms which have less privacy protection. Targeted education about the app’s technical capabilities and purpose will challenge misconceptions and assumptions and is likely to welcome adoption from a wider gamut of citizens. A national communication and action plan targeting all 5 of Rogers’ DOI adopter categories, particularly the late adopters, laggards, and citizens most wary of the government will provide tailored measures to address adoption scepticism.

To ensure compatibility, nations should strive to include citizens who do not have access to mobile phones and the internet -often those who are highly prone to health issues such as the elderly. In Australia, 2.5 million people, or ten percent of the population, remain ofline and people aged over 65 years remain the least digitally included, with afordability being the barrier to internet access (Thomas et al., 2020). Future government initiatives should expedite the delivery of afordable internet technology to the remaining population to increase citizen connectivity with e-government and potentially increase uptake of e-government and e-health initiatives.

Trusting beliefs have a negative efect on risk beliefs and increase the intention to use the COVIDSafe app. To alleviate information privacy concerns, new legislation should provide additional protections to app data. The Ofice of the Australian Information Commissioner now oversees the data. COVIDSafe datastore administrators must delete user data if requested, and no data can be collected from users who have deleted COVIDSafe (Government, 2020). To strengthen trusting beliefs, legislation should regularly update according to emerging privacy concerns. The mass promotion by government agencies, and the government’s eforts to gain trust from citizens by releasing the source code represents greater transparency and thus increased trusting beliefs. These publicised assurances positively influence trust beliefs and help decrease risk beliefs. Furthermore, reducing conflicting positions between state and federal governments by streamlining public messaging and contact tracing protocols will likely increase trusting beliefs. As the publisher and owner of the app, higher trust beliefs in the Australian government influence greater adoption of mobile contact tracing. Thus, the Australian government should strive to boost its overall trust by the Australian people, beyond the scope of the COVIDSafe app. Further strengthening the security of Australian government data across all e-government platforms from malicious actors could increase trusting beliefs over the long run. Its public communications should continue to be honest, transparent and inclusive of all constituents to boost trusting beliefs.

## 7.3. Limitations

There are a few limitations worth noting. The survey was conducted online and only one respondent had less than three years’ internet experience. Thus, Australian residents with limited access to, and experience of internet technology were unlikely to have participated. Hence, the findings may not represent the views of the population. Also, as the survey collected self-reported data, we cannot verify whether participants fully understood survey items or responded in an unbiased manner.

## 8. Conclusion

As one of the earlier works in the Australian context, this study provides an integrated model of Internet Users’ Information Privacy Concerns and Difusion of Innovation. It is a timely study of mobile contact tracing adoption during a challenging global health pandemic. The work responds to calls for research on information privacy concerns and citizen willingness to adopt contact tracing in light of privacy concerns and risks. The study of citizen use of the COVIDSafe app, which was released prior to the publication of most pandemicrelated academic journals such as those suggesting favourable design specifications or technology governance, is a tribute to the Australian government’s eforts to encourage adoption. Along with other related works, this study will support governments in refining their coronavirus strategies.
