# Memoirs from Bukhalalire: a poetic inquiry into the lived experience of M-PESA mobile money usage in rural Kenya

Neil McBride <sup>a</sup> and Samuel Liyala<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Centre for Computing and Social Responsibility, De Montfort University, Leicester, United Kingdom; <sup>b</sup>School of Informatics and Innovative Systems, Jaramogi Oginga Odinga University of Science and Technology, Bondo, Kenya

## ABSTRACT

M-PESA is the world’s leading mobile money system which supports the person-to-person transfer of money. While the institutional meaning of M-PESA concerning financial inclusion and market access is well documented, the meaning attached to M-PESA by users, particularly in rural communities, has received less attention. This study examines the meaning of M-PESA to the rural poor in villages in Busia County, Western Kenya using the approach of poetic inquiry in which the expressions of local people are rendered as poetry in order to draw out afective and lived experience. A reading of the poems of seven participants reveals the perception of M-PESA as a means of empowerment for women, alignment with their social identity and access to education. This contrasts sharply with the meaning of M-PESA as seen by commercial and non-commercial institutions which focuses on connecting the unbanked to formal banking systems and the exploitation of M-PESA as a primary revenue stream. The paper concludes by highlighting the need for regulation which is delicately balanced between independence from institutional control and the exposure of the rural poor to exploitation.

## 1. Introduction

Information systems have both purpose and meaning. The purpose concerns what the information system is intended to do. This may be found in the requirements and the business case. Meaning is derived from lived experience, from the way the information system is absorbed into the life of the user, from how it becomes not an external tool but an extension of behaviour, corralled into the life of the user. As such the meaning which emerges for the user is an interpretation which may drift away from the original institutional intention. Meaning may influence purpose but is not the same as the purpose and may be hidden or disguised. For example, the implementation of a warehousing system in a processing company was purposed as improving eficiency and integrating warehousing with materials requirements planning. However, warehouse personnel saw the system as an efort by management to control their activities and remove informal practices (Walsham, 1993, p. 85). A customer relationship management system in a large organisation whose purpose was supporting employee interactions with customers found its meaning in the dialogue between sales staf and managers concerning bonuses. Its meaning was thus in sales volume and concomitant rewards rather than customer service (Cunha et al., 2015). The UK health service’s National Programme for IT, while purposed to develop electronic patient records, was attributed with diferent meanings by diferent stakeholders including the support of an internal market and the compromising of patient privacy (Justinia, 2017).

A separation of the purpose and meaning perceived by the users of an information system from those defined and pursued by the developers and investors leaves the door open for the practice of exploitation, oppression and deprivation. As such the user becomes objectified, a means of satisfying the goals and ambitions of the information system provider. This is classically illustrated in the systems and services that Facebook and Google provide where the purpose and meaning attached to the technology by the users differs significantly from those of the technology companies.

This paper explores a similar divergence between purpose and meaning in a mobile money information system. In the case of M-PESA a similar disjunction between purpose and meaning develops over time (Morawczynski, 2011; Oborn et al., 2019).

The Kenyan M-PESA mobile money system was developed using overseas development funding from the UK Government and implemented by Vodaphone’s Kenyan communications company Safaricom to provide a channel for paying back microfinance loans. M-PESA succeeded when it was repurposed by its users and its developers who, sensitive to this, altered M-PESA’s trajectory of development (Oborn et al., 2019). This transition highlighted the diferent meanings applied to M-PESA by institutions and by the local users. It was viewed by development organisations as a means of supporting the development of business in poor communities while Safaricom saw it as an opportunity to increase e-commerce and expand its financial services (Bright, 2020).

However, while there is a significant body of research on M-PESA (Hughes & Lonie, 2007ʹ Morawczynski, 2009; Ndung’u, 2018; Van Hove & Dubus, 2019), the meanings in rural poor regions have not really been studied. This paper explores their lived experience with mobile money in order to consider the diferences between the meaning attributed to M-PESA by institutions and by rural users. We ask the following research question: to what extent does M-PESA meaning for the rural poor contrast with those of the institutions promoting mobile money? While institutional meaning may be gleaned from stakeholder discussions, reviews and policy documents, understanding the meaning users apply to M-PESA requires engagement in the field.

To get at meaning attributed by people to an information system in their everyday lives, an approach was sought which would flag the imaginative and emotional meaning. To this end the poetic inquiry approach was applied, casting the output of field research in terms of poems. From this the meanings that participants attributed to mobile money could be identified and contrasted with the institutional meaning of M-PESA.

The paper is organised as follows. Firstly, the nature of mobile money is defined; followed by a review of current literature on M-PESA. Next the context of the research is explored with reference to the use of a poetic approach, leading into a discussion of the research setting and the method of poetic inquiry. The heart of the paper is seven interviews presented as poems and subjected to an analytical reading. The discussion reflects on the meanings attributed to M-PESA in the poems and compares them to the institutional meaning of M-PESA. Finally, the paper examines the policy implications.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Mobile money

Globally about 1.7 billion people do not have access to a bank account or a mobile money provider (Demirguc-Kunt et al., 2018). Virtually all unbanked adults live in developing countries. Concentrated among poorer households, women are overrepresented amongst the unbanked (Demirguc-Kunt et al., 2018). Digital financial inclusion is a key goal of the G20 nations, whose eight principles include promoting a digital approach to financial inclusion and establishing responsible digital financial practices (GPFI, 2016). It is also seen as key to delivering the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) (UNSGSA, 2018), although the evidence connecting digital financial inclusion and SDGs is sparse (Collins et al., 2019). Mobile money is seen as a means of bringing the unbanked into the formal banking system (GSMA, 2014) and promoting financial inclusion. As a vehicle for financial inclusion, it shifts the poor and disempowered towards digital finances without incurring the costs and administrative burden of formal banking.

Mobile money involves the transfer of money between two parties using the mobile phone as the mediating technology. When two people conduct a transaction using physical money, no record of the transaction is generated. There is no requirement for either party to have a bank account, or any kind of formal authorisation. The transaction cannot be stopped or reversed. It is unregulated and untraceable. The purpose of mobile money is to act as a digital version of a physical cash transaction.

The advantages, particularly for the disempowered and excluded, are many. It is not dependent on position or status. It skirts round stakeholders who might wish to exercise power over the parties, whether state, commercial, or dominant patriarchal structures. It enables transactions to be done with parties across large distances without the intervention of others who might take the money for other purposes. It is low cost and avoids the burden of banking where access to financial services may involve unwanted marketing and debt.

This casts doubt on the GSMA (2010) definition of mobile money as “a service in which the mobile phone is used to access financial services” since mobile money is a single service in which all that is provided is an electronic system to achieve that transfer.

There must be a mechanism in which, through agents, the physical money is turned into an electronic equivalent, transferred between mobile phones and then reconstituted as physical money by a second agent for the recipient. There will be a cost for using such minimalist technology, but a fraction of the cost of using a bank account or other financial instrument.

Mobile money enables access to electricity, water and sanitation via pay-as-you-go applications and provides a means for parents to pay education fees. It ofers multi-level socioeconomic benefits when adopted and used on a massive scale (Nan et al., 2021). Mobile money may promote financial inclusion and give rural poor access to financial services without having to travel to urban centres. A study in Northern Ghana found that mobile money was cheaper and faster than using a bank. Money could easily be received for school fees or sent to suppliers (Adaba et al., 2019). Furthermore, digital financial inclusion through the medium of mobile money is seen as a powerful means of empowering women in poorer countries (Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, 2019). Financial activity using mobile phones overcomes geographical barriers as well as providing a flexible approach and the development of a variety of applications. It has become recently a topic for scholarly activity (M. Kim et al., 2018).

Mobile money is not the same as mobile banking because it has a limited function and tends to operate outside formal banking systems. Information systems research in this area has tended to focus on mobile banking and its determinants of success (Albashrawi & Motiwalla, 2020; Chiu et al., 2017; G. Kim et al., 2009). Furthermore, mobile money difers from digital currencies such as Bitcoin, which while outside regulation, requires significant infrastructure and computing power, or equivalent regulated digital currencies, such as the Aber, whose implementation is planned in the United Arab Emirates (Almuraqab, 2020).

While M-PESA is held up as a shining example of successful mobile money, studies have examined the eficacy of mobile money initiatives in other countries. In Nigeria, a mobile money system introduced though the Central Bank failed. It was subject to stringent regulation (Ndweke & Agha, 2017). One critical factor was trust in a country where trust of banks is low. Uganda, while experiencing increased mobile money usage, lags behind Kenya. Malinga and Maiha (2020) identified the importance of social influence and sensitisation in mobile money take-up. In Sri Lanka mobile money has flourished in the context of regulatory collaboration between the bank and telecommunication companies (Suwandaarachchi et al., 2020). These studies illustrate the importance of regulation which can act both as a catalyst and constraint on mobile money (Burns, 2018).

## 2.2. M-PESA success and service evolution

M-PESA is widely recognised as the most successful implementation of a mobile banking system in the developing world. M-PESA has 24.9 million subscribers and over 173,000 agents. It accounts for 34% of Safaricom’s revenue. M-PESA in Kenya grew by 12.6% in 2020, driven by increased number of users, higher velocity of funds and new use cases (Safaricom, 2020a). The value of M-PESA goes beyond money transfers to the receipt of remittance for agricultural produce and wages (Rosengard, 2016). The success of M-PESA can be further measured by the increase in the proportion of the population accessing financial services which rose from 24.6% in 2006 to 75.3% in 2016 (Ndung’u, 2017). However, this depends on how financial services are defined and whether it includes banking and loans.

M-PESA works directly over the user’s phone account. The M-PESA user exchanges money for e-money recorded as an e-float on her M-PESA account. This money can be transferred to another M-PESA user’s account using a mobile phone, or used to directly pay for services such as education and energy. A vast majority of M-PESA transactions are single person transfers and withdrawals (Jack & Suri, 2011).

The success of M-PESA has been partly due to the reach of Safaricom’s mobile network and the development of an efective network of agents (Jack & Suri, 2011). In a further study, Suri & Jack (2016) estimated that the use of M-PESA raised 194.000 households out of extreme poverty and enabled 185,000 women to move to business and retail occupations and out of subsistence farming (Suri & Jack, 2016).

M-PESA had some initial advantages. Other means of transferring money were exorbitantly expensive; Safaricom has a dominant position in Kenyan mobile communications; the regulator allowed M-PESA to proceed as an experiment. When post-election violence in 2008 trapped people in cities, M-PESA was a lifeline to get money back to families; and marketing with the slogan “Send money home” was very efective (The Economist, 2015). Other advantages of M-PESA include using existing mobile technology and a wide distribution of retail outlets to support the sending of money (Victor, 2014). Organisational aspects such as the use of a pilot project, good technical support and Safaricom CEO advocacy have been cited (Hayes & Westrup, 2012).

M-PESA depends on the network of “human ATMs” who play a key role in maintaining the social and material networks which underpin its infrastructure (Park, 2020). Mas and Morawczynski (2009) identified the importance of good management of the network of agents to ensure that money was available for customers and transactions rigorously controlled. Strong branding, simple messaging of an easyto-use system, and the ability to transfer to noncustomers are important factors. Lepoutre and Oguntoye (2018) compared the success of Kenya and Nigeria in mobile money difusion and suggested that primary diferences were in regulation and critical mass of potential users.

M-PESA, which started as a development product, has become a commercial product, requiring revenue (84.44 million Kenyan shillings (KSHs4) in 2020, Safaricom, 2020a) and profit. Fees for withdrawing money, while lower than banks are not insignificant when all money needs to go to buying food. A withdrawal of between 50 and 100 KSHs cost 10 KSHs in 2020 (Safaricom, 2020b). The rural poor become a marketplace to be monitored, marketed and monetised. M-Shwari, the mobile money banking service, while ofering a facility for micro-saving, also ofers loans. As such it raises the possibility of increasing debt. Evidence suggests only the wealthier use M-Shwari (Nan & Markus, 2019). As M-PESA obtained its own e-commerce store, Masoko, and connected with Paypal (Dahir, 2018), it drifted away from its initial purpose. Fuzila M-PESA allows customers to complete M-PESA transactions when they have insufficient fund in their account. This and other products have been developed based on data analytics (Businessdailyafrica.com, 2019).

While structural aspects such as free deposits, no minimum balance and availability of good agents may encourage the use of M-PESA, it is still dificult to understand the precise impact of a mobile finance application (CFSP, 2013). Additionally, there is uncertainty as to the extent to which mobile finance systems reach the rural poor. It is often those who already have access to banking who adopt mobile finance (Martin & Duncombe, 2017). At a macrolevel, it can be suggested that continued use of mobile finance results from its utility in achieving tasks (Osah & Kyobe, 2017), but social norms, local context and social connection may be more significant.

## 2.3. M-PESA problems encountered by the rural poor

Oborn et al. (2019) traces the innovation of M-PESA in terms of trajectory dynamic. The original intent of M-PESA to support the repayment of microloans was shifted towards money transfer. A trajectory of separation was established as users adopted ways of using M-PESA which difered from the original intent. M-PESA was used to coordinate transitory lifestyles catalysed by urban migration in which the worker earns in the city and takes the money back to a rural home. M-PESA was subsequently integrated into local business, education and utilities as a payment method.

Bateman et al. (2019) critique some of the conclusions of Suri & Jack (2016). One of the benefits of M-PESA was suggested as improved livelihoods in moving from subsistence agriculture to more profitable microenterprises. But many of these enterprises fail and, where local demand is weak, the M-PESAdriven businesses benefit at the cost of existing businesses. Bateman et al. (2019) also point to increasing debt, the profit of Safaricom facilitated by microtransactions and deposited outside Kenya and a focus on wealthier customers. Other studies have questioned assumptions such as M-PESA encouraging saving (Van Hove & Dubus, 2019) and empowering women (Wyche et al., 2016). Failures to export M-PESA to South Africa (Van Zyl, 2016), Albania (Carroll, 2017) and India (Kurup, 2019), indicate that the M-PESA model is not universally applicable (Hayes & Westrup, 2012).

Van Hove and Dubus (2019) identify a significant gender efect on the uptake of M-PESA. Those who did not benefit from M-PESA were disproportionately poor, uneducated and female. While M-PESA ofered benefits to female-led households, women were also left behind. M-PESA has empowered rural women because they can receive money quicker from husbands and others in the city without having to travel, but at the social cost of reduced visits by men working in the cities to their rural homes (Morawczynski, 2009). However, the opportunity to use M-PESA may not necessarily result in the actual functioning (See Nussbaum, 2001) since resistance from husband and family, lack of education and religious experience might intervene to inhibit its use.

M-PESA may be causing problems in increasing the debt of the poorest. Digital credit may lead to overborrowing, hardship and financial exclusion (The Economist, 2018). Conversely, Van Hove and Dubus (2019) suggest that M-PESA may reduce saving potential because, with the ability to move money around, friends, family and community expect money to be used where there is a need. Using 2013 data, Van Hove and Dubus (2019) found that the proportion of savers among the rural poor was low. The possession of the phone with access to remittances could result in pressure from social networks to pay money out quickly. Wyche et al. (2016) raised further questions concerning the emancipatory power of M-PESA. They raised problems concerning unsolicited text services, women paying for music subscriptions they do not want which are dificult to unsubscribe from, dificulty in viewing messages and in entering codes to access prepaid airtime, and incurring debt because of unintentional subscriptions to premium rate services. Furthermore, mobile money is open to surveillance due to the requirement to register SIM cards, customer due diligence practices, the monitoring of agents and suspicious activity monitoring, an example of which is M-PESA’s Minotaur system (Martin, 2019).

Thus, the institutional meanings attached to M-PESA concerning financial inclusion, the promotion of saving and its use as a platform for delivering a menu of financial services as well as information gathering and processing (Ndung’u, 2018) may difer substantially from the meanings attached to M-PESA by the rural poor as a result of their use and experience of M-PESA. Hence there is a need to understand how the rural poor view mobile money such as M-PESA, what meanings they attach to it and how the problems of empowerment and inclusion are impacted, which was the aim of the fieldwork described in this paper.

## 3. Poetry as ethnography

Demand-side studies of mobile money usage have been heavily based on quantitative studies (M. Kim et al., 2018). Quantitative studies (Demirguc-Kunt et al. (2018); Suri & Jack, 2016; Van Hove & Dubus, 2019) produce some insights as to the benefits and efects of mobile money. However, such research applies economic data developed at some distance from the local field using transaction data and household survey data (CFSP, 2013). Such accounts obscure the important contexts that close studies can provide. While diferent contexts and understanding may be apparent at a macro level (Hayes & Westrup, 2012), micro studies in the field may provide diferent understanding which may moderate conclusions of national and international studies. Thus, it is important to move away from a dependence on high-level economic studies and ask key questions in the field. Studies in the field, which involve the researcher coming along aside the participants, interviewing users in M-PESA shops, buses, homes, farms and open spaces in rural villages (Oborn et al., 2019); which involve the researcher in experiencing the context and the environment (Oborn et al., 2019) or which involve agents and users (Adaba et al., 2019), move a little closer to the understanding of the lived experience of the technology. Engagement and observation in the field, watching users (Wyche et al., 2016), produces new insights which may contradict the conclusions of wider macro studies.

While such work constitutes ethnography (Hammersley & Atkinson, 2019), including participating in daily lives, experiencing M-PESA transactions and observing agents (Oborn et al., 2019) and conducting interviews (Adaba et al., 2019), it still leaves a distance between the participant and researcher, including in background, education and cultural understanding. This distance is only increased as we leave the field: “as soon as we have filled our bags we cut and run” (Ingold, 2013, p. 5). We extract quotes to suit our purposes; the text is torn away from the participant and leaves behind an entire landscape of narratives and experience.

There remains an empathic gap between the researcher and the participant which is likely to afect the research outcome.

How can this be overcome? One approach is that the researcher is drawn from the culture and background of the participants, and has a similar experiential narrative and a life history which enables him to understand and empathise with the participants. This could involve autoethnography where the researcher is the research subject. The researcher reflects and documents her own experience. This may be a fruitful approach in researching experiential computing, such as Prasopoulou (2017) engages in while exploring the use of an activity tracker. A second approach is that the researcher aims to let the participant speak for herself, without the researcher acting as an intermediate interpreter. Here the researcher’s task is more to facilitate the expression of the participant’s lived experience. The researcher becomes a counsellor, opening up paths of discovery through questioning and listening, and leaving the interpretation open.

In this study we employed both approaches. Firstly, the second author, active in the field, has experienced rural deprivation himself and understood the struggle that the rural poor experience. He grew up in the area in which the field study was conducted. He came from a privileged background, but his family made an instant drop into the place of poverty when his father (the breadwinner) suddenly died through a road accident. He struggled to break free from a web of poverty and lack of shelter and food. As an educated researcher, returning to his roots, he could quickly develop empathic connections with the community.

Secondly, we aimed to avoid contaminating the thoughts and expressions of the participants by extracting quotes to illustrate points that we wished to pursue. Rather we searched for an approach which would prioritise the expression of the participants without interference. Our wish was that the participant would not merely express the facts of the case, but that the emotional force of the narrative would be exposed to the reader. To this end we pursued a poetic expression of the participant narrative as a vehicle to expose the contours of meaning and develop a fresh understanding of the narrative.

The skill of the researcher lies in his ability to engage empathically with the subjects and draw out their story. Sensitivity is then required to divide the text into lines and stanzas in order to draw out the deep emotions. The presentations ofer not just the possibility of presenting the text, but also an opportunity to perform the poetry in order to validate its interpretation and as a means of presenting the research. Performance ofers a means of expressing research ideas, whether it is in understanding the changing geology of a landscape (Grifiths et al., 2017), or reflecting on personal experience of the software development process (McBride, 2008). Also, poetry can not only provide a medium for presenting findings, but can be a vehicle for expressing the researcher’s interpretation, for example, in archival research into flood histories (Grifiths, 2017).

By using a poetic form, we cross the gap between the participant and the researcher. We allocate authority and control to the participant, we hand ownership back. Poetry records personal emotion and experience (Furniss & Bath, 1996, p. 7). By creating a poetic form, we expose raw emotion, intentions, and feeling. The only watertight distinction between poetry and prose is that it is set out in lines on the page. This has the efect of producing rhythm, creating pauses, highlighting and exposing each phrase. Also, the breaking up of the lines creates a spontaneity; almost a presence of the person which narrows the gap between the participant and the reader.

The poem expresses the whole experience in contrast to the isolated quotes which are characteristic of interpretive case studies. Poems express a dynamic narrative, a flow of activity. In forming poems, the researcher seeks to remove himself from the picture, to stand back and let the participant speak for herself. This creates a rawness and immediacy which connects with the reader or listener. By returning the words to the participant we increase the power to afect.

For the researchers, the poetic expressions ofer a diferent substrate for exploration and reflection and enable a diferent interpretive approach to be applied. For the reader, there is a visceral involvement. The reader becomes part of the research process, a participant in the interpretive process, applying personal strategies (Yakich, 2014).

Poetry embeds meaning and enables meaning to be derived. The use of poetry does not limit us to one meaning but encourages the reader and researcher to explore possible meanings. Furthermore, it remains open to interpretation. Heaney (1997, p. 130) suggested that “The poem does not distain intellect, yet poetry, having to do with feelings and emotions, must not submit to the intellect’s eagerness to foreclose”.

## 4. Research setting and method

This paper originates from the fieldwork undertaken by the second author between July and December 2011. Permission was granted by the loca chief and sub-chief of Bukhalalire sub-location in the border town of Busia in Western Kenya to conduct qualitative research with M-PESA users within the sub-location. Bukhalalire was selected because of its level of deprivation, its remoteness and the researcher’s familiarity with culture and language. The Bukhalalire sub-location is part of Busia County in Western Kenya. Busia County covers 1,261 square kilometres including 137 square kilometres of Lake Victoria and borders the Republic of Uganda to the west. It lies on a low fertile plain dotted with swamps, rivers, valleys and natural springs. Temperature averages 16 degrees Celsius in the cool season and 28 degrees Celsius in the hot season. The area is primarily agricultural and consists of 17 villages with around 7000 inhabitants. Around two-thirds live in extreme poverty with an average income of £45 per month.

The sub-location head introduced the researcher to his governing council, which was made up of elders drawn from the development blocks of the village. The researcher had an introductory meeting with the elders and scheduled another meeting to discuss the process of the planned research. Three research assistants were recruited, briefed and together attended a second meeting with the village elders. The meeting was aimed at explaining in detail the reasons for the research exercise, the anticipated outcome, participants’ voluntary involvement and rights, and also the manner in which the data would be processed and handled. For the interview encounters, however, the researcher requested additional permission from the participants, to allow him to use interview scripts rewritten as dramatic texts to be read out to other participants.

Focus groups were planned in cooperation with the elders to explore the understanding of particular development groups including women, schools, youth, churches and villages. Participants included oficials and others active in the community. Traditional leaders in rural African communities enjoy widespread popular legitimacy (Logan, 2013). Local chiefs in Kenya have an important role in crime control, particularly concerning the theft of livestock where decisions are largely made in a consensual manner (Mbuba & Mugambi, 2011). Working with the traditional structures provided assurance to the community of the legitimacy of the researcher.

Seven hundred invitations to the focus groups were drafted and distributed by the elders. Focus groups were conducted in a primary school, a church and the chief’s camp. Focus group outcomes were communicated back to the chief’s ofice. While the focus groups generated data in their own right, in the context of this study their value was twofold. Firstly, they provided insights and directions for the subsequent interviews as they immersed the researcher in the environment and issues which later informed the interview. Secondly, the involvement of the chief, oficials and the public resulted in the research and its purpose being known throughout the Bukhalalire sublocation. The researcher was accepted and immersed in the community. This closed the researcher-participant gap and opened up more trust resulting in candid conversations.

In order to ensure that interviewees were drawn from a place of poverty, a standard process was developed. Using a list of homesteads within the villages, and through discussion with village elders, two participants were selected from each village. Interview participants were selected based on a decision tree of indicators of poverty which address material poverty, place poverty, physical poverty, social isolation and lack of education (Hick, 2012, p. 146). Participants were visited to check they were willing to be interviewed and interviews were scheduled.

The interviews were carried out in a mix of Swahili, Sheng’, Luo, Luhya and English. The first strategy was to develop a consensus on dimensions of concern, then learn from the interviewees how M-PESA has influenced those concerns. A standard questionnaire would have been insuficient to solicit this information. Instead the researcher carried out unstructured interviews on three diferent sites. The sites were roadside market-places where traders converged every evening to sell their products.

During the interview, background information was obtained concerning basic demographics and the needs and aspirations of the participant. The intention was to understand how M-PESA helps people achieve what they consider to be valuable. However, there were no fixed questions and the interview style was empathic. Because the interviewer encouraged a relaxed conversation in an informal language, the participants were able to tell their stories producing statements which later were used as dramatised texts, carrying the voice of the participant. A total of 38 interviews were conducted. Interviews were transcribed and translated and a total of 20 were transformed into poems.

The research method follows the approach of poetic inquiry (Butler-Kisber, 2010; Owton, 2017) which has been gaining popularity within the social sciences. The approach helps reveal the lived experiences of research participants. It provides a powerful approach for engaging with those on the margins of society, such as black single mothers (Corley, 2019) and educationally marginalised youth (Gorlich, 2016). Haberlin and Davis (2019) employ a similar technique in their study of stay-at-home dads, in order to evoke emotion and to connect more deeply with the data and participants’ experience. The use of poems evokes emotions and feelings within the reader; it connects the reader with the researcher and invites the reader to respond to and interpret the poetic form and to engage in a dialogue with the researcher’s analysis (Haberlin & Davis, 2019).

The process of creating the poem is simply one of dividing the prose text into lines and stanzas. This applies to all the poems. The poetic texts are the words of the participants. It is the empathic interviewing that elicits the emotion and depth. The poetic texts were read back to participants to ensure validity and read to local women’s groups where they resonated as strong expressions of shared life experience.

The mere act of casting the prose into lines of poetry changes the atmosphere and creates involvement. But it also means there is no single right reading. In reading and re-reading the poetic text, characteristic themes emerged associated with particular interviewees. These are fleshed out in introductions to each poem. Following each poem, we ofer a discussion of the poem, our own reading which draws out the issues that matter and highlights the flow of thought we sense that the participant is expressing. Our readings, modelled on the approach of Padel (2004, p. 6) in her discussions of 52 modern poems, provides an initial step in understanding the meaning of M-PESA in the lives of the participants.

Schultze (2000) highlighted the importance of evaluating authenticity, plausibility and criticality in ethnographic research. Authenticity concerns the evidence for immersion in the field. Here we have described the field researcher’s deep engagement with the community and the methodological approach, including the involvement of chiefs, the development of focus groups, acceptance by the community and the trust of participants in the conduct of interviews. Furthermore, the field researcher’s distinctive life-story as a Kenyan catalysed a distinctive entanglement in the field which an external academic may not be able to achieve. The rigour in selecting participants, together with the foregrounding of their experience through poetic texts, indicates the validity. The fact that the poems were well received and what they said recognised, both by women’s groups and by university students during seminars and talks, also indicates their validity.

Plausibility concerns the desk work. We have endeavoured to draw out the common experiences of lived engagement with mobile money, providing linked interpretations to the poems. However, the ofering of the poems creates a transparency between participant and reader which we would expect to enable other interpretations.

Criticality, requires that we hold up the rural culture and reaction to M-PESA as a mirror to the global commercial culture so that we learn about ourselves. The use of poetry acts as a mirror in connecting our experience with the lived experience of Kenyan culture. Our critical understanding is developed in the discussion and raises concerns in both practice and policy.

## 5. Introduction to the poems

We suggest reading the poems reflectively. Each phrase, each thought, is exposed, held up, ofered as something to be handled, to be turned over. Response matters, the unconscious as well as conscious mind reacting to the poem (Padel, 2004). Having read a poem, read the analysis, and then return to the poem for new insights. Poetry requires active reading. Remind yourself that the words were all spoken by the participants and express a narrative of life and emotion.

Each poem is introduced by a short discussion of a particular theme which reflects on the impact of M-PESA. However, that does not mean it is the only theme that can be elicited from the poem: some address multiple themes.

The first five poems explore the meaning of M-PESA for participants for whom M-PESA had become transformative in their lives. However, an exploration of the meaning of a technology for users must address those whom the widespread adoption of a technology leaves behind. The last two cases consider where a woman is barred from using the technology, and an older man is left behind when technology is socially adopted. Considering two cases where there is an absence of M-PESA gives a richer insight into the lived experience of mobile money usage. In such cases, not only must people adapt to having the technology but also to not having the technology.

## 5.1. Education: Jacinta Akinyi<sup>1</sup> (50)

Education has long been seen as the gateway to prosperity. At the same time poverty acts as a barrier to education, creating a deleterious feedback circle. Costs, attitudes to education, health and distance to schools are among the barriers (Habitat for Humanity, 2019). M-PESA ofers a possibility to break that cycle by enabling money to be sent to schools for paying fees. However, as Jacinta perceptively identifies, breaking the payment cycle is only a start.

Jacinta, like many Kenyans, experiences a sudden descent into poverty, from an afluent city life in a rich family before her father passed away, to poor rural living. Desires are stripped down to basic searches for food and shelter. Poverty is experienced now: I am living it. Poverty is a journey of descent: as you travel, you get weak, abused, exposed. You collapse. You need resuscitation. Poverty is not learnt at school or discussed at a distance. It is experienced. Jacinta’s poem is one of experience: painful diseases, debt, hunger, extortion, which gives the sense of an expression of the tip of an iceberg: there are many others. For Jacinta, M-PESA ofers a dramatic intervention in a traumatic journey. The simile of drinking fresh water leaps out countering dust, disease, collapse. It has resuscitating properties. And note the connection between banking and education: bank accounts are for those who have gone to school. The use of M-PESA is not learnt in school: you don’t have to go to school to learn how to use it. M-PESA does not require education but it brings access to education. Education for my children is associated with M-PESA and access, opportunities, selfworth, confidence. However, M-PESA is only one component in countering poverty. Jacinta’s poem starts of with partial use of M-PESA which is irregular: she uses M-PESA on and of; and ends within the metaphor of drinking fresh water. But fresh water just resuscitates, raises the impoverished from collapse: then we need other things to come into play.

## 5.2. Empowerment Dora Ojwe (48)

When women have their money controlled by their husband, or men working in cities fail to send home money, then mobile money releases them from this dependency. In a country where many people move from the rural areas to cities to work, heavy drinking is prevalent and 37% of alcohol users divert resources to pay for drinking (Kendgor et al., 2018). M-PESA ofers a way out and freedom for women to control their own budgets. Also, women use M-PESA in their businesses. Women on the shores of Lake Victoria who process and sell fish can get payments from traders easily and pay the fishermen easily. M-PESA enables the women to develop other small businesses (White, 2012). People such as Dora do not have access to banks or are thrown out if they turn up. M-PESA removes the need to face security men with guns (Oborn et al., 2019). It sits outside the system which requires certainty in location, identification and income.

However, we need to be careful to consider what we mean by empowerment and the relationship between empowerment and disempowerment (Pandey & Zheng, 2019). Empowerment concerns ability to make choices. But does Dora have any more freedom to make choices? Or has M-PESA simply removed some of the bars of her prison so she can exercise some basic human rights?

Many women in Kenya carry the burden of family life and survival with a sense of stoic resignation. At the heart of Dora’s poem is this sense. The men are absent through working in the city, alcoholism, infidelity, or plain laziness. Dora’s poem pivots around the burden of men (They are my burden) and a sense of determination to make the best of things. The role of M-PESA is in helping with the burden. The women run businesses. Business is good. Dora is established. Good and better are sandwiched in between complain and the root of Dora’s complaint and her burden is men. The focus is her husband, the source of the burden. Dora seeks to draw the researcher in, to engage the imagination: imagine that man. The actions of the husband: took me, has not stopped marrying (in a culture where polygamy is traditional), pushed away, eat, drink, sleep, look for best liquor are the sources directly and indirectly of the dificulties Dora faces. Dora’s relational focus on her daughter and giving her daughter a chance of freedom from the burden Dora bears through education, is enabled by M-PESA. Her daughter joined high school, is sent money, doesn’t have to come home out of pocket. M-PESA mediates the relationship with the daughter and enables her support at a distant school. It enables Dora to override the burden of the men. It also changes relationships with institutions and companies. Conflict, disconnection, accumulation of bills and reconnection are overcome through the use of M-PESA. It addresses the fundamentals of survival and getting out of poverty: energy and education rather than any frivolous purchases of the well-of. For Dora, the transition from lazy men to diligent daughter turns on the use of M-PESA. The poem ends with a nod towards the universal use of M-PESA in Kenya. The firmly established market leader will see of any competition.

## 5.3. Discovery: Dora Ojwang (18)

The availability of technology does not necessarily mean it is going to be used, nor that the benefits will be incurred. It has to be discovered first and discovery requires a community in which the technology is used. People must be introduced to the mobile money. While marketing helps, it may require community engagement as well. In the case of M-PESA, agents helped in the discovery of the service. The technology must be discovered by practice. That practice may lead to an epiphany, a moment when there is a realisation of the impact that a technology can have on a person’s life. And once discovered, the technology becomes an instrument of discovery in itself.

Dora is an orphan, bringing up her siblings. In the quiet of her house we hear the creaking of the rickety building. The house has holes in its roof which is made out of used tins straightened out. Dora’s route to redemption is through the discovery of M-PESA. It has redeemed my life. It restores the relationship with her uncles, breaks the stranglehold of the brother, connects Dora with remote relations, and enables continuous support from her cousin: She has not stopped sending me money ever since, sometimes in the middle of the night. The theme of discovery, suggested early on: my uncles discovered M-PESA, is taken up by Dora. She has a false view of M-PESA. An encounter with a cousin wakes her to the possibility of M-PESA. She receives help from friends. Uncle, cousin, friends all connected to direct her towards M-PESA. The experience of an M-PESA agent’s little corner shop creates mixed feelings because it contrasts with her expectations of banks as glamorous and emphasises a cognitive dissonance between expectation and perception. M-PESA operates in the transient, pivoting around I take one day at a time. At the end of the poem we see that the relationship with the brother has changed. ## 5.4. Precarity Mwita Chacha (26)

Precarity is experienced by the rural poor. The social and governmental networks fail them. They are diferentially exposed to injury, violence and death (Butler-Kisber, 2010, Butler, 2016). Work is temporary, incomes cannot be predicted. The rural poor are less protected from environmental change, from flood and droughts. They do not have steady flows of income, nor social status which might support a bank account. For such groups, living in precarious states of health, wealth and accommodation the mobile phone ofers a lifeline. Mobile money is transient technology which can be picked up and used and dropped. It requires no prior commitment, monthly bills nor expenditure. It requires no fixed location (Rhoades et al., 2017). Cash turns up, flows and is dissipated. In a society where incomes are low, cash flow irregular and getting money may depend on the success of subsistence crops, M-PESA flourishes because it adapts to uncertainty.

## Fish is perishable

I am from Tarime on the Kenya/Tanzania border. I have lived here since I was a young boy. My parents were living here. I went to primary and secondary school here. When they retired They went back home. I decided to settle down here. (Laughing) You cannot tell I am not from this place (laughs) Unless you ask me my names. My business partner looks for markets And buyers for our fish. Challenges are many. The biggest is the falling value of the shilling. People used to buy our fish on a regular basis, Now they say they have to tighten their belts. Our fish is seen as a luxury. They just send us the money through M-PESA Once they have the cash. And it’s better to be paid later Than to have the fish rot. You incur losses Way too easy (laughs). Mwita expresses the sense of transience which is associated with M-PESA. He has lived in the area since he was a young boy, you cannot tell he is not from this place, and yet his parents went back home. He decides to settle. He is still a foreigner despite welcome and friends. The challenges Mwita faces are those of a volatile environment and economy: The falling value of the shilling, people having to tighten their belts, fish seen as a luxury. He is forced to give fish to regular customers. But M-PESA ofers a mechanism outside the structured, regularised financial systems. It enables debt to be collected in an ad-hoc informal manner: I just have to call them once in a while. Customers send money through M-PESA once they have the cash. Mwita lives a transient life, although invisible unless you ask him his name, and M-PESA enables transience. Daily catches must be daily sold: we are forced to sell everything. Better to get paid later than have the fish rot. And M-PESA is part of life: way too easy: all his customers use M-PESA. Yet despite this transient life-style, culturally, economically, practically, Mwita longs for permanence, for cold storage facilities and, we might speculate, a permanent bank account. His poem, located in transience, ends with a dream of permanence, and the suggestion that it would be with such permanence and assurance that growth can occur. There is the suggestion that, while M-PESA ofers relief from poverty and supports survival in an ever changing, uncertain, deprived life, it does not enable growth which may require a movement from uncertainty to the structured assurance of fixed bank accounts and reliable cash flows.

## 5.5. Absorption: Peter Pamba (41)

For technology to become embedded, as Latour (2005) suggested, the interests of the user must be translated into the interest of the technology. There must be an alignment. It becomes a black box, an essential prosthesis, a habit. And at this point it becomes a necessity, its entanglement with our behaviour is irreversible.

## 5.6. Exclusion: Monica Adhiambo (29)

In 2019, mobile usage in Kenya reached 91% (Namunwa, 2019). Mobile usage became ubiquitous, not an option for financial inclusion, but an essential for social belonging. As the technology becomes standard, alternative means of financial inclusion are gradually eliminated (Geach, 2007). People are left in an even deeper state of financial exclusion. The consequences of exclusion grow and the level of poverty increases for some. As the service availability over mobile devices grows, the efect of exclusion magnifies. Smartphone dependence may extend the digital divide, or create a new type of digital divide (Testsi & Rains, 2017), creating a technological barrier to climbing out of poverty. The efect of having one’s phone removed; taken away by a family member; or losing a mobile account because of non-payment, becomes all that much greater a hardship.

## 5.7. Identity: Jackstone Asekwa (59)

Increasingly identity is mediated or even determined by our use of a mobile phone. We identify ourselves to the phone through fingerprint, voice or facial recognition. The phone becomes our route of access to purchases, travel and social connection. Our social identity is established through social communication over the phone whether by texting or social media. We use our phone to symbolise and construct our identities (Gentina & Rowe, 2020; Hassan & Unwin, 2017). Thus, lack of phone, or exclusion from mobile mediated transaction, may cause a loss of identity. It may seem strange that our seventh poem is also about a lack of phone, but it illustrates how new technologies and services, while ofering inclusion to many, may result in exclusion for others and disruption of traditional community structures.

## 6. Discussion

An information system, purposed towards a particular goal by its sponsors and developers, may take on diferent purposes and meanings as it is embedded in a people’s lives. This entanglement of the information system and the person’s life may create meaning which diverts the information system from its envisaged purpose. This study illustrates such a phenomenon where the efect of the system on people’s lives diverges from the institutional intent and purpose. It directs attention to the meaning of individual ICT4D experiences (Andrade et al., 2017).

This paper ofers an insight to the efect of M-PESA on the lives of rural Kenyans through the vehicle of poetry. With reference to the research question: “To what extent does M-PESA meaning for the rural poor contrast with those of the institutions promoting mobile money?” we used the voices of rural Kenyans, caste in poetic form. In using poetry, narratives are created which go beyond the surface understanding facilitated by questionnaires and surveys to expose the deeper rooting of systems and services in people’s lives. In an efort to let the users of M-PESA speak for themselves, we created participant-voiced poems, constructed with minimum intervention from interviews. Poetry embraces the notion of speech as an embodied activity; it expands human experience (Vincent, 2018). It draws out the cognitive and the sensory and hence both the researcher and the reader become more attuned to the lived experience of the participants (Prendergast, 2009). In this section we examine the meaning of mobile money with regards to education, empowerment and identity, drawing on the poems and contrast this with the institutional meaning.

## 6.1. Individual Meaning of mobile money

Education is a key concern of the rural population in many African counties. For rural Kenyans, the achievement of educating their children is a source of pride. The Kenyan Financial Diaries project found that educational expenditure accounts for 18% of monthly income in areas where agriculture is dominant (Collins et al., 2015). The expenses involve not only school fees but maintenance when the child may be living away from home in order to attend school. Previous studies have suggested that mobile money transfer in Kenya has enhanced educational opportunities and reduced the absenteeism caused when a student is sent home because of none fee payment (Juma, 2020).

This study enriches the understanding of the importance and impact of an information system such as M-PESA by shifting the focus from the quantitative to the qualitative, expressing the voices and narratives of the people whose lives have been impacted by the use of mobile money. Education carries a meaning which is emancipatory. Mobile money is a route to freedom from grinding poverty. It provides a future orientation and a sense of hope which pervades the poems. School represents freedom. Dora Ojwe loses her freedom when her husband takes her out of school. Education is at the source of the good life, a route to self-worth and opportunity. In the contours of meaning exposed by poetic representation, education stands high.

Poems encompass dynamic movement; they are never static descriptions. Short lines emphasise the progression of the narrative. A theme such as school, which might appear in a N-Vivo analysis, is not actually static. It is subject to forward movement. It is not just that education is important, it is part of an evolving narrative. In Dora Ojwe’s poem she is taken from school, then her daughter joins high school, then M-PESA becomes a vehicle to pay fees. School is a motif, a musical recapitulation in the poem. The emotional narrative is retained, rather than being torn away in pursuit of objectivity; context is preserved. In the poem of Dora Ojwang, the context of the non-payment of school fees is her brothers’ misuse of the money meant for fees. The narrative progresses with the uncle’s discovery of M-PESA through which he could ensure the fees were paid. Poems reward multiple readings by which we develop an understanding of the relational networks which M-PESA supports.

Empowerment is often cited as an outcome in the deployment of ICT within developing countries (for example, Laizu et al., 2010; Maier & Nair-Reichert, 2007; Walama, 2012). However, there are problems with the claim that ICT contributes to women’s empowerment. The use of the ICT may not do what the designers originally intended, as illustrated in the case of M-PESA. The environment within which ICT adoption occurs is complex and the constraints systemic. Mobile phones may not empower if favourable contextual factors concerning education, community and infrastructure are not present (Iiiya & Ononiwu, 2021). Furthermore, applications which rely on ICT, such as microfinance may not themselves necessarily empower and could actually disempower (Ali & Hatta, 2012). Access to employment and earning enabled by ICT may ofer no significant empowerment if patriarchal norms enable men to take their wives’ earnings or abuse them in anger at the changing household dynamics (Nazneen et al., 2019).

However, the problem lies deeper than whether ICT can actually empower: it lies with the concept itself. “Empowerment” is seen as a buzz word, used casually as part of the rhetoric of development (Woodall et al, 2012). There is no universal definition. The problem is compounded in considering the term “women’s empowerment” which has lost all meaning as a term of use in the women’s movement (Richardson, 2020). In reclaiming the concept of women’s empowerment, it should be recognised that it not only encompasses the acquisition of resource and capabilities, as delineated in the Capability Approach (Nussbaum, 2001) but also involves both systemic connections at multiple levels and the development of consciousness or “the power within” (Nazneen et al., 2019). Galie and Farnworth (2019) ofer an additional concept of ’power through’. Women’s empowerment does not only require changes within and the application of agency, it also requires changes in relationships. “Power through” concerns the status of significant people, the relating of the individual to others and the perceptions of the immediate community (Galie & Farnworth, 2019). Our study illustrates the efect of M-PESA in individual lives in reconfiguring networks such that new connections are empowered and old connections broken. Dora Ojwang connects to her cousin and is empowered through financial provision. In contrast, Jackstone is disempowered as networks are reconfigured around him.

Cornwall (2016) identifies three insights concerning empowerment. Firstly, it is about changing power relationships. The poems in this study illustrate changing power relationships. Dora Ojwang is freed from a dependency on travelling to banks. She is freed from the abusive behaviour of her brother taking whatever money we had. Secondly, empowerment is relational. Lived experience is essentially social, involving relationships with family, business partners and customers. For Mwita, M-PESA changes the nature of the relationship with customers, ensuring that more fish is sold. Thirdly, empowerment is a process, a continuous state of change which involves shifts in consciousness (Cornwall, 2016). In the poems, Dora Ojwang discovers M-PESA as part of her shifting consciousness and Peter, in his journey, comes to a point at which M-PESA is part of daily life.

Monica’s poem exposes a negativity through repeated phrases: “I don’t . . . I don’t . . . I didn’t . . . I couldn’t.” It points to a sense of helplessness, of disempowerment through the loss of her phone which isolated quotes from a qualitative case study might not draw out. The disempowerment is expressed in the rhythm of the words which may disappear in dense prose. Importantly, the poetry enables the transmission of emotion: the sadness, the resignation to her state. The breaking up of the lines inserts silences in which the sound of the spoken word is framed (Street, 2012, p. 44). It directs a lingering on phrases: “I feel very sad” stands out and demands attention. We understand the emotional depth of the helplessness which Monica’s loss of her phone elicits. Such would be overlooked in the presentation of the text as prose.

Identity in rural Africa is established within community, through role and position in what are generally patriarchal structures (Silberschmidt, 2001). Identity is relational, defined particularly within the extended family. Mobile money may undermine tradi tional relational structure and hence alter identity. Power structures are changed such that, for example, the young “info-mediary” may disturb the current generational balance of power (Porter et al., 2009). The ability to easily send and receive money may undermine rural community, resulting in increased migration (Batista & Vicente., 2020). As lines of distance and proximity are redrawn, family members may take on multiple identities (Porter et al., 2009). Mobile money transfer constitutes a form of communication strengthening some relationships through gifts. Kusimba et al. (2015) studied mobile money transactions in family networks and demonstrated that M-PESA reinforced existing inclusions and exclusions; including mothers and siblings, excluding inlaws and spouses. Our study illustrates the emotional importance of such connections and their role in identity. The poetic expression draws out the meaning encased in identity as felt relationships. M-PESA reconfigures Dora Ojwang’s social network. Her relationship with a cousin is strengthened. Her relationship with a brother becomes distant.

Jackstone is excluded: he is no longer in charge. It was unheard of, that a son can give his mother money unless through the father. Now Jackstone is excluded from key patriarchal functions and his identity is eroded. The breaking up of Jackstone’s words into lines directs attention to how hierarchical roles and relationships are flattened in the level playing field which M-PESA brings. This is further amplified by the way the poem sets tone and emphasis through repetition: “Everyone is in charge. Everyone is in the know. And everyone has a bank account.” Thus, with the poetic expression, patterns are revealed which might have remained hidden.

The individual meaning of M-PESA, expressed in the words of rural Kenyans is fundamentally relational. Whether through education, empowerment or identity, it changes who I am. Whether by creating new relationships, or changing relationships with children, cousins and husbands, it frees me from stifling, oppressive relationships. It enables me to give myself to the relationships which matter. It changes my relationship with customers and partners. This is a dramatic contrast from the institutional perception of the meaning of mobile money.

## 6.2. Institutional Meaning of mobile money

For institutions a major purpose of mobile money such as M-PESA is to enable financial inclusion. As such financial inclusion is seen as engaging with banking systems and banking products including loans and savings (Vodaphone, 2020). There is a focus on financial services and increasing transactions, thus improving money velocity (Mbiti & Weil, 2013). Ouma et al. (2017) suggested that, based on a Quantitative study, mobile financial services in Kenya promoted savings amongst poor and low-income groups. Mobile money targeted the unbanked and under-banked.

However, the regulation of mobile money vehicles such as M-PESA is driven by commercial opportunity rather than social and economic development concerns. M-PESA has been used as a platform to provide fee-based access to essential services, which may impose additional burdens and responsibilities, and require additional capabilities to create the improvements in livelihood for which M-PESA provides opportunities (Natile, 2019). In early 2020, Vodacom and Safaricom, acquired the M-PESA brand from Vodaphone with the intention of developing an intelligent cloud-based platform for the smartphone age. Their aim is to expand the geographical range and facilitate e-commerce, bringing the M-PESA platform to global companies (Bright, 2020).

Institutionally, in the financing of education, mobile money is seen as a vehicle for more stable, eficient and safe remittance of school fees. For example, an implementation in the Ivory Coast was interpreted as driving cost and operational eficiencies, and providing better information access for government and enhanced value proposition for mobile money systems providers (GSMA, 2015). In terms of supporting education, Adida et al. (2018), based on their study in rural Benin, suggested that a mobile phone provider may not be the ideal partner in supporting school fees payment due to commercial pressures. Women’s empowerment is understood in development thought and practice as concerning the control of economic resources and women’s access to economic markets. The institutional view of women empowerment concerns the economic rather than the social. By accessing the market through M-PESA women become economically available, a target for marketing and commercial transactions.

Additionally, when addressing personal stories, as is done in the stories of Patrick, Consolata, Mary, Felista and Jehinda (Vodaphone, 2021), the focus is on helping businesses be more eficient, sending money, paying for ambulance services, starting an M-PESA agent business and paying for healthcare. The understanding portrayed in these Vodaphone case studies is transactional rather than the relational understanding of rural people portrayed in the poems.

## 6.3. Implications for policy and practice

The development of mobile payments system policy requires that policy makers and practitioners have a firm grip of the reasons for adoption and have empathy with the experience of users. This paper has illustrated some of the life situations and meanings attached to one mobile payment system.

The sole purpose of M-PESA, once redesigned with a trajectory of mobile money transfer, was to enable money transfer in an informal economy: within families, between urban workers and their rural homes. Its value lay in its separation from banks, its light regulation, its simplicity of interface and its support of an informal economy.

To maintain this distancing will require a delicate balance of regulation. On the one hand formal regulation and connecting of systems into the state-regulated financial sector may result in a divergence of meaning such that the users are alienated and excluded and become consumers to be exploited for commercial profit. On the other hand, no regulation exposes users to a diferent kind of criminal exploitation. A key decision early on by the Kenyan government was to exclude M-PESA from commercial regulation, putting it outside the control of the banks.

The initial intent of the information system needs to be preserved rather buried within a proliferation of functions and services. It needs to be protected from drift towards commercial or state objectives which do not align with the development objectives. This may require legislation which inhibits the appropriation of the system for other institutional objectives. A separation needs to be preserved such that the initial purpose or advantage of the system is not buried beneath an avalanche of commercial ambition.

Efectively, Safaricom, through the vehicle of M-PESA has infiltrated the dense community of relationships by which the rural poor survives. This may lead to a new dependency, where the failure or absence of M-PESA is not a case of inconvenience, but a plummet to an even more severe poverty.

Furthermore, the management of mobile payment systems such as M-PESA may require an organisational separation from commercial and state structures such that innovative systems are run by nongovernmental organisations, charities or trusts whose objectives are clearly delineated in the relief of poverty.

## 7. Conclusion

Using the method of poetic inquiry, this study illustrates the importance of emotional meaning in the use of an information system. That meaning does not necessarily correspond to the meaning attached to the institutions which develop, implement and support such systems. The study identifies diferent meanings attributed to M-PESA by rural users and by the institutions, particularly Safaricom, which run the mobile money system and draw commercial gain from it. The comparison of the experience of rural users and the expectation of institutions suggest that there is a significant gap between the two in the meaning attached to M-PESA, as an example of mobile money. For rural users the benefits are relational; for the commercial organisations the benefits, as perceived concerning the users, are economic.

The tool view of technology (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) suggests that the intervention of ICT will result in intended changes. This needs to be viewed with some scepticism (Addo, 2015). In the case of M-PESA, intended use and purpose did not appear as users commandeered M-PESA for their own purposes. Insightful designers initially went with the flow and redesigned M-PESA as mobile money. Oborn et al. (2019) viewed M-PESA as an innovation trajectory developing over time, Initially, users took the mobile money application and used it for their own purposes. This study suggest that the innovation strategy has widened the gap.

The efect of ICT lies beyond competitive initiatives to increase access to resources (Cornwall, 2016). Rather ICT addresses the power within and the power through. Therefore, we would suggest that views of the function of ICT can be too narrowly defined, missing the importance of resetting power relationships and changing networks.

Studies which uncover the user’s emotions that drive the meanings attached to an application such as M-PESA and consider the gap between individua and institutional purpose are important steps towards the development of a stronger ethics of information systems. The contrasting of the poetic expression of the meaning of M-PESA for rural users’ lives with the current and future commercial drivers of M-PESA works towards identifying a critical need for a dialogue between users and institutions. The ethical practice of information system development requires transparency concerning the motives for development and rollout. And transparency about the purpose and meaning of an information system is particularly important in environments where the users are poor, marginalised and disadvantaged.
