# Learning not to take the bait: a longitudinal examination of digital training methods and overlearning on phishing susceptibility

Christopher Nguyen<sup>a</sup>, Matthew Jensen<sup>b</sup> and Eric Day<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Logistics Management Institute, McLean, VA, USA; <sup>b</sup>University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK, USA

## ABSTRACT

As phishing becomes increasingly sophisticated and costly, interventions that improve and prolong resistance to attacks are needed. Previous research supported digital training as a method to reduce phishing susceptibility. However, the efects of training degrade with time. Therefore, we investigate overlearning as an approach that may increase skill retention through repetition and developing automaticity. We performed a longitudinal experiment crossing overlearning with anti-phishing digital training (rule-based, mindfulness, and control). Participants were tested using email identification tests (immediately following and 10 weeks after training) and mock phishing messages delivered to their inboxes (1 week and 8 weeks following training). Results showed that compared to rule-based training, mindfulness training resulted in significantly greater retention in terms of better email discrimination and less susceptibility to phishing attacks but similar levels of caution towards phishing after 2 months. Overlearning resulted in significantly less susceptibility to phishing attacks and more caution towards phishing compared to no overlearning but did not impact the digital training approaches. Even so, mindfulness was more beneficial compared to overlearning. Altogether, the results demonstrate the stability of the benefits of mindfulness training over time in terms of mitigating phishing susceptibility without influencing the chances of missing legitimate emails.

KEYWORDS

Phishing; anti-phishing training; IT mindfulness; overlearning; mindfulness training; rule-based training

According to a 2018 report by the U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation, successful phishing attacks have resulted in an estimated 12.5 billion dollars in global losses since October 2013, a 136% increase in losses since May 2016 from scams across a wide range of sectors (Federal Bureau of Investigation, 2018). In addition to financial losses, phishing attacks may also result in loss of confidence, reputation, private information, and trade secrets. Phishers utilise various social and technical tactics to steal individuals’ personal data and financial credentials by mimicking legitimate message senders in email, instant messaging, and other mediums (APWG, 2017; Myers, 2007). To help combat these attacks, countermeasures protecting end users have been successfully deployed to assist organisations in defending themselves (Png & Wang, 2009). Countermeasures include using machine learning and pattern matching to prevent malicious messages from reaching users, providing better browsing interfaces and tools that warn users of suspicious websites, and training users on how to identify phishing emails and websites (Aleroud & Zhou, 2017; Hong, 2012; Kumaraguru, Rhee, Acquisti et al., 2007). Although the first two techniques ofer an important first line of defence, these technological mechanisms are not fool proof and can be overcome as phishers increase the sophistication of their attacks. If such attacks manage to bypass automated anti-phishing protections, it is ultimately up to individuals to decide how they will respond. Thus, educating users on the dangers of phishing attacks and how to avoid their negative consequences is essential.

Past research has shown that anti-phishing training can increase individuals’ capability to identify phishing emails and can reduce their susceptibility to phishing (Karumbaiah et al., 2016; Kumaraguru et al., 2010; Sheng et al., 2010). Anti-phishing training has primarily focused on educating individuals on specific cues associated with phishing emails through a rule-based approach, but researchers have also recently begun incorporating mindfulness into anti-phishing training to better protect individuals from falling for phishing attacks (Jensen et al., 2017). In contrast to rule-based training which relies on a fixed set of guidelines, mindfulness training teaches individuals to devote more careful attention to the context in which the messages are received (Baer et al., 2004; Brown et al., 2007; Vishwanath et al., 2011). Although meaningful reductions in phishing susceptibility have been demonstrated using both training approaches, it would be beneficial for future research to examine the lasting efects of anti-phishing training beyond retention intervals of one month or less (e.g., Alnajim & Munro, 2009; Kumaraguru et al., 2009), as some studies have shown diminishing retention shortly after training has occurred (e.g., Mayhorn & Nyeste, 2012).

Due to the severe consequences from successful phishing attacks, as well as the costs of implementing anti-phishing training, it is crucial for training to include interventions that prolong the retention of knowledge and skills. Even so, there has been a lack of research examining training strategies and best practices for improving skill retention in antiphishing training. In this vein, overlearning is a training strategy that is commonly thought to be efective for increasing skill retention across various tasks and contexts by providing practice opportunities for individuals that go beyond the point of initial learning (Driskell et al., 1992). Through continued practice and feedback immediately after initial mastery or successful performance of a skill, overlearning is thought to enhance skill retention by reinforcing learned behaviours and skills into long-term memory. In this manner, performance of these skills becomes a routinised process that can be easily recalled in the future, ultimately decreasing the likelihood that the skills will decay or be forgotten over time (Arthur Jr. et al., 1998). However, overlearning has not yet been examined within the context of phishing, and it is unclear how the combination of overlearning with diferent anti-phishing training methods will diferentially afect various outcomes related to phishing susceptibility. Because overlearning is intended to improve retention and enhance automaticity in performing a specific skill, it may be beneficial for some types of anti-phishing training methods through repeated reinforcement of skills, but it may also be counterproductive when paired with other methods by reducing the attentional resources required for efective email evaluation. Further, although overlearning may reduce individuals’ phishing susceptibility (e.g., increase individuals’ capability in distinguishing a phishing email from a legitimate email), it may come at the expense of making individuals overly cautious in opening legitimate emails for fear of phishing.

Therefore, we extend (Jensen et al.’s, 2017) initial research on the efectiveness of mindfulness training applied to anti-phishing training by conducting a longitudinal study to examine the diferential efects of overlearning with two anti-phishing training methods (i.e., rule-based training and mindfulness training) across a 2-month period. Similar to previous studies (e.g., Jensen et al., 2017; Wright et al., 2014), we utilised a student sample due to the relevance of phishing to these individuals and to maintain consistency across studies. We sent two waves of mock phishing emails to individuals’ actual email accounts to examine how the efects of overlearning and training diferentially afect phishing susceptibility in a real-world setting. Additionally, participants completed email identification tests immediately after training and again after 2 months, during which we measured discriminability (i.e., capability to distinguish between phishing and legitimate emails) and response bias (i.e., tendency to label emails as phishing). Considering these two additional metrics beyond what (Jensen, et al., 2017) examined allowed us to disentangle whether individuals not only learn how to recognise phishing correctly but also if they can do so in a balanced and appropriate manner. For the purposes of the present study, all study variables were measured and subsequently analysed at the individual level to gain a better understanding of how diferent types of training and levels of overlearning influence individual performance on email identification tests and mock phishing tests. The findings demonstrate how the efects of rule-based and mindfulness training decay over a longer retention interval than has been previously studied and that overlearning can be an efective training strategy to incorporate with anti-phishing training to reduce phishing susceptibility.

## 1. Combating phishing attacks

In general, three main strategies have been suggested to help users and organisations protect themselves from phishing attacks (Hong, 2012; Kumaraguru, Rhee, Acquisti et al., 2007). First, phishing attacks can be screened using automated filters and blacklists (Aleroud & Zhou, 2017). Second, phishing attacks can be prevented by providing users with more advanced warning and credentialing systems for their online activities (Abbasi et al., 2015; Petelka et al., 2019). These strategies are efective if the technology is errorfree and users accept the recommendations made by the warning systems. However, studies have shown that many anti-phishing tools have high false positive rates and that users engage with phishing websites, despite receiving warnings (Abbasi et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2006). Additionally, as anti-phishing technology continues to advance, phishing attacks are adapting to existing protections and are becoming more sophisticated so as to avoid automated detection (Hong, 2012).

As part of a layered approach to phishing defence, the third strategy involves training users about what phishing is, how to identify phishing messages, and what to do if they encounter them. Educating individuals about security, however, can be a dificult task due to individuals’ overconfidence in their ability to protect themselves against cyber threats, lack of motivation to learn about security, and tendencies to treat security as a secondary task (Evers, 2006; Kumaraguru, Rhee, Acquisti et al., 2007; Nielsen, 2004). Nevertheless, user security education remains an important component of combating against phishing attacks because “as technology increases and becomes more prevalent, the human factor remains the most viable target for wouldbe attackers” (Purkait, 2012, p. 402). Wright and Marett (2010) found that individuals who are lower on experiential factors such as computer self-eficacy, Web experience, and security knowledge are more susceptible to phishing attacks and conclude that “experience and training appear to be the most efective tools for guarding against phishing” (p. 289).

## 2. Rule-based training

In general, research has shown that anti-phishing training can be efective in reducing phishing susceptibility and helping individuals distinguish between legitimate and phishing messages and websites (e.g., Sheng et al., 2007), click on fewer links embedded in phishing emails (e.g., Karumbaiah et al., 2016), and reduce the likelihood of entering credentials into phishing websites (e.g., Sheng et al., 2010). Most of these existing anti-phishing training methods incorporate what has been termed a rule-based approach to training (Jensen et al., 2017). Rule-based training teaches individuals how to apply a set of specific guidelines when evaluating messages (e.g., not clicking on embedded email links from unknown senders) and to watch for specific cues often associated phishing messages (e.g., urgent requests for action; Downs et al., 2006). Rule-based training works under the assumption that phishing messages share similar characteristics that are relatively stable over time. However, as phishers produce messages that are increasingly sophisticated, the cues and rules established in the past may become obsolete. Additionally, by teaching individuals to rely upon simple rules and cues designed to be embedded into an email management routine, rule-based training may also promote the use of heuristic evaluation of incoming messages (System 1) that results in less scrutiny and greater susceptibility to phishing attacks (Vishwanath et al., 2011). For example, some compliance-gaining techniques used in phishing messages (e.g., liking, scarcity, social proof, reciprocity) are efective because they exploit users’ tendencies to process emails mindlessly and prompt individuals to quickly respond without carefully considering potential consequences of their actions (Goel et al., 2017; Wright et al., 2014).

## 3. Mindfulness training

As an alternative to rule-based training, individuals could be trained to evaluate emails through systematic processing (System 2), which involves the deliberate and conscious evaluation of information (Vishwanath et al., 2011). In this vein, Wright et al. (2014) recommended that future researchers should “investigate methods for encouraging System 2 evaluation, especially when processing requests for private information” (p. 396). Accordingly, anti-phishing training methods that incorporate elements promoting mindfulness may be particularly useful for reducing phishing susceptibility.

Mindfulness refers to the attention and awareness of one’s experience in the present and provides a better understanding of the current situation and possible future implications of one’s actions (Langer, 1989). Although known for its relevance in clinical psychol ogy with treating individuals with behavioural or emo tional disorders, the concepts and skills taught in mindfulness training have also been applied to other contexts (e.g., the workplace) to improve individuals physical and mental well-being, job-related perfor mance, and work relationships (Baer, 2003; Good et al., 2016; Grossman et al., 2004; Hülsheger et al., 2013). Specifically, research on mindfulness related to information technology (IT) use has become increas ingly prevalent, in which IT mindfulness refers to “a dynamic IT-specific trait . . . whereby the user focuses on the present, pays attention to detail, exhibits a willingness to consider other uses, and expresses genuine interest in investigating IT features and failures” (Thatcher et al., 2018, p. 833). Although research on IT mindfulness has primarily focused on issues related to innovation, user adoption, and continued use of technology (Sun et al., 2016; Swanson & Ramiller, 2004), researchers have also begun examining the role of mindfulness in relation to email man agement and its benefits for individual performance (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018). In this vein, (Jensen et al., 2017) applied concepts of mindfulness to anti phishing training to help individuals better allocat and direct their attention to the evaluation of messages through several steps. First, individuals are taught to pause when they receive a request to perform some action and attend to the context in which the request was issued. By taking a broader perspective and exam ining the overall purpose and consequences of the message, rather than the specific content, individuals can get a better understanding of how to approach the interaction and recognise requests that may appear unusual. Second, individuals are taught to fully engag and direct their attention to evaluating emails through active questioning. By consciously allocating attention to reading and understanding emails, individuals can be more cautious of suspicious requests, particularly under time constraints when heuristic information processing is likely to occur. Finally, individuals are taught to withhold any truth determinations of suspi cious emails until they have checked with a trusted third party and gathered suficient evidence on how to respond. In their preliminary investigation, (Jensen et al., 2017) compared the efectiveness of rule-based training and mindfulness training and found that 7.5% of individuals who received mindfulness training responded to a mock phishing attack, compared to 13.4% of individuals who received rule-based training (p = .04). Although additional research is needed to understand the role of mindfulness training in the context of phishing, these initial findings demonstrate that training individuals to be more mindful in their email evaluation may be an efective alternative to rule-based training for protecting individuals from phishing attacks.

## 4. Skill retention in anti-phishing training

Although previous studies have demonstrated that anti-phishing training, whether it is rule-based or mindfulness training, can be an efective countermeasure for reducing phishing susceptibility, it is unclear if the benefits of training are only short-term. Several studies that have examined how the knowledge and skills gained during anti-phishing training are retained over time (e.g., Alnajim & Munro, 2009; Kumaraguru et al., 2009; Mayhorn & Nyeste, 2012) have only examined retention intervals that are less than 1 month. Some evidence suggests that such training may be efective for 2 months (Dodge et al., 2012; Volkamer et al., 2018). However, these studies have only examined skill retention for rule-based training, and (Jensen et al.’s, 2017) preliminary research on mindfulness training also examined a short retention interval (i.e., 10 days after training).

Although the lack of research in this area makes it dificult to determine the extent to which antiphishing training decays over time, it is wellestablished that skill retention decreases as time increases (Arthur Jr. et al., 1998; Wang et al., 2013). Even so, the phishing literature has only begun investigating training strategies that would be most efective to incorporate into anti-phishing training. Since rule-based training and mindfulness training teach individuals diferent skills when addressing phishing emails, understanding what retention strategies most efectively complement each type of training (or both) is necessary for protecting individuals from phishing for extended periods of time. To this end, overlearning may be a particularly useful training strategy for improving skill retention that can be applied to anti-phishing training.

## 5. Overlearning

Overlearning is defined as “the deliberate overtraining of a task past a set criterion” (Driskell et al., 1992, p. 615). Overlearning is often operationalised such that once individuals complete a task (typically without any errors) or have demonstrated that they have successfully learned how to perform the skills taught during training, they will continue practicing those skills to complete the task again. While other approaches to learning may halt after initial mastery is demonstrated or are spread across future training sessions (e.g., distributed practice), overlearning involves immediate repetition and practice within the same training session. In this manner, overlearning increases retention by strengthening the bonds between stimulus and appropriate response, reducing cognitive demand by enhancing automaticity, and providing trainees with further practice and feedback on the correctness of responses (Arthur Jr. et al., 1998; Wang et al., 2013). Through repeated practice and feedback of one’s performance, even after achieving initial proficiency, overlearning decreases the likelihood of skill decay by reinforcing the information and skills learned into long-term memory, allowing these skills to become part of one’s routine that can be recalled more easily in the future (Rohrer & Taylor, 2006). Additionally, individuals who overlearn tasks may also be more resistant to stress-related efects (e.g., narrowed attention) during performance because their tasks become automated and require less active attentional capacity (Driskell & Johnston, 1998). Researchers have also noted the importance of overlearning when there are severe consequences from forgetting and incorrectly performing a task, particularly those that are infrequently practiced and used only in emergency or crisis situations (Rohrer & Taylor, 2006; Schendel & Hagman, 1982).

In general, research has shown that overlearning is an efective training technique for retention and that “the importance of continuing practice beyond the point in time where some . . . criterion is reached cannot be overemphasised” (Fitts, 1965, p. 195). Driskell et al.’s (1992) meta-analytic results indicated a moderate efect of overlearning on retention, with greater degrees of overlearning resulting in greater retention. The authors concluded that with even just 50% overlearning, individuals can expect improvements in retention. Although overlearning may require more resources through extended training beyond initial proficiency, this initial cost may be ofset by lower costs associated with subsequent retraining or refresher training (Schendel & Hagman, 1982).

Because of the relevance of email in most individuals’ lives (e.g., school, work, or personal) and the severe consequences associated with phishing, overlearning may be an efective training strategy to incorporate into anti-phishing training to increase retention. However, the inclusion of overlearning, as well as other skill retention strategies, in anti-phishing training has yet to be examined in the phishing literature. Thus, it is unclear how the benefits of antiphishing training may difer as a function of overlearning, particularly across diferent types of training (i.e., rule-based and mindfulness training). Additionally, despite the vast number of studies supporting overlearning as an efective training strategy for bolstering retention, research also shows that its benefits eventually fade with time and likely depend on the nature of the task and practice conditions (Driskell et al., 1992; Karpicke & Roedinger, 2007; Rohrer et al., 2005). Most studies of overlearning have examined intervals of 4 weeks or less (Driskell et al., 1992), and, with the exception of a few studies that have trained real-world or practical skills such as communication tactics or job-relevant skills (e.g., Lopez, 1980; Schendel & Hagman, 1982), the majority of overlearning studies have examined simple laboratory tasks such as verbal recall tasks (Arthur & Day, 2019). By examining overlearning in a new context such as phishing, we gain a better understanding of not only how to improve skill retention in antiphishing training but also whether overlearning is an efective strategy to increase skill retention in general.

## 6. Hypotheses

Using the framework provided by (Jensen et al.’s, 2017) initial study examining the efectiveness of mindfulness training and rule-based training on phishing susceptibility, the present study sought to extend the scope of their study and address its limitations in multiple ways. First, we included more rigorous measurement of individuals’ susceptibility to phishing such that multiple mock phishing emails were utilised (i.e., two waves of five mock phishing emails each), whereas (Jensen et al., 2017) included only one mock phishing email. Second, in addition to susceptibility to phishing, we included two additional dependent variables to compare individuals’ detection performance. We drew upon Signal Detection Theory (Green & Swets, 1966; Swets et al., 2000) to measure individuals’ ability to discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails, as well as their response bias towards labelling emails as either legitimate or phishing (i.e., caution towards phishing), on two email identification tests that were designed to match their training. Including measures of discriminability and response bias allowed us to examine how individuals responded to legitimate emails and the potential costs of incorrectly labelling them (e.g., false positives). Finally, we increased the length of the experimental window from (Jensenet al.’s, 2017) 10 days to 2 months. Examining this longer timeframe allowed us to gain greater insight regarding the efectiveness of anti-phishing training over time.

## 7. Rule-based training and mindfulness training versus no anti-phishing training

Overall, receiving either rule-based or mindfulness training should be more beneficial than not receiving anti-phishing training by exposing individuals to what phishing is and strategies for combatting phishing, as consistent with previous studies that have compared anti-phishing training to a no-training control condition (e.g., Sheng et al., 2010). Specifically, rule-based training should lead to better discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails and less susceptibility to phishing attacks by providing individuals with a list of rules and cues commonly associated with phishing emails to work through when they are evaluating email messages. Mindfulness training, on the other hand, should lead to better discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails and less susceptibility to phishing attacks by providing individuals with strategies for more conscious, thoughtful email evaluation. Additionally, both rule-based and mindfulness training should make individuals more cautious towards phishing compared to not receiving antiphishing training by heightening awareness of phishing and informing individuals of the consequences associated with phishing. Thus, the following two hypotheses were examined:

Hypothesis 1: Individuals who receive rule-based training, as compared to individuals who do not receive antiphishing training, will be

a) better at discriminating between legitimate and phishing emails,

b) more cautious towards phishing, and

c) less susceptible to phishing attacks in their everyday email use.

Hypothesis 2: Individuals who receive mindfulness training, as compared to individuals who do not receive anti-phishing training, will be

a) better at discriminating between legitimate and phishing emails,

b) more cautious towards phishing, and

c) less susceptible to phishing attacks in their everyday email use.

## 8. Rule-based training versus mindfulness training

Consistent with (Jensen et al.’s, 2017) findings, mindfulness training should result in less susceptibility to phishing attacks compared to rule-based training by promoting more systematic information processing when evaluating emails. By teaching individuals how to focus on the overall purpose and outcomes associated with emails and withhold quick truth judgements before allocating suficient attention and time for evaluation, mindfulness training should be more efective than rule-based training in protecting individuals from phishing attacks (Baer et al., 2004; Brown et al., 2007; Jensen et al., 2017). Whereas rule-based training may narrow attention on a limited number of specific cues that may not be salient or recognised by individuals, mindfulness training instead promotes concepts related to heightened awareness and attention that can be applied across emails and is less context dependent, which should also lead to better discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails and greater caution towards phishing compared to rule-based training. Because rule-based training may also repeat rules and concepts that individuals have already been taught, this may increase overconfidence in individuals’ ability to protect themselves, leaving individuals less likely to apply the training and be less cautious of phishing (Jensen et al., 2017). Thus, the following hypothesis was examined:

Hypothesis 3: Individuals who receive mindfulness training, as compared to individuals who receive rulebased training, will be

a) better at discriminating between legitimate and phishing emails,

b) more cautious towards phishing, and

c) less susceptible to phishing attacks in their everyday email use.

In relation to the two anti-phishing training methods being compared, retention of knowledge and skills gained during training should be greater for individuals who receive mindfulness training compared to rule-based training. Mindfulness training teaches individuals to focus their attention on their present situations and engage more deliberate, conscious email evaluation. By learning how to allocate attention to understanding the context and requests surrounding their emails, individuals should be able to more easily recall their past email experiences through greater depth of processing. In turn, greater depth of processing should aid in more appropriate decision making in their future email evaluations, ultimately resulting in better discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails, less phishing susceptibility, and greater caution towards phishing over an extended period of time (Craik & Lockhart, 1972; Vishwanath et al., 2011). In contrast, learning from rule-based training is more superficial and relies upon heuristic versus systematic information processing, individuals may become susceptible to phishing again over an extended period of time. Thus, the following hypothesis was examined:

Hypothesis 4: Individuals who receive mindfulness training, as compared to individuals who receive rulebased training, will have greater retention 2 months after training in terms of

a) having better discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails,

b) being more cautious towards phishing, and

c) being less susceptible to phishing in their everyday email use.

## 9. Overlearning versus no overlearning

Consistent with past empirical theorisation and empirical findings, we argue that overlearning should lead to greater discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails and less phishing susceptibility. Because overlearning takes individuals past the point of initial learning, individuals who are given additional practice and feedback during training should have a better understanding of phishing that will allow them to better identify phishing emails and be less likely to fall for phishing attacks. Additionally, increased exposure to phishing through overlearning should make individuals more cautious towards phishing. In other words, overeducating individuals about the consequences of phishing and providing additional examples of what phishing emails look like and how to identify them should make individuals more sensitive to these types of threats compared to individuals who do not receive additional exposure. Thus, the following hypothesis was examined:

Hypothesis 5: Individuals who receive overlearning during training, as compared to individuals who do not receive overlearning during training, will be

a) better at discriminating between legitimate and phishing emails,

b) more cautious towards phishing, and

c) less susceptible to phishing attacks in their everyday email use.

Similarly, retention of knowledge and skills during training should be greater for individuals who receive overlearning compared to those who do not receive overlearning. Repeated practice and feedback should further reinforce what is taught such that individuals can more easily draw upon the knowledge and skills to appropriately distinguish between legitimate and phishing emails and reduce their susceptibility to phishing attacks over longer periods of time. Additionally, individuals who receive repeated exposure to and examples of phishing emails through practice will likely be more cautious towards phishing for longer periods of time. Thus, the following hypothesis was examined:

Hypothesis 6: Individuals who receive overlearning during training, as compared to individuals who do not receive overlearning during training, will have greater retention 2 months after training in terms of

a) having better discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails,

b) being more cautious towards phishing, and

c) being less susceptible to phishing in their everyday email use.

## 10. Overlearning with rule-based training versus overlearning with mindfulness training

Although overlearning should be an efective skill retention strategy to incorporate into anti-phishing training, it may have diferential efects depending on the type of anti-phishing training to which it is applied. Rule-based training provides individuals with a list of predetermined guidelines to follow and cues to watch for that can help individuals recognise phishing emails more quickly and more efectively, which may be reinforced further when combined with additional practice through overlearning. Thus, overlearning in the context of rule-based training may help individuals grasp the rules and cues associated with phishing emails more quickly and lead to better discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails, less susceptibility to phishing attacks, and more caution towards phishing. However, overlearning may be counterproductive to mindfulness training. Because overlearning can increase automaticity in responses and routinise processes, this works against the purpose of mindfulness training, which is intended to help individuals be more deliberate in allocating attention when evaluating emails. In other words, the benefits of more active, conscious processing through mindfulness training may be negated by the reduction of active attentional capacity needed for efective email evaluation through overlearning. Thus, the following hypothesis was examined:

Hypothesis 7: Individuals who receive overlearning during rule-based training, as compared to individuals who receive overlearning during mindfulness training, will have greater retention 2 months after training in terms of

a) having better discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails,

b) being more cautious towards phishing, and

c) being less susceptible to phishing in their everyday email use.

## 11. Method

## 11.1. Participants

Students at a Southwestern U.S. university were recruited to participate in this study in exchange for course credit. We conducted this study on a university undergraduate student sample due to the relevance of phishing to this age group and their particular susceptibility to these attacks (Dodge et al., 2007). A total of

517 students participated in the study, but 64 were removed from data analyses due to incomplete study participation or not following instructions, thus yielding a final sample of 453 participants (55% male, 45% female). Participants ranged in age from 18 years to 42 years (M = 19.69, SD = 1.76). Three hundred and fifty (77.3%) participants reported their ethnicity as Caucasian, 30 (6.6%) as Asian, 21 (4.6%) as Native American, 19 (4.2%) as Hispanic/Latino, 15 (3.3%) as Black/African American, 4 (0.9%) as Multiple (i.e., two or more ethnicities), and 4 (0.9%) as Other. Ten (2.2%) participants did not disclose their ethnicity. Phishing also appeared to be relevant to this sample, as 192 (42.4%) participants knew of someone who has fallen for a phishing attack, and 28 (6.2%) participants indicated that they have personally fallen for a phishing attack themselves.

## 12. General procedures

Figure 1 displays the general study procedures. Participants first attended an in-person training session administered on the computer through the online survey platform Qualtrics. After agreeing to participate in the study, participants were introduced to the 1-hour training session and told that the purpose of this study was to test new cybersecurity training that helps individuals distinguish legitimate emails from phishing emails. Participants first completed a series of Likert-scale measures of the covariates related to their email, web, and past phishing experiences. Participants then received one of three trainings (rulebased, mindfulness, or control [i.e., password creation and management] training). Afterwards, participants received one of two email identification practice sessions (i.e., no overlearning or 100% overlearning) where they read a series of email messages and identified whether or not each message was a phishing message. Practice sessions difered in terms of how many email messages participants received during practice (i.e., six emails for the no overlearning condition and 12 emails for the 100% overlearning condition). The number of practice emails used for each condition was determined through pilot testing and previous research.

After completing the practice session and a filler task (i.e., Big Five personality measure), participants completed the first email identification test. Participants then completed basic demographic questions and were debriefed about the training they received. Finally, participants were reminded that they would be sent mock phishing emails throughout the semester to test the efectiveness of the training and would be invited to take a follow-up online survey towards the end of the semester to receive additional course credit for their participation.

## 13. Training conditions

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three training conditions: rule-based training, mindfulness training, or a control training condition. Training content was adapted from materials by (Jensen et al., 2017) and delivered through a series of webpages that included text and graphics in Qualtrics. Before receiving information specific to their training conditions, participants in the rule-based and mindfulness training conditions received the same introductory content that provided background information on phishing (see Appendix A). Participants in the control condition did not receive any information related to phishing and were instead provided with training materials for secure password creation and management (see Appendix B).

The rule-based training content consisted of a list of six recommendations (see Appendix C) derived from guidelines from various anti-phishing resources in the academic, governmental, non-profit, and corporate sectors. This list was previously presented to IT security managers at the university to ensure that the material was relevant and useful for reducing phishing susceptibility. The mindfulness training content consisted of materials that were based on previous clinical research on mindfulness and adapted to an antiphishing context (see Appendix D). This training approach focused on three key steps: 1) stop (e.g., pause before taking any immediate actions such as clicking a link or replying to an email); 2) think (e.g., reflect upon the actions being requested, the overall context in which the requests were received, and the underlying motive of the sender’s request); and 3) check (e.g., seek help from a third-party source if the email’s legitimacy is unclear). Participants who received either rule-based or mindfulness training were also shown examples of legitimate and phishing emails, as well as explanations consistent with the type of training they received.

## 14. Overlearning conditions

For the email identification practice session, participants were randomly assigned to one of two overlearning conditions: no overlearning or 100% overlearning. Although the optimal amount of overlearning required is diferent depending on the type of task, there is support suggesting that a minimum of 50% overlearning is beneficial and that increases from 100% to 150% do not result in greater retention (Craig et al., 1972; Schendel & Hagman, 1982). Therefore, the present study used 100% overlearning. Further, guidance from previous research using phishing identification tasks and pilot testing was used to determine to the appropriate amount of practice that would allow individuals to reach an initial level of learning (see Supplemental Analyses for comparisons between those who did and did not reach initial proficiency during practice).

Participants in both conditions were first provided with the same six emails (three legitimate and three phishing emails). Participants in the no overlearning condition were finished with their practice session after working through these six email messages, whereas participants in the 100% overlearning condition were provided with additional practice through an additional six emails (three legitimate and three phishing emails). All emails were displayed in a randomised order. Feedback regarding correct/ incorrect answers was provided after each email message during the email identification practice session, and explanations for correct answers were consistent with the type of training participants received, with the exception of those in control condition who did not receive explanations.

## 15. Learning measures

All emails included in the present study were developed or derived from previous studies. Pilot testing was conducted to ensure equal dificulty across all emails included throughout the study. Emails were designed to be applicable and relevant to current university students and were based on actual legitimate and phishing emails (Myers, 2007).

Discriminability and response bias on email identification tests. The two email identification tests each included 10 emails (five phishing and five legitimate) that appeared in a randomised order (see Appendix E for the emails included in both email identification tests). Legitimate emails contained links that redirected participants to legitimate websites (https://www.netflix.com), whereas phishing emails contained links to bogus web domains that we purchased and controlled (e.g., nef1ix.com) that then redirected participants to legitimate websites. The two test versions were counterbalanced across the two administration times.

Using estimates of hit rates (i.e., correctly identifying a phishing email as phishing) and false positive rates (i.e., incorrectly identifying a legitimate email as phishing; Macmillan & Creelman, 1990), signal detection techniques were used to calculate individuals discrimination between legitimate and phishing emails and bias towards phishing on the email identification tests: discriminability/sensitivity (or d’) and response bias/criterion (or c), respectively (Green & Swets, 1966; Swets et al., 2000). Discriminability refers to an individual’s capability to tell whether an email is phishing or legitimate such that larger values of discriminability indicate that users have greater email discrimination and are able to better distinguish between legitimate and phishing emails. On the other hand, response bias refers to an individual’s tendency to treat an email as phishing such that negative values of response bias indicate that users are erring on the side of caution (i.e., more likely to label an email as being phishing) and positive values of response bias indicate that users are especially susceptible to phishing (i.e., less likely to label an email as being phishing). It is important to note that response bias is separate from discriminability such that two users may have the same value of discriminability (i.e., they have the same capability in discriminating between legitimate and phishing emails) but may be more or less biased in their responses or the criterion they set for labelling an email as phishing. Following guidance from previous researchers (e.g., Canfield et al., 2016; Hautus, 1995), discriminability and response bias were calculated with the following formulas:

Discriminability (d’) = z(Hits) – z(False Positives) Response Bias (c) = −0.5[z(Hits) + z(False Positives)]

Phishing susceptibility on mock phishing tests. The two mock phishing tests included five mock phishing emails each (see Appendix F for the emails included in both mock phishing tests), which were sent to participants across a 2-week period to reduce suspicion from receiving too many phishing messages within a short time period. The university’s IT department provided access to Wombat’s ThreatSim® Phishing Simulations<sup>1</sup> to generate, distribute, and track the mock phishing emails sent throughout the 2-month period. For consistency, embedded links within the mock phishing emails redirected participants to error pages. The two test versions were counterbalanced across the two administration times. Within each mock phishing test, the email distribution order, dates, and times were also randomised to minimise the likelihood of participant interactions. Emails were designed to come from university sources that participants would recognise (e.g., the university’s IT department or administration) and either pertained to account-related issues or on-campus events. Similar to past phishing research, susceptibility was calculated based on the number of clicks on links from the mock phishing emails (Goel et al., 2017; Wright et al., 2014). The number of clicks were then recoded to produce mock phishing test scores such that higher test scores reflect better performance (i.e., reverse scored to reflect less phishing susceptibility).

To protect participants, we first received approval from the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) and followed all procedures necessary to ensure participants’ privacy throughout the course of the study. Mock phishing emails used in the study were provided to the university’s IT department to ensure that the emails were not blocked from the university’s email servers. Actions were also not taken on any participant inquiries about the mock phishing emails to minimise information regarding the nature of the emails from being revealed. Mock phishing emails were only sent during typical work hours (i.e., Monday to Friday, 9:00am to 5:00pm).

## 16. Covariates

Appendix G lists the covariates included in the study. These variables were included based on previous research by Wright and Marett (2010) due to their relationship to phishing susceptibility and training eficacy. These covariates included: disposition to trust (McKnight et al., 2002), mindfulness in technology (Thatcher et al., 2018), perceived Internet risk (Malhotra et al., 2004); computer self-eficacy (Compeau & Higgins, 1995), phishing identification expertise (Jensen et al., 2017), email experience (Jensen et al., 2017), pre-training motivation (Noe & Schmitt, 1986), and the Big Five Personality traits (i.e., extraversion, conscientiousness, openness to experience, emotional stability, agreeableness; Goldberg, 1992).

## 23. Supplemental analyses

Overlearning was operationalised in this study through a duration-based approach (versus a criterion-based approach) to ensure that all participants in the same learning condition received an equal amount of learning trials or practice. Pilot testing is recommended when utilising a duration-based approach to establish an appropriate amount of learning trials that should be provided, and, as such, the learning criterion was set to correctly identifying four of the six practice emails based on previous performance on an email identification task from (Jensen et al., 2017). Following similar procedures utilised by previous researchers (e.g., Rohrer & Taylor, 2006; Rohrer et al., 2005), if participants did not correctly identify four email messages, it was assumed that they did not reach an initial level of learning. Because of this potential confound, supplemental analyses were conducted to compare results of the overall sample (N = 453) with a sample excluding participants who did not reach the required level of learning from practice (N = 300; see Appendix J for the frequency of practice and overlearning test scores among training conditions). With the exception of non-significant main efects of overlearning on the mock phishing test scores and on c for the email identification tests (ps > .05), the pattern of efects for the “true” learner sample on the remaining tests were of the same or similar magnitudes and reached the same or similar levels of significance compared to the overall sample (see Appendix K).

## 24. Discussion

The present study extends the work of (Jensen et al., 2017) by comparing the efectiveness of rule-based and mindfulness training for a longer retention interval (i.e., 10 days versus 2 months) and on two tests of skill retention of phishing susceptibility (i.e., email identification and mock phishing tests). The present results support their initial findings by showing beneficial efects of mindfulness training and limited support for the use of overlearning as a method to extend skill retention. These results ofer several contributions to theory and practice, which are enumerated below.

## 25. Theoretical implications

First, individuals who received mindfulness training were much better at discriminating between legitimate and phishing messages and transferring the knowledge and skills they gained from training to their everyday email usage (indicated by significantly lower click rates on the mock phishing email links) compared to those who received rule-based training or the control training. Although the discrimination efect of mindfulness training did decay across the 2-month period, the overall efects of mindfulness training were far superior and resulted in better email discrimination and less susceptibility to phishing attacks compared to rule-based and the control trainings. These findings also indicate that mindfulness training can reduce susceptibility to phishing attacks and, importantly, not at the expense of higher false positive rates (i.e., missing legitimate emails by incorrectly labelling them as phishing). In other words, because mindfulness training also resulted in better email discrimination, individuals who received mindfulness training can protect themselves from phishing attacks without likely disrupting their work productivity by being able to recognise and respond to legitimate emails appropriately. The results also showed that individuals who received mindfulness training were significantly more cautious towards phishing on the email identification test immediately after training and maintained this level of caution throughout the 2-month period, indicated by similar negative values of response bias at both Time 1 and Time 2. Individuals who received rule-based training or the control training also became significantly more cautious towards phishing between Time 1 and Time 2, but the changes in response bias were much more dramatic and appeared to be an overcompensation by participants as they encountered two waves of mock phishing attacks. Although Canfield and Fischhof (2018) found that phishing susceptibility was more closely related to response bias versus discriminability and called for interventions that specifically focus on response bias, our results show weak relationships among these variables (see Appendix H). Thus, it may be more beneficial to focus eforts on providing training that helps individuals not just become more cautious of phishing but to do so in a balanced manner. Through multiple forms of evaluation, the mindfulness approach to anti-phishing training proved superior to rule-based and control approaches and appears to help individuals engage in more deliberate, conscious evaluation of information when processing emails.

Second, although rule-based training resulted in greater email discrimination on the email identification test immediately after training, this finding is tempered by the overall results that showed how indi viduals who received rule-based training did not sig nificantly difer from the control training in terms of discrimination and caution towards phishing on the email identification tests or susceptibility to mock phishing attacks. These findings were also consistent in terms of retention, as discriminability and response bias on the email identification test, as well as scores on the mock phishing test, did not difer at Time 2 between the rule-based and control training condi tions. Although previous researchers (e.g., Sheng et al., 2010) found that receiving anti-phishing training resulted in better identification of phishing emails compared to receiving no anti-phishing training, (Jensen et al., 2017) argued that receiving repeated rule-based training may not yield increasing benefits and could result in desensitisation. Receiving rule based training may not contribute much to one’s existing knowledge of rules and cues associated with phishing emails and may even lead to individuals taking the training less seriously and feeling overconfident in their capability to discriminate legitimate and phishing emails (Jensen et al., 2017). It is important to note, however, that the operationalisation of the no anti-phishing training (control) condition in the present study difered from previous studies. Although participants in the control condition did not receive anti-phishing training, they did have the opportunity to practice identifying phishing emails, as well as receive feedback on incorrect and correct answers. From the present findings, it is possible that having participants work through unguided practice with generic feedback, even when they are not instructed about what phishing is and strategies for identifying phishing emails, may yield similar results as providing anti-phishing training that focuses on specific rules, guidelines, and cues to recognise and remember. In this vein, Scott et al.’s (2004) meta-analytic results also support the notion that self-paced exercises can enhance creativity. Thus, it is possible that individuals in the control training condition were able to build upon their problem-solving process, particularly when they received feedback with their practice.

Third, the present study also examined overlearning as a potential training strategy to improve skill retention. The results showed that overlearning does not seem to yield any significant benefits in terms of helping individuals better discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails. In other words, receiving additional practice during training did not result in better email discrimination and also did not result in significantly better retention in email discrimination across the 2-month period. Although these results suggest that overlearning on an email identification task may not be needed, receiving additional practice may still be valuable in terms of increasing individuals caution towards phishing. Individuals who received 100% overlearning were much more cautious of phishing during the email identification tests compared to those who did not receive overlearning. This was also consistent with the finding that individuals who received 100% overlearning scored better on the mock phishing tests (i.e., less susceptible to phishing). Although overlearning was useful such that it increased caution towards phishing and protected individuals from falling for phishing attacks, being increasingly cautious of phishing in and of itself has its own limitations. If overlearning only makes individuals more cautious of phishing but does not actually help them learn how to discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails, individuals could simply label most emails they receive as phishing. Similar limitations have been previously demonstrated in which system users attempted to identify deception (Biros et al., 2002). Although false positives can be more favourable than false negatives or misses due to the severe consequences associated with falling for phishing attacks, being overly cautious can disrupt individuals’ work productivity if important emails are ignored.

Fourth, the results show that the efects of training do not difer as a function of overlearning. We hypothesised that if the intent of mindfulness training is to better protect individuals against phishing attacks, overlearning may be counterproductive by leading to more automatic responses that require less attentional capacity (Arthur Jr. et al., 1998; Wang et al., 2013). Because a level of generalisability is required for email evaluation due to the variability in the types of emails received, incorporating overlearning as a retention strategy into mindfulness training may not be beneficial (or even logical) because the two strategies have competing intentions. Although the efects of training were much more robust compared to the efects of overlearning, particularly in improving email discriminability and increasing caution towards phishing, our results show that providing additional practice through overlearning did not negatively impact the efects of rule-based or mindfulness training and can still be a beneficial training strategy when applied to anti-phishing training in terms of reducing susceptibility to phishing attacks.

Fifth, our results provide insight to our understanding of IT mindfulness. Although mindfulness has been studied by researchers across a wide variety of domains, its applications in IT are more recent and becoming increasingly important (Thatcher et al., 2018). Even so, our understanding of IT mindfulness, particularly within the context of IT security and training, is limited and much needed due to its possible implications for preventing the severe consequences of phishing attacks. Our study extends initial work done by (Jensen et al., 2017) by not only finding additional support for mindfulness training as a novel and efective approach for reducing phishing susceptibility but also demonstrating the benefits of mindfulness training over time. Our results advance this area of research by providing compelling evidence that individuals’ IT mindfulness related to phishing can be improved through training in an easy, costeficient manner yet still have a lasting efect in reducing individuals’ phishing susceptibility. The majority of studies on anti-phishing training have focused on teaching individuals the common cues associated with phishing, ones which are likely already known. Our results have implications for researchers to consider alternative anti-phishing training methods such as mindfulness training that can help individuals to carefully and deliberately evaluate incoming emails before taking action.

Sixth, the present study has implications for antiphishing research by clearly demonstrating how training decays over time, as well as training strategies to improve skill retention. In our study, we incorporate overlearning with anti-phishing training methods to facilitate greater skill retention related to phishing susceptibility. In this manner, our study also extends overlearning research by addressing methodological issues in previous overlearning studies related to the operationalisation of the overlearning task (e.g., laboratory verbal recall tasks) and short retention intervals (e.g., less than 1 month) that have made it dificult to determine the benefits of overlearning across longer periods of time. In general, there have been a lack of studies that have manipulated overlearning in a real-world setting, in which, as stated by Driskell et al. (1992), “motivation certainly plays a role in training efectiveness . . . subjects will be more motivated to learn in studies that use relevant realworld tasks . . . than in studies that use laboratory tasks” (p. 621). By examining overlearning through a real-world task such as phishing detection across a 2-month period and incorporating tests both in and outside of the laboratory setting, our study opens new avenues for overlearning research by not only examining overlearning in a new context such as anti-phishing training but also comparing the efectiveness of overlearning on diferent training methods within the same study, which, to our knowledge, has not been done before. Along these lines, Rohrer and Taylor (2006) noted that the majority of tasks used in previous overlearning studies have merely required memorisation to learn and may not generalise across more abstract tasks. Our results show that overlearning can be applied and be beneficial to both types of tasks, as rule-based training relies more upon the application of memorised rules and cues during email evaluation, whereas mindfulness training relies upon the application of strategies that must be applied across various email evaluation situations.

Finally, the present study highlights the importance of examining phishing susceptibility and evaluating anti-phishing training efectiveness in diferent contexts. In general, when left to their own accord, individuals are much more susceptible to phishing when they are in their everyday environments. As seen in Appendix H, the relationships between individuals email discrimination and mock phishing test scores were minimal, suggesting that individuals’ capability in detecting phishing in a more controlled setting (e.g., an explicit email identification test) does not necessarily relate to their capability in detecting phishing in a real-world setting. In other words, just because individuals can discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails does not mean that they will do so when they are managing their emails in their everyday environments. These results are consistent with other research (e.g., Goel et al., 2017; Wright et al., 2014) suggesting that targets of phishing messages often miss them due to heuristic email processing or performing message evaluation as an ancillary task. The results have implications for future researchers to include multiple performance measures (i.e., maximal versus typical) in diferent settings (i.e., laboratory versus real-world) in their studies to get a clearer picture of how individuals transfer the knowledge and skills they gain from training to their everyday habits.

## 26. Practical implications

As phishing continues to be an increasingly common issue in society, it seems imperative for organisation leaders to implement anti-phishing training to protect their employees and their organisation as a whole from these devastating attacks. It is known that individuals are often overconfident in their capability to protect themselves against security-related attacks, are not motivated to learn about security, and treat security as a secondary task (Evers, 2006; Kumaraguru, Rhee, Acquisti et al., 2007; Nielsen, 2004). This can burden organisations to educate their employees about the dangers of phishing, especially because the organisations themselves are at risk if their employees fall for a phishing attack that compromises an organisational system.

The results from this study show that anti-phishing training does not require extensive resources to be an efective mechanism in terms of helping individuals learn how to discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails and become less susceptible to phishing attacks. Rather than distributing security information for individuals to read on their own (which has been shown to be inefective; Kumaraguru, Rhee, Hasan et al., 2007), it seems that teaching individuals about phishing and specific strategies to utilise when evaluating emails (e.g., mindfulness techniques) and then providing opportunities for them to practice the skills learned and receive feedback can help individuals become less susceptible to phishing attacks. Because the training was developed with simple slides containing text and graphics, organisations and academic institutions can easily implement similar antiphishing training programmes and reach a large number of employees and students quickly and eficiently.

It is important to note, however, that providing anti-phishing training will not eliminate all instances of individuals being susceptible to phishing attacks. Despite receiving either rule-based or mindfulness training, 64% of participants still fell for at least one mock phishing email. Similarly, Table 3 further demonstrates that those participants only had a 75% hit rate across the two email identification tests. Although clicking on a link in a phishing message does not guarantee data loss, many experiment participants were still susceptible to phishing attacks. Additionally, it can be argued that falling for even one phishing email is significant, as it only takes one phishing email to result in a severe business compromise. Therefore, training is best cast as part of layered approach to defend against phishing attacks. As mentioned previously, training is only one strategy for combating phishing attacks and should be used in conjunction with better automated tools that filter and warn individuals of suspicious messages to maximise protection against phishing attacks (Hong, 2012).

Although the results show the beneficial efects of incorporating overlearning during training to make individuals more cautious of phishing and, in turn, less likely to click on phishing emails, it should be cautioned that individuals must also be trained to discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails to not reduce work productivity. The results imply that rule-based training may be complemented with training that assists individuals to learn how to better discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails (e.g., mindfulness training).

## 27. Limitations and future research

There are several limitations that must be considered when interpreting and applying the results of the present study. First, although participants were not informed of the true nature of the mock phishing emails they received (i.e., they were led to an error page if they clicked on the embedded link), it is possible that participants noticed a pattern during the two rounds of mock phishing attacks. As mentioned previously, individuals who received rule-based or the control training became particularly cautious towards phishing between Time 1 and Time 2, whereas individuals who received mindfulness training maintained their level of caution over time. Because the second email identification test was taken after the second round of mock phishing attacks concluded, participants may have remained cautious towards phishing from being exposed to the mock phishing emails and erred on the side of caution when taking the second email identification test. Future research should investigate other potential factors influencing this change in caution towards phishing over time and why mindfulness training may be less susceptible to these efects.

Second, to avoid practice efects and inducing increased levels of suspicion in participants regarding mock phishing emails, tests were only administered at two time points (i.e., 1 week and 8 weeks after training for the mock phishing tests; immediately after and 10 weeks after training for the email identification tests). Because the study design did not allow for pretests to be conducted on the email identification tests and mock phishing tests (i.e., participants had to consent to participating in the study before any testing could be conducted), a large number of covariates (e.g., prior phishing identification experience) were included in the study. However, conducting pre-tests would have provided greater insight regarding whether there were actual changes in test scores after training and how those changes may stabilise to the baseline scores over time. Future research should consider extending the length of the experimental window and including additional time points to get a clearer view of what linear/non-linear relationships exist and optimal time points for training interventions (e.g., refresher courses).

Third, although (Jensen et al., 2017) noted how mindfulness training could be supplemental to rule-based training, this study did not manipulate a combined rulebased and mindfulness training condition. Because rulebased training is widely used, it is not surprising that the efects of rule-based training were similar to those of the control training. Thus, future research should explore a combined rule-based and mindfulness training to determine whether learning rules and cues associated with phishing emails, as well as mindfulness techniques to apply when evaluating emails, results in even greater protection against phishing attacks.

Fourth, the amount of overlearning that was manipulated in this study (i.e., 100% overlearning) may not be the optimal amount for this type of task. Because no prior studies have examined overlearning within the context of anti-phishing training, the amount of overlearning used in the present study was determined based on previous research on overlearning for other types of tasks. Due to the small efects of overlearning found in the present study, it is possible that 100% overlearning was not a suficient amount needed to help individuals better discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails. Thus, future research should compare the efectiveness of other amounts of overlearning (e.g., 150% and 200% overlearning) to determine if there is an optimal amount needed to help individuals become less susceptible to phishing attacks and retain their knowledge and skills gained from training. Similarly, although previous research guided our decision to manipulate overlearning through a duration-based approach, it may be beneficial for future research to examine alternative methods for operationalising overlearning – via a criterion-based approach in particular – within an anti-phishing training context that may be more efective. In this vein, the degree of overlearning needed likely varies across individuals, and using a criterionbased approach would be a worthwhile avenue for future research that compares rule-based versus mindfulness training approaches.

Fifth, despite not receiving anti-phishing training, participants in the control training condition still received practice and feedback for correct and incorrect answers to accommodate our overlearning condition. Thus, these participants may have still learned how to identify phishing emails like participants in the rulebased and mindfulness training conditions, as indicated by the significant increase in caution towards phishing and decrease in phishing susceptibility between Times 1 and 2. Even so, it is possible that these learning efects will decrease over time and eventually taper of. The decaying efects of rule-based and mindfulness training may also reach similar levels as the control training over time and ultimately follow a similar trajectory. Although the decision of utilising ANCOVAs was appropriate for making mean comparisons between our two time points in the present study, future researchers should explore other statistical methods for analysing longitudinal data, particularly if the inclusion of additional time points reveals potential nonlinear relationships. Because the efect of training may be also underestimated due to these learning efects, future research studies that include a “true” control condition without training, practice, or feedback would likely show larger efects from training when comparing training conditions.

Finally, this study only examined phishing attacks that requested individuals to click on embedded links within the mock phishing emails. Although embedded malicious links are among the most commonly used type of phishing method, phishing attacks extend far beyond these links and can include other requests such as downloading attachments, entering personal credentials onto fictitious websites, or responding to emails with sensitive information. Although the antiphishing training provided in this study also taught individuals to be aware of attack methods beyond links, future research should examine the efectiveness of anti-phishing training and overlearning in terms of becoming less susceptible to other types of phishing attacks and phishing influence tactics.

## 28. Conclusion

In summary, the results of the present study suggest that compared to anti-phishing training emphasising common rules and cues associated with phishing, antiphishing training incorporating mindfulness techniques is more beneficial by helping individuals better discriminate between legitimate and phishing emails and become less susceptible to phishing attacks but not in terms of making individuals more cautious towards phishing. These findings were also consistent after the 2-month period in terms of retention. Additionally, the beneficial efects of mindfulness training do not difer as a function of overlearning and were much more influential compared to the independent efects of overlearning, which only resulted in increased caution towards phishing and decreased susceptibility to phishing attacks. Thus, rather than focusing on only including additional practice during anti-phishing training, attention should instead be directed towards teaching individuals how to withhold truth judgements or action on email messages, critically evaluate and consider requests being made, and check with third-party sources to verify messages.
