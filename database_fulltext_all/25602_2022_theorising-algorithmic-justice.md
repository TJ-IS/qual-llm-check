# Theorising Algorithmic Justice

Olivera Marjanovic <sup>a</sup>, Dubravka Cecez-Kecmanovic<sup>b</sup> and Richard Vidgen<sup>b,c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>University of Technology Sydney Faculty of Engineering and Information Technology, School of Professional Practice & Leadership, Sydney, Australia; <sup>b</sup>University of New South Wales Business School, Sydney, Australia; <sup>c</sup>University of Hull, School of Management, UK

## ABSTRACT

The mounting evidence of unintended harmful social consequences of automated algorithmic decision-making (AADM), powered by AI and big data, in transformative services (e.g., welfare services), is startling. The algorithmic harm experienced by individuals, communities and society-at-large involves new injustice claims and disputes that go beyond issues of social justice. Drawing from the theory of “abnormal justice” in this paper we articulate a new theory of algorithmic justice that addresses the questions: WHAT is the matter of algorithmic justice? WHO counts as a subject of algorithmic justice? HOW are algorithmic justices performed? and How to address and resolve disputes about the WHAT, WHO and HOW of algorithmic justice? We illustrate the theory of algorithmic justice by drawing from a case of AADM in social welfare services, widely adopted by governments around the world. Our research points to datafication, technological inscribing and the systemic nature of injustices as important IS-specific aspects of algorithmic justice. Our main practical contribution comes from the articulation of algorithmic justice as a framework that (1) makes visible the injustices related to the “what”, “who”, and “how” of AADM in transformative services, and (2) provides further insights into how we might address and resolve these algorithmic injustices.

KEYWORDS Harmful efects of automated algorithmic decision-making; theory of algorithmic justice; datafication; technological inscribing; systemic algorithmic injustices

## 1. Introduction

Government agencies and other providers of the socalled transformative services<sup>1</sup> are now adopting automated algorithmic decision-making (AADM), with or without human oversight, at a growing rate and with unprecedented enthusiasm. Indeed, often driven by the promise of improved eficiency, perceived superiority of algorithms over human decision-making, cost cutting and technological innovation (Alston, 2019a; Alston, 2019b; Caplan et al., 2018), algorithms, especially those powered by big data and AI, are making their way into transformative services, as Eubanks (2018) observes, “at a breath-taking pace”. AADM is now routinely used to make important decisions about virtually all aspects of peoples’ economic and private lives. Examples include decisions about who should be hired, promoted, or fired, who should be given access to insurance and benefits, obtain credit, or get access to social services (Caplan et al., 2018). At the same time, new evidence is emerging about the harmful and unintended efects of these algorithms, on individuals, communities and wider society (O’Neil, 2016a; O'Neil 2016b; Eubanks, 2018; Caplan et al., 2018; Noble, 2018; Alston, 2019a; Alston, 2019b; Benjamin, 2019; Crawford, 2019; Crawford & Paglen, 2019; Gebru, 2019); Zalnieriute et al., 2019; UN, 2020).

The resulting harmful efects are often hidden behind more powerful stories of objectivity, neutrality, innovation and technological progress or superiority of algorithms over human decision makers (Alston, 2019a; Keddell, 2019; Markus, 2017; Zavrsnik, 2019, 2020). Even when recognised, these harmful efects are often considered as inevitable “dual efects” – that is, the “transitional negative side efects” that accompany any new technology (Markus, 2015, p. 58). Not limited to single algorithms, these efects are further propagated, combined and amplified in unknowable ways through systems of algorithms (Pasquale, 2015) and associated society-wide datafication processes (Galliers et al., 2017; Marjanovic & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2017).

Our research focuses on the harmful societal efects of AADM powered by AI and big data in the context of transformative services. In particular, we draw attention to social injustices underlying unfair, discriminatory, unjustified and other harmful, yet unintended efects of AADM in healthcare, welfare and disability services, public housing, policing and criminal justice, and employment – see, for example, O’Neil (2016a), Eubanks (2018), and Caplan et al. (2018), Alston, 2019a, 2019b), Park and Humphry (2019), Benjamin (2019), Ballantyne (2019), and Gangadharan and Niklas (2019). Importantly, a growing number of researchers are arguing that these efects of AI and AADM are matters of social justice and human rights (Dencik et al., 2019a, 2019b; Gillingham, 2019; Hofmann, 2019; Keddell, 2019;

Mann, 2020; Marda, 2019; Sloan & Warner, 2020; Zavrsnik, 2020).

There is little doubt that the injustices in the digital age, and especially those instigated by the deployment of AADM, are historically specific. Therefore, the contemporary injustice claims and disputes resulting from AADM are not only about whether some efects of AADM are (un)just, but also and importantly, are about fundamental questions of what counts as (un) just. These questions belong to a broader problematic of justice in abnormal times, which the social justice philosopher Nancy Fraser (2008, 2009, 2010) addresses through her theory of “abnormal justice”. Building upon a thorough critique of the mainstream theories of social justice Fraser (2008, 2010) demonstrates the need to expand our collective understanding of justice, beyond issues of equal access to, and equal distribution of justice. As argued by a number of social justice scholars, such as Hofman (2017) and Cinnamon (2017), the “distributive logic” of social justice, which is at the core of social justice theories, reaches its limit when it comes up against the phenomenon of new Information Technologies (IT), including social justice issues created by data harm. For example, while the concepts of equal distribution of justice (i.e., equal justice for all) and human rights (i.e., equal human rights) are at the core of social justice, the concept of equally distributed injustices (e.g., harm or discrimination) does not make sense. By observing justice in distributive terms, Cinnamon (2017) argues, we run a risk of missing questions about harmful efects of data that are “ontologically diferent from questions of distribution across the social spectrum” (p. 612). In response, a small but growing number of researchers in the emerging field of data justice are focusing on the harmful efects of data (see Cinnamon, 2017; Dencik et al., 2019a, 2019b, 2018; Masiero & Das, 2019). The high relevance of these studies motivated us to extend our collective attention from data to algorithms, in particular AADM and its social efects.

Therefore, in this paper, we frame the unintended harmful efects of AADM in the context of transformative services as issues of social justice and seek to propose a theoretical foundation for addressing them. Further, building upon Fraser’s (2008, 2010, 2009) theory of abnormal justice, we articulate a new theorising of what we call here ‘algorithmic justice’<sup>2</sup>. We reinterpret and extend Fraser’s theory in the context of the deployment of AADM in transformative services and propose a theory of algorithmic justice that addresses the key questions: WHAT is the matter of algorithmic justice? WHO counts as a subject of algorithmic justice? HOW are algorithmic justices performed? And, further, how might we address and resolve disputes about the WHAT, WHO and HOW of algorithmic justice? To illustrate the meaning and relevance of the theory of algorithmic justice, we focus on social welfare, a particular type of transformative service. These services are increasingly supported by the emerging category of information systems (IS), called digital welfare systems (DWS). Now widely adopted by government agencies across the world, DWS use AADM based on AI, big data, machine learning, and other advanced technologies to support and increasingly replace human decision-making (Keddell, 2019; Sun & Gerchick, 2019; Zarsky, 2016; Zavrsnik, 2019, 2020). Based on the case of DWS we demonstrate how the proposed theory of algorithmic justice contributes to recognising, debating, diagnosing and addressing harmful efects of AADM in transformative services.

Our paper makes several contributions to IS research and to practice. First, we observe and conceptualise the harmful efects of AADM as issues of social justice, which is of particular importance to the IS discipline. By re-interpreting and extending Fraser’s theory of abnormal justice, we highlight the IS-specific systemic nature of algorithmic injustices, including issues of datafication and technological inscribing. This particular way of seeing the harmful societal efects of algorithms powered by AI and big data, is yet-to-be explored in IS research. Second, we propose a theory of algorithmic justice informed by the broader literature and inspired by the growing evidence of harmful and unjust efects of DWS that use algorithms to automate decisions. In doing so we expand the existing IS body of knowledge on algorithmic decision-making, as well as pave a way to using the proposed theory for exploration of other societal injustices created by AI and other novel technologies. Third, we contribute to the emerging IS research on unintended harmful consequences of AADM and big data. This stream of research is very much needed in IS to redress what Mikalef et al. (2019) call the mainstream IS research’s celebration of “the proliferation of analytics and AI for its economic and business potential, and its capacity to create novel ways of working, organizing and developing new products and services” (pg.1).

Our main practical contribution comes from the articulation of diferent domains of algorithmic justice disputes – and the ways in which we might address them – that could be used as a framework for identification, debate, and deliberation regarding the issues of injustices created by AADM in transformative services in practice. Such a framework is highly relevant for practice given increasing evidence of harmful social efects of AADM and unresolved disputes regarding their validity and ways of redressing them.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work, while Section 3 introduces Fraser’s (2008,2009, 2010) theory of abnormal justice. In Section 4 we discuss digital welfare systems in order to illustrate various applications of AADM and their consequences. In section 5 we articulate a theory of algorithmic justice, and identify opportunities for further work by IS researchers. In Section 6 we discuss the implications of the proposed theory for future IS research. We ofer concluding remarks in Section 7.

## 2. Algorithmic decision-making in transformative services

## 2.1. Transformative services

Originally conceptualised in the emerging Transformative Service Research (TSR) literature, the so-called transformative services are human-centred services that transform human lives through a direct impact on the wellbeing of individuals, communities and wider society (Anderson & Ostrom, 2015; Anderson et al., 2013; Ostrom et al., 2015). In industry sectors such as healthcare, welfare services, education, employment and public housing, transformative services have a direct and often profound impact on human wellbeing outcomes, such as access to services, health, inclusion and, ultimately, human rights. As Anderson et al. (2013, p. 1203) explain, “the moral imperative for transforming consumers’ lives through service is founded on the concept of human dignity, which advances the development of rights and responsibilities (United Nations Universal Declaration of Human Rights)”. Committed to this moral imperative, the emerging field of multidisciplinary TSR is defined as “service research that centers on creating uplifting changes and improvements in the well-being of individuals (consumers and employees), communities, collectives, societies, and ecosystems” (Anderson et al., 2013, p. 1203). TSR is particularly concerned with services’ impact on the key aspects of human wellbeing, such as discrimination, marginalisation, and disparity in adverse conditions in population groups, as well as the issues of inclusion and access to services (Anderson et al., 2013). It is important to note that the same key aspects of human wellbeing are now impacted by AADM in unknown ways and with unexplained harmful consequences (Keddell, 2019; Marda, 2019; UN, 2020). Calling for urgent research on the unintended, yet harmful, consequences of AADM in transformative service provision, leading TSR researchers recognise this topic as a key issue in service research and wellbeing (for example, see Blocker et al. (2019)). Hence, our inspiration and motivation to situate our research in the context of transformative services.

## 2.2. AADM applications in transformative services and their expected benefits

AI and big data-enabled algorithms have been embraced with a great enthusiasm across all types of transformative services. Indeed, AADM applications are now used in welfare services, employment, education, healthcare, criminal justice and housing services (Alston, 2019a; Alston, 2019b; Benjamin, 2019; Caplan et al., 2018; Eubanks, 2018; Gillingham, 2019; O’Neil, 2016a; Park & Humphry, 2019). Specific examples include children and family services in New Zealand and the USA whereby AADM is used to predict “children at risk” (Keddell, 2019); criminal justice services where AADM is used for citizen scoring in predictive policing and sentencing (Zavrsnik, 2019); employment services where AADM is used to score and select promising candidates (Pasquale, 2015; O’Neil, 2016a; UN, 2020); Digital Welfare Systems where AADM is used to manage and administer welfare payment (Eubanks, 2018; Carney, 2018; Farell, et al. 2019); Masiero & Das, 2019; McKinnon et al., 2019; Mann, 2020); housing services where AADM is used to manage priority lists (Caplan et al., 2018), and many more (Alston, 2019a; Benjamin, 2019; Marda, 2019; Noble, 2018; Wachter-Boettcher, 2017; Zalnieriute et al., 2019).

Without any doubt, behind these AADM applications are positive intentions, driven by the expected benefits for service provision considered to be vital in any society. Indeed, the main reasons for the rapid adoption of algorithms reported in the literature include eficiency gains, improved decision-making through automation, elimination of human biases, reduced costs, greater accountability for decisions made, improved compliance with government policies, technological innovation, greater benefits for clients and improved overall quality of services (Alston, 2019a; Alston, 2019b; Caplan et al., 2018; Park & Humphry, 2019; Redden, 2018). Moreover, driven by the promise of eficiency gains and under the new digital transformation agenda, some governments, such as the Australian government (Australian Digital Innovation Agency, 2018), are now even planning to replace “all client services with an automated back ofice” (Park & Humphry, 2019, p. 937). Similar initiatives can be found in governments around the world, for example, in the UK, New Zealand, the USA and so on. These initiatives have led to the emergence of new types of IS, such as digital welfare systems (DWS) (Zarsky, 2016).

## 2.3. Evidence of harmful efects of AADM reported in the literature

At the same time, there is a growing evidence of the harmful efects of AADM in the broad range of transformative services. The efects reported in the multidisciplinary literature include racial, gender and other forms of discrimination, restricted access or denial of services, limited opportunities, exclusion, labelling and new forms of “algorithmic oppression” (Noble, 2018; Caplan et al., 2018; Eubanks, 2018; Benjamin, 2019, Kendell, 2019; D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020; UN,

2020). In addition to creating new inequalities, AADM also replicates and amplifies known inequalities and other systemic injustices (Alston, 2019a; Benjamin, 2019; Caplan et al., 2018; Constanza-Chock, 2020; Hofmann, 2019; Mann, 2020; Marda, 2019; UN, 2020). Moreover, interactions of diferent kinds of exclusions – social, digital/algorithmic and data – result in what Park and Humphry (2019) call a new type of cumulative “exclusion by design”.

The harmful efects are often long-term and sometimes life-threating, as vividly described by Eubanks (2018). These efects go well beyond individuals and often include wider communities, various segments of society, diferent minority groups, the poor and people of certain gender – see Benjamin (2019), UN (2020). Moreover, in the context of transformative services, the most negatively afected by AADM are the most vulnerable and the least empowered sectors of populations (Eubanks, 2018; Zavrsnik, 2019). As Keddell (2019) explains, algorithmic tools are more likely to be deployed in these “low rights” environments with “few end ‘users’ [who] have the power or resources to challenge tool use.” (p. 22). Moreover, marginalised communities are often not considered, let alone included in design and implementation of these tools that are meant to serve them (Constanza-Chock, 2020). Subjected to AADM, most service users “are forced to live inside someone else’s imagination, and one of the things we have to come to grips with is how the nightmares that many people are forced to endure are really the underside of elite fantasies about eficiency, profit, safety, and social control” (Johnson, 2020b, p. 1).

In addition to the intended beneficiaries of transformative services, the use of AADM has been shown to harm people involved in providing these services. For example, when AADM is used in child protection services, social workers are left “vulnerable to either excessive blame if they did not follow the risk score, or captured in a ‘moral crumple zone’ if they do” (Keddell, 2019, p. 14).

## 2.4. Current approaches to mitigation and elimination of harm caused by AADM

Apart from studying and reporting the harmful consequences of AADM, researchers are also concerned with their possible mitigation or elimination. Our review of multidisciplinary literature reveals not only diferent approaches, but also conflicting understanding of the nature of the problem of algorithmic harm, even within the same field. For example, some researchers in the field of FAT-ML (Fairness, Accountability and Transparency in Machine Learning) assume that the problem of AADM harm is largely technical in nature (Zemel et al., 2013, and Celis et al. 2019). Consequently, they see these efects as the outcomes of imperfect algorithms, data errors or biases that could be addressed or even eliminated by technical solutions. Often the main emphasis is placed on bias in data and models and the approaches to “debias them”. As the Facebook Chief AI Scientist Yann LeCun recently asserted “ML systems are biased when data is biased” (Johnson, 2020a, p. 1). For example, to address the problem of bias, IBM Research ofers an open source 360 AI Fairness Toolkit which includes “a comprehensive set of fairness metrics for datasets and models, explanations of these metrics, and algorithms to mitigate bias in datasets and models” – see Bellamy et al. (2018).

Yet, in her consideration of algorithmic harms, Dave (2019), a Data & Society researcher, warns us that the widespread consideration and theories of bias are not adequate to help us explain systemic algorithmic harms. As Dave (2019) explains: “when we say ‘an algorithm is biased’, we, in some ways, are treating an algorithm as if it were a flawed individual, rather than an institutional force.” (p. 1).

This wider view of bias and a systemic view of harm is also shared by an emerging stream of researchers within the FAT-ML community. For example, contrary to LeCun’s view about bias being contained in data, Timnit Gebru, a leading FAT-ML researcher and a former co-lead of AI Ethics at Google, argues that AI bias cannot be reduced to data bias alone, as discussed in a well-publicised debate (Johnson, 2020a). Challenging the computer science community to understand how persuasively technology is marginalising many groups of people, Gebru (2020) ofered the following take-home message from her wellknown tutorial: “fairness is not just about data sets, and it’s not just about math. Fairness is about society as well, and as engineers, as scientists, we can’t really shy away from that fact” (n.p). This view has been seconded by fellow researcher Ruha Benjamin (2019) who argues that an ahistoric approach to AI can harm people and that what is needed is a historically and sociologically grounded approach that opens up new settings and creates new opportunities. This discussion, which is much more aligned with the sociotechnical approach adopted in IS than the traditional computer-science approach to FAT-ML, points to issues of social justice created by algorithmic harm. Going beyond AI to design of technological solutions in general, Constanza-Chock (2020) proposes “design justice” as an approach that combines participatory design and social justice. The approach, guided by “design justice principles”, is intended to be led by marginalised communities with the aim of challenging structural inequalities rather than reproducing them.

Independently of these discussions, another growing group of researchers, mostly from management sciences, takes a radically diferent approach by focusing on “algorithmic appreciation” (Logg et al., 2018)

and “algorithmic aversion” (Dietvorst et al., 2016). Underneath this highly influential stream of research there is an assumption that algorithmic efects are largely perceptual. This research, neglecting the issue of harm altogether, advises managers how to avoid aversion to algorithms (Harrell, 2016) or develop appreciation for algorithms, which would allegedly lead to improved accuracy of their decisions and forecasts (Logg et al., 2018).

Against this background, we argue that issues of algorithmic harm are much deeper than accuracy and technical improvements or human preferences and aversion. As Marda (2019) explains: “It is not simply a matter of ensuring accuracy and perfection in a technical system, but rather a reckoning with the fundamentally imperfect, discriminatory and unfair world from which these systems arise, and the underlying structural and historical legacy in which these systems are applied” (p. 8). Informed by these insights, we argue for AADM harm to be approached from a social justice perspective.

## 2.5. AADM harm as a social justice issue

Our multidisciplinary literature review reveals a small, but growing group of researchers, mostly from social sciences and legal studies, who are seeing broader societal consequences of algorithms as issues of social justice and human rights. This view of harm is much wider than that which is typically discussed under the key ethical issues of fairness, transparency and accountability of predictive tools (Gurses et al., 2019; Keddell, 2019), especially when used in the context of transformative services. Moreover, as Keddell (2019) explains, the notion of fairness in social justice is fundamentally diferent from the notion of “fairness” used in statistics, and as such cannot be reduced to a computational problem. Similarly, Zavrsnik (2019) argues “building better algorithms is not the solution” (p. 9).

This “building better algorithms” approach to AADM is a part of “solutionism”, whereby tech companies aim to build technical solutions to address all social problems (Zavrsnik, 2019 citing Morozov, 2013, p. 2). Consequently, the impact of AI on human rights, democracy and social justice, as Marda (2019) explains, “is both significant (widespread and general) and bespoke (impacting on individuals) in unique ways depending on the context in which AI is employed” (Marda, 2019, p. 9). However, while the efects of AADM are now being recognised as social justice issues, the extant broader literature ofers very limited theoretical explanation concerning why and how these issues are created. One example of social theorising is the work of D’Ignazio and Klein (2020), who apply the lens of intersectional feminist theory to examine new types of data-related injustices faced by women, migrants, indigenous and other migrant communities.

In the IS field, apart from several prominent examples of research that focus on negative consequences of AADM on individuals, organisations and society – see for example, Gal et al. (2020), Clarke (2019), Galliers et al. (2017), Markus (2017), Loebbecke and Picot (2015), Newell and Mirabelli (2015), and Marjanovic et al. (2018, 2021), Schultze et al., 2018) and Mikalef et al. (2019) – IS researchers are yet to focus on this important topic on a larger scale. The harmful societal efects performed by AADM are not well understood and are still under-researched in IS. Even when investigated, these efects are often reduced to issues of privacy and surveillance (Galliers et al., 2017; Markus, 2017). In particular, the IS community is yet to recognise social justice issues emerging from AADM deployment and to engage with theoretical explanations as to how and why injustices are created.

In summary, our multidisciplinary literature review shows rising concerns with the harmful social efects of AADM, specifically in transformative services, where AADM impacts on the wellbeing of individuals, communities and society. Of particular importance in this context are the unintended harmful efects of AADM as issues of social justice, that are under researched and not well-understood in IS research. In the next section, we turn our attention to contemporary social justice theoriesin particular, Fraser’s (2008,2009, 2010) theory of abnormal justice, which we use as the foundation for our theorising of algorithmic justice.

## 3. Theories of social justice

While acknowledging a long history of diferent theories of social justice, our brief overview of the relevant theories starts from the work of the social justice philosopher John Rawls (1971, 2001). Rawls’ theory has been used extensively for research related to morality, ethics and IT (Hofman, 2017), with a number of prominent researchers investigating matters of information justice relating to distribution of information goods, including intellectual property (see for example, Drahos, (1996), Britz (2008) and Duf (2012)). However, based on a comprehensive review of multidisciplinary literature (150 papers) published by scholars in information technology, information systems, information science and related disciplines, all using Rawls’ theory of social justice, Hofman (2017) observes an important limitation about the use of this theory in IT. Hofman’s key concern with Rawls original position and its major proponents, is their focus on “the just distribution of information as a discrete and commodifiable good” (Hofman, 2017, p. 1611). Such “distributive justice”, although still relevant and vital today in the matters of equity, liberty, human rights, wealth and resources, reaches its limit when it comes to IT and information. Hofman (2017) warns: “By reducing issues of social justice, information, and technology to purely distributive terms, all we are left to talk about are problems of distribution” (p. 1613). This view obscures other dimensions that are equally important. For example, “conceiving of information as a primary good cannot tell us much about the ways in which information is collected, framed, analyzed, presented, or packaged – only about the way it is distributed” (ibid, p. 1615). Focusing on the same limitation of the distributive logic of social justice in relation to data, Cinnamon (2017) warns that a narrow focus on data distribution creates a risk of hiding other harmful efects such as dataveillance “that are ontologically diferent from questions of distribution across the social spectrum” (p. 612).

Arguing for the need to theorise social justice beyond issues of equal distribution of rewards and costs across group members, the social justice philosopher Nancy Fraser (2008, 2009, 2010) proposes a new theory of “abnormal justice”. Fraser argues that we are now living in abnormal times in which the “what is just?” as well as the “who” and the “how” of justice are deeply contested. Abnormal justice is contrasted with conditions of “normal justice”, which is “a state of general agreement among all parties about basic ontological structure and assumptions of justice” (Cinnamon, 2017, pp. 612–613). The existence of disagreements and disputes over the “what”, the “who” and the “how” creates major problems for anyone “who cares about injustice today” (Fraser, 2008, p. 402).

Consequently, Fraser argues, the three nodes of abnormality – the what, the who and the how of justice – need to be reconsidered in today’s times, in order to observe and deal with new types of injustices. Figure 1 depicts the key elements of Fraser’s theory, with the three mutually shaping nodes of abnormality, which are described next.

The “what”of justice

The first node of abnormality – the “what”, deals with the very matter of justice by focusing on the key question: What is just? As Fraser explains, in the 19<sup>th</sup> and 20<sup>th</sup> centuries, the concept of “justice” was predominantly perceived in economic terms, or more precisely, as equal distribution of resources. In the 21<sup>st</sup> century, Fraser argues, the concept of “justice”, in particular “what is just”, needs to be considered from multiple perspectives. In addition to the economic perspective, she adds two more perspectives – culture and politics. Within these perspectives, issues of injustice then become issues of maldistribution, misrecognition and misrepresentation. Maldistribution injustices relate to the lack of, or misdistribution of resources. Misrecognition injustices are related to inequalities in status of people which are created by “institutionalized hierarchies of cultural value that deny them the requisite standing” (Fraser, 2008, p. 405). Lastly, misrepresentation injustices are created by “decision rules that deny them [people] equal voice in public deliberations and democratic decision making” (Fraser, 2008, p.405–406), which results in “voiceless subjects unable to access democratic institutions or the means of social redress in an equitable way” (Cinnamon, 2017, p. 613). In her theory of abnormal justice, Fraser (2008) proposes to submit injustice claims for redistribution, recognition, and representation to the overarching normative principle of parity of participation, which “creates a single discursive space that can accommodate them all” (Fraser, 2008, p. 407). Thus “[a]ccording to this principle, justice requires social arrangements that permit all to participate as peers in social life” (Fraser, 2008 , p. 405).

## The “who” of justice

The second node of abnormality – the “who” of justice – relates to the conflicting views of “who” counts in matters of justice. In theorising abnormal justice, Fraser (2008) proposes to focus on “a distinctive conception of a political dimension” (p. 402). At the basic level, the political dimension is concerned with injustices related to ordinary-political misrepresentation which occurs “when a polity’s rules for decision making deny some who are counted in principle as members the chance to participate fully, as peers” (Fraser, 2008, p. 407). Examples of these issues include multicultural rights, gender equity, and provincial autonomy. Recognising their importance, Fraser then expands her theorising of the “who” of justice, by proposing the second level of injustices – misframing. As Fraser (2008) explains “[s]uch injustices occur when polity’s boundaries are drawn in such a way as to wrongly deny some people the chance to participate at all in its authorized contests over justice” (p.408; emphasis in the original). Observing the lack of a determinative principle that could be used to clarify disputes in conflicting interpretations of the “who” of justice, Fraser (2008) proposes to submit all claims of misframing to the all-subjected principle: “all those who are subject to a given governance structure have moral standing as subjects of justice in relation to it” (p. 411). This principle provides a critical standard for addressing misframing: “An issue is justly framed” – Fraser (2008) continues – “if and only if everyone subjected to the governance(s) structures that regulate the relevant swath(s) of social interaction is accorded equal consideration” (p. 412).

## The “how” of justice

The third node of abnormality – the “how” of justice – relates to approaches for clarifying and redressing disputes of justice. Fraser argues that the “how” requires a theorising that combines dialogical and institutional approaches. She recognises the important roles of civil-society actors and public institutions, as well as their limitations. For example, today’s civil-society actors are important for raising issues of injustice; however, they “can neither warrant claims nor make binding decisions” (Fraser, 2008 , p. 415). Public institutions, on the other hand, can make binding decisions, but often lack deeper insights into complex injustices from the perspective of those subjected (i.e., “the who”).

Thus, Fraser (2010) argues, a theory of abnormal justice must be dialogical, with the dialogical process consisting of two mutually shaping tracks – a civil society track and an institutional track. While the institutional track “requires fair procedures and a representative structure to ensure the democratic legitimacy of its deliberations”, the civil society representatives require the capacity to make binding decisions (Fraser, 2008 , p. 416). Therefore, a dialogical approach to addressing issues of injustice requires the institutional track to be in an interactive relationship with the civil track.

Fraser’s theory of abnormal justice has been applied to address injustices in disciplines as divers as education (Morrison, 2015), agriculture (Besky, 2015) and trade and geo-politics (Loveys, 2013), to name a few. Of particular interest for our research is the adoption of abnormal justice theory to framing the emerging concept of “data justice” that focuses on the relationship between data, dataveillance and social justice – see Dencik et al. (2018), 2019a, 2019b) and Cinnamon (2017). As Dencik et al. (2019b) explain, the concept of data justice is “used to denote an analysis of data that pays particular attention to structural inequality, highlighting the unevenness of implications and experiences of data across diferent groups and communities in society” (p. 875). Importantly, data justice establishes the link between social justice and harmful societal efects of data and dataveillance (Cinnamon, 2017). Seeing “corporate dataveillance practices as a threat to social justice” (p. 1), Cinnamon (2017) calls for more explicit theorisation of the social harms performed by asymmetrical personal data accumulation, datafication, and data analy tics. While this is important, and our paper in some respect responds to this call, we recognise the need to extend the concerns for justice beyond data-related issues, as per Denick et al.’s (2018) definition of data justice. Similarly, Gangadharan and Niklas (2019) – who also draw from Fraser (2008) – argue for the need to go beyond data-centric discrimination and recognise broader and systemic forms of discrimination, institutionalised oppression and injustice. We further argue that AADM based on individual algorithms and also on systems of algorithms, in a wide range of organisations, involve much bigger concerns for justice and introduce an additional layer of complexity due to AADM’s interpenetration with the personal, social, political, ethical, legal, and other aspects, that are not limited to data justice.

Against this background, we explore and address the harmful societal efects of AADM in transformative service as issues of social justice, or more specifically “algorithmic justice”, and articulate a theory of algorithmic justice based on a reinterpretation and extension of Fraser’s (2008, 2010) theory of abnormal justice. We first reinterpret Fraser’s theory in the context of AADM in transformative services based on an illustrative case of digital welfare systems (DWS), large-scale IS that have been widely adopted by governments around the world. Drawing from this case we then articulate a theory of algorithmic justice and discuss its contribution to understanding, diagnosing and redressing harmful efects emerging from AADM’s deployment as distinct types of social injustices.

## 4. Automated algorithmic decision-making in digital welfare systems

Philip Alston, a special rapporteur for the United Nations (UN) on human rights, argues that new forms of governance are emerging based on accumulating vast amounts of digital data from various sources (Alston, 2019b). This data is subjected to predictive analytics and machine learning in order to automate decision-making, while also limiting or removing the oversight and discretion of humans. In such a regime, citizens become increasingly visible to governments, but citizens do not have a symmetric visibility of the workings of government (Alston, 2019b). Digital technologies are being used by governments to verify citizens’ identities, to determine eligibility for benefits, to calculate benefit payment levels, to detect and prevent fraud, to perform risk scoring and need classification, and to automate communications between welfare agencies and claimants (Alston, 2019b). While governments around the world deployed these digital welfare systems (DWS) with plausible objectives to improve service quality and eliminate ineficiencies, the UN report documents significant negative social implications, calling attention to questions of human rights and social justice (Alston, 2019b).

In 2013, the UK began the rollout of its Universal Credit scheme, a benefit payment for people in or out of work that is administered by the Department of Work and Pensions (DWP) (www.gov.uk/universalcredit). Universal Credit aims to streamline the UK’s welfare system by replacing six means-tested benefits, which are known as “legacy benefits”, with a single, integrated benefits system. In its annual report for 2018/19 the DWP claims “Our Digital Transformation has improved the experiences of people who rely on our services, making us more efective and eficient, and enabling us to personalise delivery for customers and claimants.” (DWP, 2019, p. 7). Booth (2019a) reports in the Guardian newspaper that the DWP is building an intelligent automation garage comprising over 100 welfare robots deploying deep learning in the belief that welfare transactions will be handled more quickly and cheaply by welfare robots. The introduction of algorithmic decision-making is further motivated by the increase in fraudulent claims (Bloom, 2019). Fraud can be both systematic and individual. Systematic fraud takes place through criminal gangs working with claimants to apply for advance payments, which are then split between the criminal and the claimant (for the claimant to later realise that the payment is a loan and has to be paid back through reduced benefit payments over the next 12 months). To combat individual fraud, the DWP uses AI to assess the likelihood that a citizen’s childcare and housing costs are true when they apply for benefits under Universal Credit (Booth, 2019a).

The trend towards automated algorithmic decision-making (with or without human oversight) has been picked up by Big Brother Watch (BBW), which is “a cross–party, non-party, independent non-profit organisation leading the protection of privacy and civil liberties in the UK.” One of the campaigns organised by BBW is to explore the use of algorithms by the DWP and UK local government authorities (Big Brother Watch, 2019). BBW are seeking evidence of the use of “Risk Based Verification” (RBV) algorithms to assign a risk score to benefits applications in order to determine the level of verification needed for processing that claim. The RBV algorithm allocates claims to low, medium, or high risk. Citizens who are classified as medium or high risk will be asked for more identification information, such as details of income, rent, and capital (medium risk) or be subject to credit checks, telephone interviews, or house visits (high risk). The RBV models are provided by commercial software companies and BBW argues that the users of these models (e.g., the DWP and local government authorities) do not understand how the software arrives at its decisions due to the algorithm being black-boxed, making it dificult to assess the consistency and accuracy of the decisions.

Booth (2019b) provides numerous accounts of claimants who are struggling to negotiate the online claims system and are on the receiving end of an automated system that appears to have little or no human intervention or oversight. Examples include incorrect data that cannot be corrected, communications asking illiterate claimants to read letters, and requests for data that if unanswered lead to sanctions and reductions in benefits payments. The DWP’s “digital by default” approach to benefits claims has led to hunger, breakdown, and attempted suicide.

However, the way the DWP is building these algorithms, how they work, and what data they use is dificult to determine as the DWP refuses to answer freedom of information requests about how it gathers data on citizens (see Gantchev, 2019). Booth (2019a) says:

“The ministry has previously told parliament it gathers data from private credit reference agencies, the police, the Valuation Ofice Agency, the Land Registry and the National Fraud Initiative, which gather information from public and private bodies. But it is now declining to update the list, claiming it would “compromise the usefulness of that data.””

Simon Dufy of the Centre for Welfare Reform presents the Centre’s view of the UK’s Universal Credit programme and its use of algorithms in clear terms (Dufy, 2018):

“It claims to be improving incentives, but actually leaves the poorest paying the highest taxes, both as percentage of their income and on every next pound earned. It claims to be simpler - but in fact it leaves people dependent on computer systems that many can’t use and it disguises complexity with algorithms that most can’t challenge. It is an inhuman system and it is destined to fail - the only question is - at what cost?”

Alston concludes that Universal Credit leads to digital exclusion as the “[t]he British welfare state is gradually disappearing behind a web page and an algorithm, with significant implications for those living in poverty.” (Alston, 2019a, p. 13). Further, with the advent of the Covid-19 pandemic, the number of welfare claimants in the UK increased by 1.5 million in the period from lockdown on March 162,020 to April 212,020 (Atkinson, 2020). Many of these new claimants are people encountering the welfare system for the first time, having been forced into applying for Universal Credit as a result of losing their jobs or being “furloughed” by their employer on 80% of their pay.

In his Report to the UN Philip Alston (2019b) warned that DWS introduced in countries around the world have very often been used to slash welfare spending, introduce intrusive surveillance of citizens, impose stronger sanction regimes, and enact a “complete reversal of the traditional notion that the state should be accountable to the individual”. Australia generated controversy over its “RoboDebt” (the Online Compliance Intervention introduced by the welfare department, Centrelink), which is an automated debt-raising and recovery system that computes and recovers any overpayments given to welfare recipients (Carney, 2018; Mann, 2020). RoboDebt uses a data cross-matching algorithm between the Australian Taxation Ofice (ATO) and Centrelink to identify any declared income discrepancy. Debt letters were sent to welfare recipients requesting payment unless they could provide relevant evidence disproving the debt. The RoboDebt had a high error rate due to incorrect personal details for individuals, incorrect employment information, and most importantly the mismatch between the ATO and Centrelink data. Furthermore, welfare recipients had problems with understanding the debt claims, finding the old documents to disprove them, or getting any explanation from Centrelink staf, resulting in significant distress and fear of Government. The lack of digital literacy and Internet access, especially among the uneducated and recipients with disabilities, caused additional distress and anxiety (e.g., see Farrell & McDonald, 2019; Henrique-Gomes, 2020; Karp, 2019). After increasing public pressure, two lost law suits and a class action pending, in June 2020 the Government admitted that RoboDebt was unlawful and committed to refund 470,000 debts (Hayne & Doran, 2020).

In the US automated fraud detection made roughly 48,000 fraud accusations against unemployment insurance recipients and, without any human intervention, “the state demanded repayments plus interest and civil penalties of four times the alleged amount owed.” (Gilman, 2020). The resulting “financial stress on the accused resulted in evictions, divorces, destroyed credit scores, homelessness, bankruptcies and even suicide.” (Gilman, 2020).

In Holland, a Dutch court ordered the immediate halt of the use of AI in detecting welfare fraud on the grounds that it violates human rights (Henley & Booth, 2020). The Dutch government’s social afairs ministry developed a system, SyRI, to detect people suspected of social security fraud. The Dutch government drew data from various organisations to train an algorithm on profiles of people already involved in fraud and then used the algorithm to identify others with similar risk profiles who would warrant further investigation (DutchNews.nl, 2020). The system has been criticised by Alston as potentially discriminating “against people with little money and people with a minority background.” (DutchNews.nl, 2020).

In defence of algorithmic decision-making in welfare systems in the UK, McKinnon et al. (2019) argue that:

“Far from being “ruled by computer algorithms”, our bots don’t make decisions regarding people’s benefits. Instead they focus on everyday repetitive tasks so our colleagues can spend more time supporting vulnerable claimants face to face.”

Thus, while AI and algorithmic decision-making are intended to and may indeed provide eficiencies, safeguards, and improved targeting of welfare benefits to those in need, the mounting evidence suggests that correctness, fairness, justice, and legality of the current use of algorithms in welfare systems are deeply contested.

## 5. Towards a theory of algorithmic justice

In this section, we propose a theory of algorithmic justice that is concerned with claims and disputes regarding injustices produced by AADM deployment in transformative services. Inspired by Fraser’s (2008) theory of abnormal justice, we propose the theory of algorithmic justice that identifies four related domains of justice contestation: (1) WHAT is the matter of algorithmic justice, (2) WHO counts as a subject of algorithmic justice, (3) HOW are algorithmic injustices performed, and (4) addressing and resolving disputes about the “what”, “who” and “how” of algorithmic justices. The first two domains of algorithmic justice contestation correspond to Fraser’s (2008) “what” and “who” nodes of abnormality, while we reinterpret them in the context of AADM deployment in transformative services. The third domain of algorithmic justice contestation, however, is specific to the AADM context and identifies the distinct features of how algorithmic injustices are performed (not considered by Fraser’s theory). The fourth domain of algorithmic justice contestation focuses on addressing and resolving disputes about the “what”, “who” and “how” of algorithmic justices. It is therefore specific to the domain of AADM in transformative services, while being generally inspired by Fraser’s (2008) theory.

We present our theory of algorithmic justice by drawing from the case of DWS introduced above. As a prominent case of the deployment of AADM in transformative services, it enables us to illustrate the domains of What, Who and How of justice disputes and contestations resulting from the deployment of DWS in the provision of welfare services, as well as the fourth domain of addressing and resolving disputes. We show how the theory of algorithmic justice enables better understanding and exploration of justice issues and disputes emerging in DWS deployment. This case also allows us to illustrate distinct processes through which injustices are performed: including datafication, technological inscribing, black-boxing and inscrutability of algorithms, that are of particular interest to the IS discipline.

In Table 1 we list the domains of algorithmic justice contestation and dimensions of the proposed theory of algorithmic justice (columns 1 and 2) and then illustrate them by analysing the case of DWS (columns 3 & 4). Specifically, we summarise the related claims and disputes about algorithmic justice (column 3),and identify questions about algorithmic injustices involved in the working of DWS (column 4).

## WHAT is the matter of algorithmic justice?

Following Fraser’s (2008) theory, the “what” of algorithmic justice (i.e., “what is just?”) includes dimensions of maldistribution, misrecognition, and misrepresentation, which address economic, sociocultural, and political perspectives on justice.

With regard to the economic impact, we are concerned with how the algorithms work and how algorithmic decision-making translates into economic disadvantage and deprivation. For example, DWS are purportedly introduced by governments to improve services, to identify and reduce fraud, and to make services fairer. These goals are widely disputed as DWS worldwide are found to be instruments to increase eficiency, cut costs, and reduce welfare budgets (Alston 2019a; Alston, 2019b; Gilman, 2020). As a result of DWS deployment, welfare recipients sufer economic consequences, although these are often disputed by governments. In particular, these economic consequences often impact most heavily on the most vulnerable, the disabled, the homeless and the poorest in society (Eubanks, 2018).

As an example of how DWS undermines individuals’ integrity and specificity when using algorithmic classifiers is identifying fraud by welfare recipients. Based on the data collected about an individual (most not checked or approved by the individual), the algorithm assigns a probability to each case of the likelihood of it being fraudulent. A cut-of is then applied and cases that exceed the cut-of will be subject to further investigation and action. Thus, the world is reduced from many shades of grey to one that is dichotomised into black and white. Of the cases classified as being potentially fraudulent (i.e., exceed the probability threshold used by the algorithm) some are actually fraudulent, but some will be false positives. False positives are of particular concern for claimants since they may be subject to automatic penalties and/ or further repercussions before a detailed investigation is conducted. Merely being flagged as a potentially fraudulent claimant may have damaging implications if this outcome is stored on the claimant’s record. Often, disputes over the correctness of fraudulent claims identified by DWS algorithms are not resolved eficiently and sometimes end up in the court (as was the case with RoboDebt system in Australia).

The socio-cultural dimension is concerned with welfare claimants’ status of inequality or misrecognition of their requisite standing, their treatment as the “other” of working people who pay tax (who are considered to be the norm) and of honest and worthy citizens. These specific assumptions of standing (rarely articulated or debated publicly) concerning diferent groups of claimants (e.g., working families, homeless people, ethnic minorities, disabled, elderly) are embedded in DWS design and operation. Through the deployment of DWS welfare claimants become what they are assumed by the system to be.

More broadly, the socio-cultural dimension includes AADM’s contribution to the perpetuation of bias and unfairness in society, and discrimination against disadvantaged sections of society. As discussed, the notion of fairness in social justice is a broader concept than data bias (Keddell , 2019, Gebru, 2020; Hofmann, 2019). Algorithmic bias and unfairness reflect the very fabric of the sociotechnical environment in which algorithms are created and in which they perform. As AADM coperforms the environment, then existing biases and unfairness are amplified.

The political dimension (misrepresentation) is concerned with the motivations and agendas of those deploying AADM, the purpose the algorithm serves, and the rights of citizens to participate and to have a voice in such debates. Welfare recipients are not considered citizens with a right to participate (or be represented) in the decisions to adopt DWS nor in their design and implementation processes; they are “voiceless” subjects. Not only are they deprived of the right to participate, they are not informed about the rules encoded in algorithms or the data used to make decisions. Furthermore, when DWS are fully implemented, welfare recipients are denied access to government staf and instead directed to interact with the system. They lose their rights to contact government staf to get an explanation of the decision and the rules applied – something that was an assumed right before the introduction of DWS and AADM.

Marginalisation and voicelessness of welfare recipients are exacerbated by the hunt for so-called fraudulent claimants. There may be political capital in being seen to tackle fraudulent claims and safeguard public money. The use of DWS to identify fraudulent claims is politically fraught, often in response to tabloid newspapers stories on “welfare scroungers” and a toxic narrative of vulnerable people as a drain on society. However, it is important to note that whether a claim is fraudulent or not is shaped by how the organisation conceives fraud, which will reflect attitudes and values and past data. For example, will the identification of fraud include unintentional errors made by claimants completing online forms?

In summary, going back to the “what” is the matter of algorithmic justice, the previous discussion about various economic, socio-cultural and political disputes, reveals some important AADM-related aspects not considered in Fraser’s theorisation of this node of abnormality. First, AADM creates new tensions about the very question of “What is just?” Tensions are fuelled by conflicting goals of AADM-enabled innovation, eficiency and data integration versus the societal goal of care for disadvantaged and vulnerable citizens. AADM also amplifies existing tensions in social welfare systems, such as toxic narratives about vulnerable people. Marda (2019) observes: “AI systems, and the deliberations surrounding AI, are flawed because they amplify some voices at the expenses of others, and are built by a few people and imposed on others” (p. 9). AADM also masks “what is just” behind more powerful stories of “objectivity” and superiority of algorithms over humans. In doing so, it changes the nature of responsibility. Citing Harcourt (2015), Zavrsnik (2019). explains that “[r]elying on automated systems lends an aura of objectivity to the final decision and difuses the burden of responsibility” (p. 13).

## WHO counts as a subject of algorithmic justice?

In Fraser’s theory the “who” is concerned with misframing and the principle of all-subjected. In the context of algorithmic justice, there are serious questions about “who” counts as a subject of justice. Welfare claimants are not framed as subjects worthy of consideration by governments introducing DWS. They are not, typically, represented or considered in any way as stakeholders in debates about DWS deployment, nor in their development and implementation. Furthermore, the broader public and welfare claimants have no insight into the workings of the DWS, the use of algorithms, and the decisionmaking processes in which they are embedded.

However, algorithmic justice adds another layer of injustice not considered in Fraser’s “who”. In the theory of algorithmic justice, we need to re-conceptualise how we think about the human subjects of Fraser’s theory. In the world of AADM human subjects are “datafied humans”, whereby what is taken to be “real human” becomes a set of data points, including unchecked and proxy data (Zavrsnik, 2019). Moreover, this datafied representation of humans, is never static, but perpetually re-constructed through various, often opaque datafication processes (Marjanovic & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2017) using various sources of data, some of which are acquired through third party “data brokers and integrators” (Pasquale, 2015). These digital personas (Clarke, 2019) call for a new conceptualisation of Fraser’s notion of “misrepresentation” in the theory of abnormal justice.

When theorising “the who” of abnormal justice Fraser is also concerned about people not having an opportunity to participate in decision-making as peers. As illustrated by DWS, this is also the case with AADM. However, in algorithmic justice we observe an additional manifestation of injustice with regards to “who”, not found in Fraser. Namely, welfare claimants in DWS have no opportunity to see, let alone correct, or object to their digital (mis)

representation. As Cinnamon (2017) explains: “The separation of people from their data – the dominant situation in the new landscape of personal data . . . is a clear obstacle to parity of participation and therefore an injustice” (pg. 7). Consequently, we argue that datafication practices, which result in perpetually reconstructed digital (i.e., datafied) versions of people, should be treated as critical issues of social justice and, in particular, algorithmic justice.

## HOW are algorithmic injustices performed?

Our consideration of the “how” dimension of algorithmic justice represents an addition to Fraser’s theory of abnormal justice. Adding an explicit “How” of justice is necessary due to the performative nature of AADM, which introduces new ways of enacting injustices. By using technological means of inscribing laws and rules in algorithms that are applied to datafied individuals (typically using unchecked and proxy datasets), DWS efectively performs injustices (as reported e.g., in Alston, 2019a; Alston, 2019b). We therefore suggest a new node of abnormality: “How injustices are performed”, together with its key dimension “technological inscribing”.This addition is justified due to the specific nature of algorithmic justice which includes not only economic, socio-cultural and political processes but also, and importantly, technological inscribing and automation of these processes.

Specifically, an emerging nexus of DWS, datafication and existing power structures, is starting to transform citizen–government relations and exert new forms of power over citizens. For example, in the case of RoboDebt in Australia, the unlawful use of data and the rules to calculate debt combined with the coercive use of power in administering the debt claims created a new form of injustice. Government agency one-sidedly reversed the well-established legal logic that one is innocent until proven guilty: when RoboDebt was implemented the onus was on welfare recipients to prove that they were not guilty. Furthermore, Government failed to practice duty of care towards its citizens, especially the most vulnerable in the society. While RoboDebt has been scrapped and the Australian Government is paying back the debt collected, the deterioration of the relationship between government and citizens has been widely reported (Farrell & McDonald, 2019; Hayne & Doran, 2020; Henrique-Gomes, 2020).

Through technological inscribing and datafication processes AADM, and specifically DWS, are “performing new worlds” (Schultze, et al. 2018), in other words, worlds in which new injustices are enacted and perpetuated, often in covert ways. New forms of discrimination, misrecognition, and misframing, which are technologically inscribed and implemented, produce new categories of injustices that present a clear threat to democracy and parity of participation in social life.

## HOW to address and resolve disputes about “what”, “who” and “how” of algorithmic (in)justices?

This domain of contestation of our theory of algorithmic justice builds on the original “how” node of abnormality in Fraser’s theory of abnormal justice, i.e., how injustices might be addressed rather than how they are performed. We see the relevance of both civilsociety and institutional tracks as well as their extensions in response to new algorithmic (in)justices. In relation to the first track, we observe the emergence of new civil society groups, which could be seen as new types of “algorithmic activism”. These groups are formed to collect society-wide data on algorithmic injustices and help the “all-subjected” to redress them. On the other hand, institutions such as international governments and others with authority are engaged in the design of various ethical guidelines, frameworks, policies, regulatory schemes, and legislations, which are expected to guide development and adoption of AI-based systems in various contexts in order to prevent, or at least mitigate, their negative efects.

Thus, Fraser’s original observation about the need for a dialogical approach to redressing social injustices, still stands in the case of algorithmic injustices. As observed in the case of DWS, the welfare claimants have limited scope for appealing against an algorithmic decision and there are currently no independent AADM regulators to hold organisations using AADM accountable.

Fraser’s theory of abnormal justice argues the need for new types of institutions for addressing and provisionally resolving such disputes democratically via a permanent, ongoing dialogue. We argue that institutions are even more critical in the age of algorithmic injustices due to the tensions involved in the “what” of justice, and misrepresentations in the “who” of justice created by the datafication of individuals and the propagation of this algorithmically generated data. Most importantly, these institutions are needed due to the systemic nature of algorithmic injustice as injustices are propagated, amplified and performed across different contexts within which the all-subjected live and work (as articulated in the proposed node “How algorithmic injustices are performed”).

Taken in combination the above-described domains of algorithmic justice contestation (i.e., nodes of abnormality) of the proposed theory of algorithmic justice represents an initial framework that could be used to identify, discuss, diagnose and deliberate the matters of algorithmic justice. The questions of “What is the matter of algorithmic justice”, “Who counts as a subject of algorithmic justice”, “How are algorithmic injustices performed?” and “How to address and resolve disputes about ‘what’, ‘who’ and how” of algorithmic justice?’ are depicted as mutually dependent domains in Figure 2. The central domain “How to address and resolve disputes?” refers to three other elements as well as to their interactions (e.g., the question of “what” matters afects the question of “who” counts or should count).

As Fraser (2008) explains, “Overcoming injustice requires at least two additional conditions: first, a relatively stable framework in which clams can be equitably vetted, and second, institutional agencies as means of redress. . . . Those who would theorise justice in abnormal times must keep both sides of this equation in view” (p. 402). The proposed theory of algorithmic justice can be seen as a direct IS contribution to the first of Fraser’s conditions to overcoming injustices, and a signpost to the second.

The proposed framework is a contribution to theorising the “what”, the “who”, and the “how” of algorithmic justice to address the intended and unintended, yet harmful consequences of AI and big data-enabled algorithmic decision-making in transformative services. Our instantiation of the three nodes of abnormality from Fraser’s (2008, 2009, 2010) theory of abnormal justice, and especially their reconceptualisation and extension, ofers an important contribution to the theories of social justice in the world of AADM.

Most importantly for IS researchers, our research points to datafication, technological inscribing and the systemic nature of injustices as important IS-specific aspects of algorithmic injustice. Our main practical contribution comes from the articulation of algorithmic justice as a framework that (1) makes visible the injustices related to the “what”, “who”, and “how” in transformative services, and (2) provides insights into how we might begin to address these injustices.

## 6. Implications of the proposed theory of algorithmic justice for further IS research

The proposed theory of algorithmic justice opens future possibilities for IS research and practice, such as an implementation of new types of society-wide information systems, designed to support large-scale engagement and dialogue of all-subjected to algorithmic harm and/or their representatives with institutions, while supported and managed by an independent institution, such as a new public agency. We find support for this idea in recent research by Sun and Gerchick (2019) as well as the recommendations by the US Government Accountability Ofice (2019). They both recommend establishment of an oversight agency with relevant expertise, either as a new public agency or an extension of an existing one at the federal or state level. IS researchers and professionals can also design new types of IS to support this agency as an intermediary in a society-wide two-track dialogical process, among institutions and civil society. We envisage that the design, implementation, and ongoing refinement of such a complex, society-wide IS will open further research and practical challenges, such as the need for innovative design methodologies and new types of evaluations.

We also envisage that the proposed theory of algorithmic justice may be used as a foundation for empirical investigations of specific domains of algorithmic injustices that in turn might spark further theoretical developments. The application and further developments of the theory of algorithmic justice have a potential to make a significant contribution to efective identification, understanding and redressing of algorithmic injustices

In addition, promising future developments might be the formulation of AI ethical guidelines based on the proposed theory of algorithmic justice as well as the theory-based assessment of the existing frameworks. We base this idea on the ever-increasing number of AI ethical guidelines proposed by researchers, governments, industry associations and private companies, often without any theoretical foundations and empirical validation.

## 7. Conclusion

This research focuses on the harmful efects of automated algorithmic decision-making (AADM), which are powered by AI and big data, in transformative services. We recognise these efects as new forms of social injustices that go beyond the logic of unequal access and distribution of justice, as in prevalent theories of social justice. Building upon Fraser’s theory of abnormal justice, we propose and articulate a theory of algorithmic justice. Taken together, the key interrelated domains of the proposed theory of algorithmic justice, articulated as questions – “What is the matter of algorithmic justice”, “Who counts as a subject of algorithmic justice”, “How are algorithmic injustices performed?” and “How to address and resolve disputes about ‘what’, ‘who’ and how” of algorithmic justice? – provide a framework which we ofer to support future examinations of matters of algorithmic injustices.

Our theory is intended to spark a new debate about algorithmic justice in transformative services. Moreover, when theorising algorithmic justice, we identified and demonstrated the role of IS-specific issues of datafication, ongoing technological inscribing, and the systemic nature of algorithmic justice. We see this as an important contribution to IS research which opens future opportunities for IS researchers interested in exploring the societal efects of AI and big data powered algorithms as matters of social justice or, as we propose here, algorithmic justice. As such, our work also advances our understanding of the unintended consequences of transformative service provision, which remains one of the “gnarly issues” in transformative service research (Blocker et al., 2019).

During the course of this research, we experienced significant dificulties in accessing welfare service providers. We therefore drew from publicly available secondary sources, such as UN and news media reports. Future IS research would also benefit from extending the empirical basis of this research through the collection of primary data about the deployment of AADM in DWS (and other transformative services).

While AI and algorithmic decision-making has undoubted capacity to benefit society (for example, to eficiently handle the influx of welfare claims resulting from the Covid-19 pandemic), we cannot and should not turn a blind eye to the widespread social injustices created through their deployment and use in DWS (Alston, 2019a; Alston, 2019b; Caplan et al., 2018). It is therefore, not a question of whether or not to use algorithmic decision-making but rather one of recognising the critical and contested questions of the “what”, “who” and “how” of algorithmic justice and seeking systematic means to redress these injustices. The proposed theory of algorithmic justice, we believe, is an important step towards raising awareness of these important questions and drawing both public and researchers’ interests in addressing them.
