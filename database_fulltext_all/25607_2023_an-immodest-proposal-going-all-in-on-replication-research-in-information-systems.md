# An immodest proposal – going “All in” on replication research in information systems

Alfred Benedikt Brendel<sup>a</sup>, Stephan Diederich<sup>b</sup> and Fred Niederman<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Technische Universitat Dresden, Faculity of Business and Economics, Chair of Business Informatics, Esp. Intelligent Systems and Services, Dresden, Germany; <sup>b</sup>Faculty of Business and Economics, Chair of Information Management, University of Goettingen, Goettingen, Germany; <sup>c</sup>Decision Sciences and MIS, Cook School of Business, Saint Louis University, St. Louis, Missouri, USA.

## ABSTRACT

Genuine theory testing, also under the term “replication research”, is rarely found in Information Systems (IS) research. Based on a replication’s results, our understanding or representation of the original theory is updated. This function of replication is integral to a scientific discipline to accumulate confidence in the explanatory power of a theory and to weed out theories that did not withstand scrutiny. However, many new theories are only tested once in the original study, while subsequent studies reference it without explicitly questioning the original theory. Against this background, we examine three aspects of the current state of replication studies in IS: (1) how many explicit replication studies in key IS research outlets have been published; (2) the attitudes of senior scholars, mostly prestigious IS journals’ editors, towards publishing replication research in their journals; and (3) a new policy proposal towards replication research broadly across all journals. We propose that all journals should take responsibility for publishing replications of theory or empirical results that have formerly been presented in that journal.

## 1. Introduction

Demonstrated limitations on replicating critical studies or finding support for fundamental theories has recently brought criticism and concern, most notably in the area of psychology, which questions a large number of publications regarding their results and the developed theories (APS, 2015; Fanelli, 2009; Stroebe & Strack, 2014). Sensible advice would be not to assume all theories are necessarily “true”, even if published in a prestigious journal, but rather to take them as best statements of current knowledge. In our view, one of the underlying problems with assuming theory or findings to be confirmed once published, is the high regard for “novelty” as a criterion for publishing in prestigious journals (e.g., Baskerville, 2009; Kohli et al., 2011). In some circles, showing support for a theory is considered a lesser activity than finding some reason to create a new theory or borrow one from another field. As a result, the field becomes top heavy with theories that are thinly supported.

This problem can be addressed by a scientific process model consisting of a cycle of theorising and empirical testing, which on the one hand summarises current levels of knowledge into representations of what is known, and on the other hand compares projected findings of predictively robust theories to actual findings based on observation or experiment. Such testing provides results that support, refute, or are mixed relative to predictions based on the instantiating theory. The more we accumulate supportive findings, the more we increase confidence in the theory as useful for application in practice and in future inquiry (National Academies of Sciences – Engineering – Medicine, 2019). However, some view the cycle model of theorising-testing as an idealistic template that does not accurately describe actual scientific practice and advancement. Nevertheless, we contend that the theory-testing cycle underlies an important branch of science as Popper (1963) described it. This cycle is consistent with Deming’s (2000) general formulation of methods for continuous improvement applied to knowledge development rather than to manufacturing. Notably, this cycle of scientific inquiry can be applied across all disciplines, from physics to sociology; IS, therefore, is one such area of application (Brown et al., 2016; Dennis & Valacich, 2014; Niederman & March, 2014).

Establishing a scientific method, such as the theorytesting cycle, requires robust theorising and testing as part of the field’s fixed and routine practices. With this in mind, our study begins by assessing the current level of replication and testing in information systems (IS) research as an honoured and inclusive part of knowledge accumulation in mainstream processes.

In the following sections, we start by clarifying what we mean by theory and theorising, and we note the variety of forms that replication can take. Next, we present selected results of an initial literature review we did to present a description of the current state of replication in the IS domain. The portion on which we focus deals with the number of explicit replication studies published in IS literature. Having presented the count, i.e., the “how many” of replications, we proceed to present the results of interviews with senior IS scholars. In an efort to document current attitudes regarding replication research, we state the rationale for this kind of research, and discuss some of the factors inhibiting its expansion. Finally, we give our own recommendations and a summary of the issues involved, also explaining our commitment to the positions we take in this essay.

## 2. Background and terms

Before progressing to the “how many” and “why” of replication studies, it is important to note what the key terms of theory, theorising, replication, and falsification refer to.

## 2.1. Theory

In science there are many notions of theory (e.g., Gregor, 2006; Weber, 2012). In her highly cited taxonomy, Gregor (2006) presents five categories of theory summarised as: (1) analysis, (2) prediction, (3) explanation, (4) prediction and explanation, and (5) design theory. In this study, we follow a minimalist view on theory (Niederman 2021), based on Weber’s (2012) theory concept. Weber (2012) defines theory as one or more related representations (usually a statement, a mathematical equation, or a line and box drawing) which present a set of entities and name the relationship(s) among them. The statement has to be framed in a way that is potentially falsifiable, ideally stating the boundaries within which it is thought to apply (and outside of which it is expected NOT to apply).

Weber (2012) and Popper (1959, 1963) have asserted the necessity for theory to be represented in a way that can be falsified. Such representation allows for the possibility of gathering counter evidence. In some cases, a theory cannot be tested because there is no instrumentation to support collecting empirical evidence. However, a theory should be formulated in such a way that instrumentation can be created for collecting data that will either support or refute the representation.

## 2.2. Theorising

The major processes associated with theory, namely theorising, correspond to creation and testing. A new theory can originate in various possible ways. It can be imagined deductively with or without evidence (Siponen & Tsohou, 2018), or derived as an abstraction from data or observation. Weick (1995) proposes a distinction between a theory and a theorising process. He wrestled with the problem that a theoretical representation is not necessarily a stable entity. As researchers accumulate test results, additional information updates the representation and indicates how much confidence we should have in it (e.g., how much support the representation has). Such new information also shows whether the theoretical statement’s details remain accurate for prediction and explanation. Following Feyerabend (1975), one should give up the idea of ever arriving at a final universally true proposition, but at a given point in time a set of our best eforts can be stated. The process of theorising that Weick (1995) describes, very closely resembles the cycle of stating current knowledge as a theory, and provides for performing various rigorous tests to support or improve it.

Against this background, even without any empirical support, a theoretical representation is a theory, but one that has not earned much confidence yet. With each iteration and each test, as its components and relationships are refined, the theory’s level of support changes and it evolves towards reliability. Together, these describe a process that consists of (1) proposing theory, (2) testing theory, and (3) improving or replacing theory to better explain the investigated phenomena (Popper, 1959, 1963; Salovaara & Merikivi, 2015). Journals may have a formal or informal policy of requiring that at least one test accompany a proposed theory; however, even with the very best of intentions, self-tested theory cannot match the potency of testing by independent scholars working at arm’s length.

## 2.3. Replication

Studies aimed at testing prior results or theory come in multiple shapes and sizes, and can be categorised in various ways (e.g., Berthon et al., 2002; Dennis & Valacich, 2014; Greulich & Brendel, 2019; Niederman & March, 2014). Gomez et al. (2014) provide a significant amount of detail in distinguishing between diferent types of replication studies. An important distinction is based on the degree to which replications match the initial study, ranging from exact duplication to testing the more abstract theory while formulating the particular study diferently. Diferences occurring in attempted exact replication studies are likely to show either systematic variance in samples or in research technique. Diferences in broad theory testing replication are likely also to reflect varied assumptions and perspectives, diferences in related research questions framing, and alternative conceptualisation of the practice domain.

Berthon et al. (2002) describe diferent types of studies based on an increase in “degrees of freedom”. They consider a “pure” replication to have “zerodegree-of-freedom”, because the study intends to reproduce the original one as exactly as possible. Alternatively, studies can follow the “one-degree-offreedom” model by keeping all variables constant excepting a single dimension – either context or method. Another variation involves approaching the same problem, but using an alternative theory. This can show that only the original theory addresses the question, that either theory works equally well, or that while the original theory does address the problem, the alternative, in some way, does it better. Further, Berthon et al. (2002) discuss extension and pure generation studies. In some cases, an extension study could embed a replication. For example, if a study tests a nomological model used in prior work with an added variable, reporting the model’s results without the added variable could be viewed as a test of the original model. One could presume, however, that adding the new variable in itself can change the pattern of subjects’ responses, which distances this sort of test from the original one. Researchers could also compare the original and changed models as alternative theories, contrasted for their efectiveness in addressing the same problem. Certainly, most studies with extensions of extant models do not report on such a test, and, if they do, they rarely emphasise it. Finally, Berthon et al. (2002) discuss purely generative studies (i.e., ones that develop fundamentally new theories) which would most likely be considered outside a replication study’s scope.

## 2.4. Falsification

Popper (1963) argued that theory testing where results are essentially supportive, has no value. He reasoned that even after a thousand positive tests, the very next test could reject the previous conclusions. Therefore, a positive test is seen not to bring the kind of certainty that warrants acceptance of the theory. Although we understand this logic, we believe that supportive findings increase a statement’s support level and justifies an increased level of confidence in the theory. We can have representations that describe the empirical world where future results, at some level of probability, will mirror the past results (Godfrey-Smith, 2003; Hempel, 1966; Olbrich et al., 2017). In contrast, ambiguous or refuting results suggest various other possibilities for further illuminating the dynamics of the phenomenon (Dennis & Valacich, 2014; Salovaara & Merikivi, 2015). Then it follows that testing studies should be “neutral’ in not being predisposed to either support or refute a theory.

## 3. Investigating replication research in the information systems community

As outlined above, we want to look more closely at the size and strength of the theory-test scientific cycle within IS research. Against the above background, we first consider published replication studies, to answer the following question:

How extensively have replication studies been published in IS literature?

Building on the first question’s answer and our observations, we challenge our own assumptions and interpretation of the publication data by addressing a second question:

What are leading IS scholars’ prevailing attitudes regarding conducting and publishing replication research?

In the following two sub-sections, we briefly present two research approaches (literature review and expert interviews) and the related results.

## 3.1. How extensively have replication studies been published in IS literature?

To review how extensively IS research has engaged replication, we conducted a literature review in September and October of 2018, based on the approaches of Webster and Watson (2002), Vom Brocke et al. (2009), and Bandara et al. (2015) (see Table 1). Appendix A gives details of the research process.

The literature review reveals a substantial lack of explicitly identified replication research in the IS research field. Our sample consisted of 47 articles, with the majority of studies coming from Association for Information Systems Transactions on Replication Research (TRR) and Americas Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS) proceedings. We found only two such studies in A-ranked journals (Aguirre-Urreta & Marakas, 2012; Adams et al., 1992), both published in Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ). In comparison, the neuroscience field has more than 50 replication studies annually in a single outlet not even dedicated to replication research (Greulich & Brendel, 2019).

Further, we noted the number of replication studies recently increased: 35 of our sample’s 47 studies were published in 2015 or later. The average number of replication studies per annum increased from fewer than one before 2015 to a maximum of 13 in 2018. After the TRR was established in 2014, the number of replication studies increased considerably, so that this kind of research was counted as an innovation in the discipline. We could interpret this as an inflection point at which interest in replication began to expand. However, the renewed interest could also indicate the start of a growth and shrinkage cycle, or it could be a blip in interest waxing before waning again.

## 3.2. What are leading IS scholars’ prevailing attitudes regarding conducting and publishing replication research?

Finding very few replication studies in the IS field, raised the question as to why this was the case, and even whether others share our interpretation of the data. Therefore, to challenge our observations and to understand the prevailing climate among leading IS scholars, we undertook a second study using qualitative means to investigate researchers’ attitudes and views regarding replication. We interviewed 16 scholars who were current or past editors of the AIS’s basket of eight journals (see Table 2). For details on the interview process, please see Appendix D.

Scholars highlighted a number of concerns regarding replication studies, some of which might explain why so few are published, others suggest hurdles that might need clearing before more replication studies will be published. Still other scholars find the barriers to publishing replication studies so severe that to address them is more dificult, more expensive, or more distracting from other battles than would justify undertaking such efort. Below, we summarise these discussions. For each question our participants dealt with, we present a selection of quotes from the interviews and our own comments.

## 3.3. Is the low number of explicit replication studies problematic?

We found broad agreement that replication research is scantly published in the IS domain, but for some this did not constitute a problem, particularly where the lack of publications could represent a de facto community “vote” against their inclusion.

Two pairs of ‘basket publications’ in 26 years is a very small number and a pretty compelling tombstone [for replication research].

(Scholar 6.1)

I don’t see a lot of evidence for having problems with replication [in the IS community] at the moment.

(Scholar 14)

This view is counterbalanced by others who support more replication and specifically point out that such studies are important to identify varied human behaviours within and across settings. Accumulating repli cations (and following from the previous discussion even just independent studies on the same phenomenon) can help identify the range of ways in which humans can and do interact with technology. The result can be stronger substantiation of a theory’s eficacy, but with a narrowed down specification of when and to whom it applies.

[. . .] replication is important to all sciences, and even more so to social sciences like Information Systems, where the object of study, humans, does not behave consistently in all settings.

(Scholar 4.1)

Motivation for replication studies also refers to concern about IS research experiencing a crisis similar to the field of psychology where many very well accepted theories could not be replicated (Fanelli, 2009; APS, 2015; Stroebe & Strack, 2014).

## (Scholar 15)

In their discussions, some respondents did not reflect the careful definitions of replication studies found in the literature (Berthon et al., 2002; Dennis & Valacich, 2014). For example, some scholars did not diferentiate between a replication and another paper covering the same topic or theory. Given the depth and breadth of issues regarding the phenomena, methods, trends, and reference disciplines in IS literature and the scarcity of replication studies, it is understandable that even leaders in the field could conceptualise the definition and categories of replication imprecisely.

We don’t have nearly 5.000 unique papers that don’t build on, leverage, or replicate prior findings on related topics. [. . .] Well, for instance, there are a ton of TAM [Technology Acceptance Model] papers. [. . .] In addition to TAM, there are many additional streams – task-technology fit, [. . .] Media Richness, [. . .] TPB [Theory of Planned Behavior].

## (Scholar 2)

Comments such as the above suggest we should consider whether something less than replication, perhaps just varied approaches to the same topic, might sufice for representing the field’s accumulated knowledge. However, we argue that without specific testing eforts, we would end up with an even broader array of theories and findings than we currently have, that cannot be substantiated. It is, of course, a matter of contention whether such a collection of unrelated work would eventually shape itself into a coherent image of the domain, or whether an integrated view of a domain is important or valuable at all.

## 3.4. Does new support for prior findings make any contribution?

We have argued above that replication as testing is an essential part of the theory-test cycle scientific method. A field that only generates new propositions or extends extant ones without testing them, misses an important component of the cycle. If replication studies are needed in a field, a question about each individual study’s contribution arises. It seems logical that if there is a collective value, each study that adds to it would be making a contribution, but how much and in what way?

[. . .] when we think about theoretical contributions, then it is dificult for pure replication studies that test the same thing to make a theoretical contribution [. . .] It is diferent if we have this relationship and can then show that under particular circumstances, it is no longer true. Or it is diferent, or it depends on certain circumstances whether the findings are actually valid. That would be an actual contribution.

## (Scholar 16)

This comment seems to mirror Popper’s suggestion that only refutation has value in theory testing, although the respondent could just have meant that refutation has more value than mere confirmation. We disagree with the idea that a study’s contribution depends on whether findings are supportive, mixed, or refuting. Rewarding only one kind of finding as being eligible for publication, presents a moral concern. We suggest that supportive findings constitute a contribution that should afect our level of confidence in the theory. Of course, no number of positive findings erase the possibility that the next test could provide refuting evidence.

Negative versus positive results. I don’t think there should be any incentive to have one outcome over another. [. . .] I soundly reject the notion that one is more than the other. [. . .] The problem is, there are people who do p-hacking. So, we already have ridiculous incentives to have results.

(Scholar 10)

## 3.5. How many replications of a particular theory are enough?

In our discussion, one scholar obliquely asked when the number of replications of a particular theory or result would be suficient. Where does the field stop producing or publishing new replications of the same theory or phenomenon? In this scholar’s view, publishing even one entirely supportive replication would be a disincentive for anyone else to perform a second replication.

You replicated, and you find the exact same thing. [. . .] There is not going to be another researcher who is going to say, ‘oh wow, that was exactly the way it was supposed to be. Here, let me try it again. Oh well, mine is exactly where it is supposed to be.’ So, it closes the door.

(Scholar 11)

The disincentive for others to conduct the next replication could be a valid concern. If a first replication shows identical findings, this might dissuade others from doing a second, third, or “nth” replication. In a field changing as rapidly as IS, researchers periodically continuing to test whether the findings still hold, would not be surprising. Where replications deliver varying results, we would anticipate researchers showing a higher level of interest in examining contingencies that explain the variances. This could fine-tune aspects that would heighten confidence regarding where the theories actually apply. We expect to gain information on whether wide-spread, emerging phenomena of high economic consequence draw more replication eforts than others. 3In contrast, replications that are dificult, expensive, or require severe control might be less frequent.

## 3.6. Can “fishing” for the right theory substitute testing?

One respondent commented on what it means for a theory “not to work”.

As a researcher, I may pick up a theory that I think is going to work for a particular research question, and if it doesn’t work, then I throw it away and get a diferent one. [. . .] [As a replication study, it might be interesting] to give some more thought to “why didn’t I use that theory?” and in some cases, I might actually have collected some evidence and have some indication that it’s not working.

(Scholar 8)

At face value this statement implies that explaining an IS phenomenon is a matter of finding the right one among many possible theories, and then gathering data consistent with the theory’s predictions. One strategy would be for the researcher to collect or propose multiple theories and gathering empirical evidence to test the viability of each and compare outcomes. It may turn out, for example, that one predicts with more precision, but the other segregates instances more reliably. This would suggest trade-ofs and complexity that, depending on the importance of the topic, could merit further investigation.

## 3.7. Why are replication studies not suficiently rewarded?

Purely pragmatically, incentives for scholars to perform research are varied. There is the intrinsic value of finding the answer to a question (i.e., curiosity). Most scholars, however, also like some extrinsic reward for their efort. Such incentives can be multi-layered, including the career value of having a paper published, knowing the ideas are being downloaded, cited and used either in academia or in practice, and consequently gaining prestige and recognition in the scholarly community. However, currently, and as a number of scholars pointed out, there is little extrinsic incentive for conducting replication research.

[There is] not much in terms of a reward for doing replication research in our field.

(Scholar 11)

Any author who sent a paper in to one of the leading journals and conferences that was framed as a “replication” over the past 30 years, would probably result in a desk rejection.

(Scholar 2)

This leads to the notorious chicken and egg problem. If replications are going to be desk rejected, why undertake them? If nobody submits them, they cannot be published. If there were a collective desire to increase the number of published replication studies, it would most likely begin with editors or editorial teams issuing a call for such studies, followed by an active review process and actual publication. Such calls would signal a relatively increased publication probability, changing how scholars calculate the risk of working on a particular project, so that they would consider adding such a study to their agenda. The active review process can address issues of working out which submissions are worthy of publication, how initial submissions could be enhanced, and delivering guidelines for publishable articles.

## 3.8. Is it suficient to relegate all replication studies to specialist outlets?

Because the bulk of replication studies has been published in conference proceedings and in a single journal dedicated to this sort of work, we should ponder whether this is the best way to serve the IS community. On the one hand, the AIS sponsored journal signals a community commitment to some level of replication studies. It is an open question whether such a journal can publish enough studies to meet the demand for thorough testing of the range of IS theory. On the other hand, relegating replications to a single outlet could lead to an unread “ghetto” where the work can be paraded for show as a sort of Potemkin village, with limited actual influence on the accumulated body of knowledge.

I think it is easier to have a separate journal for replications, at least in the short term, because the acceptance criteria for replications are so much different – [. . .] [then we could] ignore the results and just ask if the methods are right.

(Scholar 7)

Published in a non-basket journal and you’ll get a small number of citations. So, if we condemn these if we put them in this prison of a non-basket journal, then they won’t get cited very much. We’ve done that to ourselves.

(Scholar 10)

The alternative, however, would be for replications to be published in a broad range of journals. Currently, such publication has little precedent in the IS field, as further comments can exemplify.

Well, the basic philosophy of most, if not all of the journals in that basket [of eight], is they want things that are new and novel. And things that people have not done before. And so, replication by definition is something people have done before.

(Scholar 11)

I am now going to invest my ideas, my time, my creativity in just re-running the exact same study, even if I expect that the results will be the same.[. . .] So, [if] there is something that has been unexplored, I am really interested in that. I want to disprove, right? But I am not going into this like, ‘this is super interesting; I am going to do exactly what they have done.

(Scholar 16)

This is an important point to consider from a number of perspectives. There is no doubt that the concept of novelty is prominent in editorial decisions and supportive results on a replication may not appear to be novel (even if the level of confidence in the theory is changed). But the comment runs deeper to suggest that if the same results are expected, there might not be much intrinsic interest in testing that particular theory. We all have limited resources, and we cannot envision a scenario where scholars would or should be forced to do replications. If incentivised to do replications, we would hope that scholars would weigh the costs against the potential benefits and be free to do the studies of their choice. We can imagine scenarios where even if modestly incentivised, replication studies would be appealing. If a scholar has doubts about the probability of a test generating identical results, the potential for replacing, augmenting, or even cautioning about its use could generate a diferent level of intrinsic interest. Another scholar might want to practice and master a methodological technique and use the ability to replicate exact results as a measure of surehandedness in applying the appropriate procedures. A third situation where replication studies could flourish, pertains to universities with large doctoral programmes where replication studies can be undertaken by student groups or students with mentors as part of the educational and training process. Additionally, scholars with limited resources could profit from undertaking high quality replications, rather than attempting to initiate novel research streams of lesser quality.

For better or for worse, scepticism can be as strong a motivator as curiosity. Referring to the debunked cold fusion experiments of 1989 (Browne, 1989), one respondent countered the case for support through replication from the field of physics. After Fleischmann and Pons reported their success in achieving cold fusion in 1989, many other scholars tried to replicate their results and failed.

[. . .] we do not have that culture where somebody comes up with something amazing and then immediately have a dozen people think ‘oh I got to replicate that because that is too good to be true.’

(Scholar 11)

One risk is that a field can become so sceptical that a culture of neutral testing shifts to one of cut-throat eforts to tear down other scholars and their work. As researchers, we should avoid such overwhelming cynicism in how we spend our working lives. From another angle, some may be concerned that with enough testing, a field will demonstrably have NO central and confirmed theory, so that the discipline appears to be devoid of content. Although this possibility cannot be dismissed, we suggest a view of science holding that all theory is somewhat tentative. Then, a robust discipline filled with theorising and testing can accumulate knowledge that at any given time presents the best representation of knowledge at hand.

## 4. Our immodest proposal

Having already committed to the value replication has for the field, considering the literature review’s findings and our interviews with scholars of whom most are editors and past editors of the basket of eight journals, we developed the following proposal.

We propose that ALL journals publishing theory or a-theoretical empirical results, should publish replication studies that address what they have already published. Our rationale for this is the belief that every journal should take responsibility for distributing the most up to date version of the content they have endorsed (i.e., published). Sadly, many scholars seem to treat published findings as uncontestably “true” since they have been screened by a leading journal. We believe those promoting findings of any kind should be open to revising or reinforcing them.

Our proposal is that all journals should at least publish replication studies of findings presented in their own earlier volumes and be obliged (also not forbidden) to publish replications of original material from alternative sources. One scholar’s comment captures this succinctly:

[. . .] every major journal should have a replication section. [. . .] [Also, such studies should] to have replication in the title. [. . .] It would be a very short three- or four-page paper about how you replicated [the study and the results]. [. . .] All details would just be online.

(Scholar 10)

Just as many journals have research notes, we can have replication sections where publication findings are given in a format of which the length is commensurate with explaining the testing method, the results, and their implications. In addition to just methods and findings, we would also want to see some discussion or explanation of the relationship between the originally published and the replication study findings. This discussion might not be definitive but should lead to possibly new understandings being considered and to future investigation.

We expect such publishing over time to result in an evolving set of fewer but stronger theories and more robust knowledge. We further expect researchers will examine IS phenomena that were assumed to be explained, using the same borrowed theory as they search for commonalities and more abstract theorising. Also, there is likely to be a growing movement of theory creators or a-theoretical researchers subtly shifting their procedures and discussion with the expectation of replication in mind. One should be wary of an unfortunate unintended consequence of rigid publication barriers to any study privileging the replicators while limiting publication opportunities to interesting and eventually worthy but possibly flawed initial studies.

We have no illusions about this article resulting in all journals immediately changing course to create replication sections; however, if at some point the practice were to become widespread, we expect the need for a dedicated journal such as TRR could disappear. This would certainly be a pity, as this pioneering efort and its stalwart editors deserve continued recognition. Still, as with the first stage of rocket launching after which the booster drops away, it can take pride in having enabled the eventual successful mission.

Some issues, such as reaching a saturation point where additional studies create little additional value, or establishing criteria that distinguish meritorious replication study submissions from ones that are not, will need to be resolved. The dificulty might take care of itself if researchers themselves recognise that additional studies will bring less value, or if editors signal that the window for more studies on a given theme will close permanently or temporarily.

Finally, editors should request authors to include references to replication studies along with the original study’s literature review. This would oblige authors to not only engage a theory’s propositions and statements at face value, but also to consider the need for updating what might have become stale theory or findings. We see this approach as a complement to introducing a replication research section in mainstream journals. The prospect of publishing in highly regarded outlets and being cited if the study “wins the race” for being among the first studies to replicate a recent best paper, could provide the necessary environment to establish replication research in our research community.

## 5. Conclusion

The discussion of replication research is embedded in a larger discussion of the purpose of science. While recognising it as one of many aspects of science and scientific method, we believe that the theorise-test cycle is fundamental to at least one conceptualisation of science, and it is promising as a general template for accumulating knowledge. Following Berthon et al. (2002) and Dennis and Valacich (2014), we see a variety of replication types as direct and efective mechanisms for testing theory and a-theoretical study results. Publishing initial and replicated results transforms individual proprietary knowledge into group public knowledge that becomes accessible for use and continuous improvement. However, our investigation shows that only a few publications in IS have presented replication research.

We, therefore, call for increased publishing of replication research. However, we understand that what is published for a community like IS researchers, is a product of many people’s efort and many conceptualisations of what counts as helpful, achievable, valuable, and publishable. Thus, we engaged with experienced scholars, the majority of whom were, or currently are, editors of highly regarded IS journals. Their attitudes to replication overall include (1) not recognising its value and viewing it largely as making insuficient contribution to justify publication, (2) recognising potential value, but also barriers like too few incentives for individuals to conduct such research, and (3) recognising real value, but not enough to take up much space in prestigious journals. We understand these views and support eforts to accommodate legitimate concerns.

Even as we consider these views, we reafirm our commitment to replication research as a central and valued part of the IS body of knowledge. We call particularly for every journal that publishes theory or a-theoretical study findings, to take responsibility for updating knowledge by including replication studies. We further emphasise that such publication should not favour positive, negative, or mixed findings as each has a role in updating current knowledge of the underlying theory. We do not expect every replication to justify a full article, especially if the coverage is very similar to the original on which it is based, therefore, we suggest an alternative minimalist structure comparable more to a research note than to a full article. Although some will propose criteria for accepting and rejecting particular replication studies, we expect community norms will only become efective once individual judgements on many individual pieces have been synchronised.

Finally, much of the theorising in IS has been borrowed from other fields such as psychology. Although, as a discipline, psychology has been embarrassed by failed replications of some of its major theories, we believe that the issue is not the range of theories in the field that need to be further investigated and improved, but rather that many promising theories have not been adequately tested and bolstered. The lesson the IS community should take here is to conduct a stronger (and hopefully more efective) programme to incorporate testing, and particularly replication, in its body of knowledge. Eforts should go towards modifying other theories to account more accurately for the full range of data, to find contingencies that either created new theory or could distinguish theory applied in diferent circumstances. Also, replication could assist in setting significantly refuted theory aside for later examination with fresh ideas, new methods, and evolving technology (and human interaction with such technology).
