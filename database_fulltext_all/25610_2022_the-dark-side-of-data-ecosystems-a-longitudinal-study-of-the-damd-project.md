# The dark side of data ecosystems: A longitudinal study of the DAMD project

Jon Aaen <sup>a</sup>, Jeppe Agger Nielsen<sup>a</sup> and Andrea Carugati<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Aalborg University, Department of Politics & Society, Aalborg, Denmark; <sup>b</sup>Department of Management, Aarhus BSS, Aarhus University, Aarhus, Denmark

## ABSTRACT

Data are often vividly depicted as strategic assets that organisations can (re)use to create value for myriad purposes. However, the same qualities that make data so appreciated – that is, their volume, their value for a plurality of stakeholders and their indefinite reuse capacity – also have a dark side: data reuse can lead to deviant data use that undermines the legitimacy of data analytics initiatives. To investigate this dynamic, we build on the notion of data ecosystems and provide empirical evidence from a longitudinal, 15-year case study of the emergence, expansion and eventual collapse of a large-scale data analytics project – called DAMD – in the Danish health care sector. We demonstrate that data reuse in the evolving data ecosystem elicited a data reuse dark side that was so dominant that it eventually resulted in the project’s demise. We conceptualise the reuse dark side’s three major mechanisms as function creep, stakeholder creep and data creep. Based on these insights, we develop an empirically grounded Data Analytics Ecosystem Model that extends the current understanding of data ecosystems and provides a view of these ecosystems as having both a bright and a dark side.

KEYWORDS Data analytics; data reuse; data ecosystems; dark side; longitudinal case study

## 1. Introduction

In the digital era, organisations collect, store and process immense volumes of data (Chen et al., 2012; Vidgen et al., 2017) and use data analytics – also known as today’s “Digital Oil” (Sivarajah et al., 2017; Yi et al., 2014) – to create value from data for various purposes, such as improved decision-making (Brynjolfsson et al., 2011) and process optimisation (Abbasi et al., 2016). Organisations are also increasingly encouraged to engage in data analytics initiatives both to obtain and protect data assets (Perrons & Jensen, 2015), as well as to explore and exploit their data-driven opportunities (Benfeldt et al., 2020).

Despite these optimistic prescriptions for data (re)use, the topic has also received considerable criticism. In particular, data analytics possess an inherent dark side because data are imperishable resources but can be reused repeatedly for multiple purposes, including those beyond the original scope (Mayer-Schönberger & Cukier, 2013). While data reusability is likely to provide new and exciting results (Chen et al., 2012; Lycett, 2013; Sharma et al., 2014), it can also lead to deviant uses (Zubof, 2015). Thus, paradoxically, the same assets that make data so appreciated – that is, their volume, their value for a plurality of stakeholders and their indefinite reuse capacity – can also undermine privacy, commodify human behaviour, and result in comprehensive control and surveillance (Taylor, 2017; Zubof, 2015).

In contrast to the prevailing, enthusiastic view of data and data analytics, our paper deals with its dark side by analysing the pitfalls of reusing data for multiple purposes far beyond what was originally envisioned. To illustrate this, we report the results of a longitudinal case study of the emergence, expansion and surprising collapse of a large-scale data analytics initiative in the Danish health care sector. That initiative, the Danish General Practitioners Database (or DAMD, for Dansk Almen Medicinsk Database) was a multimillion-dollar undertaking aimed at collecting and analysing health care data on all patients associated with general practitioners (GPs) – which, in Denmark, covers the entire population. As we show here, the DAMD project was well on its way to becoming very successful; the database aforded a wide variety of new actions that attracted a growing number of stakeholders who issued an increasing number of requests for the data analytics system. Health professionals called the database a “golden egg” (D1<sup>1</sup>), while researchers deemed it “indispensable” (D2) and the pharmaceutical industry viewed it as a “gamechanger”. Nonetheless, as the DAMD’s success grew and its light became brighter, so, too, did its dark side. The project’s initial goals were increasingly stretched as data were reused in new contexts, creating dissatisfaction among certain stakeholders. This frustration grew unnoticed until it erupted in considerable media fire and political attention, forcing the project’s sudden 2015 shutdown due to illegal data collection and sharing. This is not an isolated case. Cambridge

Analytica (Larson & Vieregger, 2019) and Google’s Nightingale project shared similar dramatic dynamics (Ledford, 2019), while Facebook and Twitter are often under fire for their data reuse practices (Lima & Overly, 2020). The dramatic fate of the DAMD and other projects raises two key questions: How could a seemingly successful data analytics project with so many potential benefits collapse? What dynamics brought about this outcome? In this paper, we address this conundrum guided by the following research question: What are the dynamics of data reuse, and how does it impact data analytics projects?

To inform our focus on data reuse dynamics, we draw on the notion of data ecosystems, in which diverse actors interact and collaborate to collect, share and reuse data (Oliveira et al., 2019). In doing so, we stress that data analytics cannot be fully grasped by looking solely at data or at individual organisations, but rather it should instead be examined as a system of multiple actors (re)using data for ever more purposes. Grounded in the in-depth empirical account of the DAMD project, we show that three ecosystem mechanisms – function creep, stakeholder creep and data creep – fuelled both the DAMD’s value and the problems that ultimately resulted in its demise. Based on these insights, we propose a Data Analytics Ecosystem Model rooted in the DAMD case, which explains the concurrent existence of the bright and dark sides of data reuse within ecosystems. By implication, we contribute to a more complete understanding of data ecosystems and data analytics that provides a balanced view of these ecosystems as having both a bright and a dark side.

Here, we first review and discuss current data analytics literature relevant for our discourse on data reuse and data ecosystems. Next, we describe our case context and research methods, and then present our empirical findings. Finally, we outline the implications of our findings for theory and practice.

## 2. Literature background: data reuse and data ecosystems

The data analytics literature generally depicts data as a resource to be processed into value for various stakeholders (Lycett, 2013). Data difer from traditional resources in three crucial aspects. First, data are not depleted by usage, but rather increase in availability and volume over time. This is a focal point in the big data literature, which portrays the surges of data and increased processing capabilities as disruptions to the information value chain (Abbasi et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2012). Second, data are persistent and are rarely deleted once they enter the digital infrastructure (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Crawford et al., 2014). Third, data are agnostic in that they can be repurposed and reused in new contexts – unrelated to their intended value and long after it has been tapped (Constantiou & Kallinikos, 2015; Mayer-Schönberger & Cukier, 2013). Together, these three characteristics enable extreme forms of data reuse and make data valuable for numerous stakeholders.

## 2.1. Data reuse

Data analytics is rooted in an increased awareness that data collected for one purpose can be reused for many others (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Baesens et al., 2016). This enables multiple stakeholders to collaborate, share and exploit vast amounts of data for new products, services and business models (Van den Broek & van Veenstra, 2018). For example, as Baesens et al. (2016) illustrate, sensors in cars provide real-time data to drivers that vehicle managing and insurance companies can also use to make better decisions via data analytics and predictive modelling. Another example is the use of Google search terms to predict flu trends; although the original collection of those data was clearly purposeful, their later use was not envisioned at collection time (Lazer et al., 2014). Given its emerging “tsunami of data” (Chen et al., 2012), health care is another example of a sector in which data (e.g., from electronic health records or biobank registries) that can be reused for multiple purposes, including predictive modelling, patient care and cost reduction (Raghupathi & Raghupathi, 2014).

Consequently, while data analytics can improve strategic decision-making, add new functionalities to large-scale enterprise systems (Baesens et al., 2016) and so on, it often unfolds where the valuecreation potential lies: in cross-organisational settings, where data are merged, shared and reused across organisational entities, departments and sectors (Constantiou & Kallinikos, 2015; Van den Broek & van Veenstra, 2018). Accordingly, the transformational impact of data analytics cannot be fully grasped by looking solely at the data or at individual organisations. Instead, it must be studied as a system of multiple organisational entities (re)using data for various purposes.

## 2.2. Data ecosystems

To establish a strong foundation for understanding data reuse dynamics across multiple organisational entities, we turn to the data ecosystems concept. Originating in biology, the ecosystem metaphor has gained increasing attention in information systems (IS) research (Guggenberger et al., 2020) for conceptualising value-creating networks, including business ecosystems (Moore, 1993), software ecosystems (Manikas & Hansen, 2013), platform ecosystems (Gawer & Cusumano, 2013) and digital innovation ecosystems (Wang, 2021). Such conceptualisations reflect an increased understanding of value creation as interconnected, collaborative processes with organisational boundaries that are fluid and dispersed (Guggenberger et al., 2020; Wang, 2021).

Recently, the data ecosystems notion has been gaining traction in IS research (Oliveira et al., 2019), where Oliveira et al. (2019) define it as “sociotechnical complex networks in which actors interact and collaborate with each other to find, archive, publish, consume, or reuse data as well as to foster innovation, create value, and support new businesses” (p. 589). Accordingly, data ecosystems not only consist of the digital infrastructure and systems used to collect, store, share and analyse data (e.g., Demchenko et al., 2014; Sumbaly et al., 2013), but they also encompass a network of organisational actors (Calzada & Almirall, 2020). Consequently, the ecosystem perspective focuses not on data analytics in individual organisations, but rather on interactions among multiple organisational entities collecting, sharing and reusing data.

While the emerging data ecosystems research has studied its promising potential in organisations and institutions collaborating and co-innovating to create value (Azkan et al., 2020; Gelhaar & Otto, 2020; Oliveira et al., 2019; De Prieëlle et al., 2020), an ecosystem perspective also allows for a critical understanding of the politics that may emerge as data are shared, repurposed and reused across organisational settings. Boyd and Crawford (2012) note that diferences in data access, control and application create a fundamentally unequal structure among data subjects, controllers, and processors. Thus, data ecosystems may entail power imbalances (Someh et al., 2019) between those who create data (both consciously and by leaving digital footprints), those who have the means to collect it and those who have the expertise to analyse it. As such, while the data ecosystems perspective is new and undertheorised, it appears to be a well-suited starting point for improving our understanding of both the bright and dark sides of data reuse.

## 3. Method

We ground our understanding of data reuse dynamics in a longitudinal case study of the DAMD project’s emergence, expansion and collapse in the Danish health care sector. Following Siggelkow (2007), we suggest that longitudinal research designs are particularly useful in providing the details needed to study the underlying dynamics of data reuse and data ecosystems as they unfold over time. We now present the research setting, along with our data collection and analytical approaches.

## 3.1. Research setting and case

In keeping with the Scandinavian welfare model’s basic principal, the Danish health care system is based on equal and free access to health care for all citizens. Approximately 84% of health care expenditures are financed by general taxes (D4), and the state is responsible for the overall health care regulatory and administrative functions. Denmark’s five regions are in charge of the nation’s hospitals, while the 98 municipalities are responsible for various primary health care services, including health promotion, rehabilitation outside hospitals and home nursing. GPs (family doctors) are the patients’ primary contact points to the health care system and the gatekeeper between sectors.

In recent decades, Denmark has advanced significantly in the digitisation of its health care (Nielsen et al., 2014), and its extensive digitised work procedures and communication among health care providers has enabled large-scale and systematic use of data (Andersen et al., 2019). The increased use of health information technology has produced massive amounts of patient data from medical records and from external data sources such as social media (Wang & Hajli, 2017). For GPs, this digitisation means the mandatory use of electronic health records, electronic referrals to medical specialists and hospitals, electronic delivery of all laboratory test results and electronic transmission of prescriptions to pharmacies (D4).

Within this context, the DAMD played a key role in developing an ecosystem for applying data analytics in Danish health care. The DAMD contained health care data on all patients associated with a GP (that is, all patients in the Danish system). As Figure 1 shows, the DAMD automatically retrieved diagnostic data using a data capture module installed in each GP’s local information system. The DAMD used these data inputs to generate quality reports that provided GPs with decision-making support tools and a patient overview (D7). The DAMD was operated by the Danish General Practice Quality Unit (DAK-E), a formal project organisation governed by and financed with resources from the collective agreement between the Danish Regions (an interest group for Danish Regions/hospitals) and the Danish Medical Association (an interest group for GPs). Appendix A has a list of acronyms used in the case analysis, while Appendix B ofers a detailed description of and screenshots from the DAMD system.

At its peak, the DAMD project was a frontrunner in deploying data analytics in health care. It was endorsed both nationally and internationally (D5, D6) and contained a wealth of data on almost every patient in the Danish primary health care system. However, in the autumn of 2014, data collection was blocked when disgruntled stakeholders raised doubts about the DAMD project’s legality. In a continuation of this showdown, the data-controlling authority for the database – the Southern Region of Denmark – announced that the already-collected data would be erased. Then several authorities intervened in an attempt to preserve the database, with the Danish National Archives determining that it was worth preserving and archiving. On May 122,015, however, the Danish Parliament decided that the DAMD should be deleted. Figure 2 shows a timeline of key events in the DAMD project.

## 3.3. Data analysis

We applied an inductive approach to analyse our qualitative data in three phases (Locke, 2001). First, we focused on creating a DAMD project timeline and identifying the involved stakeholders to map out the ecosystem. In this initial coding, we closely adhered to the empirical accounts of the project’s lifecycle found both in the vast number of documents and in our interviews with key informants. As Table 2 shows, to manage the DAMD case’s complexity and data density, we used a temporal bracketing strategy (Langley, 1999) to divide the evolving data ecosystem into four phases: emergence (2003–2005), formalisation (2006– 2009), expansion (2010–2014) and collapse (2014– 2015). These phases structure our empirical account and reflect event and activity progressions separated by identifiable discontinuities in the DAMD ecosystem’s development.

Second, within each phase, we coded the DAMD ecosystem’s development in terms of data collection and functions (i.e., the digital infrastructure and systems used to collect, store, share and analyse DAMD data) and the network of involved stakeholders (i.e., the organisational actors related to DAMD, their interests and concerns). To ensure consistency and internal reliability in the coding process, we conducted recurring coding sessions in which we presented, discussed and analysed empirical observations. ## 4.1.1. Stakeholders

The DAMD project initially had two stakeholder groups: GPs and the Danish Regions.

GPs. In the initial stage, GPs were the dominant stakeholder group. Their pilot project built on experiments from the 1990s, replacing paper-based diagnosis coding with digital coding. One of these GPs, who became the primary DAMD initiator and subsequently its manager, reported on these early experiments in his PhD thesis (D9). As a GP, he had experienced how GPs lacked a good overview of their patients – especially those with chronic diseases – and had therefore developed a vision for data-driven quality development:

At some point, you ask yourself, ‘Are you really as good as you think? Do you reach all of your patients? Do you give them the best possible treatment? ## 4.1.2. Data

In the pilot project, data collection was limited to data specifically about patients with diabetes and was based on voluntary GP participation. Commencing data collection involved both datafication and digitalisation processes. The first step, datafication, determined the data input and involved developing a common classification system to ensure consistent coding among GPs based on the International Classification for Primary Care (ICPC) diagnosis system. The next step, digitalisation, collected these data in an easy, eficient manner. Digitalisation involved developing a data capture module to integrate with the 12 diferent IT systems that GPs used for patient administration. The module’s development began in 2004, with funding from the Ministry of Health and The Association of Danish Pharmacies. As Figure 1 shows, the module automatically collected data from the GPs’ local IT systems and transmitted the data to the central DAMD server. The collected data consisted of unique patient identification numbers, dates, diagnoses (related to diabetes), medicinal prescriptions, medical services and laboratory codes. Project testing began in 2005 with 24 GPs in 12 clinics (D9).

## 4.1.3. Functions

The DAMD pilot project’s goal was to develop a data analytics system to collect data from GPs for processing into quality reports that included patient overviews and information relevant to GP decisionmaking; Appendix B shows an example. The data were then automatically processed into quality reports that were delivered back to the GPs involved in the pilot project. These reports consisted of descriptive patient overviews (e.g., “you have X diabetes patients”) and auto-generated decision-making support (e.g., “time to make an appointment for Mr Jensen”). This dataflow defined the first and primary function of the DAMD system.

The idea of the data capture module was to hang a mirror in front of a GP so that he/she can see that you do not only have the 10 diabetes patients that you can remember by name, but that you in fact have 56 patients, of whom 45 live up to ‘the good diabetes treatment’. However, there are, let’s say, 20% of the patients who have not yet been checked for the past two years. Then you can go ahead, designate the patients who are suboptimally treated and do something about it. This is where you raise the quality, and it motivated GPs to use DAMD. (interview, DAK-E Manager 1)

## 4.1.4. Impact on the data ecosystem

The initial phase was driven forward by a group of passionate GPs and their associated interest organisations. The engagement of other stakeholders, such as the Danish Regions, was primarily limited to financial support (D8, D11). As Figure 3 shows, in this phase, the initiative covered diabetes and only a few GPs participated.

## 4.2. Formalisation of the data ecosystem (2006– 2009)

The second phase began when the pilot project was transformed into a permanent organisation to develop, implement and operate the digital infrastructure and systems used to collect, store, share and analyse DAMD data (D12). This phase introduced new stakeholders and additional functions, and it increased the scope and extent of data collection. At this point, optimism and pioneering spirit were associated with the DAMD. Project coverage, for example, stressed the project’s unique circumstances: “DAMD has special conditions for volunteering from the GPs, and it has operating and establishment costs covered by public funds” (D15, p. 5). The second phase ended in 2009, when the DAMD data collection became mandatory for GPs and launched the next phase (expansion).

## 4.2.1. Stakeholders

In the formalisation phase, several new stakeholder groups joined the DAMD project.

The DAK-E Organisation. Encouraged by the pilot project’s positive results, the 2006 collective agreement between the Danish Regions and Danish Medical Association provided the foundation for transforming the pilot project into a formalised project organisation (DAK-E) to launch large-scale DAMD implementation.

The parties agree that the ambitions behind the pilot project have been met. We now have a solution that can support quality development in general practice. But if the goals are to be achieved in the coming period (i.e. a large-scale project), there is a need to establish a more solid structure (i.e. DAK-E) in which the regions play a more important role in implementing data-driven quality development in the health care sector. (D13, p. 4)

Establishing the DAK-E led to increased formalisation of the DAMD. The DAK-E management, secretary and IT department – including the server – were located in the Region of Southern Denmark. The organisation included 15–20 employees; additionally, five external consultants, mainly GPs, were hired to work 1–3 days a week (D15, D16).

Danish Regions. Part of the DAMD formalisation involved active participation from the regions. The Region of Southern Denmark – one of country’s five regions – was the host region and the authority responsible for the DAMD. Furthermore, a DAMD steering committee was established, with representation from the Danish Regions, the Danish Medical Association, and the Region of Southern Denmark (D14).

The Clinical Registries in Denmark (RKKP). An important part of formalising the DAMD was to classify it as a clinical quality database. This classification was needed for (continued) exemption from the legal requirement of patient consent for data collection. This introduced a new stakeholder: the RKKP, which constitutes and coordinates the national infrastructure for clinical databases in correspondence with the Danish Regions and the Ministry of Health. Eager to combine the DAMD’s data on diabetes in general practice with existing hospital databases, the regions took a much more active role in formalising and classifying the DAMD as a clinical quality database:

The extension of the database was established topdown. It was the regions’ managers who decided to establish a cross-sectorial database on diabetes. It is an important disease area with many patients. Data on diabetes in general practice were in the DAMD database, and it became possible to transfer them to our cross-sectorial diabetes database. We became—in close collaboration with the regions—the data controllers and [were] responsible for the data being used in accordance with the purpose. (interview, RKKP project manager)

In addition to exempting it from the patient consent requirement, classifying DAMD as a clinical quality database also resulted in additional economic funding from the Danish Regions to cover the DAMD’s transformation from a pilot project to a large-scale quality database. However, the DAMD had to meet additional requirements for the new classification and funding, including to expand the diseases covered in the database and increase participation among GPs (D17).

Research communities. As the DAMD’s data increased in volume and scope, the database aforded new action potentials for research communities, which became active stakeholders seeking to influence both the DAMD operation and data availability (D18, p. 150):

Initially, DAMD was really just an opportunity to retrieve and systematise patient data and send them structured back to the GPs as quality reports. However, when you have so much data, it starts to become interesting to look at data from a research perspective—a kind of big data approach. (interview, DAK-E Manager 2)

We agreed that these health data are very interesting for research. Yet, we needed some principles so that we could control what the data were used for. We should take care that the researchers did not start to be those who dictated the database content on the basis of a pure research interest because it was not a research database but a quality database. (interview, Danish Medical Association chairman)

To avoid conflicts of interest, the Quality and Research Committee was established to handle applications from research projects seeking access to the DAMD (D19, D24). The committee consisted of representatives from the universities, each of the five administrative regions, the Danish Regions, Danish Medical Association and the DAK-E organisation.

## 4.2.2. Data

Transitioning from a pilot project to a formalised system involved developing and consolidating a central database to collect data from the data capture module installed locally at the GPs. Although formalised in a top-down organisation supervised by a Steering Committee, GP participation was still optional:

It is the GPs who decide how many of their data are sent to the database. We cannot collect data from doctors’ systems without their acceptance. (D14, p. 3)

Because data validity depends on doctors diagnosing each patient contact in a consistent way, the new DAK-E organised reoccurring “coding courses” and appointed data consultants and super users for the various IT systems used by GPs (D30). This greatly improved the data collection quality and helped mobilise new GPs to participate. At the beginning of the formalisation phase, 14 clinics were connected to the DAMD. A year later, 154 clinics with 310 doctors were participating (D13) – and, by the end of the formalisation phase, approximately 250 clinics were connected (D30). Furthermore, the data collection now included patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease and cardiac insuficiency as well (D13, p. 24).

## 4.2.3. Functions

Two new functions were added as part of the formalisation of DAMD as a clinical quality database.

Exporting data to research projects. The DAMD provided rich data on general practice activities including medication prescriptions; vaccinations; laboratory registered data; analyses of height, weight, blood pressure and smoking status; medical case histories (epicrisis) and patient questionnaire data (D23). This data aforded research opportunities for a wide range of projects, such as coordination of the cancer treatment process and resource use for multi-diseases in general practice (D22). At its peak, the DAMD provided data to more than 50 research projects (D20, D21).

Exporting data for cross-sectorial quality monitoring. By exporting data to the RKKP, the DAMD supplemented existing health care databases (e.g., from hospitals) and thereby enabled cross-sectorial quality monitoring of the entire course of patient care (D6). The expected value of the cross-sectional data was high:

Getting the opportunity to link data between hospitals and GPs—it’s huge. If we only look at hospital quality, it’s simply just a small part of the treatment we are looking at. It is an exception that one has to collect data without the patient’s consent for other than treatment purposes. It is internationally unique that we can do so without the patient’s consent, but the purpose must be worthy, right? (interview, RKKP project manager)

GPs and other health professionals were already required to provide relevant data to the clinical quality databases. With the automatic data capture module, DAMD could fulfill this obligation on the GPs’ behalf in an eficient and time-saving manner (D25).

## 4.2.4. Impact on the data ecosystem

As Figure 4 shows, the formalisation phase resulted in the participation of new stakeholder groups (e.g., researchers) and additional functions, including exporting data to the clinical quality database programme and to research projects through the establishment of the multi-stakeholder Quality and Research Committee. Furthermore, the DAMD’s formalisation and classification as a clinical-quality database involved more top-down engagement at the regions’ managerial level and included various strategic requirements for future DAMD expansion.

## 4.3. Expansion of the data ecosystem (2010– 2014)

This phase began when GP participation in the DAMD became mandatory and the range of diseases included in the data collection expanded from four to 700, rapidly increasing the database’s scope and volume. In this phase, the DAMD was perceived as a successful data analytics project that aforded a wide range of new value-creating opportunities – from improving patient empowerment to supporting a more coherent health care sector to optimising health care administration and even serving as a driver of pharmaceutical industry growth. This expansion led to an escalation of requests for reusing data. The phase ended when the support for DAMD eroded among some GPs, initiating the project’s demise in the face of considerable media and political attention.

## 4.3.1. Stakeholders

In this phase, the DAMD project stakeholders continued to expand.

Mandatory GP participation. GP participation was initially voluntarily; however, in 2010, the collective agreement between Danish Medical Association and the Danish Regions mandated that all GPs instal the data capture module and transmit data to the DAMD (D26).

Pharmaceutical companies. Following the Danish government’s recommendation that “Denmark should lead the way in using health data and making them commercially applicable” (D28, p. 15), a national strategy was announced to integrate the diferent health databases and registries into one centralised structure and ensure a unified gateway for data access (D29). The initiative – which included the DAMD as a crucial component – was enthusiastically embraced by the Danish Association of the Pharmaceutical Industry:

It’s really a game changer. It’s brand new, and it can really put Denmark on the world map. In the improvement of health care—especially medicine— you need good data. You usually have to collect it yourself as a private actor or researcher, and this is very costly. When you meet a country that makes this data available to the industry, it will be attractive to foreign investors. (D3, p. 2)

The SSI. The SSI was charged with establishing a centralised data hub to ensure commercialisation of the data (D31). However, because SSI also had legal authority over the clinical databases (e.g., the DAMD), DAK-E feared that delivering data to the SSI would result in an unfortunate mix of interests:

It was—again—a conflict of interests. And it was very specific because the State Serum Institute believed that DAMD data should be transferred to them. Specifically, they believed that they had the right to have [the] data transferred. So, in my first meeting with the State Serum Institute as manager of DAK-E, they requested to get the data transferred. (interview, DAK-E Manager 2)

## 4.3.2. Data

With GP participation mandatory, data collection expanded dramatically; by 2013, 1,926 clinics (97% of all Danish GPs) registered in the DAMD ecosystem (see Figure 5). This significantly increased the DAMD’s size to cover data from almost the entire Danish population of patients in general practice (approximately 5 million).

Furthermore, the scope of the data collection increased radically, from four specific diseases in phase 2 to approximately 700 ICPC diagnoses<sup>2</sup> in the formalisation stage (D26, D44). A manager of DAK-E describes the situation as follows:

If you read the collective agreement from 2010, it says that all data corresponding to the PLO-XML format should be collected. And if you just have a little knowledge of general practice, then you know that it’s all the data. It was directly stated in the agreement. So, it was not secret to anyone. (interview, DAK-E Manager 2)

This expansion aforded new action possibilities and attracted further stakeholder interests in the DAMD – that is, new stakeholders seeking new potential uses for the huge amount of data.

## 4.3.3. Functions

The expansion of DAMD’s data collection and increased interests from stakeholders led to an escalation of requests for reusing data.

Exporting data to commercial applications. Over time, commercial interests in the DAMD increased (D32). In December 2013, it was determined that the data could be delivered to research projects associated with private companies if the primary data processor and the researcher were connected to a university and the project had the required legal permissions. A summary of a meeting held by the Quality and Research Committee stated that the “data may have great value to private firms and the first real applications with commercial interest have been received” (D33, p. 2). The DAK-E manager explains the commercial interests in the DAMD and why he sought to oppose them:

There was a desire from a large international IT company that wanted to make data mining. There were many wishes and many options, but I had to defend the database. My intention was quality development in general practice and secondary research. But many saw other options in the database—and the commercial opportunities they ofer are there, of course. (interview, DAK-E Manager 1)

## Another former DAK-E manager ofers a similar perspective:

The idea that data has been abused by commercial companies is one of the things that has been most troublesome for us. However, no data from DAMD has ever been used for pharmaceutical projects. But it is clear that the interest was there, and we also got applications. So, there was a conflict of interest. At the same time, there was the government’s ‘growth team’. They claimed that in Denmark, we could earn a lot of money on this data—millions or billions. It was the pharmaceutical industry who introduced the idea that one could use all the data we had to attract pharmaceutical research to Denmark, and data could be something that we could ‘sell’. (interview, DAK-E Manager 2)

Exporting data to patients through e-health platform. With the DAMD collecting data on almost every patient in Danish general practices, new DA potentials emerged regarding the ability to empower patients with better self-care opportunities through selfmonitoring their own health data (D34). This functionality ensured that the DAMD data would be available to patients through their profiles on the Danish national e-health platform, sundhed.dk (D35).

Patients could now see their own data—it was really a boost. Especially the resourceful and motivated patients could now prepare themselves before going to the family doctor. (interview, Danish Medical Association chairman)

Exporting data to health care professionals. The delivery of the DAMD data to the sundhed.dk platform also made it possible for other professionals within and across the Danish health care sector to access the DAMD data in order to support a more coherent health care system and improve the continuity of patient care (D36, D37):

Information sharing across sectors becomes very important as more and more data is collected in the primary health care system. The chronical[ly] ill persons are followed more closely by GPs than in the secondary health care system. And we need detailed information [on the patient’s treatment] flowing back and forth across sectors. (interview, SSI project manager)

The association of Danish Patients shared a similar view, stating that “no one has died from the misuse of data. But it is possible that you can die if data sharing does not occur” (D38). Although the function of delivering data to health care professionals was only partially implemented, it became part of the discussion – and disagreement – and led to the DAMD’s collapse. Numerous GPs feared that more-comprehensive data sharing could undermine doctor–patient relationships and risk having patients withhold sensitive information if they perceived doctor–patient confidentiality to be broken (D39); an SSI manager explained this as follows:

Some GPs consider the room between you and the patient as a very private room that no one else should know about. So you should be able to tell me everything about you, and they don’t want that information to be shared by anyone. (interview, SSI manager)

DAK-E warned about such GP reactions as data became more widely available:

It turned out that the GPs using DAMD suddenly also could see their diagnoses on other health platforms. This was not appropriate because it was not what we had told the GPs. We had said that the DAMD database was one we [the GPs] controlled ourselves. (D40)

Exporting data for administration and management. In August 2014, the Ministry of Health introduced new legislation mandating that GPs instal the data capture module and code their diagnoses and that individual clinics make their data available to the regions for medical-related purposes (such as planning and quality assurance) as well as for financial control (D41, §2–3). Financial control meant that the regions could use the data in the DAMD to compare the performances of GPs and eventually to link these data to their compensation. Furthermore, the 2014 collective agreement emphasised the importance of using the DAMD data for administrative purposes to “ensure a greater degree of transparency in health care” (D42, p. 12). As health administrators recognised great potential in using the DAMD data to support managerial decision-making (D43, D47), however, it created a major conflict between the two initiating stakeholders: GPs and the Danish Regions. As the data-controlling authority, the regions continually identified new opportunities to use the DAMD data to ensure more efective and coordinated health care eforts. And, in 2014, these opportunities expanded to include using the data for administrative control over the doctors’ performance (D44).

## 4.3.4. Impact on the data ecosystem

As Figure 6 shows, this phase greatly expanded the DAMD’s volume, scope and usage – involving new stakeholders and expanding functions far beyond what was originally envisioned. The DAMD was no longer simply a database to support decision-making and quality improvement in general practice. Instead, it was expected to provide data for a more coherent health care sector by enabling patients and other health care professionals to access data through a national e-health platform (sundhed.dk). This phase also opened up new commercial and administrative applications of the DAMD.

## 4.4. Collapse of the data ecosystem (2014–2015)

The final phase began in September 2014, when national news media provided a harsh critique of the DAMD’s data collection and reuse (D45). This initiated widespread public debate and media coverage, with 486 unique news entries about the DAMD from September 292,014 to May 182,015, when the Danish Parliament decided to delete the database (D46). The main criticisms surrounded the legality of the data collection, lack of patient consent, threats to doctor–patient confidentiality, and the potential dark sides of reusing personal health data for new commercial and administrative purposes (D1, D48, D49, D50). In this phase, the DAMD was suspended, and the database became a political hot potato, with stakeholders that previously supported it now calling for the DAMD to be terminated.

## 4.4.1. Stakeholders

Support among stakeholders began to contract in this stage.

Erosion of GP support. The project’s rapidly expanding scope and the fear of data being used for administrative and financial purposes eroded the support of the two DAMD project initators: GPs and Danish Medical Association. DAK-E and Danish Medical Association were strongly opposed to the regions gaining access to the DAMD data for administrative purposes and argued that the risk of being sanctioned could afect the GPs’ impartiality in terms of patient treatment and diagnostics:

There was a fierce discussion—also because health administrators had introduced ‘pay for performance’ in England. There were similar thoughts that you could use DAMD data for that purpose as well. (interview, DAK-E Manager 2)

From the GPs perspective, such a function would risk undermining data validity, as one manager described in an interview: “GPs of course wouldn’t report data that could risk compromising their own position” (DAK-E Manager 2). Thus, the GPs’ criticism consisted partly of their concerns about reusing data for the authorities’ administrative and financial control of GPs:

I think the primary reason for the collapse of DAMD was the transition from being a quality database to the desire for it to become a ‘control’ database. That was toxic. (interview, DAK-E Manager 1)

During a November 2014 Danish Medical Association conference, the criticism escalated, as two GPs announced that they had filed a police report on DAK-E and the Region of Southern Denmark for “illegal collection of data” (D52). The case was later rejected, but it marked a radical shift in attitude among the GPs and their associations. In an opinion piece in the Danish Medical Journal, one GP wrote:

The purpose of DAMD has within a few years been corrupted with a large number of other purposes that go beyond what was the originally agreed. Now our data are exported for control purposes. The Government’s requests for data delivery go far beyond the control of paid fees and insight into data for political and administrative purposes. The data is to be delivered as part of a growth plan, which must primarily attract private research companies to Denmark. Personally identifiable health data from DAMD is on display at sundhed.dk [eHealth platform] without consent. We are moving further and further away from our primary purpose with DAMD, namely an internal learning tool. (D51, p. 73)

In addition to concerns regarding the legality of data collection and the potential use of data for administrative control, many GPs were worried that the extensive data reuse would undermine doctor-patient confidentiality. For instance, the Danish Medical Association stated that “we must not gamble with patients’ trust” by reusing DAMD data for new purposes (D39). In a leading Danish newspaper, a GP seeking to mobilise public support for the deletion of DAMD apologised to patients for the data collection:

Dear Patients. GPs uphold the duty of confidentiality and the trust between doctor and patient highest— higher than the need for research in data, and significantly higher than the authorities’ interest in making money from selling data to pharmaceutical companies. But we need your help in the fight to get rid of illegally collected personal data from general practice. (D55)

Danish Parliament. Public media coverage began to heavily criticise the database in September 2014. The following month, data collection for the DAMD was suspended while the SSI conducted an investigation to determine the DAMD’s legality (D53). Despite having sought delivery of the DAMD data to the centralised database that same year, the SSI inquiry concluded that the comprehensive and broad (non-disease-specific) collection and use of data in general practice lacked a legal basis, and that the SSI had granted permission to collect data on only four specific diseases.

Unfortunately for DAMD, they didn’t have all the paperwork done. They didn’t have the permissions for all the data they collected. Our role [the SSI] in those days was that we were legal authorities that you had to ask—so we had to say, ‘this is not legal’. We actually approved the four specific diagnosis areas of DAMD, but unfortunately, in the end, we had to tell that it was illegal to have the entire database. Being a researcher myself and seeing all the benefits of the database, it was awful to have to deal with this case because it ended as it did. (interview, SSI project manager)

Subsequently, the data-controlling authority – the Region of Southern Denmark – held a meeting with the Minister of Health to determine whether it was possible to maintain the database (D52). However, in May 2015, the Danish Parliament decided that the DAMD should be deleted. The Danish Medical Association chairman concluded the following:

The problem is that authorities, such as the State Serum Institute and the Danish Health Authority, which should have maintained a high standard and safeguarded the patients, are also subject to politica control by politicians who have other intentions— that is, the intention to use information for control and resale. This means that even the authorities that should be protecting the patients in this case are, in reality, subject to other people’s interests. The Danish Regions, which want to improve the quality of the health care system, are simultaneously interested in being able to control and, if necessary, sanction the GPs for poor performance. (D56)

## 4.4.2. Data collection and function

In this phase, data collection was suspended, the data were eventually deleted, and no new functions were added.

## 4.4.3. Impact on the data ecosystem

The DAMD’s collapse illustrates a tipping point, as the expanding data ecosystem led to a dramatic change in stakeholders’ views and support of the initiative. What was once perceived as a successful data analytics project that attracted an increasing number of stakeholders seeking to (re)use data for new functions became delegitimised and considered illegal, fuelled by indignant GPs and media articles that stressed how the potential dark sides of reusing DAMD data for new purposes were overshadowing the initiative’s benefits. In this heated debate, DAMD became part of a broader discussion about data analytics in Danish health care (e.g., D57), blurring the lines between the actual use of DAMD data and the concerns of potential data (mis)use. ## 5. Discussion

In this article. we set out to answer a research question: What are the dynamics of data reuse, and how does it impact data analytics projects? In our attempt to find an answer, we analysed a data analytics project in Danish health care that was described as a golden egg, indispensable and a game changer – as well as corrupted, toxic, and illegal. How can something as innocent as data (everlasting, persistent and agnostic), used with the very purpose of saving lives turn into something toxic? Clearly the DAMD project demonstrates particular dynamics that obscured its bright side and brought a dark side to the fore. This change was so dramatic that no attempts to correct the project’s course could succeed, and the solution became simply, and literally, to pull the plug (cf. Keil, 1995).

Overall, our analysis demonstrates that, together with its growing success, the DAMD project experienced three forms of creep: function, stakeholder, and data. For every act that changed the context – more functions, more stakeholders or more data – the project involuntarily chipped away at its original idea. As we argue in the theory background section, data is typically depicted as a strategic asset that can be reused repeatedly (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Baesens et al., 2016; Constantiou & Kallinikos, 2015), yet it also carries a dark side that brings about challenges (Calzada & Almirall, 2020). In the following, we discuss this extended view of data reuse in data ecosystems. We explicitly describe the data ecosystem’s components and the dynamics so that, perhaps, in the future, we will be able to predict and eventually avoid DAMD-like outcomes.

## 5.1. Data ecosystem dynamics: the role of data reuse

According to our analysis, a data ecosystem consists of three major components, or subsystems, that align with the three forms of creep: (1) the functional/technical subsystem; (2) the stakeholders subsystem; and (3) the data subsystem. Although the extant literature on data ecosystems has identified some of these components individually, they have not been brought together in a single ecosystem.

The functional/technical sub-system is probably discussed the most in literature (Demchenko et al., 2014; Sumbaly et al., 2013), with a focus on the technical infrastructure that is required to collect, analyse and reuse the data in the ecosystem. In the DAMD case, the functional/technical subsystem grew in parallel with the new stakeholders entering the ecosystem and the new functionalities that they demanded. The literature has also studied the stakeholder subsystem (Pouloudi & Whitley, 1997), but not in relation to data analytics and data ecosystems; rather, the literature deals instead with the power, interest and influence that certain groups or individuals have on a particular project (Urbinati et al., 2020). In the DAMD case, the stakeholder subsystem grew by riding the wave of the previous project successes. The greater the success, the greater the stakeholders’ desire for a piece of the proverbial pie. This process generated the behaviour that we refer to as stakeholder creep. More stakeholders meant more funds, more resources and ultimately more data to be reused in new contexts. Stakeholder creep also decreased attention on some stakeholder groups – in particular, the patients – in favour of other groups, such as research and commercial interests. This new focus also masked the malcontent brewing among still other stakeholders (the doctors).

Finally, we have the data subsystem. In the DAMD project, this included a combination of patients, GPs and diagnostic codes. As various diagnoses were added to the DAMD system, the data subsystem grew, ultimately covering all of the reasons to see a personal physician for the totality of GPs. The data subsystem experienced staggering changes in size from the beginning of the DAMD project (appr.

1.500 patients x 12 clinics x 1 disease) to the middle phase (appr. 100.000 patients x 250 clinics x 4 diseases) to the end state (appr. 5 million patients x 1.926 clinics x 700 diseases). Given the data-centric nature of DA projects, the data subsystem is obviously of critical importance, but it cannot be separated from the individuals providing the data to the ecosystem in question: When data is reused in new contexts, data providers may also become the target of the data analysis. In the DAMD case, this shift occurred both for the patients and the doctors. The rapid growth of the data subsystem transformed the patients – and eventually also the doctors – into subjects for the health care administration to analyse and into a potential source for growth in the pharmaceutical industry, thus reversing the DAMD project’s original intention.

Our findings here help advance knowledge of data analytics’ dark side and highlight it as an essential, yet under-studied perspective in the current literature. Further, data and their value cannot be viewed in isolation, but rather must be seen as part of ecosystems that collect, use and share data (Gitelman, 2013; Oliveira et al., 2019; Wang, 2021). Once embedded in their ecosystems, data lose their agnostic characteristics and gain the dynamics of any social system: virtues and problems, order and chaos, bright and dark sides. Our analysis allows us to posit that, as the data ecosystem grows, its value will always have a bright component and a dark component. Whether the bright side or the dark will become dominant depends, we argue, on the relative dynamics of the subsystems. As the variety of elements in the diferent subsystems increases, so, too does the chance that the dark side will become preponderant; this is because such growth comes with a temptation to bend the data analytics project’s initial goals and thereby increase emergent risks in relation to privacy, transparency, data validity and stakeholder trust.

## 5.2. Evolution of data ecosystems: the dark and bright side

By putting these subsystems together in a model, we can better understand their interaction and graphically represent the emergence of the dark side of data analytics projects. Figure 8 shows our Data Analytics Ecosystem Model, which is rooted in the findings from the DAMD case. The model shows the three subsystems partially overlapping and generating a common central overlap area. The area of partial overlap represents the bright side of data analytics projects – that is, the area in which the diferent subsystems coexist harmoniously. Meanwhile, the area of complete overlap represents the dark side of data analytics projects – that is, the area in which problems go unresolved and malcontent brews in the data ecosystem.

In the DAMD case, each subsystem grew as new data were collected from an increasing number of sources, new stakeholders entered the ecosystem, new functionalities were added and data were (re) used for new unplanned purposes. Hence, the data ecosystem grew, symbolising the increased value of the ecosystem (until the collapse). However, the dark side in the middle also got bigger; it ate into the bright side until it began to dominate, the entire ecosystem fell under scrutiny and DAMD collapsed. In the DAMD project’s emergence phase, a few doctors collected data for a single disease from a very limited number of patients. The project’s value was therefore limited to a narrow group of stakeholders, and the dark side was minimal. In the formalisation phase, more doctors collected data for four diseases from about 150.000 patients, and new stakeholders with divergent interests entered the project. The project’s value increased (e.g., cross sector-coordination, research and pharmaceutical development), but so, too, did the potential dark side as the new stakeholders’ agendas were not aligned with the original project goal or the reasons for collecting data. In the expansion phase, all subsystems grew massively – as did the potential dark side efects, including control of doctor performance, unclear legal basis, and lack of transparency.

Taking this idea one step further, we suggest that, if only some of the subsystems grow, the risk of unleashing the dark side is smaller. If, for example, only the number of doctors and diagnoses grew, but not the functionalities or stakeholder variety, DAMD would have stayed closer to its original goal and the dark side would have remained smaller. Similarly, if the stakeholders grew but the data remained limited to a few diagnoses, the value would have increased without (much) increase in the dark side.

Our model is empirically grounded in a data analytics project in Danish health care, where governmental decision-makers had the power to shut down the project. However, the escalation dynamics that we have identified in the data ecosystem is a general phenomenon that also afects business initiatives. Recently, for example, large tech companies have been under media and political fire due to their possible abuses in data use beyond what the public perceives as fair (Zubof, 2015), leading to recent calls for limiting the power of large social media platforms (Lima & Overly, 2020). The reasons for this situation can be understood within the context of our Data Analytics Ecosystem Model. For example, if a search engine uses the data from searches to provide a better searching experience, this is perceived as a bright side goal. On the other side, if a search engine uses data across searches to provide services to third parties – hence increasing variety in the stakeholder ecosystem – this increases the dark side, as Zubof (2015) has also highlighted. Likewise, if a social media platform uses individual postings to improve users’ platform experience, this is perceived as a bright side goal. In contrast, if a platform shares the data with a third party (more stakeholders) that has the goal of influencing elections (more functions), this increases the dark side and can lead to extreme consequences – as exemplified by Cambridge Analytica’s spectacular failure (Larson & Vieregger, 2019). So, while we developed our model on the empirical basis of a public sector health care data analytics project, the bright and dark side dynamics are mirrored by those of private organisations that thrive on data. Therefore, while further studies on private data analytics projects are certainly desirable, private organisations can currently benefi from using the model to fine tune the growth of the diferent subsystems and thereby obtain an appropriate balance between the bright and dark sides.

## 5.3. Implications for policy makers and managers

Our study’s findings could be extremely valuable for practitioners. Indeed successful data analytics projects, especially in health care, are a rarity, and DAMD was well on its way to becoming successful. It is a pity that multiple stakeholders lost the DAMD project’s potential benefits due to a mismanagement of its dark side. We articulate the learnings for managers and policy makers along three lines. First, our model and findings may guide policy makers and managers in understanding the potential dangers of data reuse, and thereby raise their awareness in critical data-sharing decisions. Although introducing new functionalities based on an existing data analytics project can satisfy new stakeholder interests at little extra cost, such additions entail the risk of slowly building up a dark side that may ultimately backfire (as in the DAMD case). Second, our study is a reminder for policy makers and managers that data analytics is not just about data. Tensions, political manoeuvrings, compromises and negotiations are likely to be underlying drivers of large-scale data analytics projects that involve multiple stakeholders. Third, elaborating on Boonstra et al.’s (2017) perspective on competing stakeholder interests, we suggest that policy makers and managers must carefully focus on orchestrating their stakeholders, functions and data, with an eye not only on the brightside profits but also on the dark-side risks of evolving data ecosystems.

## 6. Concluding remarks

Referring to data as “the gift that keeps on giving” is erroneous, as well as dangerous. If data are left untouched or, at a minimum, if the data ecosystem is completely stable and unchanged with respect to its first instance, it may well continue generating benefits. However, in most cases, it is the changing value of data that excites organisations, and expecting a stable ecosystem runs counter to the very reason for collecting data. When considering the data ecosystem, our analysis shows that we must consider the dynamics of the functional, stakeholder and data subsystems to maintain balance and support project success.

Grounded on the case analysis of the DAMD project in Danish health care, we have extended the data ecosystem literature by providing a view of data ecosystems as having both a bright and a dark side. We have identified that the data ecosystem contains three subsystems that, if allowed to grow in an uncoordinated fashion, may lead to the collapse of the entire ecosystem. In our case, function, stakeholder, and data creep ultimately resulted in the sudden failure of the DAMD project. Given the social implications of data analytics projects such as the DAMD, surges of data, stakeholders and functions result in a creeping dark side. As this dark side stealthily develops and grows, its nature and consequences are unclear, and the few early warning signs are easily dismissed given the value such a system provides. Our study ofers an empirically grounded model to help project managers understand, analyse and even predict the evolution and balance of a data analytics project’s bright and dark sides. Hence, we provide a more nuanced view of the optimistic notion of data as “a magical diamond mine that keeps on giving long after its principal value has been tapped” (Mayer-Schönberger & Cukier, 2013, p. 104). Thus, as organisations and IS scholars show increasing interest in data analytics and the associated fields of business intelligence and big data, we hope that this study will inspire in them a more balanced attitude in their related research and practice.

Finally, given its highly sensitive data, health care provides a particularly important research area in which to explore unintended consequences of data analytics, but it represents only one example. While we intuitively can apply the data analytics ecosystem model to some contexts (such as social media platforms), other contexts may present alternative dynamics, wherein functions, stakeholders and data creeps are replaced by other context-specific mechanisms. We therefore suggest that future research strive to identify and further develop the notion of data ecosystems in a variety of settings. In particular, the dynamics of creep (function, stakeholder and data) should be explored since reusing data in new contexts continues to be a controversial issue.

## APPENDIX D

## INTERVIEW PROTOCOL

## Briefing

Introduction

● Thank you for participating. The interview is recorded, will last approx. 30–60 minutes and is structured in four themes: The first theme concerns the DAMD timeline. The second theme concerns the stakeholders involved in the project. The third theme deals with the purpose and use of DAMD data. Finally, the fourth theme concerns your reflections on why DAMD collapsed and what can be learned from the case.

## Interview presentation

● Will you start by introducing yourself and your connection to the DAMD project?

## 1. Chronology of DAMD

● How did DAMD project start?

● What do you consider to be the key events in the DAMD project?

## 2. Actors involved in the ecosystem, their roles and interests

Stakeholders

● Which stakeholders were involved at the beginning?

● What were their roles in the project?

● Did more stakeholders become involved over time? (Who, when, how?)

● How did that impact DAMD?

● Did any stakeholders abandon their participation in DAMD over time? (Who, when, how?)

● How did that impact DAMD?

Ownership, responsibility and governance

● Who had the overall responsibility for DAMD?

● Which authorities were involved? (supervisory and approving authority)

● How was the division of responsibilities and roles defined in the project?

## Interests in DAMD

● Can you describe the various interests surrounding DAMD? (Heath, research, political, administrative, commercial interests)

● How were the external interests handled?

3. Purposes, functionalities, use and reuse of data. Purposes

● What was the original purpose of DAMD?

● What was the long-term vision of DAMD?

● Were the purposes and functionalities of the database expanded along the way? And what did it mean (challenges and opportunities)?

● Were there any purposes (functions) that disappeared?

● How did the scope for DAMD expand? And how did that impact DAMD?

## Access to data

● How were the data used?

● Who gained access to data? And how?

● Was there anyone who wanted access to data who did not get it?

## Data validity

● Was data collected diferently in diferent phases of the project? How?

● What were the opportunities and challenges of (re)using DAMD data?

● Did the increase in functionalities in DAMD impact (improving or threaten) the validity/quality of collected data?

## 4. Concluding items

Reflections

● In your opinion, why did DAMD collapse?

● What can be learned from DAMD?

● Can health data be reused in a sensible way? How?

## Snowball

● Do you have recommendations for further interviewees or documents?
