# Rethinking time: ubichronic time and its impact on work

Koteswara Ivaturi<sup>a</sup> and Cecil Chua<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Business Technology & Entrepreneurship, Swinburne Business School, Swinburne University of Technology, Hawthorn, Australia; <sup>b</sup>Missouri University of Science and Technology, Rolla, MO USA

## ABSTRACT

Modern technology is ubiquitous i.e., “always there” – available to us when we want it and engaging us even when we don’t actively seek it. This constantly available ubiquitous technology influences people’s perception of time. This conceptual paper explores how ubiquitous technology creates a new time vision we call ubichronic time. We argue ubichronic time is qualitatively diferent from existing time visions and highlight the new values and behaviours associated with it. Specifically, people who have an ubichronic time vision perform disparate activities that span short durations across the day, find specific tiny units of time valuable and cram many repeated activities into a day. We also argue ubichronic time will have profound implications on the way we work and as such propose new concepts and research directions on how the way we work needs to adapt at the individual, team, and organisational levels.

## 1. Introduction

In the 21st century, digital technology has become ubiquitous, i.e., digital technology is embedded in our natural movements and interactions with our environments both physical and social (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002). Most of us have an Internet-enabled computer always within reach, be it a laptop, mobile phone, or tablet. These ubiquitous technologies aford us with ubiquitous services, i.e., services invokable at any place and time. In fact, the role of ubiquitous technologies and the services they aford extend beyond individual utility. One recent example is how mobile tracking technology and applications has been instrumental in managing COVID-19 (Trang et al., 2020; Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020)- a societal issue. This in turn shapes how we perceive time, and correspondingly enact behaviours. To illustrate, consider the following scenario:

Dr Hekemai is a New Zealand doctor who belongs to a worldwide online clinic, where doctors are consulted by patients, remotely through video or audio conference technology. On working days, Dr, Hekemai’s alarm rings at 4:30 am. She gets up, does her toilet, and sits in front of her computer to see her first patients at 5:00. Alice (seen at 5:00 am Auckland time) is a wheelchair bound geriatric patient in Atlanta, Georgia USA (1:00 pm Atlanta time). Bob (5:30) is a New Zealand IT cloud services operator just getting of the night shift with a mild fever (temperature measured through his smartphone). Charlie (6:00 am) is a school going child with chickenpox (diagnosed via video). Denise (6:30 am) is in Thailand (1:30 am) about to start her night factory shift. There are more patients, but Dr. Hekemai does not have time to address them now, so she logs out. Other doctors around the world will pick up the slack.

Dr. Hekemai then prepares breakfast and wakes her school going daughter. They leave the house by 8:00 to catch the 8:03 am tram. The fully automated tram comes exactly at 8:03 am- come one minute late, and the tram is gone. Dr. Hekemai drops her daughter at school and proceeds to her group practice where she works from 9.00 am to 2.00 pm. As is common with medical clinics, delays mean Dr. Hekemai’s backlog builds. However, the medical centre waiting area is sparsely attended. This is because patients’ appointment times are dynamically adjusted, and so patients know not to come early. At 2.25 pm, Dr. Hekemai sees her last patient- remaining patients are seen by another doctor who checked in at 1:00 pm. She walks to the tram, which arrives promptly at 2:47 pm. She goes to pick up her daughter and engages in home activities until 7:00 pm. From 7:00–8:30, she logs into the online clinic. During times when she doesn’t see patients (e.g., quiet time at the medical clinic or online clinic, lunch, waiting for the tram), Dr. Hekemai visits and answers questions at a medical advice bulletin board on her mobile or tablet. She is paid for the answers she gives.

The story above, while fictitious, describes a situation aforded by existing ubiquitous technologies. It illustrates how technology influences the way one perceives the notion of time and its impact on the way we work. First, Dr. Hekemai works nontraditional hours. She likes doing this, because it gives her time to both manage her income, and home life. Second, she works for 3 diferent companies (online clinic, medical clinic, and bulletin board), rather than for one. At times, she works for two companies at the same time- for example, she does bulletin board advice work while at her group practice. Her patients come from diferent time zones. Some aspects of her life (e.g., the tram) require her to be exceedingly punctual- a minute late and she sufers substantial inconvenience. Clearly these behaviours are enabled by the constant availability of modernday technology. Also, these new behaviours difer from existing norms of how people currently perceive and manage their time. As such, technology is changing the socially constructed concept of “society” and the very framework in which society lives (Fortunati, 2002; Wajcman, 2008), specifically, how Dr. Hekema manages time.

Dr. Hekemai can determine the current world-wide out-patient demand, plan an accurately timed tram trip, or view her dynamically adjusted patient appointments at a time of her own choosing using her ubiquitous technologies. These services allow Dr. Hekemai to manage her time diferently from a $2 0 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ century doctor and leads her to consider time in fundamentally different ways from how we consider time. Dr. Hekemai’s time perspective in turn shapes how she works.

Technology mediated behaviours and impacts on temporality are not new phenomena in IS research (O’Riordan et al., 2013; Vodanovich et al., 2011; Yoo, 2010). While some IS researchers have explored how temporality is perceived in IT-mediated teams (Shen et al., 2015), others have studied temporal complexities in the context of the information systems development failures (O’Connor et al., 2017a, 2017b) or failure of analytical tools (Conboy et al., 2020) to realise business value to firms. Recent perspectives ofer insights on how the ubiquity of digital innovations are creating a socio-technological transformation of the world (Baygi et al., Forthcoming) by challenging the fundamental assumptions related to temporality. For example, the impact of the pandemic has created a new normal with respect to how work is being done, remotely and flexibly thus changing spatial and temporal perspectives related to work (Agerfalk et al., 2020). Research on technology mediated behaviours and temporality recognises the existence of time visions- a conception of time that shapes behaviour. Prior research has articulated four distinct time visions, clock, event, timeless and harmonic time (Saunders et al., 2004).

We argue there is a new time vision associated with ubiquitous technology- ubichronic time. Formally defined, ubichronic time is a new time vision created by the embedding of people in ubiquitous technological environments. For example, email apps constantly alert users to new email. As a result, people check email just before going to sleep (Orlikowski, 2007). This, in turn, disrupts circadian rhythms leading to new sleep cycles (Murnane et al., 2015; Shochat, 2012). This paper argues ubichronic time is real and shaping peoples’ behaviour now- there is an underlying cognitive change in the way people work that explains such behavioural changes induced by constant use of ubiquitous technology. Figure 1 presents our theorised causal relationship between ubiquitous technology, the ubichronic time vision and the new emergent behaviours.

It is critical to understand ubichronic time, given the scope of impact it will have on the way we live and work. Understanding ubichronic time is also necessary to find solutions to social and organisational problems arising because of this new time perspective. To illustrate how technology shapes time perspectives, which shape work practices potentially creating problems, consider the mechanical clock which became instrumental in dissociating external events like sunrise and sunset from work. This in turn was a foundation for the hourly wage which created the 14 to 16 hour, six day work week. While considered exploitative today, the 14 to 16 hour work day was normal for more than a hundred years- the industrial revolution began in 1760 and the US (for example) only enacted the 40 hour work week in 1938- typically, nations with such laws introduced them post World War I. This example illustrates how understanding perspectives of time is critical to research- laws remained unchanged because they were based on an agrarian/guild perspective of time where hours worked were principally voluntary.<sup>1</sup> It is only when the US Congress adopted the industrial revolution perspective of time (after a long period of advocacy by unions and social welfare advocates) that the laws in the United States changed.

It is thus important to understand changes in time perception induced by modern ubiquitous technology, i.e., ubichronic time, given its potential widespread societal impact. For example, although Dr. Hekemai works non-traditional hours, on more than two jobs, and virtually – all made possible due to the use of ubiquitous technologies, societal norms, tax regulations, and workplace policies are still aligned to the traditional work week and assumption she works for one company.

The thesis of ubichronic time influenced by high adoption and use of ubiquitous technology is developed in this paper as follows. We first introduce Saunders et al.’s (2004) framework of time dimensions, which we use to structure our analysis. We present our argument on why a “new” time vision called ubichronic time has emerged. We then argue ubichronic time is qualitatively diferent from existing notions of time which has strong implications for modern work life in two significant ways: (1) work will be performed in a discontiguous way and (2) the workday will be stretched resulting in a perceived extension in working hours. We then systematically develop six future research questions around the two work style changes across three levels of analysis. These research questions are not just questions, but also introduce new concepts hitherto unexplored in research.

## 2. Dimensions of time

Time is a socially constructed phenomenon (Bluedorn, 2002; Bluedorn & Denhardt, 1988; Hassard, 2002) which means that perceptions of time vary across societies. Saunders et al. (2004) identified six diferent dimensions<sup>2</sup> (summarised in Table 1) across which peoples’ perceptions of time vary.

## 2.1. Continuity

In the continuity dimension, time is viewed as either comprising discrete time points (discontinuous) or being of one unbroken stream (continuous). For example, if one refers to the World Trade Center plane crash event on September 11 2001, one is treating time as discontinuous- this was a specific, significant time period. In contrast, those who have a continuous view of time might argue there was no specific event on September 11. That event was caused by a range of other events in the past and causes other events in the future. September 11 cannot be separated from those other events.

## 2.2. Homogeneity

The homogeneity dimension evaluates whether a person behaves as if all time is of equivalent value.<sup>3</sup>

Continuity and homogeneity difer in that homogeneity is associated with how one perceives how time is structured. For example, while discontinuous time is viewed as a series of divisible small time units, the homogeneity dimension considers the duration of these time units. For example, people who consider time as homogenous perceive each time unit to have the exact duration as another. The traditional economics view of time is homogeneous- a person earns \$X per hour. That the person can only earn that amount during ofice hours is not considered.

On the other hand, people who consider time to be epochal focus less on the duration of each time unit but rather on the event taking place in the time unit. In epochal time, it is not time itself, but the event that makes time valuable. One’s valuation of time has a stronger impact on one’s actions. For example, harvest time is more valuable than the preceding or succeeding time- if crops are not harvested during this time, their value substantially decreases.

## 2.3. Linearity

The linearity dimension captures whether one views time as proceeding onwards or looping. The linear time view occurs when one views time as a sequence of the past, present and future that is separable into discrete compartments (Graham, 1981). A linear or unidimensional view of time is associated with time having monetary value as only present and future time can be commoditized while past time is irrecoverable. The idea that time can be “wasted” is associated with “time urgency” behaviour where individuals are more attentive to the passage of time than others (Conte et al., 1995; Landy et al., 1991). On the contrary, the cyclical view is that time repeats. Winter follows Fall follows Summer follows Spring and the cycle continues. Thus, one harvest follows a planting, which follows a harvest. In the cyclical view, time has substantively less value, because missed opportunities will come again. However, time is still valuable- a missed harvest is devastating financially.

## 2.4. Dimensionality

The dimensionality view of time captures the perception of time flow. For example, time is unidimensional when it is perceived that everyone travels through time in one irreversible direction. People who view time as uni-dimensional hold the belief that time is valuable as spent time is irrecoverable. A bi-directional view of time occurs when time is perceived to move both forward and backward. This occurs often in fiction about time travel. Finally, in the recurrent view, time is viewed as endlessly repeating.

## 2.5. Subjectivity

The subjectivity dimension captures whether time is viewed as having an independent existence or is associated with an individual or group (Sucala & David, 2012). In a subjective view of time, time is individually experienced. The idea that “time flies when you are having fun” is a subjective view of time. To the person having fun, time is viewed as “flying”, but the bored person does not experience the same. Note here that “flying” is distinct from the concept of bi-directional (dimensionality) time. Both the person having fun and the bored person are travelling through time at the same rate. In the intersubjective time perspective, time is experienced as a group. Time zones are an example of intersubjective time- the group (i.e., everyone living in a particular area) agrees that this particular time is 9:00 am, but people in a diferent time zone do not. Finally, an objective view of time occurs when one believes time occurs independent of human action.

## 2.6. Chronicity

The chronicity dimension captures how one views the relationship between time and activity. The framework suggests two sub-dimensions – monochronic and polychronic time. As the name suggests, monochronic time occurs when users prefer to engage in only one activity at a time during a given time period (Bluedorn, 2002). This is typically a western view of time (Hall & Hall, 1990). People with a monochronic view of time treat schedules and deadlines very seriously (Bluedorn et al., 1992). Unplanned, unscheduled events are considered unproductive interruptions that should be minimised and not allowed to interfere with the existing schedule. Being punctual is a duty and is considered ofensive if someone is late for a meeting. Also there is a deep sense of respect for the privacy of others and prefer not to disturb or be disturbed by others unless it is necessary; others’ time is valuable. Thus, the monochronic view of time carries the idea of the economy of time (Robinson, 1980).

On the other hand, polychronic time use represents situations when users are engaged in two or more activities either simultaneously or intermittently during a given time period (Bluedorn, 2002). Polychronic time users also view time spent on procedure or activity as less important compared to the activity itself. People who following this model consume procedure and not time (Graham, 1981). Tasks are performed when the “time is right”. For example, surgeons performing a complicated surgery will not think about how long they’ve spent on the procedure as much as they think about whether they are following the procedure correctly. Also, people with a polychronic view of time are less driven by schedules and more by social relationships that they nurture and maintain. Monochronic and polychronic time use are heavily influenced by the cultural backgrounds from where people hail (Hall & Hall, 1990). The literature argues people seldom fall in either/or categories but rather have a specific dominant orientation towards either monochronic or polychronic time use. Thus, if we think of the perception of time as a continuum with one extreme being monochronic view of time and the other as polychronic view of time, then the culture one comes from will partly determine where on this continuum one finds oneself (Bluedorn et al., 1992).

## 2.6.1. Temporal behaviours and time visions

Dimensions of time are individually perceived, but when multiple individuals interact in a group setting, they converge towards a shared understanding of time leading to the emergence of a group level perception of time that Saunders et al. (2004) call time vision. These time visions difer across societies, generations, countries, hemispheres (Northern vs Southern) or even cultures (e.g., East vs. West). Several time visions including Clock time, Event time, Timeless time, and Harmonic time have been identified (Saunders et al., 2004). Time visions help predict group behaviours. For example, all who ascribe to clock time speed up or slow down working on tasks relative to the remaining amount of allocated time (Waller et al., 2002).

Time visions are also important because the interactions across time visions explain and predict behaviour. For example, Americans are often disadvantaged when negotiating with Arabs. Arabs have a continuous/epochal view of time and do not feel time is money, and hence are willing to negotiate over extended periods of time. In contrast, Americans have a discontinuous/homogeneous view of time and view time as valuable. They want to rush to a settlement, and hence often cede points to Arab negotiators (Alon and Brett 2007). Similarly, under the terms of the Treaty of Waitangi, New Zealand Europeans are obligated to work with the indigenous Maori. However, many Maori processes and cognitive elements function in event time, while European work practices are associated with clock time (Lo & Houkamau, 2012).

Technological advances likewise can lead to a clash of time visions. As new technology emerges, new norms around time will need to evolve. For example, doctors often work in event time, i.e., every patient that needs to be treated is unique. How much time each patient is allocated depends fundamentally on the patient’s condition. However, doctor scheduling systems function in clock time i.e., one is allocated a time to see the doctor that does not consider patient variability. Invariably, patients scheduled later in the day have to wait (Lummus et al., 2006).

Technological advancement should entail an increase in free time. However, time seems to be increasingly scarce creating a paradox (Zimbardo & Boyd, 2008). Technologies rather than saving time, change the nature of work and create new meanings of tasks and practices (Wajcman, 2008). For example, sociomateriality theory posits the technical and social as being part of the same sphere and one in which each configures the other constantly (Orlikowski, 2007). We argue ubiquitous technologies have created ubichronic time that is increasingly diferent from with other time visions. Below, we conceptualise ubichronic time and highlight how it is qualitatively diferent when compared to existing time visions.

## 3. Ubichronic time

We build our conceptualisation of ubichronic time using the dimensions of time framework of Saunders et al. (2004). The framework helps explain diferences in time perspectives systematically by analysing an exhaustive set of time dimensions. In addition to identifying ubichronic time on the framework, we highlight qualitative diferences between ubichronic time and the dimensions on Saunders et al.’s (2004) framework. Our argument is summarised in Table 2 below, where we compare ubichronic time across the time dimensions with the identified time visions of clock, event, timeless, and harmonic times. For a detailed description of clock, event, timeless, and harmonic times refer to Saunders et al. (2004).

## 3.1. Continuity

Ubichronic time is inherently discontinuous. Unique to ubichronic time, ubichrons engage in multiple unrelated actions over a short duration. For example, a ubichron could text a message to a friend and keep track of an online auction on eBay all while attending a work-zoom call. These three actions are not logically related (Pozin, 2016) but are triggered in some temporal lacunae. Ubichrons constantly are engaged in an information seeking mode, a mode identified as “non-directed monitoring” in other research (McKenzie, 2003). Non-directed monitoring occurs when users do not have any goal in mind but their information need is triggered when they are exposed to a new piece of information. As an example, a person finds an interesting article or video while browsing aimlessly from one website to another (Ivaturi et al., 2017). The person did not plan to read the article/ watch the video but spends time doing so.

Two consequences emerge from such ubichronic behaviours. First, ubichrons will more likely adopt flexible schedules. Flexible schedules allow ubichrons to change existing schedules on the fly. For example, an ubichron might set a schedule with a sequence of actions that involves finishing work by 12.00 pm and meeting a friend for lunch. But as a serendipitous information need gets triggered, the ubichron might send an instant message to the friend letting them know that he or she might not be able to make the appointment and instead suggests meeting for a cofee. Clearly, as ubichrons increasingly adopt flexischeduling it will be more acceptable if someone is not able to make it for an appointment, as long as the other party is informed.

Second, ubichrons will have to deal with intrapersonal coordination, defined as efort required to coordinate multiple tasks by the same individual. Failures in intrapersonal coordination can be tragic. As but one illustration, it is increasingly common for people to send emails to incorrect recipients, i.e., one individual has multiple email send tasks and as a result of a coordination failure, confuses one send task with another. At times, these emails contain confidential information not for widespread dissemination. One particularly disastrous example was when one Virgin media worker in the UK erroneously sent unwanted emails to about 130,000 of its customers by hitting “reply all”. The company was forced to apologise publicly and admit the mistake after a facing a fierce social media campaign (Williams, 2014).

## 3.2. Homogeneity

Ubichronic time is epochal in that the value of time difers based on the type of events that occur in a particular time duration. Epochs are valuable. Traditionally, “epochs” are associated with events that occur over a long period. However, there has been a societal shift to valuing epochs of increasingly shorter duration. As an example, agile perspectives on work, where tasks are done in two week “sprints” are replacing more waterfall-oriented perspectives where software development occurs over months or years. Ubichrons take this to the extreme, where epochs occur over minutes or seconds. Ubichrons do this because they view time as discontinuous. Ubichrons will work, check on the kids via webcam, and track soccer scores all while listening to music or watching a video. Ubichrons thus can have multiple valuable epochs occur over “short” periods of time.

As one example, in the world of e-commerce, many online sales and auctions are time based. Interested bidders track specific auctions to place their bids strategically just before the end of the auction, to maximise their chances of winning, a phenomenon called snipe bidding. (Ockenfels & Roth, 2002; Roth & Ockenfels, 2002). As another example, Dr. Hekemai is aware that being a minute late for her 8.03 am tram will throw her daily schedule of.

We believe two important characteristics of modern ubiquitous technology drive this short-time epochal perspective of ubichrons. First, the mobile computer has replaced the analog watch as the principal instrument for telling time (Andrew, 2010; Martha, 2007). Second, timing software in most mobile computing devices can connect (often by default) to a central timing server. As a result, the principal time keeping instrument for ubichrons is by default synchronised. Furthermore, while the hours vary across diferent time zones, the minutes and seconds mostly do not- except for certain rare countries like North Korea and Venezuela (BBC, 2015). For example, 12:30 pm Pacific Standard Time (PST) is 5:30 am Japan Standard Time. The “12” and “5” vary, but the “30” component of the time does not.

When the principal time keeping device was the watch, the social expectation was that time was not exact. It was socially acceptable for the watch of one person to be 10 minutes slower or faster than another’s. This was primarily driven by errors in the mechanical setting of time between users. Synchronisation of time was a special event, requiring extraordinary rituals. Indeed, protocols like the Berkeley algorithm (Gusella & Zatti, 1989) and Network Time Protocol (Mills, 1991) were specifically designed to manage situations where a system had multiple clocks. However, the timing software in most mobile computing devices can now connect (often by default) to a central timing server. Thus, in many modern work environments, “being late” now means not arriving at a meeting precisely at the synchronised meeting time. Prior to ubiquitous computing, other meeting attendees often had a tolerance of several minutes for late arrivals.

## 3.3. Linearity

Ubichronic time is simultaneously linear and cyclical. On the one hand, the ubichron is keenly aware of the passage of time and does not like wasting time. On the other hand, much ubichronic behaviour is centred around cycles of repeated activity (e.g., checking email, checking social media). Thus, the ubichron perceives time as a set of distinct activity cycles strung in sequence. This drives the ubichron to become increasingly more eficient in performing each cycle of activity. Thus, unlike the farmer who treats each cycle of planting and harvesting as immutable, the ubichron is driven to be faster at each task cycle, faster at checking mail or performing a search query, faster at performing home chores. This generational shift in an inherent need for speed in completing tasks is described by Prensky (2010) as “twitch speed”. During periods when Dr. Hekemai has no physical or virtual patients, for example, she logs onto the bulletin board to answer questions. Dr. Hekemai spends very little time “doing nothing”.

All societies engage in contradictory behaviour and thought (Berliner et al., 2016) The contradictions inherent in being both linear and cyclical creates numerous negative consequences for ubichrons. For example, many ubichrons sufer from an inability to cope or deal with using ubiquitous technologies in a healthy manner. This phenomenon is similar to “technostress”, a modern day condition caused by one’s inability to cope or deal with using technologies in a healthy manner (Brod, 1984). When it comes to ubiquitous technologies, extant research suggests that work overload is a dominant stressor while intrusive technology features like email notifications are key predictors of stressors (Ayyagari et al., 2011). One reason the ubichron experiences technostress is the ubichron’s belief squeezing more cycles of activity into a finite amount of time is “good”.

## 3.4. Dimensionality

Ubichronic time is uni-dimensional in that time flows in one irreversible direction. But ubichronic time is qualitatively diferent, because separate ubichrons travel forward through time at their own unique rates, i.e., travel forward in time is relative for ubichrons. As discussed in the sections on continuity and homogeneity, time to a ubichron is linked strongly to a sense of frequency, diversity, and value of specific events. Ubichrons movement through time is principally measured by how much the ubichron gets accomplished and the major factors determining the rate are socioeconomic ones like wealth. For example, ISPs will deliberately reduce or increase the speed of one’s Internet communication based on the price one is willing to pay. Similarly, whether one has an unlimited or metered bandwidth 4G or 5G connection depends on one’s ability to spend for such a connection, and place of residence. Having an unlimited 4G or 5G connection in turn shapes whether waiting time is “found time”- an opportunity to do something else (faster travel through time) (Chung et al., 2015; Google, 2012) or “idle time”, i.e., – time that is “wasted” (slower travel through time).

The ability of ubichrons to move through time at diferent relative rates has caused the emergence of new attitudes and behaviour. The perception that one can move faster through time has increased the appetite for information consumption. For example, there is Fear of Missing Out (FOMO), a social anxiety disorder manifested as a compulsive concern that one might miss an opportunity for social interaction, novel experience or some other satisfying event (Przybylski et al., 2013). Similarly, the concepts of “found time” and “reclaimed time” now exist as a counterpoint to “idle time”. “Found time” occurs when users’ momentary exposure to a new stimulus immediately triggers an urge to pursue a new task related to the stimulus (Chung et al., 2015; Google, 2012). For example, a user could be waiting at the bus stop when he sees a new ad about something, finds it interesting, and launches a search or looks through Wikipedia to find more information. As another example, salespeople while on the move in between meetings or while travelling conduct business with clients remotely using mobile devices (BenMoussa, 2005). Such “reclaimed time”, enabled by ubiquitous computing, and driven by the tension between the ubichrons’ view of time as both linear and cyclical reportedly saves people 38 days a year on average (Jayawardhena & Korsah, 2011).

## 3.5. Subjectivity

To the ubichron, time is principally subjective. Research in information systems largely recognises this, highlighting that ubiquitous technologies cause users to experience elements of subjective time like flow (Koufaris, 2002) and cognitive absorption (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). Similarly, studies demonstrate users lose their sense of objective time when engaging with ubiquitous technologies- people are unable to articulate how much time they actually spend on Facebook (Ernala et al., 2020).

Although ubichronic time is inherently subjective, there are periods of time shared with others that must become intersubjective. Thus, for example, a ubichron does not necessarily work continuously from 9–5 (objective time). While the ubichron’s oficial ofice hours may be 9 to 5, some of that time is spent performing online grocery shopping or monitoring their child with an Internet of Things camera. Similarly, when the day ends, the ubichron could bring work home.

However, because the ubichron must interact with others, there are time blocks the ubichron allocates to be shared with others, i.e., intersubjective time. For example, the ubichron may have ofice hours, and be physically in the workplace from 9 to 5. Alternately, the ubichron could schedule specific times to interact with others. Dr. Hekemai’s schedule is unique to her own personal circumstances- her having a child, and her dependency on the tram determine the times she works (subjective time). However, Dr. Hekemai nevertheless logs onto the online clinic and medical clinic at her rostered times (intersubjective time).

## 3.6. Chronicity

Ubichrons are polychronic but engage in a specific type of chronicity we call “managed polychronicity”, which occurs when polychronic work is done with monochronic values. Ubiquitous technology is allowing us to complete tasks more eficiently. It has also allowed us to more eficiently consume “dead” time. For example, one can work on one’s tablet computer while waiting at a doctor’s ofice. Some individuals hold two or three meetings simultaneously using instant messaging applications or multiple devices. Accordingly, ubichrons typically work on multiple things in a given time period (exhibiting shades of polychronic behaviour) (Waller et al., 1999). However, as per our definition of ubichronic time, ubichrons are embedded in an environment surrounded by ubiquitous technologies that continuously display the time. They are therefore more aware of the time spent on each task (monochronic behaviour). As a result, ubichrons display behaviour qualitatively different from those with monochronic or polychronic view of time.

Ubichronic time eficiency has moved into a state where limits to the human body define how eficient ubichrons can be (Luximon & Goonetilleke, 2012). As one example, many workers check their email prior to going to bed. However, blue light wavelengths emitted by computing devices trigger the brain to think it is daytime, disrupting ubichrons’ ability to sleep, thereby impeding work performance. Enter the blue light screen protector designed to filter blue light from the screen (Rea, 2019).

## 4. Research issues for ubichronic work environments

In this section, we examine a series of research questions to be addressed considering the emerging ubichronic behaviours discussed in the prior sections. As a new perspective on time, ubichronic time and its associated behaviours will have significant impacts on the workplace. ## 4.1. Work will be performed in a discontiguous manner

Discontiguous work is distinct from but related to the discontinuous view of time. “Discontinuous” relates to a particular view of time, while “discontiguity” relates to how work is performed. Discontiguous time-based work is designed so tasks occupy short blocks of time separated over time. Consider the format of an online Cisco Certified Network Administrator (CCNA) course against a typical CCNA course where participants are physically present (Bombal, 2020). Here, the course is separated into short (less than 20 minute) videos, and the modules of the course can be attended at diferent, not necessarily contiguous times. In contrast, in contiguous-time based work, work is designed so tasks occupy large blocks of time which follow each other in time. The typical physical CCNA course is multi-day (Certstafix, 2020). Both student and instructor commit to spending entire days dedicated to learning the material. Large blocks of contiguous time (e.g., multiple hours across days) are allocated to completing tasks.

Both discontiguous and contiguous work can have similar outcomes- both the classroom and online versions of CCNA courses are designed so the student can obtain CCNA certification. However, the design of work is diferent. In an online CCNA course, lessons are broken into substantively shorter chunks.

## 4.1.1. Work-day is stretched

A second major shift in the nature of work is that the work day will not be defined with fixed hours, but instead is stretched throughout the day and interleaved with non-work. As discussed, ubichrons work in a state of managed polychronicity i.e., always trying to save time with each task and yet working on multiple diverse tasks. This combined with remote work, where work does not necessarily occur in a defined location means ubichrons are increasingly performing work outside of scheduled hours and performing nonwork during scheduled hours. This blurring of the boundaries between work-time and non-work-time biases the employee to spend more time on work. This has been demonstrated in multiple studies (Gallup Organization, 2013; Messenger et al., 2017). It also causes disruption in sleep patterns (Messenger et al., 2017). For example, one increasingly observed phenomenon is “revenge bedtime procrastination” (Liang, 2020), where ubichrons sacrifice sleep to entertain themselves. Gaining personal time at the expense of sleep is unhealthy – leading to not only medical conditions like obesity (Chaput & Dutil, 2016) but also cognitive conditions that afect job performance (Barnes et al., 2011; Naitoh et al., 1990).

We briefly discuss these two impacts on the nature of work and then discuss the research implications. We moreover argue that most existing workplaces are not designed to handle the discontiguous and stretched work-day modes emerging from the ubichronic time vision. Furthermore, optimal workplace designs for such emergent ways of working remain unknown. Information system (IS) researchers are best positioned to contribute to the design and management of workplace issues induced by the emergence of ubichronic time. Such contributions will have to occur at three levels, the individual, team, and organisational level. It is not possible to cover all possible issues the ubichronic perspective will create in the workplace. However, using the individual, team, and organisational holistic framework enables a systematic enquiry. At the individual level, IS researchers will need to explore how individual contexts and work environments shape how tasks are allocated and performed. Building on this, IS researchers will need to study how teams best coordinate and collaborate on group level tasks. Finally, at the organisational level, society will need to grapple with issues related to workforce planning and design given the new shifts in temporal behaviours at the individual and group levels.

## 4.2. Individual level

(1) Research question (Discontiguous): What are efective methods to change manage the transition from contiguous to discontiguous work?

Increasingly, discontiguous work is preferable to contiguous work (Messenger et al., 2017). For example, for Dr. Hekemai, there is no single block of time allocated to work life, and single block of time for home life- her life is a discontinuous series of interspersed work and home lifetime blocks. As such, her work gets done in a discontiguous manner. However, many existing workplaces are not set up for discontiguous work. The typical medical clinic, for example, has fixed opening and closing hours. This does not fit Dr. Hekemai’s schedule.

Indeed, much modern work life is not synchronised with the needs of modern workers. Mothers with small infants, for example, find it very dificult to synchronise 8am to 5pm working hours with infant biological clocks. Infants are often nocturnal and have relatively short wake/sleep cycles of about 3 hours. This is at odds with the 24-hour wake/sleep cycle assumed in the 8 to 5 work shift. Nevertheless, modern society has been reluctant to shift to discontiguous work. Most medical clinics are open only during ofice hours, mothers with infant children often have dificulty juggling work and infant care, and American schools operate from 8:00 to 3:00 in contravention of research showing that students benefit from later starting and ending times (Pinsker, 2018). Education work, both for educators and students, is not inherently contiguous.

But transforming workplaces that are largely built to support contiguous forms of work to support ubichrons’ discontiguous work will not be easy. First, much discontiguous work is centred on subjective rather than intersubjective time. Disparate ubichrons work their own hours and take breaks at unique times. Some ubichrons might want to work at night, while others prefer traditional ofice hours. As such, general policies that apply to the entire workforce will need to be replaced by policies allowing flexibility. For example, should workers be required to attend ad hoc meetings with supervisors? In traditional 9 to 5 ofices, the unwritten rule is yes. In a discontiguous environment, the supervisor and supervisee’s schedule may not be synchronised. Similarly, as attendance at work will become dificult to measure, performance evaluation mechanisms need to become more “outcome” focused, even for positions where outcomes are dificult to measure (e.g., administrative roles).

## (2) Research Question (Stretched Workday): How can stretched work be designed to ensure it does not lead to work life-home life imbalance?

Working in a stretched fashion does not mean ubichrons take several breaks in between tasks- it is often the opposite. Ubichrons tend to not only chop up work, but also simultaneously strive for eficiencies by continuously saving time within each time block, i.e., they engage in cyclical behaviours while in linear time. Furthermore, they spend more total time engaged in work-related tasks. One consequence of working while pushing for eficiencies is employee burnout. The world health organisation already recognises employee burnout as a serious workplace issue (Chatterjee & Wroth, 2019). The problem will worsen as work evolves to be more stretched- there is a correlation between working hours and burnout (Hu et al., 2016; Messenger et al., 2017). Human resource practices are yet to catch up to address the problem of employee burnout, especially when it comes to recommendations on how work needs to be designed to ensure ubichrons who blend both work and home tasks into high-eficiency cycles do not burn out.

We remind readers of how the industrial revolution led to the rise of the mandatory 14–16 hour workday, which was then forbidden by law -for example, in the US, the mandatory workweek limit is 40 hours, in Australia it is 37.5 hours etc.). In the same way, stretched work is likely to lead to similar outcomes which will require corresponding regulations.

## 5. Team level

(1a) Research Question (Discontiguous Work): What are the elements required for asynchronous coordination to be efective?

At the team level, multiple issues around scheduling and coordination of work emerge. One problem we currently face is limitations in the language of time, which is often used for coordination. For example, the language of clock time is used to schedule when one meets with another- for example, let us meet at 1pm. However, clock time is based partially on the rising and setting of the sun. The concepts of am (ante meridian- before noon) and pm (post meridian- after noon), for example, do not make sense if one is coordinating a meeting between New Zealand and the United Kingdom (often 12 hours apart). Similarly, the concept of “breakfast” is typically associated with particular hours of the day and cafeinated beverages, which can be very frustrating for a night shift worker who wants to go to sleep shortly after their final meal of the day. Beyond having irrelevant attached meaning (i.e., before noon, including a stimulant), these concepts also do not accurately capture valuable meaning necessary for coordinating ubichronic work. For example, the current language of time does not allow us to specify when a meeting should occur when one is in a diferent time zone. Even standardising on one person’s time as the time of coordination is insuficient, because meeting parties can have daylight savings time, which causes this standardised time to shift up to four times a year.

Without an appropriate language, basic concepts necessary for interactions in ubichronic work cannot be expressed. For example, the ubichronic worker ending their shift in the morning needs to express a desire for “the meal of the day prior to engaging in sleep”. This is not correctly conveyed by the word “dinner”. The person in Australia wanting a weekly meeting with someone in the US needs to arrange a meeting time. This cannot be expressed as “My 8pm”, because that person’s 8pm changes depending on daylight savings time- because both Australia and the US have daylight savings time, the meaning of this time changes 4 times a year.

This is especially dificult given ubichrons’ pursuit of personal epochs. The level of complication increases as a team of people each with a set of personal epochs try to coordinate. One consequence is the shift from synchronous to asynchronous modes of coordination. We predict that increasingly, ubichrons will prefer coordination in an asynchronous manner. Such is possible (for example) using boundary objects (Kellogg et al., 2006). Boundary objects like Google docs and Microsoft 365 allow multiple users to simultaneously work on a single artefact asynchronously. Traditional research on coordination has principally assumed synchronous coordination (Argote, 1982; Malone, 1987; Malone & Crowston, 1994). However, asynchronous coordination requires new methods and techniques. For example, how can we design better boundary objects to be efective for coordination?

(1b) Further Research Question b (Team – Discontiguous work): How does an emphasis on transient over social capital cause a change in ubichrons’ interactions with others?

Given ubichrons perceive time as a special case of discontinuous, epochal time and that they see time being a simultaneous fusion of both linear and cyclical and monochronic and polychronic time, the number of people an ubichron engages with is relatively higher than someone having more traditional perspectives of time. One with a traditional perspective of time will build a relationship with a small number of people over time (i.e., develop social capital). An ubichron’s desire for tight cycles of work activity often mean there are more engagements with people, but these engagements are less likely to repeat. In our Dr. Hekemai example, the doctor treats patients from all over the world. The key determining factor causing doctor and patient to meet is that both find the time convenient for meeting. The likelihood that the patient will encounter the identical doctor on the next visit is remote. Real world examples of the same phenomenon include purchasing a ride or food via Uber or UberEats, or renting a vacation home via Air B&B.

We coin the term “transient capital” to capture this social capital equivalent for the ubichron. Transient capital is the sum of the positive and negative encounters of a ubichron over time. The distinct nature of the transient capital encounter creates three key diferences in their concepts from social capital:

● Transient capital is explicitly quantified (e.g., Cui et al., 2020; Kokkodis & Lappas, 2020; Newberry & Zhou, 2019). Social capital is implicitly known. Sites like Uber and Air B&B measure the number of likes or dislikes individuals have. In contrast, the social capital between two parties has no explicit number- someone trusts or does not trust you.

● Transient capital is a property of an individual. Social capital arises in the relationship between individuals (Burt, 1997). One’s account on a service has its associated transient capital number. Social capital, in contrast, is not something possessed by a person, but in the relationship that person has with another (Coleman, 1990).

● Transient capital can be manipulated by “gaming” the “system”. For example, one can create artificial accounts to falsely inflate one’s transient capital (Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Chua et al., 2007). In contrast, while social capital can be manipulated, this is done on a more personal level by manipulating the party one has a relationship with.

These diferences in the way transient and social capital work may have implications for the way ubichrons choose to interact with others. The explicit measure of transient capital is often coarse and linear (e.g., a dichotomous or likert-scale like or dislike). It doesn’t matter how good the service was- one simply receives a like or a 5-star rating. Conversely, social capital often has nonlinear growth- significant encounters like having one’s life saved or receiving truly outstanding service greatly influences social capital accumulation. This could mean ubichrons become incentivised to behave diferently in their encounters. It might be better to provide a large number of satisfactory experiences, thereby boosting one’s positive reviews. It is less important to provide a small number of memorable experiences, as both a satisfactory and memorable experience result in the identical five stars being given.

## (1) Research Question (Stretched Workday): How can meeting schedules be designed to fairly take into account preferences of the minority?

One dificult problem in the workplace is coordinating time for multiple individuals to meet. This problem is compounded for ubichrons, because there will often be a majority with shared time preferences. For example, consider a team containing one mother with a young infant. The majority of the team prefer to work during “ofice hours”. The mother with the nocturnal infant, however, prefers working on the infant’s sleep schedule and thus nights are preferred. The problem here is ubichrons simultaneously view time as discontinuous and subjective. The significant time blocks of one individual are not the significant time blocks of others.

As another example, Europeans are particularly advantaged when it comes to coordinating international meetings. It is easiest to coordinate across time preferences, when those preferences have at most an 8–9 hour diference- 8–9 hours being the length of the average work day. European working hours ± 8 hours spans most of the Pacific Ocean, and hence it is easier for Europeans to coordinate meetings with team members living in the major international financial hubs than any other region of the world (Kay, 2016).

This raises ethical issues around the scheduling of meeting times. In many meetings, “majority rules” policies are in efect- the time preferences of the majority are used to determine meeting times. However, this disadvantages particular groups. In the above two examples, mothers with small infants and Australians and New Zealanders (who have a time zone that often does not span the ±8 hour ofice hours of other nations) are systematically disadvantaged. “Regular workers” and “Europeans” are advantaged- Australian and New Zealand work hours are stretched by majority rule-based institutions. In ethics, we recognise that particular ethnicities, sexual preferences, religious perspectives, etc. are privileged over others and often have laws against discriminating against minorities. However, time discrimination, i.e., the idea that particular time zones or time preferences are privileged over others does not exist. Nevertheless, in an ubichronic world, the ethics of time preference is going to be something we must consider. As we have demonstrated, “the nocturnal”, i.e., someone who tends to be more active at night such as the teenager or the nursing mother is regularly discriminated against by society. We need to devise fair ways to take these minorities into account.

## 6. Organisational level

(1) Research Question: How will discontiguous work change how workers are compensated?

At the organisational level, new organisational forms and ways of organising will be necessary for both organisations and societies to fully leverage the work of ubichrons. In our Dr. Hekemai example, the doctor works for three separate organisations with their own temporal work rhythms. The bulletin board organisation illustrates the idea of microwork, i.e., work where tasks are completed in 15 minutes or less. While microwork has always existed (Mintzberg, 1973; Tengblad, 2006), it is only recently that mechanisms to pay for microwork on a per-task basis have manifested (Deng et al., 2016). Examples of such mechanisms include Amazon Turk, where one can (for example) pay people to take surveys or perform simple tasks that facilitate machine learning like identify locations on a map and Weibo Q&A, where people are paid to answer questions (Ye et al., Forthcoming).

The online medical consultation organisation illustrates a casual work market for professionals. The key diference is that in most professional casual work, timings are defined by the organisation. Thus, doctors seeking locum, or adjunct professors seeking to teach classes do so based on the available time slots of the organisation. In contrast, the online medical consultation organisation principally serves as a market maker, by matching time requirements of patients and doctors- neither customer nor supplier asserts market dominance. Dr. Hekemai specifies the times she wants to consult, and patients who want that time are matched to her.

Finally, the medical clinic illustrates a traditional work environment and how such work environments adjust to cater to ubichronic work. In the medical clinic, the 40-hour work week or “full-time employee” has been transformed into full-time employee equivalents. It becomes less relevant that the medical clinic maintains individuals committed to working fixed hours at the clinic and more relevant that the clinic ensure it has a certain number of individuals committed to working at each hour the clinic is opened.

In combination, the three organisations illustrate the diferent ways both individuals and organisations organise work. Dr. Hekemai does not work for a single organisation. Instead, her labour is divided across multiple organisations. It is well documented that everyday actions and the actors enacting those actions together change the temporal structuring of work practices. Similarly, new ubichronic actions and ubichrons together will shape ongoing work rhythms of the organisation(s) in which they work (Orlikowski & Yates, 2002). The point here is ubichronic work will change not only organisations, but the way society organises work.

Many existing compensation systems are objective-time-based i.e., people earn a particular wage, because they appear at the ofice every day. Often, diferences in wage do not accurately reflect differences in performance (Sackman et al., 1968). For example, the most productive programmer in an organisation could be 10x as eficient as the least productive programmer, but not earn 10x the salary. Using ubiquitous technology, it becomes theoretically possible for salaries to become more equitable, or for people to more closely align productivity with their preferences. For example, the highly productive programmer, working discontiguously, could theoretically take jobs from 10 organisations, allocating work on those 10 organisations across time, to more closely align their performance with their compensation. However, more likely, the productive programmer could take jobs from fewer than 10 organisations but reserve the rest of their time for non-remunerative activities. Of course, that such work can change individuals’ remuneration doesn’t mean remuneration will change in the way we expect.

## (1) Research Question (Organizational/stretched workday): In what ways must regulative frameworks be changed to support ubichrons at work?

Organizations as well as the societies they operate in will also need new forms of organising to extract the value from particular cycles of work. Essentially, while workers and customers desire the stretched workday format, this is only allowable if (for example) regulations permit. To illustrate, while our online medical consultation example is technologically possible, current medical regulations render this dificult to do. Issues like which drug is legally purchased over the counter, or via prescription, who is legally allowed to prescribe a drug, and the price of drugs varies significantly across countries. Similarly, employment and tax regulations make our online medical consultation example dificult to implement. Dr. Hekemai cannot see Thai patients if she does not have a medical licence in Thailand. Similarly, if Dr. Hekemai is seeing patients in Thailand, which nation does she pay income tax to?

Designing regulations to handle new types of business is particularly dificult. The first online retail transaction occurred in 1994 (Miva, 2011). After more than thirty years, efective methods for taxing online sales have not been developed.

## 7. Conclusion

The concept of time is a product of social construction (Bluedorn, 2002). As technology adoption accelerates, new concepts will evolve leading to a reconstruction of accepted notions of time. As such, new behaviours grounded in the new time vision will emerge. How a society perceives time has a profound impact on how people in society live and work (Gross, 1984). This paper argues a new time vision called ubichronic time has emerged. We propose that ubichronic time is a consequence of prevalent usage of ubiquitous internet enabled mobile technologies. Using Saunders et al.’s (2004) framework we describe ubichronic time along six fundamental dimensions of time and contrast ubichronic time against the prevailing time visions identified in the literature. By doing so, we demonstrate the ubichronic time vision is qualitatively diferent. Specifically, we suggest that ubichrons (i.e., people who have a ubichronic time vision)- (1) see time as discontinuous where multiple diverse in quick succession, (2) view time as a set of frequent but short duration epochs that difer in their value. (3) view time as valuable but work on tasks that are cyclical, (4) view time as progressing at diferent individual rates, (5) view time as subjective and has to be experienced, but allows for the possibility of intersubjective time that is shared, and (6) perform polychronic work with monochronic values.

Given these properties of ubichronic time, we present a systematic framework that helps identify new emerging workplace behaviours and new theoretical concepts and research questions that will need to be studied at the individual, team, and organisational levels. As with any new phenomenon, there is much to explore to gain an exhaustive understanding, for example, identifying antecedents, consequences, and new methodologies that help measure and compare ubichronic time with other time visions.
