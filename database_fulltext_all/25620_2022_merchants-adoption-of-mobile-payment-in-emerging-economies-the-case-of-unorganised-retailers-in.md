# Merchants’ adoption of mobile payment in emerging economies: the case of unorganised retailers in India

Vishal Mishra<sup>a</sup>, Isabelle Walsh <sup>b</sup> and Ankur Srivastava<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>IBS, Hyderabad, IFHE (Deemed to be University, under section 3 of the UGC Act, 1956). India; <sup>b</sup>SKEMA Business School, Université Côte d’Azur. France

## ABSTRACT

A substantial body of literature shows that in addition to increased convenience, mobile payments may result in greater eficiency and contribute to digital and financial inclusion. Although researchers have paid a lot of attention to individual adoption of mobile payment, few studies investigate mobile adoption by businesses, especially unorganised businesses in developing countries. To address this research gap, we use an exploratory grounded theory approach to examine unorganised retailers in urban India. Using 56 semi-structured interviews with merchants, we highlight an emerging mobile payment ecosystem that includes technology value, regulatory bodies, non-regulatory bodies, and merchant dispositions. The data highlight power asymmetry in this ecosystem and show that time-varying interactions among its components determine merchants’ behaviour and resulting (non-) adoption. We find that not only are Indian regulatory bodies the most powerful influencers in the difusion of this technology; they are also important market actors. This study provides insights into the process of technology adoption by unorganised businesses in India. Moreover, this study sheds light on the important link unorganised retailers create between businesses and bottom-of-the -pyramid consumers, as well as between the state and its marginalised citizens, thereby confirming that such retailers are indispensable partners in economic growth and technologydriven inclusion.

## 1. Introduction

In the post-internet era, one of the most significant advancements in information and communication technology (ICT) has occurred through mobile devices (Van der Heijden & Junglas, 2006). Mobile devices have not only changed the way people communicate but also ushered them into a new era of entertainment, social interaction, and productivity (Van der Heijden & Junglas, 2006). One of the most important mobile, device-based productivity tools that has emerged in recent years is mobile payment (m-payment; Chandra et al., 2010). Scholars and practitioners have spent significant energy examining the phenomena of m-payment adoption and usage. However, their eforts have been skewed, as their main focus has been on customers, rather than businesses, as users (Dahlberg et al., 2015).

Existing studies (e.g., Blumenstock et al., 2015; Donovan, 2012; Kim et al., 2018; Kumar et al., 2019; Mariscal & Rojas-Lozano, 2020; Taylor, 2016; United Nations, 2020) on m-payment adoption and usage by businesses in both developed and developing countries show that they have favourable monetary and non-monetary impacts, including faster transactions, better inventory control, better marketing, enhanced customer experience, better customer engagement, and improved service and well-being. While evidence has grown about the benefits of m-payments, particularly in developing countries in Africa and Asia, another body of literature disputes these claims (De Albuquerque et al., 2016; Harry et al., 2014). Instead, research has also argued that the lack of access and ability to use digital payments could lead to the further marginalisation of small businesses (Masiero, 2017). Inherent risks of m-payment technology also expose users to fraud (Pal et al., 2018; Taylor, 2016), and the related security and privacy concerns (Martin, 2019) could lead to an adverse impact on brands and customers (Taylor, 2016).

Given this background, this study explores the adoption of m-payment technology by small retail businesses. Scant work is available in this specific area (Dahlberg et al., 2015) and even less so in the context of small and medium-sized enterprises in developing countries (e.g., Kumar et al., 2019). The study is set in India, a developing country. For this study, we follow a classification common in the information systems (IS) literature to bracket India as a developing country. According to this classification, the developing countries/economies are the countries in the Global South, barring the advanced economies in this region (Brown et al., 2020). The key characteristics of developing countries are identified as the challenges of poverty, health, and access to infrastructure, which lead to inequalities in development (Braa et al., 2007). Furthermore, these countries are characterised by a digital divide (Avgerou, 2008) and may also have governance problems that impede their socio-economic development and economic prosperity (Meso et al., 2009).

The target group we selected comprises unorganised retailers, which form a sizeable portion of the retail market in all developing countries (Jerath et al., 2016; Kohli & Bhagwati, 2012). These retailers are considered part of the unorganised sector, which is synonymous with the informal sector of the Indian economy (NCEUS, 2008). Corresponding firms are defined by the NCEUS, a national body created by the government of India, as “all unincorporated private enterprises owned by individuals or households engaged in the sale and production of goods and services operated on a proprietary or partnership basis and with less than ten total workers” (NCEUS, 2008, 2.5.7).<sup>1</sup> Our study is particularly relevant because it focuses on an emerging economy, which has received relatively scant attention in the literature on m-payment (De Albuquerque et al., 2016; Kumar et al., 2019). Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, few studies have addressed merchants’ (retailers’) adoption of m-payment in an unorganised sector. In this context,<sup>2</sup> our research question is: How do small and unincorporated businesses in urban India adopt the m-payment technology?

To address this question, we investigated merchants’ perceptions and perspective on m-payment. We adopted an overall inductive grounded theory approach to collect and systematically analyse interview data. Some of our findings diverge somewhat from existing studies on businesses’ adoption of m-payment. First, we find that the role of the government and afiliated institutions in India is not limited to being regulators; they have become the most powerful market actors in the m-payment ecosystem that consists of various stakeholders (e.g., banks, payment service providers, telecom firms, customers, regulators) and their inter-relationships (Donovan, 2012). Second, some specificities of m-payment emerged as significant in the unorganised-sector businesses’ adoption process, though these factors went mostly unnoticed in previous literature; conversely, some variables deemed significant in previous studies did not emerge in our investigations. We attribute these discrepancies to the specificities of our context as the majority of studies have focused on organised businesses. Furthermore, we found that the merchants (unorganised retailers in our context) play an important role in the difusion process of m-payment, in that they can facilitate financial inclusion, and thus social inclusion, of a disadvantaged and underserved part of the population. Therefore, such merchants and their businesses, which constitute the majority of retail businesses in developing countries, should be nurtured and supported.

We first summarise the literature review we conducted. We then describe our methodological approach, which is grounded in empirical data. We also discuss site selection, interviews, and the data collection process before summarising our results and describing the emerging ecosystem. Thereafter, we discuss these results in light of the existing body of knowledge, along with the contributions of our study. Finally, we discuss the limitations of our research and ofer avenues for future research.

## 2. Literature Review

To develop a perspective on m-payment adoption by merchants, we reviewed the body of studies on adoption and use of m-payment technology by businesses. We conducted our research iteratively, meaning that we alternated and combined data collection, data analysis and comparison, and extant literature. As such, part of this literature review took place ex ante and part ex post, after our model had begun to emerge. Termed the “non-committal phase of review” by Urquhart and Fernández (2013), the ex ante review helped us better understand the research domain and related issues. The ex post review helped us position our work within the literature and open possible avenues for future research: we searched the literature for frameworks, theories, or models that could resonate with the structure emerging from our data (“validating emergent theory”; Birks et al., 2013). However, we present a summary of our full review in this section to enhance understanding (Suddaby, 2006).

A review of the literature in the field of internet/ mobile technology adoption highlights many studies on consumer adoption and usage but few focusing on adoption by businesses (Boateng et al., 2019; Dahlberg et al., 2015).

Among the few studies on m-payment adoption by businesses in developed countries are those by Hayashi and Bradford (2014) in the United States, Lai and Chuah (2010) in Hong Kong, Lee et al. (2019) in South Korea, Liébana-Cabanillas and Lara-Rubio (2017) in Spain, and Mallat and Tuunainen (2008) in Finland. These studies focus predominantly on medium-sized and large organisations and identify variables related to customers (e.g., buying behaviour, experience, network externality), merchants/organisations (e.g., willingness, capability, technology readiness), technology (perceived ease of use, trust, brand value), and environment (e.g., standardisation, competition, regulation) that favourably or unfavourably influence the adoption of m-payment.

Recent years have witnessed increased scholarly attention to m-payment adoption by businesses, particularly in developing countries (e.g., Ghana: Cofie et al., 2020; China: Guo & Bouwman, 2016; India and China:; Kumar et al., 2019; Uganda: Malinga & Maiga, 2020; Malaysia: Moghavvemi et al., 2021; Bangladesh: Rahman et al., 2020; Indonesia: Suhud & Hidayat, 2017). The majority of these studies follow a quantitative methodology anchored in an existing theory/model and report variables related to technology characteristics, business characteristics, CEO/ owner characteristics, environmental characteristics (including the impact of environmental actors), and perceptions related to these variables as playing an important role.

A subset of this literature has investigated the adoption of m-payment by retail businesses (e.g., Kumar et al., 2019; Moghavvemi et al., 2021), and a few studies within this subset have focused specifically on small retail businesses (e.g., Boateng et al., 2019; Fitriani et al., 2020). The quantitative studies in this literature, rooted in the positivist tradition, use existing theories of technology adoption and identify variables similar to the aforementioned as playing an important role in the adoption process. By contrast, qualitative studies (e.g., Boateng et al., 2019; Pal et al., 2018) take an interpretivist route in an attempt to uncover contextual influences that influence a retailer’s adoption of m-payment. These studies identify cash shortage, comfort and familiarity with m-payment technology, social influence, savings of time/fees/cost, competition, and the nature of business (online/ofline) as variables having a favourable influence and lack of knowledge/skills, risk, lack of trust in telecom/financial service providers, and lack of support network as variables having an unfavourable influence.

To summarise this literature, we note an increase in the number of studies on merchants adoption of m-payment, especially in developing countries, but the majority of these studies are quantitative and rely on existing theories of technology adoption. As a result, they are rather limited in the identification of novel contextual factors and their inter-relationships. Similarly, some qualitative studies use factors taken from existing frameworks (e.g., Boateng et al., 2019 use Mallat & Tuunainen, 2008 pre-requisites, drivers, and barriers framework; Stepcic & Kabanda, 2016 use the TOE framework), which limits them somewhat in capturing the novelty and dynamics of local m-payment ecosystems.

Because most retail businesses in developing countries are unorganised (Jerath et al., 2016), we argue that understanding the m-payment ecosystem for retail businesses from their perspective is important. However, to our knowledge few studies address m-payment adoption in this category. A few exceptions do exist; for example, Harry et al.’s (2014) study of the South African market shows that risk, lack of support, and time-consuming registration process are key hindrances to retailer adoption and although mobile money promotes entrepreneurship, its benefits are predominantly reaped by the formal markets. Peša (2018) reports a similar contrast in his study of mobile money agents and tellers in Zambia, observing that mobile money can promote entrepreneurship and generate employment, but it can also lead to an increase in informal business activity and create inequalities.

Two noteworthy studies have addressed adoption of digital finance by unorganised retailers in India: Masiero (2017) investigates informal and unbanked street sellers, and Pal et al. (2018) examine retailers (street-facing shops). However, both studies focus predominantly on the political backdrop in which these businesses operate. The results of these studies show that the lack of access to information, support networks, and the lack of skills/knowledge are key deterrents to the adoption of m-payment and may cause further marginalisation of unorganised businesses.

Some studies that investigate m-payment in developing countries use the concept of “ecosystem” (e.g., Donovan, 2012; Kim et al., 2018). Increased interconnectedness aforded by digital technologies together with a highly dynamic business environment makes the ecosystem perspective a relevant lens for researchers, practitioners and policy makers alike (Guggenberger et al., 2020). These studies take a customer centric view of the m-payment network and recognise key actors of the ecosystem (like banks, merchants, customers, agents, network operators and regulators) and their associations (like partnership between banks and network operators, supervision by regulators).

In summary, our review shows a dearth of literature on m-payment adoption by unorganised retail businesses. This research gap is significant because, as Jerath et al. (2016) highlight, the majority of retail businesses, especially in developing countries, are unorganised. Existing studies in this domain fall short on two counts: they either use existing theories of technology adoption with a quantitative approach, which limits the identification of contextual factors, or follow a qualitative approach to data collection and analysis: although they uncover novel contextual factors, they do not go beyond listing and discussing such factors. Both types of studies fall short of developing new theories.

Furthermore, the studies set in India (Masiero, 2017; Pal et al., 2018) were conducted immediately after demonetisation. Announced in November 2016, demonetisation was a move by the union government of India to ban the INR500 and INR1,000 currency note denominations. The immediate reason put forth for this move was to remove black money from the economy. Ensuing shortage of cash necessitated a greater use of digital transactions, thereby suggesting that another agenda for demonetisation was to promote cashless transactions and make India digital (Shepard, 2017).

Because India had predominantly been a cash economy, the suddenness of the exercise and the resulting shortage of cash created extensive unrest and dificulty in subsequent days, especially for the economically disadvantaged. As a result, we argue that the data and analyses reported in such studies (including unintended/unfavourable consequences) were influenced largely by this event. Time has since elapsed, and new elements could have emerged. Thus, a more recent perspective is required. We aim to fill this gap with this study.

## 3. Methodology

We strived to follow an approach that could provide a fresh perspective, was grounded in the context, and was not limited by existing theoretical frames (Volkof et al., 2007). Grounded theory methodology (GTM) helps in developing new perspectives on wellestablished theoretical research areas and links well to practice (Sousa & Hendriks, 2006). Therefore, we used a GTM approach for data collection and analysis (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Glaser & Strauss, 1967) in an interpretive stance. GTM is an overall inductive research approach, though it may include some deductive arguments (Walsh et al., 2015). We followed the Straussian approach to GTM and its coding paradigm, because the first author is trained in this approach, which is congruent with his own worldview (Fendt & Sachs, 2008).

## 3.1. Site selection

To begin, we decided to conduct our study in the city of Hyderabad in Telangana, India, because it was administratively convenient and a convenience sample is acceptable in an exploratory GTM study (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Small unorganised retailers throughout the city formed the original sampling frame. A few months into the data collection process, however, we faced a dificulty in interviewing due to language. Although a few retailers we interviewed in Hyderabad could understand and speak Hindi, the oficial language in which some members of the research team are conversant, the majority of retailers could better understand and express themselves fluently in the native language of the state, Telugu.

Thus, the search for a geographic region that would be more amenable to the objectives of the study led us to extend the study to the city of Lucknow in Uttar Pradesh, where the native language is Hindi. The similarity to Hyderabad in terms of history, culture, infrastructure, and demography made it more practical to conduct the second (and larger) phase of data collection in this city. Thus, we used the first data “slice” (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) collected from Hyderabad to gain insights. As the challenges due to language barriers became evident, we then theoretically sampled (Glaser, 1978; Glaser & Strauss, 1967) respondents and collected further data from Lucknow, where this challenge was less acute and we could obtain access to a larger sample of the investigated population.

## 3.2. Data collection

We used semi-structured interviews to collect data during our interactions with the merchants. Data collection and analysis is an ongoing process in GTM; therefore, we incorporated feedback from our analysis to make modifications to questions asked. During later stages, and as categories began to emerge, we narrowed our focus to probe specific aspects of m-payment (e.g., the role of government institutions, sociocultural influences). The interviews lasted approximately 20–50 minutes. We recorded and transcribed them in the native language of the respondents to prevent any distortion. We did not decide ex ante the number of interviews; rather, we kept conducting interviews until saturation was reached (i.e., until no new element [concepts or relationships] emerged from the collected data) during several interviews.

To determine all relevant factors, we decided to include both adopters and the non-adopters of m-payment in our sample. The first phase of the data collection process (December 2016–March 2017) consisted of instrument creation and refinement based on the feedback gained from the field. This phase was conducted in Hyderabad. We conducted the second phase (April 2017–July 2018) in Lucknow.

Of our final data set of 56 useable interviews, 43 were from Lucknow and 13 were from Hyderabad (see Appendix B of online supplementary file for descriptive statistics of our sample). Interviews included three instances in which we conducted the interviews via telephone. All other interviews were first audio recorded and then transcribed. Beyond verbatims, the transcripts also included field notes taken by the interviewers along with physical evidence whenever possible (e.g., a photograph of the shop). In keeping with grounded theory precepts, we reviewed and analysed each interview as soon as the recording and the transcript were ready. The process ended when no new information was forthcoming (i.e., theoretical saturation; Corbin & Strauss, 2008). To obtain further insights into the investigated phenomenon and possible contextual changes over the period of the study, we were also attentive to secondary data from sources such as government and industry reports and newspaper and magazine articles. This helped us be observant of the actions/interactions of various actors to better understand the changing context and coping strategies. Triangulation of data collected using multiple methods and from multiple sources also helped us validate the emerging concepts/categories and their inter-relationships.

## 3.3. Coding

As a preliminary step, to respect anonymity we identified interviewees with an alphanumeric code: the first character indicates the place (H: Hyderabad; L: Lucknow); the second indicates the m-payment usage status (U: user; N: non-user); the third indicates gender (M: male; F: female); the fourth indicates the interview number (1–13 for Hyderabad; 1–43 for Lucknow); and, to identify the three telephone interviews, an asterisk at the end. Thus, for example, LUM32\* is the 32nd interview conducted by phone with a male merchant operating in Lucknow who uses some form of m-payment.

The first stage of the coding process followed open coding (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). Here, we analysed each response statement for identification of events, actions, and interactions. We identified concepts behind ideas, suggestions, events, actions, and interactions and grouped similar or related concepts together e.g., Merchant’s curiosity/innovativeness; Risk; Monitoring by government; Network externality; Role of banks. We conducted this process for each respondent separately and compared the outcome with the analysis and coding of earlier interviews. This process went through multiple iterations. The second stage of coding involved axial (relating codes to each other and combining them into broader categories) and selective (choosing one category as core category and coding for all categories related to the core) coding (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). The primary purpose of this exercise was to identify broader categories and concepts (using subcategories obtained from the open coding process) and to identify relationships between them (Strong & Volkof, 2010).

With greater understanding gained from the data (both primary and secondary), we made modifications to the coding schema. We also wrote memos as we collected, compared, and recorded diferent perspectives, insights, and ideas generated during the process. This approach helped us theorise on merchants’ adoption of m-payment in India.

The “core category” (Glaser, 1978) that emerged from our work is an m-payment ecosystem, an interconnected network of key actors/institutions, and their role (Ritala et al., 2013) in this ecosystem. Beyond the merchants themselves, this ecosystem includes as key actors customers, government/state and afiliated institutions, m-payment companies, banks, telecommunication companies, and m-payment technology. We grouped these key actors into four main categories: technology value (TV), regulatory bodies (RB), non-regulatory bodies (NRB), and merchant dispositions (MD).

## 4. Results: The Indian m-payment ecosystem

The phenomenon of merchants’ m-payment adoption in India emerged as a function of the characteristics of various actors in the m-payment ecosystem and the actions of, and interactions between, these actors. This ecosystem comprises five broad components: MD, TV, RB, NRB, and the behavioural outcome (i.e., merchant behaviour of adoption/non-adoption: MB). We first define and describe the components of the ecosystem and their nomenclature and then investigate their inter-relationships (see Figure 1).

In Figure 1, we number the relationships between the various components to facilitate their identification and description. Full arrows in the figure indicate influences (e.g., regulatory influence) that are strong and take efect within a short period, whereas the dotted arrows depict influences (e.g., non-regulatory influence) that are relatively weak and appear to take efect only over a longer period. We illustrate our arguments with some verbatim quotes extracted from the interviews or researchers’ memos.

## 4.1. Merchant dispositions

As central actors of this study, the merchants are at the core of many interactions and initiatives. During the course of our interaction with merchants, and more particularly when we compared adopters and nonadopters, we realised, not unexpectedly, that factors related to the merchants influence their decision making. We categorised these factors as curiosity/innovativeness, education/awareness, and initiative to promote m-payment. Some of these factors are related to personality traits such as curiosity, innovativeness, and initiative:

![](/api/attachments/P389E6JT/fulltext/images/ec7baa31c8d77c2cc8c0a73bf62f382df34d0a5cb3969936a329d10985d5374f.jpg)  
Figure 1. The m-payment ecosystem emerging from our work.

“When a new product/service comes, there is some innate curiosity about it. Therefore, whenever I see any such opportunity, I try to use it”. LUM20 (coded as “curiosity/innovativeness”)

These factors are therefore more stable in nature than others, such as knowledge, education, and technology expertise, which are more dynamic and therefore liable to evolve and change over a shorter time span:

“When we will have some knowledge about it [m-payment], then only we can take payments from mobile”. LNM6 (coded as “education/awareness”)

We recognised that the interaction between stable and dynamic characteristics of the merchant, as well as environmental forces (including technology and regulatory/non-regulatory actors), results in the development of the merchants’ favourable/unfavourable inclination towards a technology. This inclination, which may vary both spatially and temporally, is critical to the decision of adoption and use of a technology. To emphasise the dynamic nature of this category, we decided to name it “merchant dispositions”. This nomenclature signifies an overarching construct that covers both a general disposition, which is more related to fairly stable personality traits, and a specific disposition towards information technology, which is more related to a specific technology and surrounding context (Walsh et al., 2016). Note that we identified some merchants who, despite being comfortable with mobile technology, were not using it for taking customer payments because their businesses were not sizeable enough (critical mass: Van Slyke et al., 2007).

## 4.2. Technology value

The respondents mentioned various characteristics of the m-payment technology, related to both the hardware (H/W) and software (S/W) as well as some related to the process of using the m-payment technology itself (e.g., charges on m-payment, security, physical risk, time, connectivity, cash convertibility, infrastructure):

“One of the benefits of m-payment is that one does not need to carry cash so there is no risk”. LUM9 (coded as “physical risk”)

Many actors are a part of the m-payment ecosystem; thus, the process of usage involves the interaction of these actors with the m-payment technology. As a result, some of the characteristics of m-payment technology itself are defined by these actors (“sociotechnical artifact”; see Silver & Markus, 2013). For example, the cost of m-payment technology could involve the cost of the H/W (i.e., mobile handset) or S/W (e.g., operating system, m-payment app), the cost of internet access, or payment charges (e.g., by the bank or the mobile app company). Some of the features associated with the technology clearly have a positive influence (e.g., need for change/cash) on the evaluation of technology, while others have a negative influence (e.g., security). A user’s impression is driven by the overall assessment of the value or utility derived from the technology. Following this rationale, we decided to use “technology value” for this category of related concepts. In contrast with a name that focuses on features of technology, the value perspective gives a dynamic interpretation to this category (as “value” is a contextual assessment that varies from one user to another and from one period to another).

## 4.3. Regulatory bodies

We conceptualised the significant role of what we termed “regulatory bodies” in the m-payment ecosystem through various mechanisms e.g., spreading awareness, incentives, regulations/enforcement:

“If the government says that Paytm [a private m-payment firm] is mandatory, then people will rush to act on it and within a day, will start using it. . . . If I or you say, it may not work. Some people will listen/understand, and some will not”. LNM31\* (coded as “regulation/enforcement”)

We determined from our data that the government/state is viewed as the most powerful actor/institution and holds sway over and influences all the other actors in the ecosystem. This perception is enhanced in the Indian context given the majority status enjoyed by the current central government (Laughland & Weaver, 2014).

From the interviews, secondary literature, newspaper/magazine articles, and commentaries, we inferred that “regulations/enforcement” was a strong aspect of this component of the ecosystem: certain radical policy initiatives (e.g., demonetisation, the Goods and Service Tax,<sup>3</sup> the Make in India initiative,<sup>4</sup> Jan Dhan Yojna<sup>5</sup>; Panicker, 2017) and the zeal with which they were pursued by the current government further contributed to this perception. As one of the merchants explained:

“Actually, after demonetisation there is no other option [than to use the m-payment]”. HUM12 (coded as “regulations/enforcement”)

We also realised that our respondents felt this perception even more strongly with regard to m-payment technology because of the ever-increasing emphasis of recent governments, particularly the present one, on the greater use of technology and digitisation (Rout, 2015).

We noticed that, functionally, the merchants do not distinguish between the government/state and various government (or government-afiliated) institutions that constitute the major public sector enterprises. Consequently, we pooled this category of concepts under the broad umbrella term “regulatory bodies”.

## 4.4. Non-regulatory bodies

What we termed “non-regulatory bodies” include banks, m-payment firms, telecommunication firms, and customers. During the course of the interviews, many non-user merchants admitted that to remain competitive, they may have to adopt m-payment technology in the future. This admission implies that these merchants understand that in the face of a sizeable demand from the customers, they may have to adopt m-payment or be prepared to risk losing substantial business.

In addition, we found that there are other actors in the m-payment ecosystem who also influence the m-payment adoption and usage by the merchants. These actors include m-payment companies:

“Services such as Paytm have come recently. I did not know about it earlier. People from Paytm came to me and they explained how cashless transactions can be done using their services. I also felt that this can be tried for the convenience of our customers”. HUM12

Other actors mentioned were telecommunication companies (“Internet is very cheap now. It is not costly at all [to do m-payment]”: LUM10) and banks (“If the government banks get involved then everyone will become aware about m-payment”: LNM31\*).

The strategies and actions of these actors influence not only the merchant dispositions but also various aspects of the technology and its value. Our analysis indicates that privacy, security, and speed of m-payment technology, complaint resolution, and other technical/business support initiatives are important factors in m-payment adoption. This implies that mobile H/W and S/W manufacturers, m-payment companies, and private banks and telecommunication service providers, whose actions afect the aforementioned features, are other institutions that play a critical role in the adoption and usage of m-payment. To distinguish all these actors/institutions from regulatory bodies, we decided to name this category “non-regulatory bodies”.

## 4.5. Relationships between constructs

In this section, we examine all relationships between the components of our proposed ecosystem and shed light on the nature (strong/weak) of these relationships with the help of a few examples. We have provided illustrations of these relationships extracted from primary data (verbatim quotes extracted from interviews with merchants) and secondary data (as observed in extant literature, reports, and newspapers/magazines) in Appendix C (See online supplementary file).

As noted previously, we found that merchants are subject to various influences, including those related to technology, their own personality traits, and other external factors that culminate in favourable/unfavourable merchant dispositions. These dispositions influence a merchant’s decision to adopt m-payment (Path 0: MD → MB). Merchant dispositions towards government policies for m-payment and towards m-payment-related strategies of nonregulatory actors also serve as feedback mechanisms to both the regulatory and non-regulatory actors (Path 10: MD → RB; Path 3: MD → NRB). Changes in policies, market strategies, technology, and infrastructure by the regulatory and non-regulatory actors (e.g., creating new technological platforms, enhancing security, creating awareness, providing training, ofering monetary incentives) can be traced back to this feedback.

We acknowledge the influence of technology through the category technology value, a holistic assessment of the m-payment technology that contributes to the development of the merchant’s (un) favourable dispositions (Path 1: TV → MD). Furthermore, the assessment of technology can also result in automatic feedback to other actors and institutions. This feedback may force government and afiliated institutions to modify or create m-paymentrelated policy(ies) (Path 8: TV → RB). Such an assessment may also force market actors to modify their design, development, and marketing strategies (Path 7: TV → NRB). An example of such influences in our context is the improvement in interface of m-payment apps and enhancement of m-payment security by m-payment companies in India. The launch of a new m-payment app, Bharat interface for money (BHIM) by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), and subsequent creation of a new version of the BHIM app for iPhone users are other examples of the same.

We found that by virtue of the power and responsibility vested in people’s representatives, the state (and afiliated institutions) is the most powerful of all actors in our ecosystem. This finding holds particularly true for a stable majority government in a democracy, such as that in India since 2014, such that the state enjoys the privilege of policy making and regulation. The regulatory bodies thus have a strong impact on the dispositions and consequently the behaviour of individual subjects (in this case, unorganised merchants; Path 2: RB → MD). Furthermore, this influence naturally extends to other market-oriented actors/institutions (non-regulatory bodies) that are also the subjects of the state (Path 5: RB → NRB). A prime example of this influence in the Indian context is the policy of demonetisation and its overnight implementation, which afected individual citizens and businesses alike. We observed that the government and afiliated institutions in India, in addition to their traditional role (policy making, regulations, and enforcement), are actively involved in technology development and market activities (e.g., through NPCI, the m-payment interface, which has developed the unified payments interface in current use). These activities also include the role of public sector banks and telecommunication companies as infrastructure creators for m-payment. As a result, we find that the influence of government and afiliated institutions (i.e., regulatory bodies) also contributes to increasing/decreasing the value perception of technology (Path 4: RB → TV).

Another external influence we observed is attributed to actors/institutions other than government and afiliated institutions (i.e., non-regulatory bodies). Their actions influence m-payment technology value by contributing to technology development, development of requisite infrastructure, and other aspects of m-payment technology access and usage such as pricing and security (Path 6: NRB → TV). Nonregulatory bodies influence merchant dispositions directly (Path 11: NRB → MD), for example, through m-payment promotion activities of the associated actors. Because these actors also represent market forces, they influence policy formulation through activities such as lobbying for favourable policies and supportive infrastructure related to m-payment (Path 9: NRB → RB). Some examples are the provision of a partial banking licence for m-payment companies by the banking regulator, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and compensation of banks for discounts ofered to individual users of m-payment.

Our immersion in both the primary and secondary data helped us attain an increased awareness of the socio-economic and political context of our study. It caused us to realise that the relationships and the ensuing influences that we observed are a function of the power wielded by a given actor/institution. This helped us classify the influences into two types: strong and weak (see bold lines for strong influences and dotted lines for weak influence in Figure 1). Whereas regulatory influences on merchants are strong, the influence of a merchant on regulatory and nonregulatory bodies are weak. As the latter two actors are more powerful than a merchant, the impact of a merchant on these actors is unlikely to manifest immediately. Similarly, the impact of technology and non-regulatory actors on regulatory bodies are examples of weak influences that are likely to take longer to manifest than the impact of (stronger) regulatory bodies.

An example of the weak influence observed in our study is related to the launch of the m-payment technology application BHIM by NPCI. This app was initially developed only for Android phones, and there were technical issues with its earlier versions.

In subsequent months, building on the feedback from various stakeholders including end users (MD → RB), NPCI sorted out these issues. Furthermore, to cater to Apple’s iPhone users, the firm created an iOScompatible version of the BHIM app (Path 8: TV → RB). Another example of weak influence was the interplay between private m-payment service providers such as Paytm, the government, and the RBI. Starting in 2015, when Paytm first applied for the licence to perform banking activities, the interactions between these institutions resulted in a policy that in 2017 granted permission (see “payments bank” in Pani, 2017) to m-payment companies such as Paytm and telecommunication companies such as Airtel to carry out certain banking operations (Path 9: NRB → RB).

## 5. Discussion: Where existing literature does not explain it all

In this section, we highlight our contribution in relation to classic adoption theories and broad theoretical frameworks often applied. We then discuss our results and theoretical contributions with regards to the specific literature related to m-payment adoption: we highlight the main discrepancies between this extant literature and our results, the limitations of our work, future research avenues, implications for development, and practical contributions.

## 5.1. Extending classic adoption theoretical lenses

The focus of this study is on understanding the factors that influence merchants’ adoption of m-payment in unorganised retail businesses. Such businesses have the characteristics of both an organisation and an individual (Thong, 1999). Therefore, we argue that some of the most widely used adoption theories, which are specific to either an individual or an organisation (e.g., TAM, UTAUT, TOE) have somewhat limited applicability in our context. In addition, the use of existing theories in many cases limits the ability to unearth new contextual factors. By adopting an exploratory inductive approach, we move beyond classic adoption theories and the broad theoretical frameworks often applied in IS research.

By observing bi-directional influences operating between various actors (e.g., entrepreneur, technology, government) in our m-payment ecosystem, our study incorporates merchants’ feedback, which is an integra part of a dynamic business environment. It also involves causal loops between constructs, which are mostly absent in many existing adoption models. Furthermore, by decoupling the environmental factors as regulatory and non-regulatory, our work expands the

TOE framework (Tornatzky & Fleischer, 1990), which is the preferred theoretical lens to investigate technology adoption by businesses (e.g., Cofie et al., 2020).

We respond to Orlikowski and Iacono (2001) call to focus on technology as an artefact and take into account m-payment technology characteristics, which contribute to the value perception of technology. Thus, we treat technology as an object that has material characteristics. M-payment technology characteristics (e.g., those associated with the hardware, software, internet, telecommunication network) influence and are influenced by other actors/institutions and create the perception of value. By highlighting the interactions between technology (through the process of technology value creation) and other actors/institutions, such as the merchant and regulatory and nonregulatory bodies, we identify the role of technology as an active actor/institution (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Volkof et al., 2007). Consequently, while staying congruent with institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983), structuration theory (Giddens, 1984), and actor network theory (Law, 1992), which are all widely used theoretical lenses in the IS field, our work addresses the concern that technology is considered a passive actor in many applications of these theories. Furthermore, merchant dispositions in the proposed ecosystem highlights the role of agency along with the institutions, thereby overcoming a criticism of institutional theory (Volkof et al., 2007).

Thus, our study addresses some of the concerns raised by experts in the IS field related to some classic adoption theoretical frameworks (e.g., Orlikowski & Barley, 2001). First, by exploring technology value, we give the role of technology as an artefact due consideration (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). Second, by taking into account the merchant dispositions, the study puts the requisite importance on the role of individual forces as distinct from institutional forces. Finally, our ecosystem explicates time-varying dynamic influences, which have been missing in extant literature.

## 5.2. The m-payment ecosystem in India

We found that the m-payment ecosystem in India is characterised by openness, power asymmetry and the presence of a central powerful actor, which appears to be a mix of a “sociocentric ecosystem” and an “orchestrating actor ecosystem” (Guggenberger et al., 2020). While there are other studies that have adopted the ecosystem perspective to study m-payment, these studies are predominantly end-user/customer centric. They do not appear to investigate in depth the interrelationships between actors (e.g., Donovan, 2012) and, hence, they miss out on relational influences that emerged from our work as significant, e.g., the “reverse” impact of merchants, technology and other actors on regulators. Some of these studies (e.g., Kim et al., 2018) do not even consider technology as an actor of the ecosystem.

Although India is classified as a developing country, we found that the Indian market conditions fluctuate on a continuum, with developed market characteristics on one end (e.g., mobile technology advancement, mature banking and telecommunications sectors, political freedom) and developing market characteristics on the other (e.g., population age, general infrastructure, urbanisation, income inequality, high crime rate, low standards of living). The most striking feature of the Indian m-payment market that we noted is the predominant role of the state and afiliated institutions. While previous studies highlight the role of the state/government in technology difusion (e.g., Braithwaite, 2006; Guo & Bouwman, 2016), they mostly investigate it from an economic and public policy perspective and less so from a market-oriented function perspective. We observe that the role of government in the m-payment ecosystem in India can be likened to that in other countries. Existing literature also shows that governments have been proactive in promoting the difusion of ICT, especially in developing countries (Kim et al., 2018), such as China (Xu et al., 2020), Kenya (Mugo et al., 2017), and Mexico (Mariscal & Rojas-Lozano, 2020).

In the context of businesses’ m-payment adoption, Fitriani et al. (2020), Guo and Bouwman (2016), and Mahakittikun et al. (2020) report a similar role of government. We note that the traditional role of a welfare state and a regulator results in an imbalance of power in favour of government and afiliated actors/institutions. This imbalance is experienced by individuals and businesses alike and engenders fear, particularly among the economically/ socially disadvantaged and marginalised groups. Indeed, Masiero’s (2017) and Pal et al.’s (2018) studies on the adoption of digital payments in the context of demonetisation show that certain unorganised businesses in India, especially those lacking skills and access to resources, could be subjected to further relegation. Somewhat in line with Sherer (2010), we find that the government and afiliated institutions are the most powerful actors and therefore also exert a coercive pressure along with a normative/mimetic pressure on other actors in the ecosystem. As indicated earlier, this observation is similar to what has been observed for other developing countries, who despite the challenges of inequalities and poor socio-economic development are characterised by strong and proactive government institutions. However, the role of these institutions in India is not limited to that of a regulator; we show that the government and afiliated institutions in India are also involved as market actors (in terms of the m-payment technology/platform, banking, and telecommunications) in the m-payment ecosystem. This finding has not been reported elsewhere, especially in developed economies, where governments/states do not usually actively partake in the market.

King et al.’s (1994) suggestion that institutional actors influence others by persuasion or by regulation resonates with our understanding of the role of regulatory and non-regulatory institutions in the context of a merchant’s adoption of m-payment. While many studies (e.g., Cofie et al., 2020; Liébana-Cabanillas & Lara-Rubio, 2017; Rahman et al., 2020) have considered factors related to the technology and the organisation, the inter-relationship of these factors has not been fully explored. Furthermore, some authors (Fitriani et al., 2020; Mahakittikun et al., 2020) distinguish between the role of regulatory institutions and other government institutions, a distinction that did not emerge as such from our data.

Existing studies (Guo & Bouwman, 2016) also report the influence of factors such as labour, trade, and customer associations. As these associations represent the interests of their respective advocacy groups, they have influence over formal and organised businesses, which are well represented in society and economy. We find that these factors do not wield any influence over unorganised retailers and therefore do not influence their adoption of m-payment. Similarly, we find the influence of supply chain partners (i.e., suppliers) absent in our study. Moreover, barring a few odd instances, we did not find many reports of the impact of competition (i.e., competitors adopting m-payment) on m-payment adoption; we suspect that the impact of competition was felt indirectly (i.e., through customer demand for the facility of m-payment).

Some studies in developing countries (Kim et al., 2018; Peša, 2018) highlight the role of mobile money agents who operate independently on behalf of service providers. This role is important to ensure last-mile access, especially in remote areas. Such agents (business correspondents) also work on behalf of banks in India to promote financial inclusion in areas where bank branches/ATMs do not exist. However, we did not find such agents for m-payment service providers in the Indian context, possibly because of either the (perceived) comfort of urban retailers with mobile and m-payment technology or their (perceived) easy access to information/knowledge about this technology. These perceptions of service providers make the need of support/help in the form of agents redundant in our context.

Our study further contributes to the literature by identifying factors that, though they are critical to the given context and contribute greatly to merchants’ m-payment adoption in India, have largely been absent from the literature: personal safety, cash

convertibility, and transaction proof. Personal safety, which is related to the crime rate in a given geographic area (not an unusual phenomenon for developing countries), has a favourable influence on technology value. Carrying cash attracts the attention of criminals and petty thieves, and merchants noted that plastic money and m-payment, in addition to helping with the problem of counting and carrying change, also provide some level of physical security. Cash convertibility is related to the speed with which mobile money (or any such virtual currency) can be converted to cash. M-payment companies and banks (both public [i.e., regulatory bodies] and private [i.e., non-regulatory bodies]) are key actors responsible for cash conversion of mobile money and consequently afect technology value. This feature is extremely relevant in the cash economies that characterise many developing countries. Moreover, many merchants in the unorganised sector are small-scale, low-volume players who procure and sell goods on a daily basis, thereby necessitating the availability of cash at the end of the day. Finally, we found that transaction proof is critical in mitigating the uncertainty associated with m-payment transactions. The merchants suggested that a receipt in the form of an email or SMS is important, as it serves as proof of transaction and can help in delays and disputes. Again, m-payment companies and banks are key actors responsible for generating this proof. Regarding the documentation or proof/record of a transaction, many unorganised merchants in India do not maintain (or submit) proper records of their transactions to the government agencies in order to evade taxes (Kumaraswamy, 2019). Digitisation initiatives such as m-payment ensure that the transaction records are available to government agencies (e.g., the income tax department) and thus serve as a deterrent to these merchants’ adoption of m-payment. Therefore, while some merchants may desire transaction proof as a backup in case of a problem, others may not want it because it serves as proof that is accessible to tax authorities. This aspect is governed completely by regulatory actors/institutions and has a negative impact on technology value. We can relate this finding to that of Martin (2019), who highlights a lesser studied phenom enon associated with mobile money: monitoring/sur veillance of (1) users by service providers and government agencies and (2) service providers by government agencies. Whereas we observed surveillance as a deterrent for potential adopters, Martin (2019) regards it as an outcome of the usage of mobile money and, thus, an undesirable (from the perspective of users and/or service providers) consequence of mobile money. Diaz (2017) puts forth a related, and widely discussed, perspective: in the context of developing countries, monitoring and data access by the governments and service providers are tantamount to violation of the fundamental right to privacy and freedom of communication.

## 5.3. Some discrepancies in relation to extant literature

In this section, we highlight some aspects that have been deemed significant in the literature but did not emerge as such in our results. We begin by comparing our results with existing studies on unorganised retailers and then move on to discuss notable results specific to customer-related variables, trust, privacy, and some organisational factors.

We note that, in general, extant studies have taken a favourable view of m-payment technology. However, a few studies (e.g., Harry et al., 2014; Masiero, 2017; Pal et al., 2018; Peša, 2018) have contested this assessment. In our study, we noticed the potential of marginalisation of those retailers that (due to old age) were unable or reluctant to move with the times and learn the new technology. However, in general, we observed that the m-payment technology was perceived as beneficial. We attribute this perception to wide proliferation of mobile phones (including smartphones) in both rural and urban India. As explained earlier, this diference from Pal et al.’s (2018) and Masiero’s (2017) studies could be because they were conducted immediately after demonetisation. In comparison, our data collection continued till well into 2018, by which time the targeted individuals/businesses had had suficient time to adjust and ease into the changes brought about by demonetisation and increased digitalisation of financial transactions.

Our study shows that, apart from demand from the customers (critical mass), no other characteristics or aspects related to these customers play a role in unorganised retailers’ adoption of m-payment. While our findings are similar to those of some studies in developing (e.g., Moghavvemi et al., 2021) and developed (e.g., Mallat & Tuunainen, 2008) countries, they difer from those of some studies on m-payment adoption by formal and organised businesses in developed countries that report other customer-related variables such as customer service and impulse purchase (Mallat & Tuunainen, 2008), customer payment habit and buying behaviour (Lai & Chuah, 2010), customer shopping experience, and data control (Hayashi & Bradford, 2014) as playing an important role. We find that the absence of these variables in our study is due to the unorganised retail set-up in India, which is typical in other developing countries as well. Unlike mom-and-pop retail stores in developed countries, the unorganised stores in India are not walk-in stores where customers can select the merchandise and then proceed to the billing counter. These stores operate over the counter, where the shopkeeper or the help takes the order and then hands over the products (across the counter) to the customer. This implies that factors such as shopping experience, ambience, and layout are not too relevant for unorganised retail businesses in developing countries such as India.

Similar to many studies on ICT adoption, studies in both developing (e.g., Boateng et al., 2019; Fitriani et al., 2020) and developed (e.g., Lee et al., 2019; Liébana-Cabanillas & Lara-Rubio, 2017; Mallat & Tuunainen, 2008) countries find trust as an important determinant of m-payment adoption by businesses. In our context, we divide this into trust of m-payment technology and trust of regulatory/ non-regulatory actors/institutions. We did not find either as being critical to m-payment adoption. For the former, the reason is due to retailers’ familiarity and comfort with the mobile technology and its various applications. Fairly monitored and regulated financial markets in India, including digital payments, also contribute to trust in m-payment technology not being a major concern; this trust is taken as de facto. With regards to the trust in regulatory and non-regulatory institutions, we noted that while the expectations of both, particularly of regulatory bodies (i.e., government and afiliated institutions), were high, it was more of a hope than trust.

Similar to many other ICTs, some studies in both developing (e.g., Suhud & Hidayat, 2017) and developed (e.g., Hayashi & Bradford, 2014) countries on businesses’ m-payment adoption show that privacy is an important factor in the adoption process of m-payment technology. However, we did not find any mention of privacy concerns during the course of our study. A key reason for this could be the socioeconomic characteristics of the unorganised retailers. These retailers, while comfortable with mobile technology, are no experts, and therefore their awareness of privacy issues is low. Furthermore, their socioeconomic and work environments underscore their limited means and challenges, suggesting that, unlike their more sophisticated counterparts in India and in developed countries, data privacy issues are not a priority concern for them.

Finally, and probably because many studies on m-payment adoption focus on small, mediumsized, and large organised businesses, organisational factors such as business model, transaction strategy, resource mobilisation absorptive capacity, financial strength, human resources, managerial skills, management support, and sales channel play an important role in these businesses’ m-payment adoption. However, we did not find these factors to be relevant in the unorganised businesses investigated in our study.

## 5.4. Limitations and scope for future research

This study has several limitations. The first is linked to our interpretive stance: although we aimed to illuminate merchants’ perspective, our own worldview and perspective are likely also reflected in our interpretation. Furthermore, our choice of sites for conducting the study poses a limitation in the scope of our study. The merchants that form our sample have business operations in an urban area. While many factors (e.g., government rules and regulations) uncovered in this study may have a similar influence on merchants not only in urban but also in rural and tribal areas, some (e.g., technology infrastructure, merchant literacy, mobile phone ownership) may vary depending on the location. As a result, we suggest that future studies evaluate the diference between merchants’ adoption in urban and rural/tribal areas. In addition, reaching the small population of elderly merchants, who are generally uncomfortable with new technologies, would be a worthwhile avenue for further research. Engaging the elderly and other marginalised groups, which we could not accomplish in our study, could shed new light on technology adoption behaviour. Finally, future research could investigate whether our results and proposed ecosystem could be extended beyond m-payment technology and beyond merchants to the adoption of other technologies by some unorganised entities in the context of other developing countries.

## 5.5. Implications for development

In developing economies, small, unorganised, and informal businesses, in both urban and rural areas, generally cater to customers who belong to the lower socio-economic strata (Tarafdar et al., 2013). Greater conviction about the benefits of the m-payment among unorganised retailers could result in greater difusion of this technology to their customers (Kumar et al., 2019), the majority of whom are part of the marginalised populace. This can happen organically, without any intervention, or inorganically through merchants’ conscious eforts to educate and motivate their customers. Our results show that in many instances, unorganised retailers went beyond their formal role as merchants and acted as “informal intermediaries” (Majchrzak et al., 2016) for the difusion of m-payment. Thus, these businesses, by virtue of being the last mile, connect with poor consumers and form an important link between producers/marketers and consumers, as well as between the state and marginalised citizens. We argue that a low scale of operations and a small catchment area of unorganised businesses (Tarafdar et al., 2013) that result in greater social exchange and bonding with their customers (Leonardi et al., 2016) makes them more efective instruments in the difusion of m-payment technology than other stateorchestrated awareness/education/training initiatives. An “on-field” hands-on route to promote m-payment usage (as indicated in the preceding examples) can contribute to a lasting behaviour modification of marginalised consumers and make these businesses potent partners in the agenda of digital and financial inclusion (Mariscal & Rojas-Lozano, 2020). In their study on mobile services for bottom-of-the-pyramid women in Mexico, a developing country, Mariscal and Rojas-Lozano (2020) observe that the digital deposit of money in the beneficiary account leads to increased well-being, better decision making, and greater empowerment. Other studies in settings across the globe have made similar observations (e.g., Leonardi et al., 2016), showing that financial inclusion is a pre-requisite for social inclusion of the disadvantaged and the underserved. Thus, the difusion of m-payment through merchants could have significant implications for development.

## 5.6. Implications for practice

Our practical implications reach several stakeholders of the highlighted ecosystem. As the most powerful actor/ institution in the Indian context, government and afiliated institutions are important in driving the economic growth. In the context of m-payment adoption, we found that government and afiliated institutions constituencies expect these agencies to have a multidimensional role: promote innovation, spread awareness, provide technology, support infrastructure, formulate and implement policy, arbitrate, and drive monitoring and corrective actions. While regulatory forces (government and afiliated institutions) are perceived to be the most powerful actors, our analysis shows the need for greater coordination and collaboration between these and other actors (e.g., banks, m-payment companies, telecommunication companies). Such partnerships can help reduce the barriers to adoption by providing monetary and non-monetary incentives, sharing costs, and creating a reliable and robust infrastructure to address issues related to connectivity, speed, privacy, and security of m-payment. Governments must continuously reinforce the message related to the benefits of increased automation and digitalisation, while setting up quick recovery mechanisms and grievance redressal procedures in coordination with other institutions.

M-payment firms (e.g., Paytm, Mobiqwik, Freecharge, Oxigen) should take advantage of the tremendous push for cashless transactions by the government. Given that the typical merchant in the unorganised sector is from a modest background and has modest means, m-payment firms should actively participate in promotion, spreading awareness, and training wherever required. This activity, in addition to customer (merchant) acquisition, will also aid in brand building and extending these merchants’ reach to retail consumers through positive word of mouth. Mobile technology is dependent on internet connectivity; therefore, the telecommunications network forms the backbone of the m-payment ecosystem. As several merchants noted, telecom firms need to further enhance the speed and security of the internet. A secure and uninterrupted data service may further contribute to revenue and market share by ensuring that the same provider is used for voice and other related services, especially in remote areas, where telecom infrastructure is still not developed. Finally, banking infrastructure is critical for money transfer that is facilitated by m-payment firms and banks themselves. Given that some of the m-payment companies (e.g., Paytm) are also involved in banking services, the traditional banking institutions face the largest threat from the emergence of m-payment technology. The higher cost of branch banking and lack of innovation have further reduced access to the untapped market in the unorganised sector and in remote areas. Embracing technology and creating innovative business models (e.g., the payments bank) are therefore essential for banks to tap the larger market and contribute to the agenda of financial inclusion.

## 6. Conclusion

This study explores businesses’ perspective of the adoption and use of m-payment, the fastest and most widespread ICT across the globe, in a developing country. We conduct a study of unorganised retailers in urban India, where the tremendous potential ofered by m-payment is particularly evident, as it is in many developing economies. Our results allow us to propose an ecosystem that explains merchants’ behaviour with respect to m-payment as a function of the influence of key actors (i.e., technology, government and related institutions, non-government institutions, and merchant dispositions) in the business ecosystem. We find that the regulatory influence of the state/government, a powerful actor in most developing economies, is the most critical factor determining merchants’ adoption of m-payment. We highlight some specificities of m-payment in India that have a significant influence on unorganised businesses’ adoption of m-payment. We further provide important implications for development and financial inclusion, as well as managerial recommendations.
