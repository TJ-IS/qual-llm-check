# Leapfrogging Healthcare Service Quality in Sub-Saharan Africa: The Utility-Trust Rationale of Mobile Payment Platforms

Joseph K. Nwankpa and Pratim Datta

Department of Information Systems and Analytics, Farmer School of Business, Miami University, Oxford, Ohio, USA; Department of Management and Information Systems, Kent State University, Kent, Ohio, USA and University of Johannesburg, Johannesburg, South Africa

## ABSTRACT

Can mobile payment platforms (MPPs) elevate Sub-Saharan Africa’s (SSA) healthcare service quality (HSQ)? In a region home to more than one billion people yet beset by a paucity of services and infrastructure, mobile devices and platforms are rapidly gaining ground. Rising healthcare needs and costs along with the fact that more than 60% of SSA lives in areas nearly 1.5 miles away from paved roads that lead to hospitals and banks, accessibility to traditional financial services remains a serious concern. As Sub-Saharan hospitals increase billing access via mobile payment platforms, there is little understanding of whether and how mobile payment platforms influence healthcare service quality (HSQ).

Drawing upon economic utility and trust perspectives, this study develops an integrative model to examine mobile payment platform utility’s impact on patient trust, commitment, and HSQ perceptions. The model is empirically tested using a survey of 527 patients from the West African country of Nigeria. Results reveal a distinct amalgam of MPP utility, trust, and commitment as antecedents to HSQ perceptions, thus generating important insights and contributing to the ongoing inquiry of how digital platforms in developing countries can influence socioeconomic development. Overall, the results support the study’s hypotheses and underscore the important role of MPPs.

## 1. Introduction

Healthcare service quality is vital to improving overall healthcare outcomes (WHO et al., 2018). Yet, healthcare in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) continues to be a major global concern. Despite SSA accounting for about 13% of the world’s population, it represents 24% of the global disease burden (World Bank Group, 2019), owing to one of the lowest UHC health service coverage of any region (42 out of 100). Unsurprisingly, healthcare systems in SSA countries are marred by inadequate health infrastructure, mismanagement, and corruption, leading to poor healthcare service quality (HSQ, hereafter) (Kifle et al., 2006). Subsequently, afluent SSA individuals that can aford healthcare seek quality medical care abroad, exemplified by the double-digit growth in medical tourism in countries such as India from SSA countries such as Nigeria and Ethiopia (Makinde et al., 2014).

## So, what can be done to bridge SSA’s HSQ gap?

Research has linked SSA’s poor healthcare quality with a dearth of eficient processes and mechanisms aimed at ensuring quality healthcare service delivery (Ampaw et al., 2020). WHO defines healthcare quality as “the extent to which healthcare services provided to individuals and patient populations improve desired health outcomes” (WHO-OECD-Word Bank, 2018). Improved healthcare quality depends on process efectiveness, particularly healthcare payment processes, given the lack of road and banking infrastructures (ibid). In support, a 2017 McKinsey study by Chironga et al. (2017) highlights how SSA is the world leader in using mobile money payments, citing over 280 mobile payment services and over 100 million mobile payment users recording a 33% CAGR (compounded annualised growth rate) between 2013 and 2016.

Given that most SSA households have broadband mobile access despite lacking access to clean water (WorldBank 2017), international agencies and various stakeholders have advocated leveraging mobile payment platforms as a way of bridging SSA’s infrastructure gap (Opoku et al., 2017). Prior research note remark how mobile access helps patients better understand various available treatment options (Steinhubl et al., 2013) and as a result helps them make informed and accurate treatment decisions (Ghahramani & Wang, 2020).

In SSA, healthcare systems often require cash payments before ofering services. It is not unconventional for SSA patients to travel long distances to access pharmacies and banks only to be told that medicines are unavailable, banks are closed, and, in some cases, for patients to be directed to ofsite financial institutions for payments of medical services before treatment (McLaren et al., 2014). With no overarching health insurance infrastructure, out-of-pocket payment remains the predominant mode of healthcare financing in the majority of sub-Saharan countries (Muller et al., 2019). Furthermore, travelling long distances with huge amounts of cash can pose a security risk for many patients seeking medical care. The perceived lack of trust in the institutions and the lack of transparency in medical billings and other healthcare system processes has led to diminished confidence in the quality of care provided (Østergaard, 2015). Therein lies the need for a robust healthcare mobile payment platform (MPP).

A healthcare MPP refers to an integrated financial technology (FinTech) interface that enables the use of mobile devices to process financial transactions by engendering convenience, security, and transparency, aimed at boosting patient satisfaction (e.g., Muller et al., 2019). To that efect, a healthcare MPP is built on a technology-driven ecosystem of healthcare facilities, independent healthcare providers, payment processors, insurers, and regulators via a digital channel to streamline multi-stakeholder healthcare payment exchanges.

Healthcare MPPs are not black-box solutions. Healthcare MPPs are distinguished by three facets, convenience, security, transparency – features central to driving patient satisfaction. In 2015, Burkina Faso launched its mHealth interactive health system, originally built to increase HIV health information and care delivery (Yé et al., 2018). The mHealth project exemplifies how MPPs are not just a platform but also an ecosystem of banks, telecoms, data servers, APIs (Application Programming Interfaces) that allow mobile applications to integrate the data, and payment clearance gateways to seamlessly transfer payments across various vendors and financial institutions. Despite the reticence towards MPPs in predominantly cash-based societies, the 2020 COVID-19 (SARS-CoV -2) pandemic accentuated the need for MPPs in Sub Saharan Africa. COVID-19 forced digital payments transformation insofar as to reduce physical transactions to reduce contagion while maintaining operational continuity.

Despite healthcare MPP innovations, it is still unclear how MPPs shape SSA HSQ, engendering uncertainty surrounding widespread adoption and use (Muller et al., 2019). There are limited examples of empirically validated models that seek to examine the role of mobile platforms on HSQ within SSA. Yet, understanding MPP utility is particularly important as healthcare advocates continue to promote the use of mobile platforms to obtain and bridge healthcare gaps in developing countries (Yé et al., 2016; Muller et al., 2019).

This paper’s objective is to examine the impact of MPP utility on HSQ and the mechanisms through which MPP utility shapes users’ HSQ perceptions. Motivated by the desire to understand the underlying conditions through which emerging digital platforms impact socio-economic challenges (Venkatesh & Sykes, 2013), such as HSQ in developing countries, this study fills an important gap in the literature by examining the link between MPP utility and perceived HSQ.

This study addresses these specific research questions:

1. What is the impact of MPP utility on perceived HSQ?

2. What role does MPP trust play in the relationship between MPP utility HSQ?

The study makes two primary contributions to the digital platform use and information and communications technology (ICT) for development literature. First, this study is one of the first to empirically examine the direct role of MPP utility on HSQ within SSA. Second, results reveal that this link is not entirely direct and isolated. Complementing prior investigations on the role of trust on technology adoption and usage (e.g., Wang et al., 2016), focusing on MPP utility can engender trust in the platform, mediating and efectuating positive HSQ, downstream.

The article proceeds as follows: the next section reviews the theoretical foundation, the background MPP literature, technology trust, commitment, and HSQ. The subsequent section introduces the research model and hypotheses. Then, the study presents the methodology and data analysis. The final section discusses the results of the empirical investigation, the contributions to research and theory, as well as the study’s limitations, and future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

This study investigates MPP utility on HSQ at the intersection of psychological “trust” and rational “utility”. lenses. This paper’s purview of shifting trust from bureaucratic, cumbersome, and ineficient healthcare payment traditions to an MPP requires trust transference.

Essentially, MPP signals digital transformation by digitally disrupting the status quo, compelling the simultaneous need to psychologically trust the system while gauging the system’s inherent economic utility, both proposed and perceived.

## 2.1. The role of Trust

Healthcare MPP is a socio-technical system and trust remains a crucial component in user behaviour. In SSA, a region occasionally beset by healthcare infrastructure woes, trust remains a core constituent in MPP use (Bhan, 2014). Bhan (2014) remarks how “People seem to trust the mobile operators more than they do banks”, there remains a level of scepticism regarding MPP use for private healthcare data. With widespread social mores and stigmas surrounding diseases and health in SSA, there is an exigent and compelling need for trust in using MPP for quality healthcare services.

Prior literature has typically modelled trust as an antecedent of user adoption intention behaviour of emerging technologies (Wang et al., 2016). Lu et al. (2011) emphasised the role of MPP trust as a strong driver of customer intention to use mobile payment services. Building trust on a mobile platform appears to be a challenging task because it is subject to interface design problems, communication and network speed, and spatial device limitations (Sarkar et al., 2020). A recent survey of online users revealed how trust in MPP is critical to the success of any mobile payment adoption (Gong et al., 2020; Kar, 2020). To this end, leapfrogging from conventional modes of health payments to the use of MPPs for better HSQ requires trust transference.

## 2.2. The role of commitment

It is well established that commitment is rooted in and a consequence of trust. While trust garners adoption, commitment garners post-adoption continuance (i.e., continuous use) (Datta et al., 2018; Wang & Datta, 2009). Wang and Datta (2009) investigated how individual and organisation trust in technologies prompted adoption but a lack of commitment towards their use led to shelfware, a phenomenon where technologies are adopted but never used, leading to utility loss and sunk costs. Inasmuch as MPP use is imperative to better healthcare service satisfaction, MPP commitment is not only the downstream efect of trust but a necessary precursor to continuance (Datta et al., 2018).

The concept of MPP commitment draws on the organisational commitment literature rooted in social psychology and organisational behaviour. MPP commitment is based on the premise that MPP users, through their interactions and engagements with the focal MPP technology, form or fail to form a sense of attachment with the technology, helping smooth the learning curve and increase satisfaction.

In tandem, increasing trust and commitment are vital to any healthcare initiative’s success (Akter et al., 2013). MPPs can facilitate the healthcare provider’s ability to build trust and commitment by streamlining business processes, driving financial inclusion, and restoring confidence with patients (WHO et al, 2018). Without trust and commitment in even the most utilitarian endeavour, building positive HSQ remains an elusive and dificult undertaking (Akter et al., 2013).

## 2.3. MPP and economic utility

Utility is a well-established economic concept that establishes how much a particular product or service fulfils a user or customer’s need (Fishburn, 1968). Rational users, when faced with a multiplicity of options, are likely to weigh each option and choose an option that maximises the user’s perceived sense of utility. Thus, perceived utility determines individual preferences and customer value expectations.

The choice of perceived utility as a guiding antecedent is justified for our study. SSA has long sufered from the throes of economic and social development and is beset by corruption and a severe lack of infrastructure options- from capital markets to information access (Datta et al., 2005). Faced with a severe dearth of options, MPPs can serve to be a necessary, rather than a perfunctory solution to healthcare needs.

In healthcare in developing countries, where the population often faces existential choices, MPP utility takes centre stage. This research investigates MPP utility by delineating MPP features that aid user’s perception of access, understanding, using (paying), and monitoring.

In a region plagued by access to physical capital market infrastructure, a general lack of security, and a low density of ATMs and banking infrastructures, healthcare MPPs ofer a digital platform available 24/7 for transparent and accessible monetary payments and transfers.

Thus, in today’s increasingly digital landscape, MPPs use economic utility as a value proposition (Ondrus et al., 2015). An MPP is a digital platform that integrates complex information systems to ofer a common set of features on which new services, applications, and data resources can be developed and added (Nwankpa & Datta, 2017). Consider the AWS (Amazon Web Services) digital platform that ofers a multitude of services that can be used by various companies or clients. A common set of features include secure logins, web and mobile application hosting, data repository, analytics engines, payments ecosystems, voice recognition extensions, among others. AWS digital platform, therefore, serves as an economic utility gateway for various clients to host, develop, and complement their services over the cloud.

A healthcare MPP is a digital platform enhancing economic utility by connecting diferent healthcare ecosystems user groups (i.e., patients, hospitals, insurers, payment service providers) for transparently accessing and transacting various payment services. Definitionally, our research considers healthcare MPP as a secure, mobile-device-based platform, both proximal (e.g., NFC and Bluetooth) and distal/remote (e.g., mobile and wi-fi networks) that consolidate interactive payment configurations across a diverse stakeholder ecosystem.

Central to the healthcare MPP service proposition is the concept of MPP economic utility. Beyond economic utilities of accessibility and availability, healthcare MPPs add transparency to the healthcare ecosystem transactions. Compared to paper-based payment invoices, MPP invoices aford systems availability to drill down (a way to check for constituent pricing and costs) for specific line-items as designated by regional healthcare disclosure laws. Line-item clarifications and explanations can be assigned on MPPs compared to traditional paper-based invoices where every line-item clarification might require a phone call or physical visit. Thus, MPPs can ofer users the added economic utility of being able to understand various aspects of medical billing related to a healthcare procedure, while maintaining encrypted discretion and security related to sensitive health data.

In summary, in an environment plagued by a dearth of ICT infrastructure, healthcare MPPs ofer payment and transactional availability, accessibility, and transparency: ingredients essential for users’ economic utility maximisation, trust, and commitment.

## 2.4. Perceived HSQ

Delivering quality healthcare service is a social imperative. Poor HSQ can undermine a country’s human capital and create a social-economic challenge for the poor and vulnerable segments of the society (James et al., 2017). According to a joint report by Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), WHO, and World Bank, poor HSQ continues to undercut progress on improving health in all countries and across all income levels. Examples of poor HSQ include inaccurate diagnosis, medication errors, unsafe clinic facilities or practices, and inadequate training of healthcare providers. Unsurprisingly, HSQ is an essential outcome in the pursuit of better health outcomes for patients (Büyüközkan et al., 2011).

Healthcare providers recognise that matching a patient’s perception of service quality is crucial in delivering quality healthcare services (Singh & Prasher, 2019). Patients’ assessment of service quality builds an interaction between patients and hospitals that leads to the development of patient overall confidence (Suki et al., 2011). A patient’s perception of HSQ is strongly related to the actual healthcare service outcome (James et al., 2017). A survey of patients from Deloitte’s Center for Health Solutions showed that patients judged the quality of the care they receive by the service experience rather than the outcome (Green, 2015). As a result, understanding a patient’s perception of HSQ creates shared value for both healthcare service providers and patients.

Building upon the background literature, our research model utilises the interplay of MPP utility, trust, and commitment to understanding HSQ in SSA.

## 3.1. MPP utility and perceived HSQ

The application of mobile platforms in the healthcare industry is increasingly seen to increase accessibility and HSQ delivery in SSA (Muller et al., 2019; Opoku et al., 2017; Watkins et al., 2018). Countries like Burkina Faso have initiated mHealth projects that leveraged mobile platforms to enhance improved access to health information and care delivery for mothers and people living with HIV (Ye et al., 2016). In South Africa, the use of mobile devices in poor and remote rural communities has resulted in improved healthcare access and delivery (Watkins et al., 2018). Anecdotal evidence suggests that MPP utility can play an important role in improving quality healthcare delivery within SSA. MPP utility can strengthen HSQ by providing the mechanism for patients to ubiquitously engage and interact with healthcare facilities.

Prior studies have noted the benefit of MPPs compared to online and traditional ofline payment options (Zhou, 2013). First, ofline payment methods may require patients to estimate the medical bills in advance and put in place contingency plans that involve, in some cases, carrying large amounts of cash. However, the use of a healthcare MPP enables timely information flow on medical bills. Second, healthcare MPPs ubiquitously allow users to transact payments without spatial and temporal limitations (Zhou, 2013).

Healthcare MPPs improve eficiency in service delivery through digital queuing and payment options and simplified billing solutions. Furthermore, MPP utility can streamline and improve the drug supply chain by validating prescriptions and vendors and eliminating informal payment chains that can lead to fake drugs and corrupt financial practices (e.g., bribes). Mobile platforms may help patients to better cope with their health conditions by reducing related stress, pressure, and anxiety associated with medical information access (Ghahramani & Wang, 2020), including the rapidly increasing costs of healthcare.

During the 2020 SARS-CoV-2 crisis, SSA was subjected to uncertainties related to what vaccinations were available, their costs, and recommended vaccination facilities. Healthcare MPPs could leapfrog inherent uncertainties by ofering patients transparent, convenient, and secure health information and vaccination channel.

Utilisation or use is the requisite culmination in the utility maxim. Perceived utility underpinned by health payment access, understanding, and monitoring is the denominator for utilising MPPs for healthcare transactions. Therefore, this study expects healthcare MPP to be better positioned to efectively deliver better HSQ outcomes. Therefore:

H1: A patient’s perceived MPP utility encompassing access, understanding, monitoring, and transacting payments is positively related to perceived HSQ.

## 3.2. Efects of MPP Utility on Trust and Commitment

In line with transparency, healthcare MPPs ofer users the advantages of monitoring or auditing their medical bills related to corresponding medical procedures. A patient can monitor one’s entire sequence of procedures and pharmacology throughout one’s medical visit and associated costs related to each procedure and drug administration. MPPs can also allow patients and authenticated users to examine their medical bills in detail within and across visits at one or more institutions.

Patients’ perceived healthcare MPP utility contributes to their long-term MPP trust and commitment. For example, access and availability of EHR (Electronic Health Records) are shown to engender conditions of trust among patients (Bozan & Datta, 2016), leading to continued use of the system platform as a signal for trust and patients’ returning to the platform over time to check for and monitor information as a signal for commitment. MPP trust refers to the willingness of a patient to be vulnerable to the use and actions of the MPP based on the expectation that the MPP will perform actions important to the patient without taking advantage of the patient’s use of the platform.

In hospitals around the world, patients are commonly in the dark about what they will have to pay after visiting a primary-care physician or undergoing an inpatient procedure. In 2019, the US government’s Afordable Care Act required Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) to create a publicly accessible database called the CMS Open Payments website to increase financial transparency.

A transparent understanding and monitoring of hospital procedures and billing are shown to improve patient trust, consequently impacting patient commitment towards the platform and subsequent visits to the hospital (Kanter et al. (2019). The authors found that patients’ perceptions of healthcare payment transparency increased trust in the CMS Open Payments database and their subsequent commitment towards using the CMS platform for ongoing cost information, albeit reducing patient trust in individual hospitals that the patient felt were practicing unscrupulous billing and unnecessary procedures.

In summary, we posit that patients’ ability to use MPPs to pay for healthcare services is likely to engender greater trust and subsequent commitment. Therefore:

H2a: A patient’s perceived MPP utility encompassing access, understanding, monitoring, and payment increases patient trust in MPPs.

H2b: A patient’s perceived MPP utility encompassing access, understanding, monitoring, and payment increases patient commitment to MPPs.

## 3.3. Efect of trust in MPP and commitment

Prior research has traditionally recognised trust as an important mechanism through which commitment can be achieved in a relationship (Casalo et al., 2007; Garbarino & Johnson, 1999). According to Morgan and Hunt (1994), when individuals establish trust, they will be committed to maintaining their relationship. Trust can be viewed as an important enabler of confidence levels between both parties in the relationship, leading to commitment (e.g., Wang & Datta, 2009).

Trust influences commitment by diminishing the risk and likelihood of opportunistic behaviour by parties in the relationship (Garbarino & Johnson, 1999). From a technology perspective, prior research has shown the positive efect of trust placed by a consumer on a website and the commitment to the website (Casalo et al., 2007) as well as the positive role of trust on the commitment to use internet banking (Yuan et al., 2019). Users tend to develop high levels of trust when they perceive the system to be of high quality, which in turn encourages them to commit to the system (Gao & Waechter, 2017).

Bhan (2014) explains that Kenya’s users seem to trust mobile operators more than financial institutions, thus explaining the steadily growing, loyal, and active M-PESA userbase. Increased MPP trust signals reliance and increases commitment (Koloseni & Mandari, 2017). Thus, we expect that a higher level of trust in MPP will positively impact the commitment to MPP. Therefore:

H3: For patients participating in healthcare MPPs, a higher level of MPP trust increases MPP commitment.

## 3.4. Moderating Efect of MPP Utility

While MPP trust drives MPP commitment, the relationship remains fragile unless bolstered the economic rationale of MPP utility. If users feel that utility is lacking, they are more likely to abandon their continued commitment, even when the technology remains trustworthy but loses its utilitarian edge.

Psychologically, although MPP trust enhances MPP commitment, the rationality of MPP utility amplifies the relationship. As patients’ perceived MPP utility increases, they are more likely to heighten their MPP trust-based commitment.

MPP utility adds relevance and currency by enabling users to continuously evaluate MPP quality (Sarkar et al., 2020). As perceived MPP utility increases, patients smoothen their learning curves and gain a better understanding of MPP features leading to a better understanding of medical procedures, payment options, and various medical billing aspects.

User trust driven by MPP utility increases their perception of the technology vendor’s ability, professionalism, and integrity (Kim & Tadisina, 2007), leading to a continued commitment to the technology. An increase in perceived MPP utility reduces transaction costs (Bromiley & Cummings, 1995) and increased the perceived competence of the system (Lee & Turban 2001), and overall reliance on the system culminating in a continued commitment to the platform. Prior research suggests that improved access and availability of electronic health records (EHR) not only foster trust among patients (Bozan & Datta, 2016) but accentuate their committed use of EHR.

Therefore, this study expects the relationship between trust in MPP and commitment to MPP to be stronger for patients with higher MPP utility. Therefore:

H4: MPP utility positively moderates the efect of MPP trust on MPP commitment.

## 3.5. Efect of MPP Trust on HSQ

Trust is the expectation that the trusted party will fulfill its commitments despite the trusting party’s dependence and vulnerability (Gefen et al., 2003). More recently, a growing number of researchers have shown users’ willingness to place trust in IT artefacts (Lankton et al., 2015; Mcknight et al., 2011). Trust in technology typically reflects the user’s willingness to believe that a specific software has the necessary attributes and features to perform as expected in a given situation in which undesirable outcomes are possible (Mcknight et al., 2011).

In this study, MPP trust is the willingness of a patient to be vulnerable to the use and actions of the MPP based on the expectation that the MPP will perform actions important to the patient without taking advantage of the patient’s use of the platform.

Technology Trust is imperative in developing countries (Datta, 2011; Meso et al., 2006). In studying e-commerce use in developing countries, Datta (2011) remarks on the importance of facilitating conditions in engendering trust and subsequent technology use. The research highlights a vicious cycle. A lack of facilitating conditions (e.g., access, infrastructure, culture) increases the digital divide – a socio-economic, socio-cultural, and geographic demarcation between the information haves and have-nots. A digital divide relegates individuals to rely on and trust traditional mores, thus reducing their technology trust.

Trusting relationships are key to better healthcare outcomes (Thorne & Robinson, 1988). Without a trusting relationship between a patient and healthcare providers, patients may be unwilling to access critical services and remain reticent about sharing financial information using MPPs, worsening the digital divide.

As patients accumulate trust from using their healthcare provider’s MPP, the trust and reliance from MPP utilisation can reduce their traditional medical billing concerns from a lack of transparency, the inherent suspicions, and complexities typically associated with medical billings.

A Deloitte’s Center for Health Solution survey revealed that patients often judged the quality of care received from a facility by the service experience rather than by the outcome. Medical billing and transactions are core to a patient’s service experience. MPP trust can nurture faith and confidence in the healthcare facility and providers, resulting in greater medical billing transparency and engagement. Therefore, as MPP users gain more trust and utility from the platform, patients’ perception of HSQ should increase. Therefore:

H5: For patients participating in MPPs for healthcare payments, patients with higher MPP trust are more likely to perceive higher HSQ.

## 3.6. Efect of Commitment on Perceived HSQ

Research suggests that interdependence increases relational commitment (Ozcan & Santos, 2015). A lack of commitment can erode a healthcare MPPs ability to positively shape HSQs. Without MPP commitment, MPPs can turn into shelfware. Wang and Datta (2009) investigated the cost of shelfware, i.e., software adopted and used once and then shelved, found that commitment is key to satisfactory adoption and use. However, when commitment is high, software quality perceptions are positive.

Commitment has three dimensions. While afective commitment arises from an intrinsic love or desire, normative commitment from social and cultural norms, calculative/continuance commitment arises from users’ sense of attachment to a technologydriven by cost concerns surrounding the choice of alternative products (Wang & Datta, 2009).

Given that this study uses MPP utility as the lens to investigate downstream efects of MPPs on health service quality, we focus on continuance commitment. In the face of lacking infrastructure, corruption, and governance ineficiencies, continuance commitment in MPPs is particularly applicable in the context of SSA. Continuance commitment underlies the emergent MPPs’ value proposition, cost advantages, and gained eficiencies from using MPPs to pay for services, especially for hospital services that are often larger and require more frequent transfers.

To reduce the probability of turning MPPs into shelfware, increase continuance commitment, and enhance HSQ outcomes, healthcare MPPs in SSA are trying to drive commitment by building large ecosystems. Healthcare MPP ecosystems are spread across multiple financial service providers (e.g., banks, clearinghouses, money transfer services) and payment options (e.g., linking bank accounts, credit cards) using open application programming interfaces (APIs) that allow for seamless and securely connecting across multiple parties.

Continuance commitment leverages both upstream and downstream interdependencies. Upstream, continuance commitment interdependencies include inter-organisational connections with various banks and payment services providers. Downstream, continuance commitment interdependencies rely on linking unbanked consumers to the MPP by simplifying the banking experience, reducing costs, and reducing bureaucracy.

We argue that building such interdependencies as a participle of continuance commitment can aid health service quality with swifter payment solutions, reduced probabilities of missing payments and being denied medical services, and being able to transparently monitor and understand medical costs and expenses. Therefore:

H6: For patients participating in MPPs for healthcare payments, patients with higher MPP commitment are more likely to perceive higher HSQ.

## 3.7. Mediating Efect of Trust in MPP

MPP utility is unlikely to afect HSQ in a vacuum unless users developing an acceptable level of MPP trust. Indeed, a patient’s ability to leverage MPP features such as payment options and streamlined payment processes for positive HSQ outcomes relies on the level of MPP trust (Montague et al., 2010). Fostering MPP trust is crucial to an MPPs ability to shape HSQ.

The mediating role of trust is central to understanding the intersection of utility and psychology, i.e., perceived satisfaction in our study. According to Bjørnskov and Méon (2015), higher levels of trust reduce formal and informal transaction costs in any economic activity, leading to higher perceptions of quality. MPP utility is derived from both “formal” MPP formal rules (e.g., EULA (End User Licence Agreement) as well as “informal” social contracts of convenience, security, and transparency. Given that transaction costs underlie user interactions with MPPs, lower transaction costs signify higher utility, leading to trust, and its downstream efects on MPP service quality satisfaction.

Arguably then, MPP trust is a prerequisite for positive HSQ perceptions. As patients develop trust in the MPP, they are more likely to be confident with the healthcare provider and the facilities in general, inevitably creating an environment that fosters efective communication, positive attitude, and confidence in the healthcare services (Kanter et al., 2019), leading to sustained MPP use. Thus, for patients participating in the MPP for healthcare payment, their ability to leverage the system to attain higher HSQ may depend on how trusting they are of the MPP. Therefore:

H7: Trust in MPP mediates the relationship between MPP utility and perceived HSQ.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Development of Measurement Instruments

The survey instrument was developed based on appropriate measurement from previously available instruments and in some cases, modifications were made on the existing scales to make them suitable within the context of MPP (Appendix 1). Based on our extensive review of prior literature, we generated the initial version of the items for the construct in the conceptual model. We developed the four items we used to measure MPP utility by examining prior studies that investigated technology platforms and use (Ondrus et al., 2015). The scale was designed to reflect our conceptual definition of MPP utility that delineates information access, understanding use, and monitoring. We followed the MacKenzie et al. (2011) procedure to develop the new instrument for MPP utility. We also had other researchers and practitioners with knowledge of the domain area examine the items before the items were validated by a focus group and pilots in the area. We adopted Mcknight et al. (2011) and Lankton et al. (2015) and modelled trust in MPP as a - reflective second-order construct composed of three dimensions, namely reliability, functionality, and helpfulness. Our choice of trust in MPP as a reflective higher-order construct is based on the theoretical suggestion that trust dimensions will vary together and be reflective of the overall focal construct (Lankton et al., 2015; Mcknight et al., 2011). The scale of reliability comprises of three items developed by Mcknight et al. (2011) and subsequently adapted by Lankton et al. (2015). Similarly, the three items measuring functionality were adapted from Mcknight et al. (2011), while the three items measuring helpfulness were also adapted from Mcknight et al. (2011) and Lankton et al. (2015). The commitment construct was measured from four items adapted from prior studies (Wang et al., 2016). Finally, HSQ was measured by adapting four items from prior research (Brady & Cronin, 2001).

A panel consisting of two university professors, three healthcare providers, and three patients familiar with mobile payment platforms reviewed every questionnaire for clarity (construct validity) and purpose (content validity). Based on the feedback from the panel of experts, some items were revised to better fit the intended construct and to improve readability. Furthermore, these reviewers were invited to match the survey question to the appropriate constructs to determine whether the items represented our model’s constructs. This step helped us to establish face and content validity. All constructs in the model are based on a reflective multi-item scale and measured on a seven-point Likert scale that ranged from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Appendix 1 contains the final set of measurement items we used to measure each construct and the source of these measurement items. The initial version of the survey instrument was pilot tested on 52 mobile payment platform users, and the result indicated that the scale items displayed the same psychometric properties. As a result, no item was removed from the scale based on the preliminary factor analysis.

## 4.2. Sampling and Data Collection

To test the research model and the associated hypotheses proposed above, data was collected using a survey questionnaire at 10 hospitals across four regions of Nigeria between July and September 2019. The population of interest was current MPP users. From this population, we developed a sampling frame comprising of patients who actively used MPP for transactions and engagements during their visits to certain classified hospitals in Nigeria. Specifically, the respondents were patients that were randomly selected at these facilities within three months. We started the data collection by using a government list of 3734 registered hospitals and clinics in Nigeria. Using multiple measures such as the number of beds, the number of patient visits, the number of full-time doctors and nurses in the facilities, the list was narrowed to 55 active large hospitals that were the dominant providers of care within the regions. In this sense, our study’s sampling frame comprised of MPP users at these 55 large hospitals across the country that primarily served the region’s severe healthcare needs. To get a broad representation of the socio-economic segment of the society, area-wise cluster sampling was used to select 10 hospitals across multiple cities, sub-urban and rural areas. Thus, the survey was conducted in 10 hospitals and healthcare facilities across multiple cities, sub-urban and rural areas across Nigeria.

## 4.3. Control Variables

To minimise the confounding efects of spurious correlation, this study’s research model included four control variables: age, gender, location, and the length of time a patient has been using the healthcare provider’s MPP. We controlled for the length of time a patient has been using MPP because it could afect the level of trust and commitment they place on the device. More experienced users could be more trusting and committed to the technology. Similarly, we controlled for location because hospitals located in cities and urban areas are likely to be experienced in the use of MPP. We controlled for age to account for any advantage that more established users might have with MPP use and HSQ.

## 5. Data analysis and results

For the data analysis, we followed the two-step approach recommended by Anderson and Gerbing (1988). The first step involves analysing the measurement model to determine satisfactory levels of reliability, convergent, and discriminant validity. The second step involves examining the structural relationship among the latent constructs and estimating whether the hypothesised structural paths are statistically significant. The two-step approach is aimed at ensuring adequate reliability validity of the measure before assessing the structural paths in the model. SmartPLS 3.2.8 was used for the analysis. Partial least square (PLS) does not necessitate the assumption of multivariate normality (Chin et al., 2003), places minimal constraints on the measurement scales, sample size, and residual distribution (Chin & Newsted, 1999), and is suited for more complex models including models with hierarchical (second-order) constructs (Chin et al., 2003). A bootstrap procedure was used to assess the statistical significance of the loadings and path coeficients (Ringle et al., 2005). To achieve increased robustness and statistical validity, we used a bootstrap resampling procedure (Hair et al., 2017). The moderating relationship was examined using the product indicator method first recommended by Kenny and Judd (1984) and later implemented by Chin et al. (2003) and Ringle et al. (2005). The product indicator method involves creating an interaction term by multiplying the indicators of the predictor and moderator constructs.

## 5.1. Measurement model

To assess the measurement model’s adequacy, we evaluated the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the constructs in the model. Each construct demonstrated acceptable reliability, with composite reliabilities greater than the commonly accepted threshold of 0.70 for all the constructs (Nunnally & Bernstein 1994). Furthermore, the average variance extracted (AVE) of every latent construct was greater than 0.70, suggesting that the principal constructs capture much higher construct-related variance than error variance (Fornell & Larcker 1981). As a result, we concluded that the instrument had a high level of reliability. We performed a confirmatory factor analysis to establish the convergent and discriminant validity of the measurement model. Table 3 presents the factor loadings of the indicators on their latent factors.

We found evidence of convergent validity using two criteria (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). First, all item loadings were greater than 0.70 as recommended by Hair et al. (2017), indicating that the items represented their respective constructs. In addition, the item loadings were found to be much higher than all crossloadings. Second, the AVE statistics were greater than 0.50 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) and exceeded the variance due to the measurement error for that construct.

Also, discriminant validity was assessed by testing whether each construct shared more variance with its measurement items than with the other constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Table 4 presents the correlation matrix among all constructs shows that the square root of the AVE of each construct is greater than the correlations between the construct and all other constructs. Thus, this analysis provides evidence of the discriminant validity of the constructs. Collectively, our analysis provides support for our construct being reliable and convergent.

Thus, this analysis provides evidence of the discriminant validity of the constructs. Collectively, our analysis provides support for our construct being reliable and convergent.

Given that our research model was susceptible to the problem of multicollinearity, we investigated the variance inflation factor (VIF) score for all the constructs. The highest VIF score was 2.5. In summary, our measurement model met various reliability and validity criteria. As a result, constructs developed by this measurement model could be used to test the research model and accompanying hypotheses.

## 5.2. Structural model

In PLS, structural model testing examines the structural paths to determine the significance of the hypothesised paths, the R-square scores of the endogenous variables, and the explanatory power of the structural model. We assessed the structural model by using the PLS statistical method (SmartPLS 3.2.8) to estimate the explanatory power of the structural paths. Furthermore, we used a bootstrapping procedure (1000 re-samples) to examine the path coeficient’s significance. To test the moderating efect of MPP utility on the relationship between trust in MPP and commitment, we followed the product of sum procedure first recommended by Kenny and Judd (1984) and later implemented by Chin et al. (2003) and Ringle et al. (2005). This involves creating the interaction terms by multiplying the indicators of the predictor and moderator constructs (Goodhue et al., 2007). As recommended by Ringle et al. (2005), before confirming the interaction, we centred each indicator of the moderator and the predictor. Figure 2 shows the structural model results in which the explained variance of endogenous variables (R<sup>2</sup>) and the standardised path coeficients (β) are presented.

The model explains 53% of the variance of the dependent variable perceived HSQ through the latent variables MPP utility, trust in MPP, and commitment to the MPP. Similarly, the model is capable of explaining about 31% of the variance of trust in MPP through MPP, while 34% of the variance in commitment to the MPP was explained through MPP utility.

In support of hypothesis 1, MPP utility had a strong positive relationship with perceived HSQ $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 ,$ $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Similarly, MPP utility had a significant direct efect on trust in MPP $( \beta = 0 . 4 2 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and commitment to the MPP $( \beta = 0 . 3 2 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ in support of H2a and H2b. In support of hypothesis 3, we found a positive relationship between trust in MPP and patients’ commitment to MPPs $( \beta ~ = ~ 0 . 3 8 ,$ $\mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Hypothesis 4 concerns MPP’s moderating role on the relationship between trust in MPP and commitment to the MPP. First, we test the path of the relationship between Trust in MPP and Commitment $( \beta = 0 . 3 8 , \gamma < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , then we test the moderating efect. As hypothesised, we found that MPP utility moderates the path of trust in MPP and commitment to the MPP $( \beta ~ = ~ 0 . 1 7 , ~ \ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 1 )$ Furthermore, we tested our finding’s robustness by examining our control variables’ moderating efect on both mediating and dependent variables in our model, and we did not find any significant relationship. Similarly, the influence of trust in MPP on HSQ was significant $( \beta ~ = ~ 0 . 2 9 , ~ \ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ), supporting hypothesis 5, while the results reveal that commitment to the MPP has a significant relationship with HSQ $( \beta \ = \ 0 . 3 5 , \ \ p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ thus, providing support for hypothesis 6.

To test for the mediation hypothesis H7, that trust in MPP mediates the relationship between MPP utility and perceived HSQ, we adopted the two-step procedure prescribed by Preacher and Hayes (2008) and elaborated by Zhao et al. (2010). First, we tested the significance of the indirect efect of the mediating variable trust in MPP (Zhao et al., 2010). This step determines the extent trust in MPP mediates the efect of MPP utility on perceived HSQ. Second, we examine the direct efects of MPP utility on perceived HSQ to ascertain the degree of mediation. The results revealed the significant indirect efect of trust in MPP $( \beta = 0 . 1 5 ,$ $\mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ on the relationship between MPP utility and perceived HSQ. In addition, the result revealed that the direct efect of MPP utility on perceived HSQ was significant $( \beta = 0 . 5 2 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . However, when trust in MPP is introduced to the model, the strength of that relationship weakened $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Following Zhao et al. (2010) and Hair et al. (2017)’s classification of mediation, we conclude that trust in MPP partially mediated the relationship between MPP utility and perceived HSQ.

Furthermore, we used the causal steps procedure and calculated the Sobel mediation test statistic (Baron & Kenny 1986). Without the mediator (Trust in MPP) MPP utility had a significant, positive efect on perceived HSQ $( \beta = 0 . 5 2 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Regressing on trust in MPP as a dependent variable, MPP utility had a significant positive relationship with trust in MPP $( \beta = 0 . 4 2 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Trust in MPP had a significant positive relationship with perceived HSQ $( \beta = 0 . 2 9$ , $\mathrm { ~ p ~ } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . However, when all three are introduced simultaneously in the model, we find a diminished significant direct efect of MPP utility on perceived HSQ $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Hence, the relationship between MPP utility and perceived HSQ is partially mediated through trust in MPP.

## 5.3. Assessment of common method bias

Common method bias is a possible concern because our investigation employed survey methods (Kang et al., 2012). This study addresses this concern in several ways by following Podsakof et al. (2003) suggestions and recommendations to mitigate the efect. First, we attempted to reduce the respondent’s evaluation apprehension by emphasising no correct or incorrect answers. Second, the survey mixed the ordering of questions to ensure that the respondents were engaged and by separating the predictor and criterion variable items through a lengthy survey. Third, the respondents were assured of complete anonymity. The potential for common method bias was formally tested in several ways. First, we conducted a Harmon one-factor analysis to check if the variance in the constructs was attributed to common method bias. A single factor accounted for 29.82% of the variance, which is below the 50% cut-of for Harmon’s one-factor test. Second, we also examined common method variance using a marker variable technique (Lindell & Whitney, 2001). In our result, the correlation between the marker variable and the unrelated variable was not considerable. The smallest positive correlation value between any two constructs in the correlation matrix was 0.07. After adjusting for all observed correlations, we did not find statistical differences among the observed and adjusted correlations. Thus, our analysis provided no evidence of common method bias afecting our results (Podsakof et al., 2003).

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Implications for theory

This study makes several theoretical contributions. First, our research is one of the first studies that provide empirical evidence on the interplay between MPP utility and HSQ. The proposed theoretical model identifies MPP utility as a key antecedent of perceived HSQ, thus, advancing our knowledge on the mechanisms through which emerging digital platforms such as MPP can influence perceived HSQ within SSA. The key role played by MPP utility perceptions deserves particular mention. The strength of the direct, positive relationship between perceived MPP utility and perceived HSQ ofers a singular insight. Users’ perceived sense of utility from healthcare MPPs positively shapes users’ HSQ perceptions. The finding is particularly interesting because it highlights two important aspects generalisable across SSA healthcare systems. First, a healthcare MPP platform appears to be an artefact in demand, owing to a predominantly lacklustre financial infrastructure and a traditionally opaque healthcare billing system. Second, the SSA healthcare sector could elevate patient perceptions of HSQ by ensuring a successful rollout of a healthcare MPP (or possibly linking the hospital’s billing system to a $3 ^ { \mathrm { r d } }$ party MPP via APIs. As scholars advocate the need to re-evaluate the role of digital platforms and ICT4D (Information and Communications Technologies for Development) on developing countries and how knowledge production appears skewed towards western academic and theories (Walsham, 2020), our study yields significant insights on the socio-economic implications of platform technologies from the perspective of SSA, a region with a unique set of characteristics.

Second, the study reveals that MPP trust and MPP commitment are key enablers of perceived HSQ. Although prior studies suggest that a patient’s trust in medical technologies can impact usage and healthcare service outcomes (Montague et al., 2010), less is known about patients’ trust in non-medical technologies and how process-driven digital platforms influence perceived HSQ. This study advances our theoretical model by developing and testing a model that investigates how using and trusting non-medical technologies and platforms can impact HSQ.

Third, our study further contributes to the IS research by showing the importance of MPP utility, which is an understudied area of IS domain. We showed that MPP utility is a viable, independent path to perceived HSQ. It captures users’ attempts to ubiquitously engage and interact with healthcare facilities. We demonstrate that MPP utility can enhance the user’s propensity to commit to a technology by providing the level of engagement need to attain trust in the technology.

Fourth, we contribute to the IS literature that employed trust-commitment theory to explain usage relationships (Srivastava et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2011) by examining the moderating role of MPP utility in the relationship between trust and commitment. Prior IS research has used trust-commitment theory in IT artefacts usage and relationships such as IT outsourcing (Goo & Huang, 2008), customer-to-business groupbuying websites (Wang et al., 2016), and continued website use (Li et al., 2006). However, these studies have focused exclusively on the role of trust on commitment and intention to use the technology without considering the interaction efect of higher IT artefact usage. Thus, our study extends this body of work by examining the moderating relationship of IT artefact usage. The results ofer interesting cues. More specifically, the results indicate that patients with higher MPP utility were more trusting of the MPP and as a result demonstrated more commitment to the platform.

Finally, we contribute to an emerging body of literature on trust in technology (Lankton et al., 2015; Mcknight et al., 2011) that seeks to focus on the efects of trust placed in IT rather than trust in people or processes (McKnight 2005). Our study identifies MPP utility as a key enabler of trust in MPP technology while also highlighting the positive role of trust in MPP as an antecedent of HSQ and commitment to the platform technology. As a result, this study extends prior work on trust in IT artefacts by delineating the antecedent and consequence of trust in technology within the context of HSQ in a region characterised by poor service delivery, socioeconomic challenges, and a digital divide.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

Our study is of practical importance to healthcare providers and government agencies grappling with how to streamline healthcare processes and seeking to understand the mechanism through which mobile platforms can foster HSQ. The insight emerging from this study reveals that derived MPP utility can help healthcare providers engender greater transparency for hospital procedures and billings – resulting in increased trust in the platform. Increased trust in MPP is likely to translate into increased commitment and perceived HSQ. MPPs are a part of the greater Financial Technology (FinTech) growth in SSA. The gravity of healthcare delivery as both a service and a cost make understanding MPP adoption for the healthcare sector. For hospitals investing in mobile platforms for service deliveries, our result reinforces the importance of mobile platforms as the foundation of financial inclusion and access in a region dominated by discrepant service oferings between urban and rural communities with remote rural areas lacking access to financial services and institutions.

An additional insight emerging from this study is that mobile platform utilisation can help foster trusting relationships between patients and the MPP of healthcare providers. Trusting relationships are key to better healthcare outcomes (Montague et al., 2010; Thorne & Robinson, 1988). In the absence of trusting relationships, especially in SSA where patients are suspicious of healthcare providers, patients may be unwilling to access critical services and may withhold medically pertinent information. Our study reveals that deploying MPP can lead to trust in the platform. Such emerging trust in the MPP can nurture patients dependency and confidence in the healthcare facility and providers alike, leading to improved perceived HSQ. Hospital administrators and stakeholders within the industry can take advantage of this finding by seeking ways to use mobile platforms to engage and interact with patients throughout the healthcare delivery process.

The findings lend support to the idea that MPP usage combined with trust in MPP is likely to yield a higher commitment of continued usage of the platform. However, our results also show that MPP utility can afect the degree to which trust in MPP impacts commitment to the platform. Similarly, the efect of commitment to the MPP on perceived HSQ will be interesting to healthcare providers.

## 6.3. Limitation and future research

The findings of our study must be judged in the context of its limitations. First, for parsimony, the study emphasised a limited number of variables that may influence perceived HSQ. Even though MPP utility, trust in MPP, and commitment to the MPP all play important roles in influencing perceived HSQ, these factors are by no means exhaustive. Other factors such as ease of use, system quality may afect how patients interact with the system. Future research that considers a comprehensive nomological network of MPP use and HSQ will be more insightful.

Second, although we controlled for various individual-level factors, there may be firm-level factors that afect HSQ. Thus, readers should interpret our results with caution. Third, this study adopts a cross-sectional view when measuring the constructs in the model. As a result, this study only captures a snapshot of users. Such a design may not suficiently capture the interactions and relationships among the constructs and cannot establish causality. Future research might benefit from a longitudinal study that may enrich the findings of our results.

While this study focused on healthcare MPPs as rational, utilitarian decisions based on the pragmatics of scarcity and opportunity costs, future research could expand such a utilitarian viewpoint to accommodate other perspectives such as afects and norms, both regulatory and cultural. Another investigation area is understanding MPPs in the cybersecurity context. For example, while a utilitarian view ofers a value-rationale, the same value-rationale can be threatened in the face of cybersecurity breaches. Future research could examine how, as MPPs mature in difusion and adoption, the moderating influence of cybersecurity concerns can shape and sway trust, commitment, and healthcare services perceptions dynamics.

Finally, while our research design uses perceived healthcare quality as the outcome, perceived healthcare quality is not necessarily concomitant to meaningful healthcare. Using the ICT4D lens, meaningful care laudingly expects technology solutions that positively impact overall healthcare outcomes, from prevention, detection, and mitigation to a sustained increase in societal health. Yet, as Chipidza and Leidner (2019: 148) note, “accountability and eficiency logics usually prevail over the patient health outcomes logic”. Although our healthcare MPP research attempts to bridge the rational, sociotechnical logic of an MPP (i.e., convenience, security, transparency) and tie it to individual perceptions of healthcare quality, findings derived herein need further granular and longitudinal considerations for inductive inferences.

Whereas certain ICT4D projects are tactical, healthcare MPPs are strategic. MPPs leapfrog existing infrastructure and developmental barriers that have often been the spurious variable accounting for ICT4D failures (Awowi, 2010; Chipidza & Leidner, 2019). For example, Awowi (2010) discussed Ghana’s teleinformation initiative failure owing to a lack of electricity and telephone landlines. MPPs are mobile-first solutions that leverage untethered wireless telephony and require a very low energy operational footprint. A few inexpensive and portable 20-watt solar panel arrays can efectively charge a mobile phone. However, achieving sustainable healthcare benefits in developing countries requires considering challenges and opportunities beyond technical and operational domains.

Developing countries are often mired in bureaucracies (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019), misinformation due to a lack of education (Datta, 2011), and traditions (Meso et al., 2006), issues that impede further development. While healthcare payments underscore this paper’s context and MPP as the artefact, findings from this study may be captive to a “projected” patient satisfaction from the artefact rather than a long-term health outcome. First, future research using time-lagged longitudinal studies can shed greater light on whether MPPs can subsequently promote sustainable healthcare. This paper believes MPPs can aid sustainable health solutions in developing countries, premising our argument that MPPs ofer an initial gateway to greater healthcare information and transparency. Second, future research could further our understanding of how similar payments platforms might be leveraged and designed to integrate telehealth and health-education to raise overall health outcomes, in the true spirit of ICT4D.

## 6.4. Conclusion

To conclude, this research empirically tested the efect of MPP utility on perceived HSQ. Furthermore, the study investigated the mediating role of trust in MPP and the moderating role of MPP utility. Using survey data collected from 527 patients across 10 hospitals, we find that MPP utility positively influences perceived HSQ. Of great importance are the significant roles of trust in MPP and commitment to the MPP on perceived HSQ as well as the mediating role of trust in MPP on the relationship between MPP utility and perceived HSQ. Overall, this research provides useful insights on the role of MPP unity on perceived HSQ within SSA.
