# Beyond technical measures: a value-focused thinking appraisal of strategic drivers in improving information security policy compliance

Charlette Donalds<sup>a</sup> and Corlane Barclay <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Mona School of Business & Management, University of the West Indies at Mona , Mona, Jamaica; <sup>b</sup>Smart Projects 360, Kensington Crescent, Kingston 5, Jamaica

## ABSTRACT

The evolving sophistication of threats and the impact of security breaches have caused managers to continually grapple with strategies to reduce these risks. One common security control is the adoption of information security policies (ISPs) geared at improving employees’ compliance behaviour. However, there is mounting empirical evidence that shows that ISP compliance is a challenging undertaking with less than satisfactory outcomes. Further, little attention is placed on developing economies in the study of this phenomenon. This research adopts a values-based methodology to determine fundamental and means objectives in maximising employees’ compliance with ISPs in a developing economy context. The research identifies 30 objectives and demonstrates that risk mitigation, people, technical and organisational factors are essential to improving compliance. The results contribute objectives, contextualised to the people for whom the results are relevant, thus promoting deeper understanding. The research ofers utility to managers in the design and implementation of InfoSec strategies and policies. The findings can also inform investment decisions regarding compliance tools, methods and technologies. Recognising that security (information and cyber) threats are a global dilemma, we contend that investigating forms of security risks and potential solutions can mitigate the social and economic costs of security incidents.

## 1. Introduction

With the growing reliance on information and communication technologies (ICTs) (as evidenced from experiences arising from the COVID-19 pandemic), researchers (e.g., Walsham, 2012; Zheng et al., 2018) continue to investigate how ICTs can help tackle development challenges. A key outcome of these studies reinforces that the efective use of ICTs can have a significant impact on the social and economic wellbeing of the lives of people. In other words, the transformative potential of ICTs can result in improvement in eficiencies, reduction in costs, enhanced information capabilities and economic empowerment (e.g., Osei-Bryson & Vogel, 2014; Walsham, 2017). Conversely, the risks associated with the use of ICTs, namely cybercrimes and information security (InfoSec) breaches, termed the dark side of ICTs (Unwin, 2017; Zheng et al., 2018), can slow the pace of development. This outcome becomes particularly evident when contextualised within the economic cost of the rising cybercrimes and InfoSec breaches (e.g., Ponemon Institute, 2020), which is estimated to exceed US\$1 trillion globally (Malekos Smith & Lostri, 2020). Thus, information and cybersecurity, and more specifically, sound security measures have transformative potential that ofers strategic and development significance to organisations and economies.

Given these high risks and costs, organisations continue to explore ways to improve their security outcomes. Information security policies (ISPs) are often relied on as a measure to aid in employees compliance (Sommestad et al., 2014) and thereby improve security outcomes. According to Boss et al. (2009), defining security controls such as security policies, and evaluating behaviours are efective measures in convincing individuals that security controls are mandatory. Organisations are further motivated to implement these security policies to manage reputational, financial and legal impacts (Spears & Barki, 2010) and to guide employees’ compliance with technical standards, local and international regulations (Wall et al., 2013).

Despite the potential benefits associated with ISPs, organisations continue to experience challenges with employees’ compliance. Industry reports highlight that insider threats are increasing (e.g., Ponemon Institute, 2020), where the most common threats are attributable to negligent users, credential thefts and malicious insiders. Intuitively, insider threats will likely reduce with increased adherence to good security practices such as those prescribed in the organisations’ ISPs. However, scholars (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010) report that despite the presence of ISPs, employees generally fail to comply with them. Consequently, a significant challenge remains regarding how to influence or encourage employee compliance with ISPs (D’Arcy & Greene, 2014).

While the literature contributes to improving our understanding of the complex issues surrounding information security policy compliance (ISPC), several gaps are identified. Cram et al. (2017) establish that most studies focus on understanding the drivers of ISPC and that relatively fewer studies consider how and why security policies are designed and implemented. Moreover, according to Cram et al. (2019), many of these studies have resulted in inconsistencies in findings and competing theoretical perspectives, thereby hindering eforts to obtain a clear understanding of the phenomenon. A consequence of, or limitation in applying these traditional theoretical frameworks, is that they constrain the studies; that is, the theoretical frameworks enforce limits and boundaries about what is and can be investigated within the specific decision context, that being ISPC among employees.

InfoSec, and by extension the maximisation of employees’ compliance with security policies, can be more efectively managed if the emphasis extends beyond the technical means of protecting information resources (e.g., Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001). The implementation of IS security should look beyond the technical and organisational factors to consider facets such as ethical and human factors (e.g., Trompeter & Elof, 2001). As the information security landscape evolves, it may be dificult to have an accurate or complete assessment of changing security threats, behaviours and consequences without considerations for non-technical factors. Additionally, information systems (IS) researchers highlight the importance of understanding the assumptions and values of stakeholders for successful IS-related outcomes (e.g., Barclay & Osei-Bryson, 2010; Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001). These values can be used to generate decision opportunities and enable more eficient planning by focusing on the activities that can help reach the fundamental objectives (Keeney, 2008). This view, scholars argue, provides useful analytic perspectives for explaining and anticipating actions and meanings that are not easily obtained with other theoretical lenses (Dhillon & Torkzadeh, 2006; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994).

It becomes clear that there is a need to better understand values associated with managing security risks or the dark side of ICTs, including ISPC. According to Zheng et al. (2018), “the ICT4D field needs to pay more attention to the ‘dark side’ of ICT4D”. Similarly, Unwin (2017) states that it is imperative to investigate the darker side of ICTs as these darker aspects are crucial to understanding ICT4D, especially since they can more seriously impact the less advantaged people and countries. We contend that ISPC is deeply connected to issues of the dark side of ICTs since non-compliance with good security practices (including ISPs) heightens security risks. These risks, if left unchecked, can lead to increased illegal or inappropriate acts by employees as indicated in the aforementioned report of escalating insider threats. Further, secured ICTs are necessary for development in that secured processes and infrastructure impact security resilience through the adoption and use of appropriate policies, such as ISPs.

The research therefore seeks to examine the key values of InfoSec experts regarding their ISPC decisions, which should help explain how employees compliance behaviour can be improved or maximised. Our research question then, is: what are the key values and objectives in maximising employees’ compliance with ISPs? The research, situated in a developing economy, relies on the value-focused thinking (VFT) approach in identifying contextual-based values and objectives for improving or maximising employees ISPC. The VFT is appropriate because of its “valuecentric” approach to identifying core objectives associated with an organisational activity or process, such as ISPC decisions, which serves as an aid to better decision-making. Multiple studies apply the VFT approach to elicit values from stakeholders in addressing specific decision problems (e.g., Keeney, 2008; Smith et al., 2018). This study continues that scientific tradition.

The study ofers several contributions. It contributes to the literature by adopting a value-centric lens to tackle the inconsistencies in the findings on ISPC that are grounded in traditional behavioural theoretical lenses. The research is further situated in a largely unexplored domain of developing countries to extend the dialogue on ISPC by taking into account the context of ICT4D. It also contributes to practice by identifying important factors that can inform the planning and design of InfoSec strategies and compliance policies that can in turn improve employee compliance and strengthen organisational security posture. The rest of the paper proceeds as follows: the next section discusses the relevant literature and brings to the fore the research context and the gaps that are being addressed in this study; we next present the research methodology and its application in this study; the results and implications of the study are later presented followed by the concluding discussion.

## 2. Relevant literature

Development can be construed as positive change, growth, advancement or progress in a particular area. Within the context of human development, researchers describe development as enhancing capability (Anand & Sen, 2000) or as a tool enabling people to attain their highest level of ability (Sen, 2001). These perspectives highlight that there is some level of growth, transition or transformation from an unsatisfactory state to an improved level of well-being or operational maturity. This is consistent with the transformative potential of ICTs to “make a better world” as described by Walsham (2017) in the ICT4D discourse. While many ICT4D studies seek to investigate the economic and social changes in developing countries resulting from the use of ICTs (De et al., 2018), there is still an ongoing debate on the constituents of ICT4D research. Zheng et al. (2018) argue that certain perspectives, such as the attention on the ICT artefact itself instead of on its transformative possibility, results in a pursuit that ignores the need to understand the implications of ICTs for development. This argument can also be extended to the InfoSec environment where the implications of InfoSec strategies, policies, investments and practices are often not contextualised as a development initiative or ICT4D research.

## 2.1. The quest to improve employees’ ISPC behaviour

ISPs are essential management tools with the potential to transform organisational security maturity, through sound design and a high level of compliance. ISPs are formalised rules and guidelines to which employees should adhere, to properly safeguard and use the organisational IS resources (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2009). According to an international standard, ISPs also provide management direction and support for InfoSec in accordance with business requirements and relevant laws and regulations (ISO/IEC, 2013). ISPs could therefore be considered the backbone of an organisation’s InfoSec eforts.

To better understand how ISPs can impact and afect employees’ course of actions scholars have primarily examined the role of employees’ behaviours. A review of the literature highlights that empirical studies have relied on various theories such as deterrence theory (DT) (eg., Balozian et al., 2017), protection motivation theory (PMT) (e.g., Sommestad et al., 2019), theory of planned behaviour (TPB) (e.g., D’Arcy & Lowry, 2019) and InfoSec awareness (ISA) (e.g., X. Chen, Chen et al., 2018) to explain employees ISPC behaviours. A summary of some studies employing these theories is presented in Table A1 in the online supplemental material. The literature analysis also highlights several opportunities/gaps such as: i) limited ISPC research in the developing or ICT4D context; ii) contextual factors are often not considered in ISPC studies; and iii) there are inconsistent and contradictory results regarding the impacts of the diverse behavioural constructs on ISPC.

DT emphasises that employees can be dissuaded from engaging in unwanted behaviour when punishment is highlighted. However, DT received mixed support in the InfoSec literature (Cram et al., 2019). While Balozian et al. (2017) report no support for sanctions on ISPC intention, Li et al. (2010) report support for detection probability and no support for sanction severity, yet others report support, although the impacts of deterrents may be positive as well as negative (e.g., Herath & Rao, 2009; Khan & AlShare, 2019).

PMT focuses on employees’ threat and coping appraisals. In the InfoSec context, threat appraisal is the employees’ assessment of the level of security risk while coping appraisal refers to the capability of the employee in complying with the ISP and whether such compliance is efective in reducing security risks. Empirical results provide some support for PMT in explaining ISPC (e.g., Y. Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Moody et al., 2018; Siponen et al., 2014), however, the results are mixed (Johnston et al., 2015). Cram et al. (2019) note that there are inconsistencies in terms of the predictive strength of the PMT’s constructs and question whether certain PMT-based relationships hold under diferent circumstances. Moreover, response costs (Boss et al., 2015) and rewards (Cram et al., 2019) are often not considered in PMT-grounded ISPC models.

TPB focuses on what motivates employees’ ISPC behaviours, their attitudes towards such behaviours and their intentions. The relationships described in the TPB are among the most frequently investigated in models of ISPC (Cram et al., 2019; D’Arcy & Lowry, 2019; Sommestad et al., 2019). While empirical results reveal strong support for the attitude to intention link in ISPC compliance (e.g., Sommestad et al., 2019), it is not significant in Herath and Rao (2009) study and Moody et al.’s (2018) unified model of ISPC. Moreover, empirical results also highlight the importance of moral and social influences on ISPC (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Herath & Rao, 2009; Li et al., 2014), yet these are not investigated in Moody et al.’s (2018) unified ISPC model.

Scholars also identify ISA as a key influencer of employees’ ISPC behaviour. The main argument is that ISA should improve employees understanding and knowledge of potential InfoSec risks they may face and the appropriate actions to take to protect the organisation’s information assets (Donalds, 2015), as well as to educate them about their roles and responsibilities concerning those risks (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). It is expected that ISA will influence employees to go beyond activities formally stated in ISPs, thereby increasing InfoSec. The extant literature provides empirical support for the influence of ISA on improving InfoSec related behaviours such as users password compliance behaviour (Donalds & Osei-

Bryson, 2020), IS misuse intention (D’Arcy et al., 2009) and ISPC intention (X. Chen, Chen et al., 2018). While some studies report a direct influence of ISA on compliance behaviour (e.g., X. Chen, Wu et al., 2018; Donalds & Osei-Bryson, 2020) others report an indirect influence (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010; D’Arcy et al., 2009; Koohang & Anderson, 2020).

From the preceding, the extant literature, grounded in traditional behavioural theories, such as those cited above, “can be (and will continue to be) fruitful areas for understanding the motivations of individuals” (Warkentin & Willison, 2009, p. 102) such as ISPC behaviour, however, “inconsistencies in the reported findings [of ISPC studies] have yielded certain unresolved conflicts” (Cram et al., 2019, p. 526). Additionally, Dhillon and Torkzadeh (2006) note that while traditional theories improve our understanding of diferent aspects of InfoSec, they may not be suficient to help us develop a comprehensive list of IS security values as perceived by InfoSec experts. We therefore argue that the equivocality in the findings as well as the inherent limitation of traditional theories in identifying a comprehensive list of individual InfoSec values, that the question of what are the key antecedents of ISPC, remains an open one. Cram et al. (2019) note that “there is a lack of consensus regarding the key drivers of security policy compliance and uncertainty regarding if/how these drivers perform under diferent conditions”. Further, Warkentin and Willison (2009) urge InfoSec scholars to incorporate new avenues of exploration into their repertoire, expanding their horizons by incorporating not only new theoretical foundations but new methodologica approaches as well. We heed this call in applying the VFT approach in this study. Our research approach to identifying antecedents of ISPC difers from the traditional method for decision-making, mostly employed in the extant InfoSec literature. In our approach, InfoSec experts are encouraged to identify ‘what they care about’ with regards to maximising ISPC, whereas, in the more traditional approaches the security experts base their choices of solutions on availability (Keeney, 1992). In this way, our approach helps InfoSec experts to be proactive in decision-making by creating valuebased options instead of being limited to the available alternatives.

Of note, much of the ISPC models test relationships posited in traditional behavioural theories such as TPB, PMT and DT (see Cram et al., 2019). However, we cannot assume that the influences of such factors are uniform across or generalisable to all contexts, such as an ICT4D or developing economy context. Indeed, empirical results of prior works emphasise that contextual factors such as national/organisational culture (e.g., Y. Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Khan & AlShare, 2019) and organisational security climate (Goo et al., 2014) have diferent impacts on employees behaviour towards InfoSec. These and other similar findings underline the importance of context-based research in InfoSec, and further support the need for studies focusing on ISPC in an ICT4D context.

Our review of empirical studies also highlights that while employee ISPC behaviour is well investigated in more developed economies, it is rarely emphasised in the developing economy or ICT4D context. The scholarly works in the developing economy context focus on InfoSec related behaviours such as security and privacy concerns in social network services (Barrett-Maitland et al., 2016), employees’ ISPC behaviour (Balozian et al., 2017; Y. Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Koranteng et al., 2020) and cybersecurity policy compliance (CSPC) behaviour (Donalds, 2015; Donalds & Osei-Bryson, 2020). The few studies focusing on InfoSec related behaviours and ISPC emphasise that ISPC investigation is still in the embryonic stage in the developing economy context, creating many opportunities for context-based research about the phenomenon. Davison and Martinsons (2016) make the point that the efectiveness of policies and practices depend on rigorous research in relevant contexts and that the impact of information technology (IT) will often depend critically on the context. Moreover, scholars highlight that “one size does not fit all” for achieving employee ISPC and that it is yet to be determined which techniques work best for diferent organisational employees (Balozian et al., 2017, p. 1). By conducting this research in a developing economy context our research addresses these concerns as well as the void in the literature by aiming to identify local InfoSec experts’ values to maximise employees’ compliance with ISPs.

## 2.2. Applying VFT in organisational decision contexts

Keeney (2008) describes the VFT approach as having the potential to lead to better decisions, which consists of deciding or identifying what you want (or wish for) and thereafter figuring out how to get it (Keeney, 1992). These aspirations or values, according to Keeney (1994) are key to decision making. IS scholars also highlight the importance of individual values in achieving successful ISrelated outcomes. According to Dhillon and Torkzadeh (2006), researchers across various fields highlight the importance of values as a means to understand socio-organisational aspects to achieve success in the management of organisational technological initiatives and other IS-related outcomes. As an illustration, values are identified as important in better decision-making (e.g., Keeney, 1994) and project performance criteria and measures (Barclay & Osei-Bryson, 2010).

The VFT approach provides decision-makers with a technique that enables better decision-making regarding a particular decision problem or situation, as a result of a deliberate efort to extend the consideration beyond simply considering alternatives and subsequently making decisions about those alternatives. Stated diferently, no limit is imposed on identifying what stakeholders consider important or care about in making a particular decision (Keeney, 1994). The VFT approach therefore enables organisations and decision-makers to achieve their true values (Keeney, 1992), particularly since the determination of values is a critical step in the strategic decision-making process. This approach is used to investigate multiple phenomena across various disciplines to include conflict management and negotiation (Keeney, 1994), customer values related to internet commerce (Keeney, 1999) and ERP systems planning (May et al., 2013).

The VFT is also applied in identifying stakeholders values within diferent developing economy contexts. Barclay and Logan (2013), in examining Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), found that success was framed within short and long-term requirements including satisfaction with the learning experience, preparedness for the working world, viability of the online ofering, access to learning tools and maintaining a reputation for quality in relation to curricula and service standards. Barrett-Maitland et al. (2016), in examining the conditions necessary to maximise users’ security and privacy in social networking sites across six Caribbean countries found that information privacy, the confidentiality of personal information, the integrity of social networks, security controls, individual and corporate social responsibilities are important values in maximising security and privacy concerns.

Within the IS domain, the VFT approach is successfully applied to determine stakeholders’ valuebased objectives, although fewer studies have relied on the VFT to investigate InfoSec generally, not ISPC specifically (see Table A2 in the online supplemental material). We address this gap with the intent to identify important values associated with ISPC and to discuss how organisations can, utilising the identified values, maximise employees’ compliance with ISPs. To accomplish this, the VFT approach provides structured guidelines to identify objectives (means and fundamental), based on stakeholders’ values, as well as guidelines about how to organise these objectives. The utility of the VFT is further highlighted in Table A2 as the summary emphasises that there is a polymorphic array of complex, layered and interwoven factors, extending beyond technical means, that impact the eficacy of IS outcomes. This reinforces the utility of the VFT approach in determining multidimensional objectives to support decision-making in diverse decision contexts.

## 3. Research methodology

In this study, we apply the VFT approach to understand the values of employees’ compliance in relation to organisational ISPs. Keeney (1994, 1999) emphasises that any decision situation should begin with values as they are central to decision-making (Keeney, 1994, 1999). The VFT considers the best outcome and works towards making it happen (Keeney, 1992). It involves identifying, articulating, and organising the objectives which are then relied on to guide all facets of the decision process (Keeney, 2008). As highlighted in the previous section, the VFT is successfully applied in studies where the values of specific decision contexts are being investigated to help inform the understanding of diverse phenomena. Researchers (e.g., Barclay & Osei-Bryson, 2010; May et al., 2013; Smith et al., 2018) rely on the VFT to help answer how a particular decision problem can be addressed or a situation can be improved or maximised. This type of application is consistent with our research objective, i.e., how to improve or maximise employees’ ISPC behaviours, which makes the VFT approach an appropriate choice in this study. We utilise in-depth qualitative interviews to apply the principles of the VFT approach for identifying and classifying the values of local InfoSec experts. According to Rowley (2012), interviews are used in conducting qualitative research where the researcher is interested in collecting “facts”, or gaining insights into or understanding of opinions, attitudes, experiences, processes, behaviours or predictions. Key characteristics of in-depth interviews are the combination of structure and flexibility, interactivity and the utilisation of probes and other techniques to achieve the depth of answers (Legard et al., 2003). Our interviews include a mixture of structure, based on the VFT guidelines, and flexibility, based on the interviewee’s responses as we probed deeper for greater insights into values or why a particular value is important.

Each interview session ranges in duration from 1 to 2 hours. The sessions are audio-recorded and written notes are taken to support the data analysis. The six local InfoSec experts interviewed are employed to organisations operating in Jamaica that comprise the banking, insurance, law enforcement, higher education and government services sectors. Each expert has over 10 years of experience in the InfoSec field and is primarily responsible for the design and implementation of ISPs and other security measures in their respective organisations. A summary of the professional profiles of the experts is provided in Table A3 (see online supplemental material), while maintaining anonymity.

We observe that there was data convergence after the fourth interview, i.e., no new values were identified. However, we continued to the sixth expert to maximise responses from available experts and to verify the set of shared values. This interviewing procedure is consistent with the method used by other researchers who employ the VFT approach. Drevin et al. (2007) note that no new values were identified after the fourth interview despite interviewing seven participants. Nah et al. (2005) also observe that after their seventh interview no new values were identified even though ten participants were interviewed. This qualitative research standard refers to “data saturation point”, which involves interviewing new participants continually until the data set is complete i.e., data is gathered to the point of diminishing returns as indicated by data replication or redundancy (Bowen, 2008). In other words, the saturation point occurs when additional interviews do not provide any new insights into the phenomenon under investigation (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Therefore, although it may be argued that the use of six experts is not a suficient sample, we observe this generally accepted qualitative procedure in conducting the interviews and determining the suficiency of interviews. Notably, the debate on how many interviews is enough or valid continues (e.g., Becker, 2012; Rowley, 2012). It is argued that context and purpose largely inform the numbers, in addition to whether there is convergence in the responses received (Becker, 2012). Research experts indicate that where the purpose is to establish if something is possible or to provide a rich account, a single qualitative interview (or case) is appropriate (Becker, 2012). Conversely, where the purpose is to establish commonalities or allow comparison, a larger number is likely to be needed (Baker & Edwards, 2012). On this basis, interviewing six InfoSec experts can be considered suficient in providing a rich account of the values associated with ISPC.

## 4. Application of the VFT Approach

We employ the following five-step process (Figure 1) to identify, articulate and organise the values and objectives elicited from the InfoSec experts. The steps are not entirely sequential as there are several instances of iteration to refine the outcomes of each step.

Step 1 involves two activities, framing our interview questions and identifying the experts. The framing of our questions for the interviews is based on the VFT guidelines provided for eliciting values and objectives. Typical questions include: “What do you want?”; and “What do you value?” (see Keeney, 1992). We therefore adapt the questions in the guideline to the ISPC context, with the decision context being to improve or maximise ISPC behaviours. Table 1 shows a sample of these questions.

Identified values are then converted to a common form, generally in the form of objectives. Values identified by the experts may result in one or more objectives.

Step 2 involves three activities, eliciting values, probing to obtain in-depth understanding and converting to a common form. Values are those principles that one strives to achieve and define all that one can care about in a specific situation (Keeney, 1994, 1999). Therefore, we elicited from the experts the set of conditions that would influence improved or maximised employees’ compliance behaviours as the basis of understanding their values in maximising compliance in their organisations. Further, the discussion highlighted that they should have no constraints or limits in their thinking, which sets the stage for identifying what they cared about in maximising compliance behaviours. The utilisation of the set of questions developed in Step 1 facilitated further probing based on the responses of the experts.

![](/api/attachments/YCDDQT32/fulltext/images/c861e85a646bf70d7a279b30bf05457bef27f819196a99b4ebbb4fbbbeaa7210.jpg)  
Figure 1. The VFT research approach.

The objectives should specify the stated value in greater and more coherent detail (Keeney, 2008) and is characterised by three features, namely: a decision context, an object and a direction of preference. Therefore, the value “manage risk” can be interpreted as to reduce or minimise security threats. The decision context is related to “threats”, the object is “security” and the direction of preference is to “reduce” or to have “fewer security threats”. It is also possible to derive more than one objective from a specific value. It should also be noted that probes are sometimes used to gain an in-depth understanding of a value. For instance, the expert may state “maintain user privacy” as a value, a suitable probe could be “do you think there are problems with maintaining user privacy”? The response could generate further values such as “only persons authorised to view private data should have access”, etc. Duplicate objectives are removed and similar objectives retained (Keeney, 1999).

Step 3, structuring the objectives consist of three steps. Following the determination of objectives from Step 2, a process to distinguish between means and fundamental objectives is performed. The means objectives are regarded as those whose attainment will influence the degree to which the fundamental objectives are attained while the fundamental objectives refer to the objectives underlying the essential reasons for the problem under consideration (Keeney, 1999). To distinguish between the objectives, we utilise the “why is this important” (WITI) procedure. Each objective is evaluated against the WITI question and if an objective is found to be important because it helps achieve another objective, it is categorised as a means objective; otherwise, it is a fundamental objective. For example, in determining why the promote awareness objective is important, the experts indicate that they wish to reduce security breaches or to improve security knowledge. This gives rise to several objectives: reduce security risks; reduce security breaches; and improve security knowledge. With further interrogation, these objectives are found to be means objectives to some fundamental objectives such as to improve risk management practices in the organisation and to maintain confidentiality, integrity and availability (CIA) of ISs.

We introduce a second form of structuring of the objectives, which is referred to as the strategic fit test and is based on categories of strategic factors. Within our context, strategic factors are those interests that are essential to the success of organisational InfoSec compliance. We identify these to be risk mitigation, technical, people and organisational factors. These categories bear similarities to the main consideration in devising security strategies (e.g., Barclay, 2014) and accords with current practice. To structure the objectives, we ask the question, “what strategic factor is impacted by the objective?” Similar to the WITI test, each objective is evaluated against the strategic factors. For example, to promote user awareness programmes is deemed a people factor because employees would be impacted by that process. We find that several objectives are crosscutting and could therefore fit into more than one category. This is unsurprising given the nature of strategic endeavours. For example, to develop a culture of compliance can be viewed as a people factor but has a better fit as an organisational factor. Similarly, to minimise user impact can have both risk mitigation and people impacts. For the purpose of the reporting however, a single category is chosen. The final step involves organising the means and fundamental objectives to highlight the relationships among them and to also highlight the category of strategic factor that each means objectives is deemed as the best fit.

Step 3 resulted in 6 fundamental objectives and 24 means objectives (see Tables 2 and 3). In Step 4, the outcome is the means and fundamental objectives for maximising ISPC which highlights relationships among the objectives. In other words, the results of the previous steps are depicted graphically. Figure 2 depicts the relationships between objectives, i.e., between the means and fundamental objectives and the strategic factor category under which a means objective falls. Two types of information can be communicated in this step, the traditional means-ends network and the classified means-ends objectives. Figure 2 depicts the latter while the former is used in the next step (validating objectives).

In Step 5, we validate our means and fundamental objectives by sharing the means-ends network with the experts to obtain their perspectives on whether the objectives and relationships accurately reflect the discussions and are consistent with their understanding of the security compliance domain. We explained to each expert that the means-ends network is an aggregate of perspectives from all experts therefore, objectives not identified by that expert, but by other experts, would be included in the diagram. They were asked to provide insights on any missing values and objectives and why those missing objectives are important. Except for questions about why particular means and fundamental objectives are included in the network but not identified in their interview, the experts expressed satisfaction with the means-ends network. We also used prior literature as further sources for validating the study’s results, which help to inform the categorisation described in Step 3. This verification step is consistent with Creswell’s (1998) suggestion to use prior literature for “supplemental validation”.

## 5. Findings and discussion

## 5.1. InfoSec objectives for better policy compliance management

In this section we report and discuss the results of Steps 5 and 6 of our approach, i.e., structuring and reporting of the objectives. We present the objectives of the experts framed within the context of maximising ISPC behaviours. Table 2 shows the fundamental objectives while Table 3 shows the means objectives. Each table highlights sub-objectives that constitute each objective based on the process of conversion of related values to a common form as described in Step 2. Figure 2 shows the means objectives, organised based on the 4 identified strategic factors, that support one or more fundamental objectives. The strategic factor categorisation can further aid the decisionmaking process by highlighting the main strategic factors that can contribute to maximising employees ISPC behaviour.

## 5.2. Fundamental objectives to maximising ISPC behaviours

We identify 6 fundamental objectives: improve risk management practices; maintain CIA of ISs; improve regulatory compliance; enhance customer trust and confidence in ISs; maintain business reputation; and maximise revenues. These fundamental objectives are deemed to be critical end objectives in employees compliance with ISPs. These objectives emphasise why it is important for managers and executives to improve or maximise their organisational policy compliance mandates. The findings highlight the tight coupling of technical and strategic objectives to meet an organisation’s ISPC objectives. This reflects an extension of the boundary of considerations in improving ISPC in organisations, where according to Dhillon and Backhouse (2001), InfoSec studies are replete with “technical and functionalist preconceptions”. The findings also align with academia and practice where there is an appreciation that multidimensional perspectives are required to efectively respond to the current ISPC conundrum and InfoSec challenges in general.

This experience emphasises the challenges in minimising security risks and the evolving nature of threats organisations face. A similar observation is made by Niemimaa and Niemimaa (2017) who state that organisations face pressure to adopt best security practices to manage InfoSec risks to their information assets. ## 5.2.2. Maintain CIA of ISs

The CIA triad is considered an important cornerstone in InfoSec management. When we enquire about the importance of security in the work environment, Expert 5 declares: “persons want to be assured of the integrity of the data they are using, [the users] want to ensure the CIA of our systems”. This underscores the expected relationship and correlation between improved compliance practices and the CIA of organisational systems. The COVID-19 pandemic also demonstrates the critical nature of the CIA as many organisations shifted to remote work and increased or expanded electronic service oferings. This phenomenon becomes more evident in developing countries, such as Jamaica, where some organisations may have been several steps behind in their quantum of electronic service oferings, such as contact tracing, vaccination bookings and home delivery services. According to Malekos Smith and Lostri (2020), it takes an average of 19 hours for most organisations to move from the identification of an incident to remediation. It is reasonable to expect that organisations constrained with limited access to resources (e.g., skilled expertise and security technologies such as AI-based technologies) or have less security maturity may take even longer. In this regard, the critical nature of the CIA becomes even more evident as the unavailability of a system or service, or the lack of trust in the integrity of systems or data can be more damaging for lessresourced organisations and economies.

## 5.2.3. Improve regulatory compliance

Organisations particularly in the financial sector highlight the importance of meeting strict regulatory requirements such as GDPR (general data protection regulation), anti-money laundering/counter-terrorism requirements and Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI-DSS). According to Expert 4: “as a regulated entity, the business has to tick a number of compliance checkboxes”.

The experts also reveal that many of their organisational compliance initiatives, including awareness programmes are geared towards ensuring that they meet specific regulatory guidelines. There is also a direct link to other objectives/sub-objectives such as to reduce security threats, minimise security costs including reduced regulatory fines and penalties as means to improve risk management practices. Additionally, the experts afirm that regulatory compliance improves security performance and accountability in security mechanisms and procedures. This is consistent with studies that identify regulatory compliance as crucial to InfoSec eforts (e.g., Dlamini et al., 2009).

## 5.2.4. Enhance customer trust and confidence

Enhancing trust and confidence in the organisation’s ISs is identified as an important objective for the experts. The experts explain that customer trust will have a positive impact on their general business success since that trust would likely translate into the initial and continuance use of their systems. Expert 6 highlights the importance of trust and the nexus of trust, security and the success of the business: “we are in the business of trust, [therefore], if the business cannot prove to [customers, business partners, etc.] that it can handle their data in terms of CIA, then there is no way [they] will be doing business with us. This in turn impacts revenue and market share”. Expert 5 further reiterates this relationship and its significance and states: “we work in a business that is highly dependent on customer trust and loyalty”. Further, the experts emphasise that a high level of trust will be established if there are perceptions of satisfactory and safe experiences by the users and customers. These views are consistent with studies that report that trust has a positive impact on users’ intention to use a system (e.g., Hoehle et al., 2012).

Means objectives such as to improve technical controls and to improve net promoter scores are identified as key to enhance customer trust and confidence in ISs. Therefore, InfoSec policy compliance initiatives centred on strengthening the relationships between these and other objectives will likely yield success.

## 5.2.5. Maintain business reputation

Business or corporate reputation is viewed as a competitive advantage in which a business takes pride. According to Expert 3: “[The company] sees itself with pride. It works hard to not have its name sullied”. The statement underscores the extent to which businesses will go to minimise security risks and perhaps not report security breaches in an efort not to tarnish their reputation. This is a reasonable position as it has been shown that security breaches can afect a business’ bottom line. According to Andoh-Baidoo and Osei-Bryson (2007), security breaches can invariably lead to lower sales revenue, higher expenses, decreases in future profits and dividends and a reduction in market value.

Projecting a good security posture, such as highlighting investments in security infrastructure, is found to be an important part of improving or to maintain business reputation: According to Expert 6: “when customers see that we are investing in security they receive some level of assurance that we are serious about protecting them”. The security behaviours and practices of an organisation therefore influence its reputation, particularly when viewed through the lens of its customers. Objectives such as develop a culture of compliance, improve security eficiencies and improve security infrastructure are identified as means to maintain business reputation of organisations.

## 5.2.6. Maximise revenues

Profit-seeking organisations are generally not altruistic by nature and thus money serves as a strong motive. Compliance activities are therefore weighed against factors such as costs and revenues. The experts acknowledge the importance of minimising security costs, including compliance-related costs in an environment where expenses are seen as a hindrance to profits. Expert 5 explains: “what is important is that the organisation can meet its [business] objectives”. The ability to meet business objectives are typically framed in terms of the business performance and its bottom line. Further, studies reiterate the importance of maximising revenue as a key component of the growth and competitiveness of an organisation (Casadesus-Masanell & Ricart, 2011) which are key business performance indicators. The nexus of a strong security posture and business performance is further highlighted as the experts referred to sub-objectives such as maintain profitability and maintain market share as important motivations for seeking to improve InfoSec policy compliance among employees. This demonstrates the need for managers to be able to skilfully balance what may appear as competing demands to achieve satisfactory outcomes. In developing economies, where organisations such as government services may have significantly limited financial resources, there may be the temptation to forego security initiatives, including targeted compliance programmes. However, this may not be the best decision for the business given the cyber threat landscape.

## 5.3. Means objectives to maximising ISPC

Twenty-four means objectives are identified as important preconditions to the fundamental objectives in maximising employees’ ISPC behaviour. These objectives may be viewed as shorter-term actions, preferably undertaken collectively to achieve the longerterm goals of maximising employees’ compliance with information security policies in an organisation. Financial considerations are found to be important means to achieving some organisational goals in relation to compliance. This further underscores that gaps in organisational security budgets will inevitably lead to increased risks and vulnerabilities. For instance, access to finance is important because of the possibility to improve security infrastructure, promote user awareness programmes and promote staf education and training. Similarly, the importance of minimising business and user impacts are connected to the goal, minimise security costs. For less-resourced organisations, these may be particularly important as they seek to manage their scarce resources while seeking to improve compliance. The means objectives also suggest potential organisational strategies that can inform InfoSec policy design; such as adopt good security behaviours, improve technical controls, promote behavioural consequences of compliance and noncompliance, increase user breach reporting and others.

The subsequent discussion of the means objectives is framed within the strategic factor categories of people, technical, risk and organisational. The categorisation reafirms the importance of a holistic and strategic method to addressing ISPC problems (see Figure 2). The risk mitigation factor includes measures or coordinated actions taken by the organisation to reduce InfoSec risks. Additionally, as seen in Figure 2, while the risk-based means objectives emphasise the need to reduce the various forms of security risks (e.g., insider threats, external breaches), it also promotes protecting user data and good security practices in the organisation (e.g., access controls, firewalls, appropriate user policies and guides).

The people factor category is concerned with coordinated actions (e.g., learning, training, sharing) taken by the organisation to efect positive changes in employees’ behaviours regarding good security practices. Means objectives that can influence human behaviour include promote user awareness programmes on an ongoing basis and actively promote staf education and training on current threats and trends in security. Awareness is a common value identified by all experts with Expert 2 declaring that their organisation tries to put “the fun in compliance” to promote good security practices among staf, and ensure that “awareness training is delivered in a digestible form”. The latter also highlights the importance of identifying approaches that resonate with employees including the types and length/duration of awareness content. This can ensure that the information is understood, and that knowledge assimilation takes place. Expert 6 highlights the approaches adopted and the general attitude to promote awareness in their organisation: “there is investment in training and awareness. In addition to newsletters, bulletins, we also have annual assessments, periodic video training. New hires will also be involved in an intensive induction in the [company’s] culture that includes security [training] and what it means to be an employee of [the company]”. The impact of training and awareness coincides with studies that recognise the influence of ongoing awareness, training and security education in improving InfoSec-related behaviours, whether directly or indirectly (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010; D’Arcy et al., 2009; Donalds, 2015; Donalds & Osei-Bryson, 2020).

Behavioural consequences provide insights into the mechanisms to enhance healthy security behaviours. The sub-objective increase sanctions is consistent with the findings from the ISPC literature as scholars identify sanctions as a key antecedent of ISPC (e.g., Khan & AlShare, 2019; Li et al., 2010). However, Cram et al. (2019) note that reward in the ISPC domain is not often investigated and argue that today’s managers may have to choose between rewards and sanctions to enhance ISPC. However, our findings indicate that organisations should focus on both elements to achieve optimal results.

User engagement comprises the extent of employ ees’ buy-in or commitment regarding ISPC compliance. Thus, promote user engagement will likely promote voluntariness of sharing as a result of objectives such as promote staf education and training and promote user awareness programmes and an alignment of the employees’ security attitudes and mindsets with good security practices. Several experts recount that employees would contact the IT department when they believed they encountered any suspicious act/ event, such as a phishing attempt. This reafirms that user engagement is strengthened when there is a convergence of positive security culture, knowledgeable employees who are aware of how to identify and report security incidents or suspicious acts, and the organisation is actively engaged in continuous security awareness mechanisms.

Technical measures are the coordinated actions taken by the organisation to secure its physical infrastructure and build resilience, based on measures associated with aspects of the computer-physical system. The objectives to improve security infrastructure, improve technical controls and invest in compliance technologies highlight the importance of technical safeguards (such as threat detection and incident management tools) to minimise security risks. While in recent years the InfoSec discourse advocates for greater focus on the human aspect of InfoSec, these objectives reafirm that the technical element remains a critical component to achieve improved InfoSec. For instance, Trompeter and Elof (2001) argue that addressing the technical element of IS security is important and Dhillon and Torkzadeh (2006) identify, as a sub-objective, the need to balance awareness of the technical aspects of InfoSec.

Organisational factors are those business decisions, taken by the organisational leadership, in particular, that can impact security actions adopted by organisations. The organisational factors therefore highlight the concerns of strategic leadership in ways such as obtain board support for ISPC initiatives, develop of a culture of compliance, improve access to finance and improve net promoter score. The impact of leadership on the security outcomes of an organisation is also supported by researchers (e.g., Donalds, 2015; Khan & AlShare, 2019) who found statistically significant support for the influence of top management support on employees’ security compliance behaviour.

The means objectives and their categorisation (Figure 2, Table 3) reafirm the need for organisations to place balanced attention on all elements of security such as risk mitigation, people, technical and organisational, in improving their InfoSec outlook, particularly regarding employee compliance behaviours.

## 6. Implications for theory

This study contributes to the ICT4D and InfoSec research stream in several ways. It is well established and accepted that IS research should demonstrate rigour, relevance and make an impact. According to Van Bijon and Osei-Bryson (2020), to address relevance, ICT4D researchers must consider the people for whom the expected results are relevant. While the extant ISPC literature has addressed relevance in terms of the research problems being addressed, Davison and Martinsons (2016) suggest that theories developed and tested in one context might not be adequate or valid for another context. They note that ICT4D research should elicit localised knowledge about the phenomenon being studied, framed by contextually-developed lenses (Davison & Martinsons, 2016). Localised knowledge can be described as the knowledge of people that live in a certain place that has developed over time. Therefore, relevance should involve the consideration that there may be diferent ‘best’ determinants of employees ISPC behaviour depending on the context. However, the issue of context and its potentia impact on behaviour is rarely emphasised in the ISPC literature (e.g., Y. Chen & Zahedi, 2016) or together, in the ICT4D research stream. Taking into account researchers’ advice regarding relevance and context, we apply the VFT approach to identify factors that maximise employees’ ISPC behaviours in organisations, by eliciting localised values from InfoSec experts in a developing economy context, Jamaica. To the best of our knowledge, the elicitation of contextually-developed factors that influence employees’ ISPC behaviour in an ICT4D context has not been previously investigated, evaluated nor empirically tested. Moreover, this type of inquiry, contextualised to the people for whom the research results are relevant, no doubt, promotes a deeper understanding of the phenomenon in the ICT4D context.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first studies to investigate employees’ InfoSec behaviours via the VFT approach. Our analysis reveals that only one study has applied the VFT approach to identify organisational InfoSec objectives (Dhillon & Torkzadeh, 2006), while no known study has applied it in the ISPC domain. We also went a step further by creating an additional step that allows for greater clarity on the types of objectives that can advance organisational compliance eforts. This conceptualisation ofers a new perspective in understanding antecedents that may influence and determine employees ISPC behaviours, which we believe supplements research grounded in other popular behavioural theories such as DT, PMT and TPB.

Our study ofers several key findings. Our results identify a novel objective/factor promote voluntariness of sharing as important in maximising employees compliance with ISPs. This factor refers to users of the IS proceeding from his/her own will or choice to share suspicious emails, potential threats, etc., with their IT departments even when not required to do so. Our findings reveal that other factors such as to promote user awareness programmes, promote staf education and training, among others, influence users’ voluntariness of sharing. These findings are consistent with the expectation that ISA and security education, training, and awareness (SETA) programmes will influence employees to go beyond activities formally stated in ISPs, improving security protection of the organisation’s information assets.

Although the extant ISPC literature has put a strong emphasis on the human/people element of InfoSec, this study calls for attention to a holistic set of factors spread across several strategic areas of organisational, risk mitigation, people and technical, that organisations should consider when seeking to improve employees’ compliance with ISPs. These results support the claim by scholars that a multi-perspective approach is more appropriate to protect InfoSec resources (e.g., Herath & Rao, 2009).

## 7. Implications for practice

Our findings also have important implications for InfoSec management practices. First, our results suggest to practitioners that it is certainly important to promote ISA, education and training programmes, since industry reports continue to highlight that a significant number of InfoSec incidents are caused by negligent employees (e.g., Malekos Smith & Lostri, 2020; Ponemon Institute, 2020). Moreover, our results suggest that to realise other objectives such as reduce security breaches, improve compliance knowledge, and promote user engagement, necessary objectives are to promote user awareness programmes and promote staf education and training. Therefore, we suggest that practitioners, including those in developing economies, design security awareness and training programmes to emphasise achieving these objectives.

Our results also suggest that practitioners can improve ISPC by providing rewards to employees for executing good security behaviours. This finding is also significant in light of prior research that reports no support or indirect support for the influence of rewards on employees’ ISPC behaviour (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Moody et al., 2018; Siponen et al., 2014). This study’s results also provide support for the influence of rewards to develop a culture of compliance. We therefore suggest that when developing reward programmes practitioners should also promote behavioural consequences associated with non-compliance.

Practitioners should be aware that employees voluntariness of sharing can be an efective mechanism to increase the protection of the organisation’s information assets. Several InfoSec experts note this user behaviour was a result of a fear of losing access to and usability of the systems, either due to a prior security incident that resulted in system loss or exposure to security awareness content. We therefore recommend that practitioners not only rely on ISPC mandatoriness measures, but should also encourage their constituents to be proactive, requesting expert advice on any suspicious email or activity on the network. This kind of activity has implications for the organisational compliance strategy where users now become a crucial part of the compliance support resource instead of the traditional stance of being merely a threat vector. This perspective could prove to be a useful strategy for developing countries or organisations that are less resourced where there is an increased reliance on a proactive user base to assist in measures such as identifying and reporting threats and vulnerabilities.

Our findings also illustrate that the type of business and the business environment influence the dominant ISP compliance style or strategy employed by the organisations. Those with strict regulatory compliance requirements tend to apply stricter sanctions for incidences of insider threats, such as dismissal. This phenomenon can be explained by the risk of loss of business reputation and revenue, which in turn would drive the specific incident response strategies and sanctions. The converse is also true, as organisations that are least monitored by regulations are less likely to be sanction driven. While we previously emphasised the importance of context from a developing perspective, this finding also highlights the role organisational context may play in driving a specific organisational InfoSec strategy. Specifically, the organisational context has implications for ISPC measures where the priority of the fundamental objectives influence the appropriate strategies in that context. Additionally, as the InfoSec landscape evolves with increased regulatory requirements, such as GDPR, there may be a shift in the dominant compliance strategies adopted by organisations.

The relevance of context and the business environment is also illustrated with the identification of resource-based objectives, such as access to finance, and minimise security costs as factors that can influence the implementation of an organisational InfoSec strategy. Contextualised in a setting where financial resources are generally limited, such as smaller or lessresourced organisations, the recognition of the relationship of these objectives to Infosec compliance outcomes may be amplified.

Finally, this study accords with other ISPC literature (e.g., Dhillon & Backhouse, 2001) that highlight that InfoSec programmes that emphasise only certain or limited aspects are less likely to be efective. For this reason, elevated security maturity and posture will demand a more comprehensive InfoSec programme to include InfoSec awareness and training, improve security infrastructure, invest in compliance technologies, access to finance, board support, protect user privacy, improve technical controls, promote behavioural consequences, etc.

## 8. Conclusion

It is dificult to ignore that the escalating threat landscape is having a deleterious efect on development. We contend that improved compliance minimises security risks and the costs associated with breaches (risk events), and thereby positively impacts development. Our study focuses on ISPC by investigating strategies to improve compliance with the adoption of a valuesbased assessment methodology. The research identifies 6 fundamental objectives and 24 means objectives to maximising employee ISPC behaviours. The 6 fundamental objectives include improved risk management practices, increased levels of CIA, increased customer trust and confidence, increased business reputation and revenues. The means objectives comprise training and education of staf and users, financial considerations such as access to finance, a culture of compliance, secured infrastructure, increased voluntariness of user sharing of experience and other considerations. The results demonstrate that a combination of risk management, organisational, technical and organisational measures drive the planning, design and implementation of InfoSec policies. This holistic view, it is argued, can enhance the efectiveness of the organisational InfoSec compliance approach.

The results provide compelling implications for organisations’ InfoSec compliance activities. The findings demonstrate the transformative potential of improving compliance through an understanding of the values that are important to the stakeholders and the importance of technical and non-technical factors in achieving that goal. The values and objectives bring attention to the importance of resources, the full cycle of compliance and the tight coupling of compliance goals with business goals. It is important to note that while the findings uncover resourcebased objectives that may resonate with developing economies or less-resourced organisations, such as SMEs, the data also reminds us that strategies and techniques to address InfoSec challenges, specifically employee compliance behaviours, can transcend geography and contexts.

Despite the ostensibly small sample of experts used, our research and findings have broad appeal and extend the dialogue of organisational strategies to address the ISPC problem and the role of InfoSec strategies in development. The results ofer utility to decisionmakers in the design of InfoSec strategies, the creation of ISPs that can increase the value to employees and investment decisions regarding compliance tools and technologies. Possible research directions include delving deeper into the means objectives to identify successful methods and strategies to achieve fundamental compliance objectives in diferent business contexts and through the utilisation of diferent research methods and theories. Future research can also explore how organisations can leverage the fundamental objectives to sustain improved compliance practices while examining the relationships between these objectives and common theories. The relationship between the business environment, culture and the values associated with ISPC could also be investigated.
