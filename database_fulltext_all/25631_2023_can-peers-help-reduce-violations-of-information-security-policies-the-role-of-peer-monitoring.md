# Can peers help reduce violations of information security policies? The role of peer monitoring

Adel Yazdanmehr<sup>a</sup> and Jingguo Wang<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Paul H. Chook Department of Information Systems and Statistics, Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, The City University of New York, New York, NY, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, College of Business, The University of Texas at Arlington, Arlington, TX, USA

## ABSTRACT

Peers may help others avoid violating organisational information security policies (ISPs). This study explores how peer monitoring reduces employee ISP violation intention. We propose that peer monitoring discourages employees from violating ISP. Moreover, trust plays an important role. Trust not only facilitates peer monitoring, but also moderates the efect of peer monitoring on employee ISP violation intention. In addition, collective responsibility leads to peer monitoring. We test our research model with data from two waves of surveys of 254 employees in the United States conducted two weeks apart. We utilise four scenarios in the second wave of surveys capturing the dependent variable and measure all other constructs in the first wave of surveys. Our results suggest that peer monitoring decreases one’s intention to violate ISPs. Furthermore, both collective responsibility and trust contribute to peer monitoring. Finally, trust amplifies the efect of peer monitoring on employees’ intention to violate ISPs. We discuss the theoretical contributions and practical implications.

## 1. Introduction

An information security policy (ISP) is a set of directives, procedures, regulations, and guidelines that prescribe employees’ roles and responsibilities in properly using and safeguarding an organisation’s information resources (Cram et al., 2019; Lowry & Moody, 2015). Organizations often design and implement their ISP following various regulations, based on their particular industry. For example, organisations in the finance industry refer to the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX), retailers follow the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI-DSS), healthcare organisations are bound by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), and government agencies are regulated by the Federal Information Security Management Act (FISMA).

Employees may violate such ISPs with no malicious purpose to harm their organisation. Such violations could be intentional or unintentional, and they are often linked to a lack of adequate training, weak commitment, carelessness, and a lack of motivation (Willison & Warkentin, 2013). Examples of such violations are abundant, including failure to change passwords, encrypt sensitive data, or logof when leaving one’s desk (Willison & Warkentin, 2013).

Ponemon (2020) shows that the frequency of security incidents caused by negligent employees is almost 2.67 times higher than those caused by malicious insiders.<sup>1</sup> Ernst and Young (2020) report that 20% of the threat actors behind confirmed breaches were insiders who had no malicious intent, while this number was around 12% for insiders with malicious intent. IBM Security (2020) reports that 23% of the root causes of data breaches were categorised as human error, while only about 3.5% of the data breaches were caused by malicious insider actions.<sup>2</sup> Given the importance and magnitude of non-malicious ISP violations, methods for reducing non-malicious violations of ISPs have attracted much attention (see Moody et al. (2018) and Cram et al. (2019) for an extensive review of the literature).

Groups provide a setting in which members can interact, relate, and perform their tasks, thus developing common experiences (Melnick & Woods, 1976). These experiences arise from sources, including the personal expectations of group members or the cues that exist in the group (Crews & Melnick, 1976). Further, to achieve group objectives, members often coordinate their collective eforts towards these objectives (Barker, 1993; Stewart et al., 2012). Ensuring information security can be considered as one of these collective eforts (Johnston et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2020) because information security failures can negatively impact the group (e.g., lower productivity, increase workloads) (Dourish & Anderson, 2006; Hsu et al., 2015; Da Veiga & Elof, 2010). One member’s failure to comply with an ISP may endanger the group’s ability to achieve its collective objectives (Hsu et al., 2015). Thus, the group as a whole can benefit when peers actively detect or prevent ISP violations.

Peer monitoring occurs when one’s peers, who may not have formal authority to influence the behaviour of others (in our case, ISP compliance), ensure that misconduct is corrected or even reported to management (De Jong & Elfring, 2010; Loughry & Tosi, 2008; Marks, 2001). Peer monitoring can be considered a prosocial organisational behaviour because it is voluntary; it requires extra personal time and efort, and it is carried out to promote the welfare of the group or organisation (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986; Dozier & Miceli, 1985; O’Reilly III & Chatman, 1986). The empirical investigation on peer monitoring in the context of information security behaviour could be relevant and could provide another viewpoint for understanding how to curb non-malicious ISP violations.<sup>3</sup>

When employees violate ISPs due to a lack of understanding of their mistakes and a lack of proper training, group members via peer monitoring may prevent violations or may guide their fellow members towards better compliance by helping them rectify any wrongdoings. Peer monitoring may also convey others’ expectations of compliance by altering employee perceptions using peer pressure. In other words, peer monitoring could be promising in reducing non-malicious ISP violations because it can identify and take advantage of opportunities to assist, rectify, and improve employees’ ISP-related behaviour. Thus, the first objective of this study is to investigate how peer monitoring influences an employee’s intention to violate an ISP. We suggest that peer monitoring in a group decreases employees’ intent to violate ISPs.

The second objective of this study is to understand how peer monitoring is shaped. Drawing upon the research on organisational social capital, this study proposes that collective responsibility and trust are the primary social capital components that shape peer monitoring.

Collective responsibility refers to employees’ perception of the extent to which they are held responsible for other group members’ misconduct due to their group association, whether they took part or not (Hamilton, 1978; Lickel et al., 2003; Sanders et al., 1996). Collective responsibility (a) emphasises a group’s shared responsibility for the common good, (b) creates a social context that aligns individual group members’ interests with the group’s interests, and (c) promotes peer monitoring as a result (Trevino & Victor, 1992).

Trust refers to employees’ willingness to be vulnerable to the actions of other group members, based on the shared expectation that they will perform specific actions important to the group, irrespective of their ability to control such actions (Breuer et al., 2016; Mayer et al., 1995). In a group with a stronger sense of trust, individuals (a) are more likely to seek options (e.g., peer monitoring) that enhance mutual interests (Weber, 1995) and (b) react more favourably to peer monitoring (c.f., Kelley & Michela, 1980). Thus, by influencing how group members perceive each other’s behaviour (Robinson, 1996), a strong sense of trust may not only facilitate peer monitoring but also alter their interpretation of the monitoring. As a result, a high level of trust could improve the efectiveness of peer monitoring in decreasing ISP violations.

We tested the proposed research model using data gathered from two waves of surveys conducted two weeks apart. These data were collected through the Qualtrics panel, with 254 valid responses received from working professionals in the United States. In the first wave, we measured trust, collective responsibility, and peer monitoring. In the second wave, we provided four ISP violation scenarios and measured employees’ intention to violate the ISP. Using two waves of surveys may reduce the impact of potential common method bias on the results (Salamon & Robinson, 2008). Our results suggest that peer monitoring reduces employee ISP violation intention. Trust positively moderates the efect of peer monitoring on employee intention to violate an ISP; this efect is stronger when the perception of trust is higher. Last, collective responsibility and trust both contribute to peer monitoring.

This study is among the first to investigate the efects of peer monitoring on reducing employee ISP violation intention. Peer monitoring emphasises that the employees themselves are a part of the solution for ISP violations. We recognise the importance of collective eforts in ensuring successful information security outcomes, consistent with recent studies in the information security literature (Johnston et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2020). Help from peers within a group may provide a complementary approach to organisationally wide programmes of deterrence for reducing ISP violations (D’Arcy & Herath, 2011; D’Arcy et al., 2009; Johnston et al., 2016; Willison et al., 2018).

Employees may change their behaviours based on the direct interventions of their peers. This is noteworthy as opposed to peer influence on ISP compliance in the form of a security-oriented climate, culture, or norms (e.g., D’Arcy & Herath, 2011; Guo et al., 2011; Hsu et al., 2015; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016) (see Appendix E for more details). Peer monitoring may influence team members’ ISP-related behaviours not only during the task engagement process but also via the expectations of compliance that are conveyed.<sup>4</sup> Peer monitoring may have an instant efect on individuals’ behaviour and thus reduce ISP violations on the spot. It may also have an efect by generating an expectation that ISP compliance is what others expect one should do.

Further, this study argues that collective responsibility prompts peer monitoring. Collective responsibility represents a social context that aligns the interests of the group with those of its members, providing an environment that encourages peer monitoring (Trevino & Victor, 1992). Contexts such as collective responsibility have not been empirically validated in the prior information security literature.

Finally, with the understanding that peer monitoring can be interpreted diferently, this study contributes to the literature by stating that trust not only facilities peer monitoring but also amplifies its efectiveness in discouraging employee ISP violation intention. This is important because the boundary conditions for the efectiveness of peer monitoring in reducing employee ISP violations have not been investigated.

## 2. Literature review

The information security literature provides various explanations for how social influence changes employees’ ISP compliance and violations. Most studies have examined the role of social influence through the lens of social norms, in which the behaviours and expectations of peers and important group members regarding an ISP are significant. For example, an employee’s perception of how peers behave has been often measured by constructs such as descriptive norms and co-worker behaviour (Anderson & Agarwal, 2010; Cheng et al., 2013; D’Arcy & Lowry, 2019; Herath & Rao, 2009b, 2009a; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016). An employee’s perception of what co-workers (including the important ones) think they should do has been captured through subjective norms and normative beliefs (e.g., Bélanger et al., 2017; Bulgurcu et al., 2010; D’Arcy & Lowry, 2019; Siponen et al., 2014; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016). In addition, some researchers have examined social influence phenomena through the lens of employees’ shared perceptions of the procedures, practices, and policies in their organisation, as well as employees’ shared beliefs and values that are deeply embedded in the organisation’s environment and structure (see Chan et al. (2005) for further discussion) (please see Appendix E, Table E.1, for the full list of studies).

The above-mentioned studies are examples of social influence that occurs when employees change their ISP-related behaviours based on their perception of their peers’ behaviours and expectations. This type of social influence changes one’s behaviour rather passively. Similarly, peer monitoring can passively influence ISP compliance by generating an expectation that ISP compliance is what others think and expect one should do – as members go out of their way to ensure ISP compliance via peer monitoring.

In addition, social influence can occur when employees change their ISP-related behaviours as a result of direct interaction with their peers. Such social influence changes one’s behaviour not only passively but also actively. For instance, Warkentin et al. (2011) show that factors such as verbal persuasion contribute to an informal learning process, enhance employees’ self-eficacy, and in turn increase employees’ intention to comply with information privacy policies. In addition, it has been shown that employees’ involvement in organisations’ security issues, employee participation in meetings that foster organisational ISPs, and employee engagement with peers in the area of information security issues enhances attitudes towards ISPs and strengthens employees’ intention to comply with ISPs (Ifinedo, 2014, 2018). Moreover, the efectiveness of ISPs is significantly improved by employees’ extra-role behaviours, which consist of guiding peers in learning and following ISPs (i.e., helping) or ofering comments to enhance information security operations (i.e., voicing) (Hsu et al., 2015). Finally, employees are more willing to report computer abuse or non-compliance when users are encouraged to report computer abuse and when an anonymous reporting procedure is in place. As a result, information security is enhanced overall (LowrY et al., 2013).

Peer monitoring can help a group achieve its desired goals. Peers can promptly handle or resolve wrongdoings and thus encourage appropriate behaviour (e.g., ISP compliance) (c.f., Loughry, 2001). In other words, peer monitoring can help the group actively steer its members towards ISP compliance, since members rectify and prevent each other’s ISP wrongdoings. In line with this view, the information security literature (see Appendix E, Table E.1) reveals that peer monitoring can be related to (a) helping (i.e., guiding or assisting peers), (b) voicing (i.e., ofering comments to enhance information security operations) (Hsu et al., 2015), (c) verbal persuasion (i.e., receiving feedback from others) (Warkentin et al., 2011), (d) sharing security knowledge or advice (Dang-Pham et al., 2017b, 2017a; Safa & Von Solms, 2016; Safa et al., 2015), and (e) whistleblowing (Keil et al., 2018; LowrY et al., 2013).

Organizational deterrence may be complemented by peer monitoring in reducing ISP violations. Organizational deterrence postulates that individuals will abstain from ISP violations if they perceive that the punishment for their violations is severe and certain (Nagin & Pogarsky, 2001; Straub, 1990). This assumption has led to the establishment of strategies that emphasise the risk of being caught and punished in order to compel employees to abstain from ISP violations (D’Arcy & Herath, 2011; D’Arcy et al., 2009; Johnston et al., 2016; H Li et al., 2018; Silic et al., 2017; Willison et al., 2018). In contrast to approaches based on organisational deterrence, peer monitoring emphasises that employees’ interests in a group are aligned, and employees may help each other because ISP violations may harm group interests.

Peer monitoring occurs within a group, making it unique in various ways. Peer monitoring can be more efective, more accurate, and better localised in adapting to group dynamics. This may not be the case for deterrence, which views violations in a broad form not tailored to group-specific tasks. Peer monitoring is more likely to be efective in helping the group identify wrongdoings in a timely manner, ofer corrections, or focus on prevention (Barron & Gjerde, 1997; Tyler, 2003). This may be because members in the group have knowledge and understanding of their peers specific tasks (Welbourne & Ferrante, 2008), since they have similar training and experience. Therefore, they can better assess their peers’ behaviour (Barron & Gjerde, 1997; Tyler, 2003). Also, given that peer monitoring occurs in real time, it can prevent or remedy concerns regarding violations without delay (Loughry, 2001). On the other hand, deterrence approaches often involve a time lag in detecting employee wrongdoings, making it challenging for the organisation to manage or prevent the potential damage of an ISP violation.

It is noteworthy to mention that prior studies show that peer monitoring enhances employee performance (Langfred, 2004; Marks & Panzer, 2004) while reducing employee theft (Hollinger & Clark, 1983), social loafing (Harkins & Petty, 1982), free riding (Wageman & Baker, 1997), escalation of commitment (Kirby & Davis, 1998), and accounting fraud (Merchant, 1985; Towry, 2003). This study examines how peer monitoring is motivated and how it discourages employee ISP violations.

## 3. Theoretical background

Peer monitoring consists of three key actions: peer noticing, peer correcting, and peer reporting. Peer noticing refers to employees noticing their colleagues behaviour to ensure that they follow the rules as expected. Peer reporting refers to employees reporting their colleagues’ wrongdoings to higher authorities (i.e., management). Peer correcting refers to employees correcting their colleagues’ wrongdoings (Loughry & Tosi, 2008). Because peers generally understand other employees’ tasks and observe their behaviour in real time, they are well-equipped to ofer guidance and correction (Fama & Jensen, 1983; Holmström, 1982; Loughry & Tosi, 2008). Peer correcting can be considered a way of helping co-workers with job-related matters (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986; Smith et al., 1983). Peer reporting can be classified as a way to protect the organisation (i.e., civic virtue). For instance, an employee reporting ISP violations may seek to protect organisational information assets and, in the long run, the organisation and its members (Dozier & Miceli, 1985; Podsakof, 2000; Smith et al., 1983). Such actions may be viewed as an extra role that often exceeds the requirements of a specific role (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986) and involves additional personal time and efort (e.g., assisting peers, reporting misconduct) (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986; O’Reilly III & Chatman, 1986).

Peer monitoring occurs during task engagement. Peers’ active participation and immediate intervention during employees’ task engagement is what makes peer monitoring an active type of social influ ence. The efect of peer monitoring can also be achieved through the expectations conveyed via the mutual interactions among group members (c.f., Stewart et al. (2012) for review). Because of its interactive nature, peer monitoring may help shape shared beliefs regarding what is valued in the group and how group members handle their tasks. Peer monitoring occurs when members notice what their peers are doing and let them know whether their actions are accepted (Manz & Sims, 1987). It may act as a coordination force to ensure that group efort (i.e., collective action) is aimed in the right direction (Stewart et al., 2012). It forges a shared understanding of what constitutes an accepted behaviour among members, requiring them to adhere to this understanding (Loughry, 2010; Ouchi, 1979). Employees in groups that engage in peer monitoring may experience peer pressure to pursue group interests in order to be accepted by other group members (Stewart et al., 2012). Thus, peer monitoring may strengthen employees’ consideration of the group and may lead them to act according to the group’s interests (Cravens et al., 2004; Loughry, 2010; Mcgrath, 2001; Thompson, 1967). In sum, peer monitoring conveys that ISP compliance is what other group members think one should do and that peer monitoring is expected regardless of whether the behaviour is observable (c.f., Stewart et al., 2012).

## 3.1. Social capital

It is pivotal to recognise social capital in a group to understand the motivation behind peer monitoring (Costa et al., 2009; Moreland & Levine, 2002). Social capital refers to a resource that reflects the nature of social relations within a group, as perceived through members’ levels of collective goals and shared (i.e., mutual) trust (Johnston et al., 2019; Leana & Van Buren, 1999). Its primary components are group members’ tendency to subordinate their individual goals to achieve the group’s goals and group members inclination to be vulnerable to the actions of their peers with the expectation that their peers will not exploit these vulnerabilities (Leana & Van Buren, 1999).

To ensure that member eforts are directed towards achieving collective goals, there is a need to establish norms or systems for holding one another accountable (Leana & Van Buren, 1999; Trevino & Victor, 1992). Group members’ ability to interact, and their tendency to subordinate their individual goals to collective goals, suggests that social capital incorporates the idea of collectivism (i.e., benefiting the group) (Leana & Van Buren, 1999). Similarly, collective responsibility emphasises collectivism by creating a reward structure that aligns individual group members’ interests with the group’s interests (Lickel et al., 2003; Trevino & Victor, 1992). As such, social capital can be represented by collective responsibility.

Trust establishes a set of agreed-upon values among group members that allow cooperation among them (De Jong & Elfring, 2010; Shapiro, 1987). This can be attained without relying on the knowledge of any particular member but rather by relying on behaviours that can be generalised to the group members as a whole (De Jong & Elfring, 2010; Putnam, 1993). It can be viewed as a sign of confidence among individuals (i.e., the group members) such that one party’s actions will not harm or put another party at risk, and no party will exploit another’s vulnerabilities (Jones & George, 1998; Sabel, 1993). Accordingly, trust facilitates collective actions such as peer monitoring (Coleman, 1990) by coordinating expectations, interactions, and behaviours within the group (Costa et al., 2009).

## 3.1.1. Collective responsibility

Peer monitoring can be instigated by collective responsibility. This is because in a group with a sense of collective responsibility, group members have an incentive to take actions such as monitoring fellow members to ensure their actions are aligned with group interests (Olson & Maio, 2003). The idea of joint liability has been widely investigated in the context of borrowing groups with high levels of relational capital (Besley & Coate, 1995; Cassar et al., 2007). It has been shown that joint liability lending promotes monitoring, since members try to ensure their peers repay their shares in order to prevent punishment (Ghatak & Guinnane, 1999).

In an organisational context, the social association, in the form of task groups (i.e., co-workers), creates such joint liability (Denson et al., 2006; Lickel, 2000; Lickel et al., 2003). Accordingly, collective responsibility may encourage peer monitoring when (a) it is perceived that one person’s misconduct threatens the interests of the other members or (b) it is perceived that any group member is responsible for the misconduct of other members (Trevino & Victor, 1992).

Individuals may opt to benefit the group to maximise their own interests (Olson & Maio, 2003). However, individuals may risk their own interests in the long run if they ignore the group’s needs.

As a real-world example, politicians and social activists often try to motivate individuals to act prosocially by appealing to their self-interest. They may encourage people to act pro-environmentally (e.g., using public transportation) by warning that failure to respond to such societal needs may have a negative efect on both themselves and their children (e.g., global warming, air pollution).

## 3.1.2. Trust

Trust arises from individual members’ experiences, and it is often generated through collective sensemaking of shared perceptions and experiences (De Jong & Elfring, 2010). In the absence of clear expectations of behaviour, engaging in peer monitoring can be risky because co-workers may perceive peer monitoring as pejorative. Trust establishes that the goal of peer monitoring is mutual benefit, thus it eliminates the ambiguity of intent beyond peer monitoring (Kreuter & Lezin, 2002). In groups with a high level of trust, members are more likely to take part in constructive interactions (e.g., peer monitoring) that contribute to group objectives (e.g., fewer ISP violations) (Jones & George, 1998; Spreitzer & Mishra, 1999). On the contrary, when the perception of trust is low in a group, group members tend to avoid constructive interactions (e.g., peer monitoring) to protect themselves from perceived vulnerability to the actions of other members (Dirks, 1999; Mayer & Gavin, 2005). Therefore, they do not contribute to group goals (e.g., they refrain from peer monitoring) (De Jong & Elfring, 2010).

In fact, trust can contribute to peer monitoring in two ways. First, trust is often considered an indicator of confidence (Jones & George, 1998; Sabel, 1993). Therefore, in a group with strong levels of trust, peer monitoring is more likely to be perceived positively as an act that benefits the group and its members. In such a group, members rely on their peers with confidence, knowing that their peers will not abuse their vulnerability (Barney & Hansen, 1994) even in scenarios that ofer an opportunity for personal gain (Krishnan et al., 2016). In addition, such a group communicates that the threat of opportunism is minimal, so employees take it upon themselves to ensure that others’ actions align with the group’s common goals (e.g., engaging in peer monitoring).

Second, such a group signals that employees look after each other and their organisation, which then positions peer monitoring as a constructive act rather than ill intended (Uzzi, 1997). In such a group, members interpret each other’s ambiguous actions constructively (Mcevily et al., 2003) because members have a bilateral expectation that the actions of their peers will serve the interests of all the members of the group. This is why prior research shows that a group with an environment of trust is more suitable for the promotion of the extra-role behaviours (e.g., peer monitoring) required to achieve the group’s common goals (Creed et al., 1996; Oliver, 1997; Powell, 1990).

Trust can alter the perception of peer monitoring, and this can change employees’ responses. Peer monitoring may be perceived as pejorative. Trust shapes how the risk in the relationship is assumed by group members, influencing their risk-taking (Luhmann, 1988). It generates a set of expectations among group members, enabling them to better deal with the uncertainty or risk associated with peer monitoring (Jones & George, 1998). It undermines the negative interpretations of peer monitoring that indicate a lack of trust among group members. It also fosters dedication towards achieving the group’s common goals (e.g., ISP compliance) (Bijlsma-Frankema & Costa, 2005).

A group with more trust provides cues that influence the interpretation (via social information processing) of work-related behaviours and events (e.g., peer monitoring) (Salancik & Pfefer, 1978; Shamir & Lapidot, 2003). Because of trust, the actions of group members are most likely to be received as beneficial, irrespective of the resentment that may result from these actions (Gambetta, 1988). In addition, others may be considered trustworthy even if rational thinking would suggest the opposite (De Jong & Elfring, 2010; Mollering, 2001). Thus, trust within a group indicates that peer monitoring protects the group’s common interests, highlighting that peer monitoring is benign and acceptable (Bijlsma-Frankema & Costa, 2005).

## 4. Hypothesis development

## 4.1. Peer monitoring

Peer monitoring can compel members to adopt behaviours expected by the group (Cooper & Withey, 2009; Meyeret al., 2010; De Jong et al., 2014), such as following ISPs. First, peer monitoring can act as an implicit coordination mechanism for appropriate ISP-related behaviours. Relevant information or feedback is communicated (without request) via this mechanism, so help is ofered proactively, activities are regularly supervised, and actions can be adjusted to meet group expectations (Rico et al., 2008). This leads to a common interpretation that following the ISP is an accepted and expected behaviour (Bowen & Ostrof, 2004).

Second, those who engage in peer monitoring signify the importance of following ISPs by demonstrating that they are willing to spend extra time and efort to ensure ISP compliance. Third, peer monitoring among members may enhance the perception that any violation of the ISP will be observed and reported by their peers. This creates a perception that violators are more likely to be caught (Greenberger et al., 1987; Victor et al., 1993) and face potential punishment.

Finally, through peer monitoring, accepted ISP compliance behaviour could be communicated to members who do not comply, and accountability will be heightened (Loughry, 2010). Research suggests that peer monitoring may increase employees’ tendency to act in support of group decisions and interests. For example, peer monitoring may discourage employees from jeopardising information assets through ISP violations (Meyer & Allen, 1984; O’Reilly III & Chatman, 1986; Posey et al., 2016). Accordingly, we hypothesise:

$\mathbf { H } _ { \mathrm { 1 : } }$ Peer monitoring is negatively associated with employee intention to violate an ISP.

## 4.2. Trust

Stronger trust among group members may facilitate risk-taking behaviours, such as open dialogue about mistakes, sharing feedback, or helping behaviours. It can direct group members’ attention towards the needs and goals of other members as well as those of the group in general. This enhances their willingness to protect the integrity of information assets through peer monitoring. Such a positive redirection of attention boosts the perception that when it comes to information security, group members are indeed looking out for the interests of the group and its members. This creates an environment that ofers moral support to peer monitoring of ISP compliance behaviour. In fact, members in this environment may expect their peers to react in a similar manner (e.g., peer monitoring) because each group member assumes that her peers are acting in her best interests (Dirks, 1999). In this view, trust signals the acceptability of peer monitoring, so it is a force that directs a group’s attention towards furthering the goals of the group (Dirks, 1999). This is theoretically supported (c.f., task motivation theory by Kanfer (1990)). Similarly, the information security literature<sup>5</sup> has acknowledged the vital role of trust in facilitating extra-role behaviours such as knowledge sharing (Safa et al., 2015; Tamjidyamcholo et al., 2013). In fact, it has been stated that employees prefer discussing security matters with co-workers they trust (Dang-Pham et al., 2017a, 2017b).

It is noteworthy that prior studies have also supported the notion that trust facilitates peer monitoring and enhances its efectiveness (e.g., Mcintyre & Salas, 1995; Salas et al., 2005). From their perspective, peer monitoring is an indicator of interest and concern because employees diligently follow their peers’ progress towards their collective goals (Bijlsma-Frankema & Costa, 2005). They label trust as a feed forward phenomenon, which generates a desire to contribute to a shared goal; and they view peer monitoring as a feed back phenomenon that enables the fine-tuning of group members’ behaviours (Bijlsma-Frankema et al., 2008). Accordingly, we hypothesise:

## $\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { : } }$ Trust is positively associated with peer monitoring.

We propose that the efect of peer monitoring on employee intention to violate ISPs can be a function of the perception of trust within the group. Strong trust communicates that group members emphasise confidence, friendship, and caring (Jones & George, 1998; Sabel, 1993; Uzzi, 1997). Employees with such a perception are more likely to understand and respond favourably to peer monitoring by complying with the ISP. The rationale is that employees who have a positive perception may conclude that their peers are looking out for them rather than trying to control them (Bijlsma-Frankema et al., 2008). On the other hand, given a lack of confidence and caring in the group, employees may interpret peer monitoring negatively as an inappropriate act, thus they may react adversely by ignoring or contradicting the ISP. Accordingly, we hypothesise:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } { \mathrm { : } }$ : Trust negatively moderates the efect of peer monitoring on employees’ intention to violate an ISP such that this efect is stronger in an environment with more trust.

## 4.3. Collective responsibility

In the context of this study, collective responsibility indicates that in the case of an information security incident caused by ISP violations, each group member believes she may be held responsible regardless of who committed the violations (Denson et al., 2006; Lickel et al., 2003; May, 1987). The perception is that (a) any ISP violations may imperil the interests of the group and its members, and (b) all members will be held responsible in case of any ISP violations. Collective responsibility generates an environment in which peer monitoring can be seen as a solution to prevent blame as well as to serve the group and its members. Accordingly, collective responsibility encourages peer monitoring by aligning the interests of group members with those of the group as a whole.

In groups that perceive ISP violations as having no impact on the group members’ welfare, group members may not show interest in actions such as peer monitoring to prevent or remedy such violations (Miceli & Near, 1984; Trevino & Victor, 1992). Thus, to set peer monitoring in motion, groups must create an environment that encourages members to spend extra time and efort (e.g., peer monitoring) to ensure they will be not held responsible for the ISP violations of others (Mccallum et al., 1985; Trevino & Victor, 1992). Collective responsibility can drive peer monitoring by aligning the interests of group members with those of the group as a whole (Kandel & Lazear, 1992; Welbourne et al., 1995). Thus, we expect that collective responsibility will lead employees to conclude they have a responsibility to monitor others’ ISP-related actions to protect themselves, which in turn protects the group. Accordingly, we hypothesise:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } \mathbf { : }$ Collective responsibility is positively associated with peer monitoring.

## 5. Research methodology, analysis, and results

We use two waves of surveys conducted two weeks apart to collect data and test our research model. Such a survey program enables us to establish a temporal relationship between peer monitoring and employee intention to violate an ISP. In addition, this design is efective in reducing the common method bias (Burton-Jones, 2009; Salamon & Robinson, 2008), especially when the measures are internally oriented (e.g., perception of ISP violations) (Rindfleisch et al., 2008). We use structural equation modelling to test the research model. We used SPSS AMOS 24.0 to analyse the data. The steps of our data collection, measurement development, construct validation, and model testing are described below.

## 5.1. Data collection

To ensure the external validity of our results, we recruited participants from the Qualtrics panel of U.S. employees. To ensure the right respondents were targeted, the screening questions asked participants up front whether they had a full-time job. For those who did, we then provided the definition of information security policy along with illustrative examples. Next, we filtered out participants whose organisations did not have an established ISP, as well as participants whose jobs did not require ISP compliance. Finally, we asked participants whether they worked in a group such as a team, department, or work unit; we filtered out participants whose job did not require working in a group.

In the first wave of the survey, we asked individuals about their perception of trust, collective responsibility, and peer monitoring in their group, as well as deterrence in their organisation. We conducted the second wave two weeks later. We provided participants with four ISP violation scenarios, then we asked them whether they act the same way as the subject in the scenario. We collected 254 valid responses (refer to Appendix A, Table A.1, for a summary of the sample demographics).

## 5.2. Measures

We adopted pre-existing instruments from prior research, and we made any necessary adjustments to tailor them to our research context. All the key constructs in the model were measured using multiple items. We chose scenarios to measure ISP violation intention. This method was selected for the following reasons. First, due to social desirability bias, it is often dificult to measure actual violations by asking participants directly (Barlow et al., 2018). In the information security literature, the use of scenarios has become a standard for accessing ISP violation intention (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2009; Hu et al., 2011; Siponen & Vance, 2010). Scenarios provide a context in which participants can report whether they would act in the same manner as a character in the scenario (Trevino, 1992). Thus, scenarios eliminate any feelings of accusation while capturing intentions indirectly (Crossler et al., 2013; Vance et al., 2015). In other words, scenarios provide researchers the means to capture realistic responses about sensitive issues (e.g., ISP violations) in a way that is not intimidating (Guo & Yuan, 2012). Second, scenarios ofer a way to enrich the realism of decision-making situations by providing contextual detail while simultaneously ensuring that these details are uniform across respondents (Siponen & Vance, 2010).

We developed these scenarios in two phases. First, we reviewed the literature to identify the most relevant information security violations (see Appendix F, Table F.1, for a detailed review of these studies). We identified the following types of common ISP violations: password sharing, password write-down, copying sensitive data to an insecure USB device, failure to logof a workstation, unauthorised access to company data, and the installation and use of unauthorised software.

Second, we presented the identified scenarios to a panel of information security experts, including five faculty members who specialise in information security research, five information security professionals, and five MBA students familiar with ISPs. This panel identified the four types of common ISP violations that were the most relevant to our context: password write-down, copying sensitive data to an insecure USB device, failure to logof a workstation, and installation and use of unauthorised software. To depict these behaviours, we adapted the scenarios employed by D’Arcy et al. (2014) and Guo and Yuan (2012), which were developed based on guidelines for IS security field surveys (Siponen & Vance, 2013). In addition, following the feedback of the expert panel, we revised the survey questions and scenarios to make them more contextual and more realistic. Third, after the initial scenario development process was completed, we conducted a small pilot study of a convenient sample (i.e., 50 graduate students) to ensure the discriminant and convergent validity. In addition, we further revised the wording of survey questions based on the feedback we received from the pilot study.

Keeping with our conceptualisation of peer monitoring, we adopted direct peer monitoring items from Loughry and Tosi (2008) and De Jong and Elfring (2010). We chose items applicable to the context of ISP compliance (i.e., peer noticing, peer correcting, and peer reporting). We then modified their wording to correspond to an information security context. For example, we used the phrases “complying with the ISP” or “ISP compliance” to replace descriptions of group performance outcomes such as “completes their work on time”.

In line with the definition proposed by Jarvis et al. (2003), we conceptualise peer monitoring as a second-order reflective construct consisting of a set of observable phenomena (i.e., peer noticing, peer reporting, and peer correcting), all of which reflect the underlying concept of peer monitoring. We adapted items for collective responsibility from Denson et al. (2006) and Lickel et al. (2003), while we adopted items for trust from Langfred (2004). Originally, these constructs were conceptualised to capture the perception of individuals in cases of an incident or a negative act. We categorise ISP violations as a negative act, and we reworded the items accordingly. All the items were measured on 7-point Likert scales. Our expert panel examined the measurement items to ensure their clarity and content validity (refer to Appendix A, Table A.2, for the list of items).

We should note that this study seeks to model employee ISP-related behaviour through their perceptions of the level of peer monitoring, collective responsibility, and trust in their respective groups. The dependent variable is an individual-level variable, although the theoretical justification for the relationships proposed in our research model corresponds to group-level theories. Therefore, it is appropriate to rely on the perceptions of a single individual from within a group and not require collective responses from all group members.

It is noteworthy to mention that we measure peer monitoring independently from the scenarios. At the beginning of the surveys, we provided the definition of ISP as well as examples. Participants responded to peer monitoring items before they read any scenario. In other words, our model proposes that the existence of peer monitoring in a group discourages ISP violations regardless of the type of violation (see Appendix C for more discussion).

## 5.5. Post hoc analysis

Peer monitoring may require the observability of violation behaviours. Group members cannot monitor each other when they cannot observe wrongdoing. Higher observability allows for a more reliable and valid assessment of peers’ behaviour, and it discourages detrimental behaviour (Reilly & Chao, 1982). As a result, one can conclude that peer monitoring could be more efective in managing violations that are more observable.

We conducted a short survey to test for variations in the observability of diferent violations to confirm the results of the proposed model for each scenario. We asked a new set of participants about the perceived observability of ISP violations (as described in each scenario) by others in their workgroup. The results suggest that writing down passwords and failing to logof a computer are easier to observe than installing unauthorised software or copying sensitive data to portable media such as USB drives.

We find no significant diference in observability between writing down passwords and not logging of a computer, nor between copying sensitive data to portable media and the installation of unauthorised software (see Appendix C, Tables C.1 and C.2, for a detailed analysis). Then, we conduct a regression for the more observable cases by combining the password write-down scenarios and log-of failure scenarios. We conduct another regression for the less observable cases by combining USB drive scenarios and unauthorised software scenarios.

The results present two interesting insights. First, peer monitoring has a stronger impact on reducing employee intention to violate ISPs for more observable violations. This suggests that the efect of peer monitoring is more substantial for behaviours with higher observability. Second, peer monitoring reduces employee intention to violate ISPs for all the scenarios. These results may indicate that peer monitoring discourages ISP non-compliance both actively and passively. In sum, we find that peer monitoring works in all scenarios, but it may be most efective in the more observable scenarios. This is consistent with the arguments proposed in this paper. We describe the results further in Appendix C, Table C3.

Our survey sample includes more females than males. To alleviate concerns that the unbalanced gender composition may skew the results, we conduct a separate analysis by applying the research model for each gender in addition to controlling for the efect of gender (which is shown to be small and insignificant). The results show no diference in the path coeficient (peer monitoring ≥ intention to violate ISP) between females and males (see Appendix D, Table D.1).

## 6. Discussion

This study empirically tests the role of peer monitoring, its antecedents, and its working mechanisms in light of the growing interest in finding socio-technical approaches to efectively discourage employee ISP violations. Our results confirm that peer monitoring is efective in mitigating employee intention to violate ISPs. Our results are consistent with previous studies showing that peer monitoring is a possible solution to mitigate co-workers’ deviant behaviour (e.g., social loafing, free riding) (Harkins & Petty, 1982; Wageman & Baker, 1997) and ensure employee productivity (Langfred, 2004; Marks & Panzer, 2004).

Further, our results show that a group’s social capital plays an important role in employees’ engagement in peer monitoring. We confirm that peer monitoring is more likely in a group environment where employees trust each other. This is consistent with previous studies showing that active approaches (e.g., knowledge sharing) are impossible in the absence of trust among co-workers (Dang-Pham et al., 2017a, 2017b; Safa et al., 2015). Finally, our results show that collective responsibility encourages peer monitoring. This is in line with the notion that efective information security prevails in an environment where employees recognise that ensuring information security is each member’s responsibility (Knapp et al., 2006; Stanton & Stam, 2006; Van Niekerk & Von Solms, 2010; Da Veiga & Elof, 2010).

## 6.1. Contributions to theory

This study contributes to the information security literature in various ways. First, some studies hint at the importance of peer monitoring (Colwill, 2009; Hsu et al., 2015; Williams, 2008), but these studies have not conceptualised peer monitoring nor systematically examined its impact and formation. Our study bridges this literature gap by suggesting that peer monitoring could efectively reduce employee intention to violate ISPs. In addition, our study supplements prior literature on social norms and group environments, which mostly assumes that employees comply with ISPs due to the expectations of their important others. Unlike these studies on the normative influence of peers and group environments, we focus on peer monitoring, which could help prevent ISP ofences both at the moment of occurrence (Straub, 1990) and by creating expectations of compliance and peer monitoring regardless of the observability of the behaviour.

Second, peer monitoring requires considerable efort from an individual to act upon any observed deviation from an ISP. Therefore, it seems irrational that individuals voluntarily contribute their time and efort to peer monitoring, especially if it is not rewarding (Costa, 2003; De Jong & Dirks, 2012). This study suggests that extant social capital elements (e.g., trust, collective responsibility) can prompt peer monitoring.

Trust facilitates peer monitoring and enhances its efect by signalling that group members are trustworthy (Mayer et al., 1995) and their actions are not opportunistic. Our results show that peer monitoring is more likely in groups that have a strong sense of trust. This gives a new perspective on the role of trust, which has not been well investigated in the context of ISP compliance. Trust indicates a covenantal relationship, cooperation, social exchange, and group dedication to achieving a shared goal (Caldwell & Karri, 2005). It signifies that employees are expected to help and care for those who put their trust in them in the form of responsible behaviour (Jones & George, 1998).

Our results also contribute to theory by confirming the contextual role of trust. We show that trust among group members prepares the groundwork for efective peer monitoring. To the best of our knowledge, the contingency role of trust has not been well investigated in the context of ISP compliance. This is important because peer monitoring may be perceived as pejorative among peers (Langfred, 2004; Mcallister, 1995). Trust among members could alleviate these concerns about the intention behind peer monitoring because it shows a covenantal relationship (Caldwell & Karri, 2005).

Further, it has been suggested that social contexts that promote each group members’ wrongdoing may threaten the group, thus its members’ interests motivate employees to engage in peer monitoring to protect themselves and their group (Victor et al., 1993). Though prior studies imply the potential importance of peer monitoring, studies on group contexts that facilitate such behaviour have not been conducted. Our results suggest that the perception of collective responsibility encourages members to help and monitor each other. This is important, as previous studies have not explicitly considered the notion that one member’s ISP wrongdoing could adversely afect other group members by jeopardising their job or job performance. This study provides a foundation for considering the efect of ISP non-compliance on the group, and it posits that peer monitoring can help groups achieve their compliance goals.

This study contributes to the information security literature by considering the role that the group and the dynamic between members play in enhancing ISP compliance. It puts forward peer monitoring as a group-based tailored solution in which peers can ensure that workarounds (e.g., possible ISP violations) specific to the group are detected and rectified (e.g., Schaefer & Welker, 1994). This is important, as it has been noted that at the workgroup level, ISP requirements are sometimes antithetical to employees’ practical urges (Y Li et al., 2019). Groups often convey to employees that it is their responsibility to further group goals (e.g., ensuring ISP compliance).

## 6.2. Practical implications

This study explores the efectiveness of peer monitoring, and it ofers several implications for practitioners. First, our results may help managers choose more appropriate interventions based on a better understanding of the relationships between peer monitoring and employee ISP violation intention. For example, organisations are naturally inclined to ramp up surveillance or increase restrictions on information system usage in response to concerns about employee ISP violations. The result of this study suggests that peer monitoring discourages employee ISP violation intention. Peer monitoring might be preferable for two reasons. First, it is less costly because the employees themselves do the work of peer monitoring while completing their tasks, unlike other methods that rely on other agents (Fama & Jensen, 1983; Loughry & Tosi, 2008). Second, peer monitoring captures a broad range of information regarding employees behaviour, which is typically dificult for other methods (Fischer & Hughes, 1997; Trevino & Victor, 1992).

These features have promising implications. Recent reports have indicated that, in many cases, it is dificult and time-consuming for surveillance systems to detect ISP non-compliance (Cappelli et al., 2012; Verizon, 2016). For instance, a hospital computer monitoring system could not easily identify a doctor who repeatedly takes photos of patient profiles using a cell phone (i.e., ISP non-compliance), or it may be dificult for surveillance systems to detect co-workers using each other’s passwords. Moreover, this study suggests that, because peer monitoring consists of immediate intervention (e.g., verbal interaction), it can enhance employees’ understanding of accepted ISP compliance behaviours. For instance, when a group member notices that her peer has written his personal password on a sticky note, she can intervene by explaining the correct practice regarding passwords. This can enhance employees’ understanding of acceptable password policies and thus steer their decisions towards ISP compliance.

Second, peer monitoring, as an extra-role behaviour, cannot be mandated or imposed by the direct intervention of the organisation. Consistent with our results, we suggest that organisations invest in creating greater collective responsibility and trust among group members to promote peer monitoring. There are two promising paths that highlight the importance of collective responsibility and building a culture of trust to help employees engage in peer monitoring.

For example, organisations can emphasise the fact that protecting their information assets is in every employee’s best interests, and even one employee’s negligence can damage the organisation and its employees’ interests and well-being. Thus, one practical approach could be to raise awareness of the potential impacts of security incidents on the group as a whole and thus increase the sense of collective responsibility among the group members. Organizations could educate employees that any information security incident could harm everyone in the group (Victor et al., 1993). The current evidence in information security suggests that organisational data breaches often cause adverse consequences not only for the wrongdoers who caused the data breach but also for other employees because they are subjected to its detrimental impacts (e.g., increased workloads, bad publicity, subpar financial performance, bankruptcy). Thus, employees who did not directly contribute to the information security incidents could still be victims of such an incident. For example, last year, the American Medical Collection Agency (AMCA) of New York filed for bankruptcy protection after a catastrophic data breach that afected at least 20 million U.S. citizens, after citing a severe drop in their business.<sup>6</sup>

In addition, employees’ experiences at work influence their evaluation of risk and vulnerability, which in turn influences their degree of trust. Therefore, organisations should engage in trust-building activities (c.f., Morgan & Zefane, 2003) so employees view peer monitoring not as a sign of distrust or as an act of ill-intent but rather as a sign of care and civic virtue. Trust-building is an interactive process that involves at least two individuals who determine each other’s level trustworthiness. Trust could be built when group members are involved in various ways, including participation, decision-making, and empowerment in various contexts (e.g., information security) (Morgan & Zefane, 2003). Thus, activities that require frequent interactions (e.g., work retreats, team-building exercises, department lunches) could serve as efective team-building exercises. To be more specific, groups can have meetings in which members discuss their ISP and provide recommendations. Further, creating an open culture in which individuals can have constructive and open dialogues, judgements, or debates could enhance involvement and thus contribute to group trust-building.

## 6.3. Future studies and limitations

There are a number of limitations that we may overcome in future studies. First, the proposed model in this study explains only 16% of the variance in employee ISP compliance and 32% of the variance in peer monitoring. Our model could be regarded as suitable and substantial because it explains more than 10% of the endogenous constructs suggested as an acceptable value of $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ (Falk & Miller, 1992) and used as a guideline in many information security research (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Guo et al., 2011; Ng et al., 2009; Peace et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2009, 2011). However, our model clearly does not consider other contributing factors that future studies can investigate further. A possible extension of the present research model could investigate how various task characteristics such as task visibility, interdependence, and routineness (Jones, 1984) can alter the success of monitoring mechanisms (Kirsch, 1996).

Although our findings set us on the path to determining causality, future studies may use longitudinal and controlled laboratory experiments to test the relationship between trust and peer monitoring. Due to the nature of cross-sectional data, our study does not provide the temporal ordering of how these constructs are realised.

It is noteworthy that the scenarios used in this study do not include all possible types of ISP violations, although we employed the scenarios that are common in industry reports and research studies. In addition, these scenarios, despite being common, do not provide a certain insight into malicious security violations (e.g., sabotage, stealing) because such violations deliberately occur in secret. Future studies could investigate more ISP violation scenarios and verify the generalisability of these findings. Moreover, although we took various measures to prevent bias in responses (e.g., using hypothetical scenarios, ensuring the anonymity of the survey participants), there is still a possibility that social desirability bias has skewed the responses towards reporting less ISP violation intention (e.g., Chen et al., 2013). Thus, we suggest that future studies employ approaches in which employee ISP violations are measured objectively.

For our analysis, we created a composite measure for Intention to Violate ISP by aggregating (summing) the items across the four scenarios following prior studies (D’Arcy & Devaraj, 2012; Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012) (see Appendix C). An alternative method to analyse such a dataset is to use multilevel analyses to consider (a) individuals’ responses to scenarios as the repeated measurement of intention and (b) the fixed efects of scenarios and the random efects of individuals. Future studies can further explore this topic using such methods.

In addition, our data are gathered from various industries (e.g., financial services, government). However, industry-specific features could impact the generalisability of our results.<sup>7</sup> For instance, healthcare professionals might be more sensitive to peer monitoring due to certain specific healthcare practices and because they are regulated by specific policies (e.g., the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)). Future studies could compare the efect of peer monitoring on the intention to violate ISPs in various industries. In addition, the majority of our respondents (69%) worked in the following industries: healthcare (20%), government (13%), retail (12%), finance (9%), manufacturing (8%), and information technology (7%). And the number of men is half that of women. We recommend that these results be interpreted with caution.

Our model has not accounted for the potential role that culture (Eastern vs. Western) could play in influencing the perception of collective responsibility, trust, and peer monitoring. We recommend that the generalisability of our results be inferred with caution. We suggest that future studies investigate the role of culture. In addition, given that the observability of a behaviour can play a role in the efectiveness of peer monitoring, future studies of ISP violation and compliance should further explore the role of observability.

We should acknowledge that although higher education was shown to contribute to higher intention to violate ISPs, the significant efect may not be related to an individual’s education alone. Rather, it may be the case that more senior employees are more likely to violate ISPs or to be less influenced by peer monitoring because they may feel confident that their subordinates or other group members with more junior job titles are less likely to report them. Thus, education could serve as a proxy for an employee’s position, thus future research should examine whether an employee’s position in a group influences the efect of peer monitoring on their intention to violate the ISP.

This study argues that in a more trusting environment, employees expect less vengeful intention from their peers. As such, peer reporting can be considered as an extra-role behaviour to protect the group and its assets (i.e., civic virtue) rather than a way of spying. Future studies may also explore how peer monitoring works in environments where employees compete or where employees are judged relative to their peers (e.g., a competitive climate<sup>8</sup>) (Brown et al., 1998). These studies could investigate the drawbacks associated with peer monitoring, such as its impact on productivity.

## 6.4. Conclusion

This study begins to leverage group-rooted approaches (e.g., peer monitoring) to untangle the complex issues associated with improving ISP compliance. With continued research in this area, we can collectively improve our understanding of how organisations can secure their most vulnerable assets: their employees.

4. Passive social influence occurs when employees change their behaviours (i.e., ISP-related behaviours) based on their perception of their peers’ behaviours and expectations. In contrast, active social influence happens when employees change their behaviours (i.e., ISP-related behaviours) because of the influence they receive through their direct interaction with their peers.

5. Although beyond the scope of this study, it should be noted that employees’ beliefs that they are trusted by their organisation has been shown to reduce computer abuse incidents (Posey et al., 2011) as well as reactive computer abuse (Lowry et al., 2015). Similar claims have been made by other studies (e.g., Lee et al., 2004; Stanton & Stam, 2006). However, even in such cases, it has been suggested that high levels of unjustified trust may lead to more serious information technology misuse (Moore et al., 2008). This suggests that mere reliance on trust is not suficient, and some form of control is necessary (Williams, 2008).

7. We run the regression between peer monitoring and intention to violate ISP for various industries. However, due to the small sample sizes, we find no significant relationship, even though standardised coeficients were relatively large for the majority of industries.

8. We find competitiveness particularly interesting because it could result in rivalries in which employees’ performance is compared to their peers. In such an environment, employees may seek to outperform their peers or engage in actions that undermine others’ performance. This could result in employees being more cautious about exposing themselves to the stigma of ignorance and the implications of failure (Boh & Wong, 2013). Given this, investigating peer monitoring, how it is perceived, and how it influences ISP compliance could be an interesting area of research for future studies.
