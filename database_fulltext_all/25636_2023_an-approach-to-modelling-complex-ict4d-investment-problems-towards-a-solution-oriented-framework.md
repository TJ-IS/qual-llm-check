# An approach to modelling complex ICT4D investment problems: towards a solution-oriented framework and data analytics methodology

Sergey Samoilenko<sup>a</sup> and Kweku-Muata Osei-Bryson<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of CIS/CS, Averett University, Danville, VA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, VA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Economies of the world invest in ICT with the uniform purpose of obtaining socio-economic benefits. Determination of the most appropriate path of success linking ICT to socio-economic benefits is a complex multidimensional problem that is not amenable to methodological approaches based on a single perspective. Policy analysts and researchers are, therefore, in need of adequate tools for modelling and analysing the complex ICT4D investment problem. In this paper, we present a consistent solution-oriented theoretical and methodological approach that focuses on discovering pitfalls and best practices that the experience of travelling “ICT → outcomes” path may ofer. Specifically, we ask: “What are some lessons that ‘newer entrants’ (represented by the economies of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)) may learn from ‘earlier travelers’ (represented by Transition Economies (TE))?” Among many findings is the diference in the sources of growth in productivity between the SSA- and TE-dominated groups of economies. Besides practical insights, this study ofers several contributions to policy-makers and researchers including: a theoretical framework that allows for conceptually complex inquiries into the matters of ICT4D and a multi-method methodology that can be used to identify (a) important areas suitable for benchmarking and (b) diferences requiring a customised context-specific approach.

KEYWORDS ICT4D investment modelling; ICT Capabilities; data analytics; Sub-Saharan Africa; transition economies

## 1. Introduction

Public policy decision-makers concerned with investing in Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) for national social and economic development face a complex multidimensional problem that no single method of data analysis can address. Fundamentally, the complexity results from the wide variety of ICT investments contexts, their core aspects and interaction dynamics. In some cases, investments bring the desired benefits (e.g., telemedicine in rural communities) and in other cases they can result in deterioration of the status quo (e.g., cellular networks increase terrorist activities and drug traficking). In general, the policy analyst must attend to three interdependent issues when considering ICT investment decisions. The first is: Where to invest to maximise impact? Careful selection of ICT investment contexts is essential to obtaining the desired socioeconomic impact. Investments must be allocated to engines of the impact; for example, to wireless communication which can drive productivity growth and minimise transaction cost across various sectors of the economy. The second component is identified by answering the question: How is it going to work? This is because the engine of the impact triggers a chain of events that, eventually, lead to the desired socioeconomic outcomes. Furthermore, even in the presence of the engine of the impact, outcomes of ICT investments are afected by the state of the existing infrastructure, complementary investments in education and skill levels of the labour force. In other words, the eficiency of the engine of the impact is dependent upon contextual factors. It follows then that a third concern for the policy analyst is: What complementary investments are necessary to achieve desired impact? Investments in ICT alone will not facilitate the desired socio-economic outcomes (Bollou & Ngwenyama, 2008). Whatever the context, complementary investments in such as ICT skills, specialised university education infrastructure, electricity generation and so on, will be necessary for efective ICT adoption (Ngwenyama & Morawczynski, 2009). For example, suppose an ICT for Development (ICT4D) policy analyst decides to target the agriculture sector for investment; beyond ICT investments, s/he will need to consider complementary investments in transportation networks, logistics and skills training at various levels (farmers, truckers, port managers, ICT specialists). In the absence of such complementary investments, the ICT infrastructure will fail as an engine of impact triggering multi socio-economic outcomes in the sector. Consequently, a key issue in ICT4D policy analysis is identifying and understanding the core aspects of each ICT investment context and their complex interaction dynamics.

In this paper, we propose a theoretical framework and associated data analytics methodology to assist ICT4D researchers, government policy analysts and decision makers in the modelling and analysis of complex ICT4D investment problems. We believe our framework and methodology ofers comprehensive decision support that could enable ICT4D policy analysts to make more informed decisions that could advance national social and economic development. The justification of our intent is based on a rational approach to decision making, where scientifically sound initiatives are preferred to ad hoc attempts (Cabantous & Gond, 2011; Gregor, 2009). While no two economies of the world are alike, the contextspecific diversity of challenges in implementing and managing ICT4D programmes is commonly augmented by the presence of three general issues. The issues pertain to the challenges regarding: (1) obtaining investment funds; 2) optimal allocation of the investments; and 3) ensuring the eficiency of the impact of the investments. In this paper, we focus on last two issues to demonstrate to our reader how a theoretically-sound and logically consistent framework, paired with the multi-method data analysis, are capable of modelling and solving complex investment ICT4D problems. We illustrate our approach and methodology with an analysis of data from two groups of developing economies, Transition Economies (TE) and Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).

Our approach relies on benchmarking- a widely used technique for the purposes of improving performance of individuals, organisations, and economies. However, there is much context-specific variation with respect to the achievement of the benchmarking objectives (Bollou & Ngwenyama, 2008; Heeks, 2002) and theories developed and tested in one context might not be adequate or valid for another (Davison & Martinsons, 2016). This suggests the importance of factoring context in any investigation that aims to explore characteristics of relatively efective transformation paths that associate investments in ICT with their socioeconomic impacts. In this study our focus is at the level of the national economy (Bollou & Ngwenyama, 2008; Meso et al., 2009), and so we aim to engage in such a context-sensitive investigation by doing a comparative analysis of the Transition Economies (TE) and Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The term Transition Economies (TE) refers to a set of Eastern Europe countries of the former centrally planned Soviet Union economy that have transitioned to market-oriented economies (Clarke, 2004; Nellis, 2001).

With respect to context, there are three perspectives that could be chosen to support an inquiry into the impacts of ICT on development. The first perspective is at the level of a grand theory (e.g., economic (R.M.

Solow, 1956) or IT-specific (Rogers, 1962; Venkatesh et al., 2003). While generally and widely applicable, such a perspective discards the specificity of the context and lacks the “boots on the ground” insights. The second perspective is at a level of a case study, where the context reigns supreme, but, often, at the expense of generalisability of the findings (Hayes & Westrup, 2012; Mengiste & Aanestad, 2013). Third is a middle way that seems to ofer an optimal solution – for investigations that rely on frameworks consistent with grand theories and yet allow specific attention to context (as advocated by Avgerou (2010), Thompson and Walsham (2010)). This option ofers a good opportunity for benchmarking of the best practices, where less efective economies could emulate leaders or learn from their more successful counterparts.

Benchmarking is considered to be a reliable tool for improving efectiveness and eficiency of economic units (Samoilenko & Osei-Bryson, 2019a, and in order to utilise it we need a suitable peer group to benchmark. We suggest that the Transition Economies could serve as a peer group for comparison to SSA. At the beginning of the transition from centralised to market-driven economy, Transition Economies (TE) were, just as SSA now, characterised by unfavourable initial conditions and inadequate levels of institutional development. At this point in time, as a whole, the TE have a lot to show in terms of their greatly improved levels of socio-economic development. For example, since the beginning of the transition GDP per capita has increased over 500% in Poland, over 600% in Hungary, and almost 800% in the Czech Republic (Baker, 2019).

While we cannot consider TE to be peers of SSA at the same point in time, we can consider them peers with regard to the path of socio-economic development. Both groups share important similarities, for they adopted principles of democratic governance and exhibited opening of political space. TE and SSA are similarly heterogeneous in terms of economic development of the members of each group, have exhibited continuous economic growth and engaged in international trade. Both groups are recipients of foreign direct investments, they undergo internal structural changes, and invest in ICT to improve the already existing IT infrastructure. The similarities hint that the ICT4D-related diference between TE and SSA is in the point where the groups are on the path of development- while SSA are closer to the beginning of their development trajectory, TE are farther along on their route to socio-economic wellbeing (Nielsen, 2011; World Bank, 2019). Based on this model for the comparison, SSA represents an earlier point on the trajectory of the development and TE represent a later point, the point where SSA could be in the future. Simply put, in this scenario TE serve as a proxy of SSA in the future.

Theoretically, these two diverse groups represent a context that does not violate any assumptions of the Neoclassical Growth model (which we use as a theoretical foundation of the study)- in both groups economic growth can be explained in terms of capital, labour, and technological progress, with policies of liberalisation, stabilisation, and privatisation being important factors of the development. At the same time, SSA and TE do share some important contextspecific diferences, and the theoretical framework of this study, presented later in the paper, allows for reflecting and capturing the diferences at the “boots on the ground” level. Consequently, the illustrative general research question (GQ) of this study is:

## What are the context-specific factors and relationships that diferentiate SSA and TE in terms of the socio-economic impacts of ICT?

Our approach for addressing GQ is based on the following reasoning. First, in many cases (and particularly for complex problems), the general research question of a study is not conveniently and directly answerable, but can be indirectly addressed via a set of appropriate lower-level research questions (RQs). Second, the process of generation of the set of RQs from the GQ can be accomplished in the presence of a research model supported by an appropriate theoretical framework. Third, each RQ can be operationalised into its set of specific questions (SQs), where for each such SQ, there exists one or more available evaluation methods for adequately addressing it. Finally, given the set of SQs and its set of associated evaluation methods, an adequate research methodology can be constructed that involves a synthesis of these evaluation methods.

## 2. Dimensions of the problem space

The problem space of national ICT4D investment comprise a set of interdependent sectors (agriculture, health, education, manufacturing, etc.) each having their own internal interacting components. Modelling and analysing a single sector is a complex undertaking, but often the ICT4D policy analyst must interrogate multiple interacting sectors to understand their potential impacts on national ICT4D outcomes. Moreover, benchmarking is needed for monitoring and evaluating developmental progress in order to adjust and target investment policies to facilitate the engine of growth. Adding to the complexity are sector- and context-specific variations which must be analysed with respect to achieving and benchmarking ICT4D objectives (Bollou & Ngwenyama, 2008; Heeks, 2002) and ICT4D approaches efective in one sector or context might be inadequate or invalid for another (Davison & Martinsons, 2016). Consequently, contextual features must be included in any modelling and analysis aimed at exploring the relative efectiveness of transformation paths that associate ICT4D investments with potential outcomes. The complexity of the ICT4D problem space has been noted by various researchers who examined the challenges faced by a range of ICT4D initiatives (Avgerou, 2010; Heeks, 2002). Some researchers argue that macro-level analysis is insuficient to maximise impact of ICT4D investments, and encourage policy analysts to give explicit attention to micro-level contextual factors (Barjis et al., 2013). This investigation, which synthesises micro-level frameworks (Kaufmann et al., 2008; World Economic Forum, 2015) into one that is consistent with macro-level grand theory (R. M. Solow, 1957), follows the “notoriously dificult” to implement (Avgerou, 2010, p. 16) call for reconciliation of the macro-view of the “technologists” and the micro-focus of the “contextualists”. Taking such a synthesis-oriented approach would seem to be advantageous (Madon et al., 2007; Walsham, 2017), particularly given the abundant evidence of failure as a result of consideration of technology without context (), and vice versa (Hayes & Westrup, 2012; Madon et al., 2007). In the next section we will present our theoretical framework that involves the type of synthesis mentioned earlier in this paragraph. However, before doing so, it is important that we present our conceptual model of the dynamics of the ICT4D problem space.

Results from previous investigations indicate some key aspects that require specific attention when analysing and modelling the ICT4D problems space. First, it was noted that a level of economic development of a country impacts the level of investments in ICT, where more afluent economies allocate more resources to ICT , and that investments in ICT are allocated within a socio-technical context, where a better developed labour force and a higher level of existing technological infrastructure allow for more eficient and efective utilisation of such investments (Samoilenko & Ngwenyama, 2011). Second, investments in ICT are allocated towards creating ICT Capabilities that enable economies to become more efective and eficient, but ICT capabilities are passive resources, that are activated via enabling engines of the impact of ICT (Samoilenko & Osei-Bryson, 2019b). Third, while ICT-enabled engines of the impact of investments in ICT are context-sensitive, there are general best practices that could be identified and benchmarked (Heeks, 2002). Finally, it was noted that ICT-enabled engines of the impact of investments in ICT produce socio-economic impact aimed at improved quality of life and economic wellbeing, which, in turn, contributes to the level of economic development of economy, thus allowing to allocate investments in ICT.

## 3. Theoretical perspective

## 3.1. Conceptual considerations

The theoretical foundations of our framework and methodology is the Neoclassical Growth Accounting (NGA) theory/model (Norsworthy, 1984; Oulton, 2007), which explains the economic growth in terms of capital, labour, and technological progress, where governmental policies of liberalisation, stabilisation, and privatisation are central elements of the development efort. Contrary to other perspectives of economic development (e.g., the International Dependence model), Neoclassical theories (Hahn, 2010) point to domestic, not international issues for the lack of economic development, where it is internal issues of poor resource allocation, price distortion, and corruption that apply the brakes to economic development.

The NGA conceptualise economic development as a continuous cycle of Strategic Investments → Technological Progress → Economic Growth→ Strategic Investments. However, allocation of strategic investments is conditioned upon the attractiveness (or readiness) of sectors, that is the state of associated infrastructure and technical capabilities for efectively utilising strategic investments (Roller & Waverman, 2001). In this regard, the NGA provides techniques for benchmarking performance, technical progress, factor productivity growth, and public value (Benhabib & Jovanovic, 1991; Oulton, 2012). Moreover, the development cycle postulated by the NGA is applicable to all sectors of the economy, agriculture, manufacturing, health, etc., as well as national economic growth (Fernández-Portillo et al., 2019). This theory/model provides principles for the analysis of investments and economic growth in terms of capital, labour, and technological progress, where governmental policies of liberalisation, stabilisation, and privatisation are central elements of the development efort. Neoclassical theory also recognises that social, political (legal and regulatory institutions), and cultural aspects are important, and that economic development could fail to materialise to its fullest extent due to insuficiently developed socio-technical infrastructure and inefective delivery of public value. Overall, the NGA model fits well with the purposes of our inquiry for multiple reasons. First, it focuses on internal sources of growth under the purview of policy makers, and its measures (factor productivity, etc.) are generally applicable to a variety of economies. Second, the NGA views limitations in information transparency as barriers to economic growth and considers adequate government services, necessary to economic development. Both of these are dependent on the level of ICT infrastructure and the quality of ICT services. It is also important to note that the NGA model is compatible with and complementary to the Development as Freedom’ perspective of Amartya Sen (1999). The NGA is concerned with how to generate wealth, while Sen’s model is concerned with how to allocate generated wealth to ameliorate poverty and improve social and human development at the microlevel.

From the perspective of the NGA, we can formulate a general model of ICT4D as follows: Economic Attractiveness→ Investments in ICT (ICT Labour, ICT Capital, ICT Capabilities)→ ICT-driven Technological Progress → Economic Development. Figure 2 represents an inquiry-specific instantiation of the general model of the ICT4D problem space, which we use to illustrate our data analytic methodology in the following sections. The figure highlights a set of context-specific factors to which the ICT4D policy analyst must attend when building an analytical model for the domain of interest. The first factor, Economic Attractiveness, conditions investment decisions on, for example, government regulations, the state of infrastructure (roads, electrification, etc.), labour force, education system. Investors (foreign or domestic) are influenced by the level of preparedness of the sector when deciding on investment options (Eklund, 2013; Junankar, 2008), consequently the analyst must consider measures economic attractiveness in modelling the problem space.

In the context of ICT4D, the level of ICT Capabilities is impacted by the state of the general infrastructure and the availability of the qualified labour force. It was noted that economies with highquality technical tertiary education and developed socio-technical infrastructure tend to have higher levels of ICT Capabilities (Armey & Hosman, 2016; Fagas et al., 2017), which allows for increasing the eficiency and efectiveness of transactions (e.g., public services). Additionally, economies with better developed ICT Capabilities tend to also have higher levels of Quality of Life (QoL) Expenditures, such as those allocated towards medicine, education, and so on (Cordella & Bonina, 2012; Tan et al., 2018).

One of the functions of any government is to maintain and improve, via allocation of resources and policy making, a standard of living of its citizens. It is also reasonable to consider that higher levels of QoL, such as those allocated towards healthcare, education, quality of the labour force, and so on, will result not only in a higher level of economic development of a country but also in a higher level of a social impact (Furceri & Zdzienicka, 2012; Haile & Niño-Zarazúa, 2018). Below we put forward the following propositions explaining the relationships between the components of our context-specific model for analysis. While we expect that constructs of this general model would remain in most ICT4D research, we also anticipate that future inquiries may add other constructs relevant to the empirical situation of inquiry.

General Propositions:

P1: The rate of Economic Attractiveness impacts the Labour-Technical Infrastructure of an economy.

P2: The level of the development of the Labour-Technical Infrastructure of an economy impacts the level of ICT Capabilities of that economy.

P3: The level of ICT Capabilities impacts the quality of public services as represented by the level of Public Value and the level of Quality of Life Expenditures.

P4: The level of Public Value and the level of QoL Expenditures impact the socio-economic bottom line of an economy.

It should be noted that logical justification for each proposition appear in the Appendix document.

RQ1: With respect to ICT Capabilities, what are the diferences between these two groups?

The main goal is to test the assumption of homogeneity of the context. We aim to accomplish this goal by means of two objectives: first, to inquire whether or not SSA and TE are similar, and, second, if SSA and TE are dissimilar, then to inquire into the sources of diferences. Because the focus of this study is on ICT4D, we center this RQ around ICT Capabilities.

RQ2: What are the similarities and diferences between the groups with respect to the strength and the eficiency of the links between the interacting constructs (e.g., interaction of Economic Attractiveness with Labour & Technical Infrastructure) of the Research Model?

The main goal is to inquire into the strength and the eficiency of each link between the interacting constructs. The goal will be accomplished via achieving two objectives: first, to determine the significance of the level of the impact, and, second, to determine the level of eficiency, as well as of the change in eficiency over time, of each link. The reader should note that this RQ will be explored with respect to each direct interaction link that appears in Figure 3.

The main goal is to describe each group of the sample in terms of the measures of the most relevant constructs - ICT Capabilities and ICT Impact. This goa will be accomplished via two objectives: first, generating descriptions for ICT Capabilities, and, second, generating descriptions for ICT Impact. Given a partitioning of our sample into n-groups, we would like to inquire into the presence of the leveldependent association rules describing each of the n-groups. The reader should note that this RQ will be explored with respect to each direct interaction link that appears in Figure 3.

## 4. Methodology

Our research methodology is comprised of a set of complementary data analytic techniques, namely: Cluster Analysis, Decision Tree Induction, Multivariate Regression, Data Envelopment Analysis and Association Rules Mining. By integrating these analytic techniques, we are able to partition the problem space into its core aspects (or areas) for stepwise systematic analysis that results in a holistic understanding of the problem. We will now briefly overview each analytic technique with the exception of multiple regression (MR) which most readers are familiar with. For more in-depth discussion of these techniques, see Samoilenko and Osei-Bryson (2017a) and Osei-Bryson and Ngwenyama (2014). Clustering is a popular unsupervised data mining technique that allows investigators to test the assumption of homogeneity of the sample. While in our case, we have two groups of economies, the grouping is not based on relevant to ICT4D factors, but on social, political, and geographic criteria. Consequently, clustering allows us to determine if, indeed, the SSA and TE groups are suficiently diferent in terms of those factors. However, clustering does not ofer any insights regarding the source of the diference; while we know that the groups are dissimilar, we would not know what diferentiates the groups. And the answer to this question can be provided by decision tree induction (DTI).

A decision tree (DT) is an inverted tree structure (e.g., root at the top, branches are in the middle, and leaves are at the bottom) that can be used to answer a question what diferentiates the multiple groups comprising the data set? DT induction (the process of generating a DT) provides an answer by identifying the variables that diferentiate the groups the most, and making the splits in the data set based on those variables. Because the root node is represented by the entire dataset, the variables selected for the top splits are considered to be the most important in diferentiating the groups. Thus, using DTI in conjunction with CA is useful for the purposes of discovering the presence of naturally occurring groups in the sample, as well as identifying the most important variables that diferentiate the groups. To illustrate, if given a group of analysts tasked with preparing reports, then CA allows us to identify the naturally occurring groups within a sample (e.g., senior analysts and junior analysts), and DTI allows us to identify the factors that diferentiate the groups (e.g., years of experience, level of education, etc.).

Every economy in our sample can be represented as a system that receives a set of inputs and produces a set of outputs. For example, given an input in the form of socio-economic infrastructure, every economy has an output in the form of ICT Capabilities. While CA and DTI allow us to discover the naturally occurring groups in the sample and the variables that diferentiate these groups, these methods cannot tell us anything regarding the eficiency of conversion of inputs into outputs. Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) is a wellestablished method for evaluating the relative eficiency of decision making units (DMU) in the sample described by the same DEA model, which defines the same set of inputs and outputs for each DMU. A DEA model is not a true production model, in the sense where metal ore is an input and steel is an output, but a model that makes sense to a decision-maker and can adequately describe a DMU within a given context (e.g., “number of hours spent studying → grade on a test”). Consequently, by using DEA in our methodology, we will be able to determine if the diferent groups in our sample (e.g., SSA and TE) also difer in terms of the relative eficiency of conversion of inputs into outputs.

Additionally, DEA allows us to estimate the change in eficiency over time (growth in productivity) by using the Malmquist Index (MI). MI consists of two components that can be used to indicate the source of growth in productivity: change in eficiency (EC) and technological change (TC). By using DEA to calculate MI, we can determine the primary driver of the improvement in relative eficiency over consecutive time periods. For example, in the case of overall growth in productivity (e.g., in the case of MI > 1), EC > TC indicates that more eficient practices are the primary cause of the improvement, while TC > EC indicates that the technology is the main driver of the improvement. To illustrate the concepts of DEA and MI, let us again consider a group of analysts tasked with preparing reports. DEA allows us to identify the most eficient performers in the group (e.g., those with the best report/time ratio), and MI allows us to identify the most improved performers (e.g., those with the best change in report/time ratio) and to pinpoint the sources of the improvement (e.g., skills vs. equipment).

While the previously described methods allow us to identify the presence of natural occurring groups in the sample, the sources of the diferences between the groups, as well as to compare the relative eficiency, growth in productivity, and the sources of growth for each group, no technique we used so far allows us to describe each group in general. But, it is useful to compare groups in terms of associative structures that describe the broader context. The objective of Association Rules Mining is to find such general associations, where each Association Rule (AR) has a form (A (antecedent) Þ B(consequent). For example, in the case AR(MilkÞ Bananas), Milk does not cause Bananas to appear in a shopping cart, but simply indicates the associated presence of Bananas. Using our running example (e.g., group of analysts preparing reports), ARM would allow us to describe the characteristics of diferent groups of analysts, so we could gain insights into the nature of the diferences between, let us say, highly eficient junior analysts and less eficient senior analysts (e.g., IF (more educated, more experienced, married) THEN (senior analyst), and so on.). We use these complementary techniques to compose a multi-stage methodology for our data analysis, where each stage focuses on answering one research question and its specific sub-questions (see Table 2).

## 5. Data collection and analysis

## 5.1. Representation of the constructs

The discussion about the selection of the representation of the constructs is important to our study, because it allows for demonstrating to our reader that the empirical situation selected for our study is appropriate for observing and investigating the interplay between the factors reflecting the problem space of ICT4D. In our efort to represent the constructs of the framework, we used two conditions. First, we considered the availability of the data by asking a question What sort of relevant data could be obtained from reliable sources? Second, we considered the logical consistency of a measure with the construct that it is intended to represent by asking a question Can we make an argument for why a chosen measure could serve to represent the given construct?

Consequently, we deal with a two-tiered assessment of, what could be called in the reference to the domain of structural equation modelling, a measurement model. The assessment of the first tier is associated with assessing the validity and reliability of the compiled data set. While we do not have any control over the quantity of the available data (e.g., we cannot make more data available than the reliable public sources could provide), we ofer our reader an opportunity to assess the veracity of our decision-making process regarding the assessment of the quality of the data. The main criterion that we use in the assessment is based on the validity and reliability of the sources of the data, which, in our case, represented by World Economic Forum’ Networked Readiness Index (World Economic Forum, 2016) and the Data Bank of the World Bank (The World Bank, 2016). These are two public sources of the archival data that are widely used and trusted by practitioners and researchers alike, and we assume that the data provided by the sources are reliable and valid. Consequently, our assessment of the validity and reliability of the data comprising the data set used in this study is based on the reputation of the data sources.

The assessment of the second tier, namely, a validity and reliability of the representation of the constructs via selected measures, was also conducted based on two criteria. The first criterion relies on the condition of precedence and the second criterion relies on logical argumentation. Meaning, we used a particular variable to represent a construct only in the case if: (a) the variable was designed to represent a construct we are using and there is a reported precedence of the usage of such representation in practice or in previous investigations, or (b) we can present a transparent and rational argument for why we think the variable should be used in representing the construct. Such constructs as ICT Capabilities, Socio-Economic Outcomes, as well as Public Value component of the construct Public Value and Quality of Life Expenditures, successfully pass the assessment based on the criterion of precedence; those representations of the constructs by the associated variables were designed by the data sources and the usage of the corresponding representations was previously reported. As for the representation of the rest of the constructs, we ofer our reader to evaluate our logical argumentation that follows below. Detailed definitions of the measures representing the constructs are provided in Appendix (Tables A1-A7).

The construct Economic Attractiveness (Birnleitner, 2014; Groh & Wich, 2009; Kwang-Hoon, 2016) is represented via three measures: GDP growth (annual %), GDP per capita growth (annual %), and Foreign direct investment, net inflows (% of GDP). GDP growth has been traditionally acknowledged as one of the key measures of economic performance, and GDP per capita growth allows us to account for the diferences in population among the economies. FDI was chosen to gauge the level of attractiveness of a growing economy to outside investors (Borensztein et al., 1998; Carbonell & Werner, 2018).

We represent the construct Labour & Technical Infrastructure via two components. The first component, Labour, is represented by the measures (Hipple, 2016) Employment to Population Ratio, ages 15-24 – Male (%) and Employment to Population Ratio, ages 15-24 – Female (%). Undoubtedly, the state of the labour force of any economy is reflective of its level of development. We have chosen to concentrate on age 15 to 24 because of the significance of this group to socio-economic development (DESA, 2019; Fox et al., 2016). For example, the UN “ . . . has long recognized that young people are a major human resource for development and key agents for social change, economic growth and technological innovation” (UNDESA, 2020).

Additionally, according to the Youth Envoy of the UN (UN Report, 2021):

● This age frame represents 25% of the total working age population (ILO & FAO, 2013).

● This age frame is three times the adult unemployment rate (ILO, 2015).

● About one in three youth aged 15–24 sufers from a deficit of decent work opportunities (UN MDG, 2015).

● In sub-Saharan Africa, more than 40% of young workers are unpaid (UNDP, 2014).

● Overall, in both developed and developing countries, young women face obstacles in entering the labour market thatwhich are significantly higher than those for young men (ILO, 2016).

The second component, Technical Infrastructure, is represented by the following measures: Access to Electricity – Urban (% of urban population), Access to Electricity – Rural (% of rural population) and Automated Teller Machines (ATMs) (per 100,000 adults). The measures are general and are intended to represent infrastructure-based measures of inclusiontechnical and financial. Technical inclusion is important because an access to electricity is representative of the state of a general technological infrastructure of any economy and is instrumental to the development of any economy (Baresa et al., 2011; Bridge et al., 2018). The third measure, represented by the number of ATMs, is widely considered to be a suitable representation of the current state of ICT (Banker & Kaufman, 1988; Bátiz-Lazo, 2018) and the extent of financial inclusion that underlies a variety of socio-economic benefits (Barajas et al., 2017; Lee, 2016). Furthermore, the number of ATM reflects the current extent of IT-based distribution of the services (Binugo & Aregbeshola, 2014) ofered by an industry (e.g., banking sector) that is very responsive to innovative technologies (Polasik & Wisniewski, 2009). Moreover, this proxy depicts the current state of adoption of ICT by an economy that makes it compatible with the requirements of the interconnected by ICT global market place (Nkiru et al., 2018) and reflects the extent of IT-driven productivity gains (Haynes & Thompson, 2000). Simply put, the number of ATM shows extent of advancement of ICT in the highly competitive business environment of a changing economy (Luka & Frank, 2012).

In order to operationalise the construct ICT Capabilities, we turn to the Networked Readiness Index (NRI) framework, as the NRI serves as an established measure of the ICT Capabilities, or, drivers, of socio-economic development (World Economic Forum, 2016). Within this framework, every economy is assigned a score – a value of NRI, which, in 2016, ranged from 2.2 for Chad (the lowest) to 6.0 for Singapore (the highest). Consequently, in this study, the construct ICT Capabilities is represented via three sub-indexes of NRI and their associated pillars: Environment (Political & Regulatory, Business & Innovation), Readiness (Infrastructure, Afordability, Skills), and Usage (Individual, Business, Government). It is worth noting the diference between representation of the constructs of Infrastructure Readiness of ICT Capabilities and Technical Infrastructure of Labour-Technical Infrastructure. Within NRI, Infrastructure Readiness is represented by means of the following four indicators: Electricity production, Mobile network coverage, International Internet bandwidth, and Secure Internet servers. Thus, it can be easy to see that there is no overlap between the two constructs and their measures. Instead, it is easy to demonstrate that production of electricity, for example, reflects, indeed, a capability of economy. But the capability cannot be utilised unless the infrastructure to do so is in place (e.g., access to electricity).

The third construct of our model is reflected by two components – Public Value and Quality of Life Expenditures. The first component, Public Value, is represented via the dimensions provided by the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) of the World Bank (World Bank, 2016). The dimensions are Political Stability & Absence of Violence/Terrorism, Voice & Accountability, Rule of Law, Regulatory Quality, Government Efectiveness, and Control of Corruption. Such representation of Public Value is considered to be acceptable for cross-country and over-time comparisons (Kaufmann et al., 2010a), for WGI is a long-standing, legitimate, and widely accepted tool for a representation of governance (Kaufmann et al., 2010b).

To represent the construct Quality of Life Expenditures, we use the measures of Current health expenditure (% of GDP), Domestic general government health expenditure (% of GDP), and Domestic private health expenditure (% of current health expenditure). As our reader may notice the chosen representation is biased towards health expenditures. The reasoning is straightforward, for such spending, while varied in size, is common to all economies of the world and is considered to be one of the most important indicators of development (Boyacioglu, 2012). Additionally, the level of private, outof-pocket health expenditures of an economy is indicative of the level of development and relative afluence of that economy, that was the reason why we included the last measure into the representation of the construct. To operationalise the last construct, Socio-Economic Impact, we use Impact sub-index of NRI, which is comprised of Social and Economic components (or, pillars of NRI). Resultantly, within the framework of our inquiry, Socio-Economic Impact is represented by Social Impact and Economic Impact (World Economic Forum, 2016).

## 6.1. Stage 1: cluster analysis and decision tree induction

We ran K-means cluster analysis with K = 2 (e.g., two groups of economies) and identified a twocluster solution that complies with the accepted heuristic criteria (Samoilenko & Osei-Bryson, 2007) that requires the size of the smaller cluster be at least 90% that of the larger cluster and the size of the dominant group of one cluster be at least 90% of the size of the dominant group of the other cluster. In the case of our analysis, the size of the smaller Cluster 1 is 119 and that of the larger Cluster 2 is 126 (e.g., the ratio is 94.4%) and the size of the dominant group of Cluster 1 is 110, and that of the dominant group of Cluster 2 is 100 (e.g., the ratio is 100%). The detailed membership of the chosen solution is presented in Table 4, where Cluster 1 is SSA-dominated (labelled “SSA dom” in later tables), and Cluster 2 is TE-dominated (labelled “TE dom” in later tables).

It is worth noting that only four economies – Tajikistan (TJK), Cape Verde (CPV), Mauritius (MUS), and South Africa (ZAF) – ended up being assigned to a “non-native” group. As of today, IMF (2015) classifies Tajikistan as lowincome, Cape Verde and Mauritius as lowermiddle, and South Africa as an upper-middle income economy. The results of Stage 1 allow us to formulate the following answer to research question RQ1:

Perhaps, it is worth noting that based on the results of DTI, TE difer from SSA in terms of five out of eight pillars of NRI. Let us recall that our approach to improving the efectiveness of ICT4D initiatives is based on benchmarking, and Stage 1 of our methodology helped us to identify a target group of higher performing economies, as well as a set of criteria that diferentiates them from their less efective counterparts.

## 6.3. Stage 3: association rules mining

The last stage of our methodology is dedicated to generating a set of association rules that diferentiate SSA and TE in regard to ICT Capabilities and the socio-economic impacts of these capabilities.

The results of this stage (see Table 8) allow for answering the last research question of this study (RQ3), as follows:

There are group-specific and level-dependent “If⇒Then” association rules describing the level of ICT Capabilities and a level of ICT Impact each group, where a high level of Female Employment and a low level of Technical Infrastructure are associated with a low level of ICT Capabilities, and, in turn, a low level of impact of ICT Capabilities.

Let us consider the importance of the findings ofered by this stage to benchmarking in the area of ICT4D. Previous steps allowed us to identify a target group for benchmarking, and a group that can benefit from the best practices of the target group. We also identified the important diferences between the groups, and pinpointed some of the routes by which the benchmarking eforts could produce the intended improvements. This stage allows us to describe the environment of benchmarking in more general, but not less important terms. It may be tempting to a policy-maker to advocate for a higher level of investments in ICT, because it would appear that this would produce desired improvements in socio-economic outcomes. However, as the results of the last stage indicate, this will not be a wise decision if the general technical infrastructure is insuficiently developed. Consequently, a more informed policy would advocate for a step-by-step approach of improving the overall technical infrastructure, then improving ICT infrastructure, then increasing the level of ICT Capabilities, and only then trying optimise the impact of capabilities on ICT-related socio-economic outcomes.

## 7. Discussion of the results

The reader would recall that the aim of this study was to do a context-sensitive exploration of the characteristics of relatively efective transformation paths that involve investments in ICT and their socio-economic impacts. We used a framework that allowed us to consider not just the direct (e.g., Public Value) and indirect impacts (e.g., Economic Impact) of ICT Capabilities, but also direct (e.g., Technical Infrastructure) and indirect (e.g., Economic Attractiveness) antecedents of ICT Capabilities. We provided logical arguments (JP1-JP4) with support from the research literature to justify the reasonableness of this framework, and our data analysis also provides support for the direct and indirect consequences, as well as the direct and indirect impacts.

Given that benchmarking is considered to be a reliable tool for improving efectiveness and eficiency of economic units, our exploration involved a comparative analysis of SSA economies and TE.

However, although the countries of SSA and those of the TE may be considered as belonging to separate groups for historical and geographic reasons, it is possible that a particular economy may be more similar to the overwhelming majority of the economies of the other group with respect to the level of ICT Capabilities. Therefore, it was important to perform cluster analysis. The result was a SSA-dominated cluster and a TE-dominated cluster, each with at least one “non-native” economy (e.g., Republic of South Africa in the TE-dominated cluster). It seemed more appropriate to do our focused benchmarking based on the groups (i.e., clusters) that resulted from data-based partitioning, than to simply use the groups based on geography and some historical factors.

An evaluation of the summary statistics for this pair of clusters (see Table 9) shows that the TE-dominated cluster outperforms the SSA-dominated group with respect ICT Capabilities, as well as the corresponding indirect (i.e., Overall Impact, Economic Impact, Social Impact) and direct outcome measures (e.g., Public Value). However, for the Youth Employment to Population Ratio measures, the SSA-dominated cluster has higher averages than the TE-dominated cluster. Considering a common belief that unemployment rates are high in SSA, the reader may have expected that the TE-dominated cluster to also outperform the SSA-dominated cluster with respect to the measures for the Youth Employment to Population Ratio. An explanation may be that for the TE the 15–24 age group demographic are less likely to be in the labour force than for the SSA-dominated group and more likely to be engaged in learning and formal training to obtain Skills associated with Readiness sub-index of the NRI. This explanation is supported by the fact that Skills pillar of the Readiness sub-index is one of the pillars that diferentiated the two.

The two clusters also share similarities and differences with respect to the relationships between the constructs (see Table 10). Similarities are important for relationships that hold for both the TE-dominated cluster and the SSA-dominated cluster, for they suggest that if an economy in the SSAdominated cluster raise the level(s) of the antecedent component of the given link, then they should expect to achieve similar results as to what now occurs for the TE-dominated cluster. For example, both the MR analysis and the ARM indicate that level of Access to Electricity is a critical antecedent of ICT Capabilities. This is of course not surprising that it would afect the Infrastructure pillar of the Readiness sub-index or all the pillars of the Usage sub-index.

The diference may indicate that some contextspecific issues may be at play, such as where a given economy is on the trajectory of a technological and socio-economic development. For example, let us consider the AR “High Level of Female Employment is associated with Low Levels of Innovation Environment and Individual Usage of ICTs” and its possible interpretations. While a literal and narrowly scoped reading may be suggesting that a high level of Female Youth Employment Ratio is undesirable, the more informed understanding of the rule is that the jobs that female youths tend to occupy are, in general, involved in situations for which conditions for Innovation are low to absent, and also do not allow female youth to aford higher levels of Individual Usage of ICTs. And, given that Individual Usage of ICTs has been associated with Public Value and QoL, then there would also be an interest in raising the level of Individual Usage of ICTs by female youths.

## 8. Conclusion

In this paper, we set out to present and illustrate a more integrated approach and a methodology for interrogating complex ICT4D problems. Our intention was to ofer ICT4D researchers and policy analysts a set of theoretically sound principles and guidelines for generating robust developmental policies. The results of our inquiry ofer conceptual, theoretical, and practical contribution to the field of study associated with ICT4D. Our conceptual contribution is delivered in the form of the model of the dynamics of the problem space of ICT4D. To our best knowledge, our research is novel in regard to the representation of the problems space of ICT4D in the form of a feedback loop-like structure. And while the model is supported by the previously reported analytical components (e.g., constructs and links), the synthesis of the model is uniquely ours. Gregor (2006) suggested that there are several theory types including Explanatory Theory (i.e., What, How, Why, Where, When), and Design & Action Theory (“which says how to do something”). The theoretical contributions of this study involve these two types: Explanatory, and Design & Action.

## 8.1. Explanatory theory

Our conceptual model of the dynamics of the ICT4D problem-space (see Figure 1) can be considered as a theory of the problem in the sense of Majchrzak et al. (2016), where such a theory as noted by Ngwenyama et al. (2021) “is an important first step in devising a theory of solution strategies”. It thus was used to develop our other explanatory-type theoretical contributions: the Theoretical Framework and the

Research Model. It has been noted that while for a given problem there could be multiple justifiable theories of the problem, what matters most is that a given theory of the problem ofers substantive insights for research advancement including guidance for the development of an adequate theory of the solution (Majchrzak et al., 2016; Ngwenyama et al., 2021).

The Theoretical Framework (see Figure 2), which served as a basis for the research model of our investigation (Figure 3), ofers another theoretical contribution of the inquiry. This theoretical framework incorporated the established NRI framework in a manner that is consistent with the established grand theory, and it presents the two models within a single feedback loop-type of a cycle that allows for conceptually complex and non-linear inquiries into the matters of ICT4D being accommodated via relying on linear deterministic causal relationships such as those of our research model. This research model can be considered as ofering an explanatory theory as the links of the model were justified logically by associated arguments, which included support from the literature, and were also justified empirically using MR analysis. Further Majchrzak et al. (2016) noted that “A theory of the solution is . . . (an argument specifying relationships among conceptual elements) of how and why ICT is expected to make a diference with respect to a specific important substantive problem in a specific context”. Within the context of this definition, our theoretical framework and research model could together be considered to be a theory of the solution, and as demonstrated in our data analysis, the application of our methodology that is based on this framework and model do in fact result in the identification of “specific configurations associated with success”.

## 8.2. Design and action theory

Typically, quantitative empirical IS research involves using a single data analytic method such as SEM or regression analysis; but it is known that each such method has both strengths and limitations. For example, while SEM or regression analysis allows for answering the question as to whether a given construct X has a statistically significant impact on another construct Y, they are unable to answer the question as to the conditions under which X has statistically significant impacts on Y. On the other hand, methods such as decision tree induction (DTI) and regression splines analysis (e.g., Ko & Osei-Bryson, 2004) allow for answering such a question. Given a set of research questions, the researcher should therefore consider which data analytic methods are appropriate.

In this paper, we present and used a methodology that incorporated the methods of data analysis and data mining that were used in a recent study (Samoilenko & Osei-Bryson, 2019a). Any similarity of these two methodologies is primarily with respect to the fact that they use the same set of data analytic evaluation methods, but it should be noted that they difer in several ways including the ordering of these methods and purpose for which each is used. As noted earlier, this methodology was selected because it allows to answer our set of RQs (see Table 1). Benefits of using our methodology include:

● Discovering natural subsets (groups) within the sample;

● Identifying the sources of heterogeneity between the groups;

● Calculating the scores of the relative eficiency of each group;

● Identifying the reasons for disparities in the scores of relative eficiency between groups;

● Uncovering sample and group-specific nonobvious causal structures.

It is reasonable to expect that there are other research problems for which this methodology could be applied in its original or customised form could be relevant. The reader may recall that the development of our methodology involved the following investigative path:

$$
\begin{array}{l}G Q \rightarrow \text { Theoretical   Framework } \rightarrow R Q s \rightarrow S Q s \rightarrow\\E v a l u a t i o n M e t h o d s \rightarrow M e t h o d o l o g y.\end{array}
$$

We would expect that adoption of a similar investigative path would result in the identification of an appropriate methodology for the given GQ. “The reader may note that this approach is consistent with the recent study by Samoilenko & Osei-Bryson (2021) which presented a set of templates that could guide the researcher in selecting appropriate methodological modules to appropriately address the given set of RQs.”

## 8.3. Contributions to practice

Praxis-wise, our investigation ofers several benefits to ICT4D policy-makers and other practitioners including the following:

(1) A theoretically consistent modelling and analysis approach to assist ICT4D policy-makers:

In considering a given ICT4D investment problem at the level of the national economy, our theory of the problem would prompt the policy-maker to the consider what are the ICT Capabilities that would result from ICT investments, what are the complementary investments, and what is the “engine of growth”. Although in this study, we have considered a specific “engine of growth” and have used one of the available representations of ICT Capabilities, it should be noted that other corresponding choices that could be considered and substituted by the policymaker. Our theory of the solution provides for the development of an appropriate data analytic methodology whose application allows for answering the questions: 1) Where to invest to maximise impact? 2) How is it going to work? 3) What complementary investments are necessary to achieve desired impact?

(1) An approach that allows for consideration of complex questions:

Our theoretical framework provides policy-maker with a clear way for exploring a set of more complex socio-economic development-related questions. For example, instead of “What is the impact of ICT Capabilities on socio-economic development?”, a practitioner may ask a question “If ICT Capabilities have a positive impact on socio-economic development, then does this result in consequent desired increase in ICT Capabilities?” This is important, because it is becoming increasingly clear that “If → Then” linearity, while having its benefits, is not suficient for addressing complex developmental issues. Simply put, our study allows for asking “Whether a goose that lays golden eggs gets enough feed from the proceeds to keep laying golden eggs?”

Also the application of our methodology provides results that could prompt ICT4D policy-makers to ask questions such as: “What should we do to capitalize on the available human capital?” And, given the cyclic nature of our inquiry, the answer could be obtained via two follow-up questions: first, “What should we do to prepare (e.g., education/skills development) the youth to enter the labor market?” and, second, “What should we do make the youth’ labor force more productive?”

## (1) An approach that ofers guidance for developing policy:

In formulating a policy, it is of benefit to be assisted by evidence for why target goals could be achieved by the selected means. The “If-Then” ARs such as those provided in response to RQ3 can be considered to be actionable rules that could be used by policy-makers to facilitate the determination of the appropriate level of ICT Capabilities to indirectly influence the achievement of the desired socio-economic outcomes. The results from the MR analysis and AR mining (ARM) also allow policy-makers and other decision-makers in the SSA-dominated cluster to understand both what are the characteristics of the similarities and diferences between them and the TE, which should help in development of feasible plans of action to achieve the desired socio-economic outcomes.

## (1) Guidance on how to do appropriate benchmarking in the ICT4D decision space:

First, we highlighted the significance of context to the process of benchmarking and demonstrated the importance of an objective determination of the target of benchmarking based on clustering on an ICT construct (i.e., ICT Capabilities). This approach ofers a much firmer foundation to a rational decision-maker than a reliance on such considerations as geography and previous political and economic characteristics. Second, our investigation allows for construct benchmarking, where a target economy may serve as a guide to follow in terms of the levels of the values representing the constructs. Given the feedback model of development (Figure 2), a weaker economy could benchmark its more eficient and efective peer based on the selected construct (e.g., ICT Capabilities). Finally, the study also allows for link benchmarking, where an aspirant economy may benefit from emulating the efectiveness and eficiency of the links between the constructs exhibited by the target economy. This should allow the aspirant economy to monitor the level of the impact of one construct on another (e.g., the impact of ICT Capabilities on Public Value & Quality of Life Expenditures).

## (1) An approach that addresses the issue of eficiency in the transformation path and identifies the source of any improvement in eficiency:

In our context-sensitive exploration of the characteristics of relatively eficient transformation paths that involve investments in ICT and their socio-economic impacts, we considered the issue of relative eficiency as policy-makers would have an interest in knowing how to produce more output from the same input, and for each situation in knowing what was the cause of any improvement in relative eficiency. Below we mention the type of results from such data analysis:

For the TE-dominated cluster the source of growth in productivity tended to be change in eficiency (i.e., EC), and for the SSA-dominated cluster tended to be the change in technology (i.e., TC). This finding has a very important practical, as well as policy-making implications for both groups. For all intents and purposes, members of TE-dominated cluster are superior in utilisation of existing technology. This speaks of a high quality of the workforce, which could be further improved via upgrading to a newer technology, because the workforce is ready for it. And, given that the costs of ICT are continuously going down due to economies of scale, this would be a simple path to further improving efectiveness and eficiency of the workforce, with all the positive socio-economic impacts such improvement entails. Consequently, at this point, the policy regarding the routes for further development should consider allocating more resources towards the acquisition of technology, than towards the development (e.g., training and education) of the workforce. Simply put, the workforce of the members of the TE-dominated cluster needs better technology to fully utilise their skills; it is the quality of the technology that must play catch up with the quality of the skills.

The results for the SSA-dominated cluster tell a diferent story, for the growth in productivity for this group comes from technology, and not from the eficiency of utilisation of technology. For all intents and purposes, members of the SSA-dominated cluster have a workforce that does not take a full advantage of the available technology. The obvious reason would be associated with the lack of skills and insuficient training/ education. Under such circumstances, from the perspective of crafting a policy, it would not be a wise move to mandate the allocation of resources towards the newer technology. Instead, available resources should be allocated towards workforce development initiatives. The route for further development for the members of SSAdominated cluster is much more expensive and laborious, for a well-educated and skilled workforce can be acquired neither fast, not cheaply. Speaking plainly, the workforce of the SSA-dominated cluster needs better skills to fully utilise their technology; it is the quality of the workforce that must play a catch-up game with the quality of the available technology.

## (1) An approach for developing an appropriate customised data analytic methodology:

The development of our methodology involved the following investigative path: GQ → Theoretical Framework → RQs → SQs → Evaluation Methods → Methodology. We have demonstrated that the resulting methodology could adequately address our illustrative general research question (GQ). Practitioners could use or adapt this investigative path to construct an appropriate methodology that fits their purposes.

## 8.4. Limitations

One of the main limitations of this study, from a practical standpoint, is associated with its inability to capture the role, presence, and the impact of the informal economy (Medina & Schneider, 2018; Schneider & Enste, 2002) in both contexts. While, undoubtedly, both group have its share of shadow economy (Adegoke, 2019; Bayar et al., 2018), and the ranking for some economies could be obtained (Schneider et al., 2010), it is fair to say (in our defence) that accurate estimation of a relative size and impact of each economy in the sample, as well as the relative comparison of TEs vis-à-vis SSA in this regard would require a separate investigation of a non-trivial complexity (Buehn et al., 2010). This could be more adequately addressed in future research. Another limitation of the study is associated with a lack of consideration for the diversity of the sectors of employment of the youth’ labour force. For example, while we have an employment-related data, we are unable to discern the relative size of the labour force per type of industries (e.g., agriculture, manufacturing, high technology, etc.) consequently, we cannot compare economies in this regard vis-à-vis each other and to juxtapose TE vs. SSA. However, this is a very important consideration, because if, let us say, 60% of the youth labour force of SSA is employed in agriculture, while 60% of the youth labour force of TE is employed in high technology manufacturing, then such disparity would account for a large portion of the diferences in the productivity of the overall workforce. This limitation opens an opportunity for future investigations.
