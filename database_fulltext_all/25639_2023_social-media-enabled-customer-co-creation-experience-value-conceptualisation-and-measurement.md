# Social Media-Enabled Customer Co-Creation Experience Value: Conceptualisation and Measurement

Hong Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Patrick Y.K. Chau<sup>b</sup>, Bin Wang<sup>c</sup> and Xin (Robert) Luo <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Management, Wuhan University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, P.R.China; <sup>b</sup>University of Nottingham, Ningbo, P.R.China; <sup>c</sup>Robert C. Vackar College of Business and Entrepreneurship, University of Texas Rio Grande Valley, Edinburg, USA; <sup>d</sup>Anderson School of Management, University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, USA

## ABSTRACT

Despite the dominance of customer co-creation experience value (CCEV) in the value co-creation research, its theoretical domain has not been systematically analysed and developed. Specifically, little is known about the nature of social media-enabled CCEV. To address the knowledge gaps, this study employs a five-step procedure to develop a conceptualisation and instrument of CCEV construct in a social media context. First, drawing on the relevant literature, we determine the conceptual framework, definition, and assumptions of CCEV. Second, we employ grounded theory methodology to analyse customer review data from three representative social media sites and identify the dimensions of CCEV (four themes consisting of 10 dimensions). Third, we create an item pool of CCEV based on the prior work and in-depth interviews of 10 customers. In the fourth and fifth steps, we collect three waves of quantitative data to develop and validate the instrument including item refinement (n = 234), instrument validation (n = 521), and nomological validity examination (n = 615). The results indicate that the instrument exhibits satisfactory reliability and validity. Our work contributes to theory and practice by presenting a contextspecific and fine-grained conceptualisation of CCEV and a reliable and valid survey instrument.

## 1. Introduction

Value co-creation, as a fundamental area of information systems (IS) and marketing research, sheds light on the value creation process from a new perspective. Prahalad and Ramaswamy (2000) originally predict the transformation of customers from passive value recipients/consumers to active co-creators in value creation. In a similar vein, service-dominant (S-D) logic presents a newly customer-centric view of value creation according to which value is co-created with the customers and determined by customers on the basis of value-in-use (Vargo & Lusch, 2004) or value-in-context (Vargo & Lusch, 2008). Value co-creation thus is characterised by an overarching focus on the customers and their proactive engagement in the value creation process (Hollebeek et al., 2019).

The rapid infusion of social media in customer-firm and customer-customer interactions in particular has provided companies with unique and inventive opportunities to harness customers’ innovative potential and knowledge (Zhang et al., 2020). This has resulted in various approaches to collaborate with consumers across all areas of a value chain. For example, Toyota’s Scion brand launched virtual car models in Second Life – a virtual world, for virtual three-dimensional product experiences, and motivates users to create almost anything imaginable while sharing their actions with others. Chinese IT manufacturer Xiaomi encourages customers to actively engage in the creation of new products on the Xiaomi brand community, such as generating and evaluating new product ideas; elaborating, evaluating, or challenging product concepts; and creating virtual prototypes.

In a social media context, the experience-centric and technology-enabled nature of value co-creation heralds the dominance and uniqueness of customer co-creation experience value (CCEV) in this environment (Xie et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020). Although prior studies have confirmed its central role within a nomological network of value co-creation, such as encouraging customer participation and engagement (Füller et al., 2009; Nambisan & Baron, 2007) and determining organisational performance (Fang et al., 2018; Roy et al., 2019), the theoretical construct of CCEV has not been conceptualised in an integrative and consistent way, and a comprehensive survey instrument has not been rigorously developed and validated. Our extensive review of the literature (see Tables A1 and A2 of Appendix A) reveals three ambiguities underlying certain conceptualisations and measurements of CCEV. First, we observe that the conceptualisations of CCEV are rather inconsistent and its technology-enabled characteristics are still vague. The existing research is rooted in diferent theoretical archetypes to conceptualise CCEV in diverse and often contradictory ways (e.g., CCEV is characterised as an experience process versus a derived

outcome). This may lead to equivocal understanding and potential conceptual confounding (Ranjan & Read, 2016). Second, some studies do not proactively take the sociotechnical co-creation context into consideration in the conceptualisations and measurements. They appear to have ignored the interactioncentric and technology-enabled nature of value cocreation and only adapt the conceptualisations and survey instruments from the general experience/ value literature. Third, most extant research develops the measurements of CCEV in a specific co-creation context, such as consumer experience co-creation in smart servicescape (Roy et al., 2019), collective consumption of orchestral music (Kelleher et al., 2019), and brand value co-creation (Merz et al., 2018). Although these multiple and somewhat disparate measurements grounded in specific contexts reveal rich insights on customer value co-creation, these studies do not present a comprehensive view of CCEV on typical social media platforms. In addition, the limited generalisability of these isolated insights is concerning (Hollebeek et al., 2019). In the growing digital economy, social media have become pivotal in bringing together large numbers of customers to share knowledge, spark novel ideas, and collaborate on inventions (Chen et al., 2018). Therefore, a social media-based investigation of CCEV not only addresses recent calls for more value co-creation research that centres around the IT artefact (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Trang et al., 2021), but also resonates with the rubric of social media age and the associated practice evolvement.

Against the backdrop of these issues, we think from the ground up about the general theoretical nature of social media-enabled CCEV to advance the research questions: What is an integrative view of social media-enabled CCEV? What are the core elements of social media-enabled CCEV? And how can we develop a comprehensive empirical measurement? Given the dynamic nature of social mediaenabled customer value co-creation and the inconsistency or ambiguity of existing theories and research, a mixed-methods design, which can provide strong inferences and significant insights, is well suited to our work (Venkatesh et al., 2013). Following the ten-step procedure of MacKenzie et al. (2011), this study employs a mixed-methods design (see Figure 1) to formulate an integrative conceptual framework of social media-enabled CCEV based on a systematic literature review, explore the hierarchical dimensionality of social media-enabled CCEV through a grounded theory methodology (GTM), and develop a survey instrument of social mediaenabled CCEV with a balanced view of the comprehensiveness and parsimony of this instrument.

Our mixed-methods procedure includes five stages: construct domain specification, identification of construct dimensions, construction of items, development of measurement model, and construct validation. In the first stage, we focus on the value co-creation literature to systematically investigate the conceptualisations and measurements of customer co-creation experience/value, and then we integrate the existing knowledge with the peculiarities of social media-enabled customer value co-creation to articulate the conceptual framework, definition, and assumptions of social media-enabled CCEV as our epistemological stance. In the second stage, we employ a partial portfolio of GTM procedures (theoretical sampling, open coding, axial coding, and selective coding) to collect, structure, and analyse the review data from three social media platforms to identify the dimensions of CCEV. In this level, we attempt to match the definition of each dimension elicited from the original dataset with the existing literature. Next, based on the work of the prior two stages and in-depth interviews of 10 customers participating in value co-creation, an item pool of CCEV is generated. We evaluate the face validity and content validity of these items based on expert opinions. In the fourth part, we develop a measurement model and iteratively validate its reliability and validity using the data collected from two rounds of surveys $( \mathrm { n } _ { 1 } = 2 3 4 , \mathrm { n } _ { 2 } = 5 2 1 )$ ). Finally, we further evaluate the psychometric properties and establish the nomological validity of this CCEV construct by the third-round survey $( \mathbf { n } _ { 3 } = 6 1 5 )$

## 2. Specifying the Construct Domain of CCEV

The first stage of our five-stage procedure is to clarify the construct domain of CCEV because the lack of a precise specification may lead to inaccurate conceptualisation of this construct and threaten construct validity (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). This section first analyzes how customer co-creation experience/value is conceptualised and measured in the value co-creation research to build a cumulative research tradition. Then, we delineate the uniqueness of social media-enabled customer value co-creation. Finally, we integrate the novel contextual insights with the cumulative theories and research to determine the conceptual framework, definition, and assumptions of CCEV.

## 2.1. Conceptualisation and Measurement in Value Co-creation Literature

We review the ways in which customer co-creation experience/value has been conceptualised, the levels of analysis at which it has been examined and its roles in value co-creation research. Our literature review in Table A1 of Appendix A shows that CCEV forms the theoretical base for 53% of the studies (23 papers), and is used in hypotheses or models for 33% of the studies (14 papers). The pattern reveals that CCEV plays a prominent role in research. In addition, we note that most literature treats CCEV as an individual-level construct.

Although theoretical and empirical work has blossomed in the past twenty years, investigations of CCEV have not adhered to a consistent theoretical archetype. The three categories used for this conceptualisation of CCEV are: (1) it is characterised as an experience process from an experiential perspective; (2) it is characterised as a derived outcome of customer value co-creation from an output-based perspective; and (3) it is characterised as the emerging customer value from the synergies of service systems through dynamic interactions from a systemic perspective. Although the three categories of conceptualisations are not mutually exclusive, there still exist fundamental diferences among them in terms of theoretical archetype, epistemological assumption, and scope. The first category of conceptualisation is rooted in the theoretical archetypes of S-D logic (Vargo & Lusch, 2004), service logic (Grönroos, 2008), customer-dominant logic (Heinonen et al., 2010), and customer experience (Helkkula et al., 2012; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004), and premises that customer value is inherent in an idiosyncratic experience/consumption process termed as value-in-experience or value-in -use. Due to its solid theoretical foundations, most studies (33 out of 43 papers) employ this conceptualisation. The second category of conceptualisation is consistent with the economics-directed customer value theory, which views customer value as a customer’s ex-post appraisal of the meaningfulness of his/her participation in value co-creation by assessing what is contributed and what is realised (Fang et al., 2018; Nambisan & Baron, 2007). Originated from the theoretical archetypes of service system (Vargo et al., 2008) and service ecosystem (Edvardsson et al., 2011), the third category of conceptualisation emerges and characterises CCEV as value-in-context from a scope of social systems.

We also examine the various ways in which customer co-creation experience/value is operationalised. Within the value co-creation literature, 19 studies directly measure CCEV via survey mechanisms. Table A2 in Appendix A details these studies by context, source, operationalised components, and sample items. While researchers have investigated related value co-creation phenomena, we observe a tendency for the development of diverse measurement tools that engender fragmentation in CCEV research and have only limited applicability. First, most research focuses on a specific value co-creation sphere and develops the measurements of CCEV from diferent perspectives of service experience (e.g., Roy et al., 2019), brand value (e.g., Merz et al., 2018), and new product development (e.g., Füller et al., 2009; Nambisan & Baron, 2007). Although these multiple measurements reveal rich insights on customer value co-creation, they are grounded in specific customer value co-creation domains (for instance, the co-production or usage stage) and fail to present a fuller spectrum of customer value co-creation journey. Second, recognising the potential of IT to revolutionise customer value cocreation, some recent studies develop the measurements in specific IT-enabled contexts, such as smart servicescapes (Roy et al., 2019), video websites with DanMu system (Fang et al., 2018), and Internet of Things (IoT) retail technology (Balaji & Roy, 2017). However, these studies are restricted to a single technology/platform and its specific usage and thus the scopes are relatively narrow.

Motivated by the equivocality of existing understandings of the value co-creation concept, Ranjan and Read (2016) developed a complete conceptualisation and measurement instrument of value cocreation. However, their core efort is specific to this overarching value co-creation concept rather than focusing on customers’ experience value in the emerging sociotechnical co-creation context. Thus, given the ubiquitous penetration of social media that cover a varied collection of technologies or platforms (e.g., social networking sites, micro-blogs, virtual communities, video sharing sites, and virtual worlds) in customer value co-creation, our study situates on the overall social media-enabled customer value cocreation journey to develop a more universal and comprehensive conceptualisation and measurement of CCEV.

## 2.2. Social Media-Enabled Customer Value Co-creation

Social media are a sociotechnical assemblage consisting of technical components and social components, which combine user-generated content with social networking features to create, initiate, and circulate information within online social networks (Castillo et al., 2021). Social media are regarded as an important enabler of customer value co-creation for two main reasons. First, from a technical perspective, social media integrate the latest technological functionalities (e.g., design and evaluation toolkits) and interactivity features to support real time, media-rich, and personalised social interactions among multiple actors (Hofman et al., 2017). The rich interactions facilitate customers to eficiently share information, knowledge, and culture within their online social networks, develop social relationships, integrate complementary digitised resources, and forge dialogical-coordinativecollaboration capability, thus creating innovative solutions and new value (Mandrella et al., 2020).

Compared with other technologies, social media involve customers in a broader value creation space and empower them at an unprecedented level. Second, from a social perspective (i.e., technology acceptance and usage), social media achieve a high market penetration, which unleashes generativity and opens up rich and novel opportunities for customer value cocreation (Zhang et al., 2020). A large number of geographically disperse and heterogeneous individuals use social media to create and circulate diverse and attractive content. The content representing “wisdom of crowds” combined with the interacting energised customers cultivate a fascinating and pleasant atmosphere (Kohler et al., 2011).

Therefore, social media spark the shifts of customer value co-creation in terms of content and form, as well as scope and scale and extend its framework into a dynamic and systematic scope of service ecosystem (Helkkula et al., 2018).

## 2.3. Conceptual Framework, Definition, and Assumptions of CCEV

The dominant theoretical archetypes in the field of value co-creation – S-D logic, service logic, customerdominant logic, customer experience, service system, and service ecosystem – provide a broad understanding about the premises of CCEV. The seminal theoretical archetype – S-D logic – is originally tied to the value-in-use meaning of customer value (Vargo & Lusch, 2004). Furthermore, service logic and customer-dominant logic proposed by Grönroos(2008) and Heinonen et al. (2010) provide a “customerecosystem” lens and characterise value co-creation within a direct customer-provider interaction context. The theoretical lens of customer experience proposed by Prahalad and Ramaswamy (2000, 2004) supports the concept of value-in-experience. Grounded in the S-D logic, the service system view surfaced and has evolved into a promising service ecosystem perspective. These two interdisciplinary theoretical archetypes highlight the sociotechnical contextual nature of value and argue for a compelling notion of value-in-context (Edvardsson et al., 2011).

Grounded in the sociotechnical peculiarities of social media-enabled customer value co-creation, we integrate these theoretical archetypes and present a conceptual framework of CCEV in Figure 2, which schematically visualises its characteristics and connections with four related concepts including social media, service ecosystem, customer experience, and customer value. As diagrammed on the left of the framework, customer experience and value are cocreated through interactions among customers and other actors in social media, which takes place in a wide space termed as value co-creation space and is encapsulated in a broader social context of service ecosystem (Edvardsson et al., 2011). The right-hand side of our framework depicts a summary of the characterisation of CCEV, and its dynamically intertwined, interpenetrating, and overlapping relationship with customer experience and customer value. Grounded in the four propositions of “value in the experience” outlined by Helkkula et al. (2012), we delineate CCEV in a multi-dimensional sense making: (1) from a contextual stand, we characterise social mediaenabled CCEV as individually intrasubjective and socially intersubjective since it embodies not only individuals’ heterogeneous experiences, but also networking social practices, connective action (i.e., actors spontaneously coproduce and circulate content based upon an issue of mutual interest) (Vaast et al., 2017), and shared value-in-context (Kelleher et al., 2019); (2) from a temporal dimension, CCEV is characterised as longitudinal; and (3) from a spatial dimension, CCEV is lived and imaginary/virtual.

In this study, we adopt the perspective of individual customers and define CCEV as the emerging customer value during social media-enabled co-creation experience through social network-wide information sharing, knowledge exchange, and social capital linkage (Zhang et al., 2020), digitised resource integration (Xie et al., 2016), and capability co-evolvement (Mandrella et al., 2020) among customers, firms, and social media platforms resulting in reciprocal wellbeing and synergies. We also note three important assumptions underlying CCEV: (1) CCEV depends on customers’ interactive value co-creation experience; (2) CCEV is context-dependent; and (3) CCEV is a multi-dimensional construct.

## 3. Conceptualisation of CCEV via Grounded Theory Methodology

Due to the novelty of social media-enabled CCEV and the limitation of the current literature, the second stage undertakes an exploratory grounded research for illuminating how customers iteratively make sense of subjective experience value in the social media-enabled co-creation context, and combines the results with the specified construct domain to identify the dimensions of CCEV. This is a critical step for precisely capturing the meaning of CCEV and achieving a theoretical cohesion (Ranjan & Read, 2016).

In line with the phenomenological nature of CCEV (Helkkula et al., 2012), we adopted a partial GTM process (theoretical sampling, open coding, axial coding, and selective coding) as an interpretive research strategy. We chose this GTM to build the theoretical domain of CCEV for three reasons. First, GTM is designed to discover and develop theory. It is particularly relevant for such research domains where limited studies have been conducted and theory building is needed (Wiesche et al., 2017). There is a scarcity of research describing this multi-dimensional theoretical nature of social media-enabled CCEV. This provides an opportunity for this construct to be developed inductively via GTM. Second, GTM “allows the researcher to develop a theoretical account of the general features of a topic while simultaneously grounding the account in empirical observations or data”. The context-dependent assumption of CCEV makes it well suited for a grounded approach. The construct that is both inductively developed and grounded in empirical data has the advantages in novelty and logical coherency (Eaton et al., 2015). Third, GTM has been chosen frequently to study sociotechnical behaviour evolution in emerging IS research domains (Birks et al., 2013; Matavire & Brown, 2013), which provides guidelines regarding its use and thus ensures the rigour of the research contributions.

## 3.1. Theoretical Sampling

We selected Xiaomi’ online community, IBM’s Facebook site, and Threadless’ Instagram account as the loci for our grounded research for the following reasons. First, these three companies – – Xiaomi (a fast-growing manufacturer of mobile phones and smart home devices in China), IBM (an American high-tech company with a large and diverse portfolio of products and services), and Threadless (a promising American company termed as a crowdsourcing platform that empowers users to create and select new T-shirts designs to be produced and marketed) – – involve the high-tech and traditional industries, and represent diverse business models. This sampling method reduces sampling bias and increases data coverage, thus ensuring the completeness and generalisation of the conceptualisation (Ranjan & Read, 2016; Wiesche et al., 2017). Second, Xiaomi’ online community, IBM’s Facebook site, and Threadless’ Instagram account are all exemplary co-creation platforms where large numbers of heterogeneous customers and the enterprise interact with each other to generate an energetic response to two types of topics, namely, firm-sponsored topics and customer-initiated topics. Third, these three platforms are all highly active and successful with respect to engaging customers in value creation activities, such as iteratively contributing, evaluating and improving novel ideas, interactively discussing new product concept prototypes and optimal solution details, testing and experiencing new product/service features, as well as disseminating marketing information. Last, due to the popularity and high profile of these three companies and their associated social media platforms, a rich and extensive body of secondary data can be generated, which allow for an in-depth empirical analysis to achieve theoretical saturation (Chang et al., 2011).

## 3.2. Qualitative Data Collection

Since the regular customer commentaries constitute highly suitable secondary information (Eaton et al., 2015), the empirical data (posts and customer comments) were tracked and collected from publicly available sources on Xiaomi’ online community, IBM’s Facebook site, and Threadless’ Instagram account. We believe that the empirical data provide rich customer experience/value information for the following reasons: (1) relevance as these comments include subjective and idiosyncratic customer perceptions concerning their participation in value co-creation activities (Gunarathne et al., 2017); (2) richness in terms of data volume and content; and (3) comprehensiveness through integrating and cross-checking multiple data sources. Thus, an integrative and detailed qualitative analysis of customer comments provides a productive approach to conceptualise CCEV. First, these data, that is, all posts and comments on these three social media sites during the oneyear period from April 1 2018 to April 4 2019 were scraped on April 7 2019. In total, we obtained 951 posts and 165,150 reviews from Xiaomi’ online community, 260 posts and 4,839 reviews from IBM’s Facebook site, and 608 posts and 8,448 reviews from Threadless’ Instagram account. Then, the original publicly available data were checked and cleaned for accuracy and validity. The first author reviewed all posts to identify those related to the value cocreation activities or the product/service. Using Python software, two Ph.D. students preprocessed the review data and removed nonmeaningful or repetitive reviews. By the end of this process, our dataset contained 1,763 posts and 75,637 reviews for subsequent analyses.

## 3.3. Qualitative Data Analysis

Based on the recommendation by Strauss and Corbin (1990) and Wiesche et al. (2017), we used three forms of coding (termed open, axial, and selective) to code the resulting qualitative data and distill them into the most essential concepts related to CCEV (see Table 1). To ensure the logical coherency of coding, we developed a common coding framework (Kelleher et al., 2019). Based on key constructs in the customer experience/value literature, an initial list of coding categories was developed. This list was refined after a content analysis of 5,000 randomly selected comments to combine literature-based insights and new perspectives gained from these comments. Once the research group agreed on the list, five Ph.D. students who were unfamiliar with the study separately pre-coded the same 5,000 comments. We compared their results and discussed the diferences to reach an agreement on the categories and future coding procedures (Chang et al., 2011). Thus, the final coding scheme was developed to provide not only structure for coding but also flexibility for unearthing novel findings.

We used open coding to identify concepts related to CCEV that were salient in the data, while remaining as open and unconstrained by prior literature or theories as possible. The first and second group of Ph.D. students independently coded all data to transfer them into chunks of meaning, and then compared their interpretations and examined the divergences, which resulted in 1,870 initial codes. Next, the research group iteratively merged the codes with similar meanings and reduced them into 42 sub-categories using the coding scheme, see Column 2 of Table 1. The inter-coder agreement ratio was assessed to ascertain whether these two groups of coders classified the same words into the same sub-category, and the average inter-coder consistency of 0.92 indicates a high reliability of the open coding (Rust & Cooil, 1994).

We considered the peculiarities of social media-enabled CCEV and removed functional value that is independent of the interaction context (Ranjan & Read, 2016). Thus, this axial coding led to the identification of 10 dimensions/constructs.

Subsequently, the selective coding further clustered these 10 constructs to identify higher-order constructs for advancing the development of theoretical parsimony and convergence of value dimensions (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). The dimensions sharing a common theme will be theoretically abstracted to a higher level. Based on Holbrook (2006)’s typology of customer value (extrinsic value that serves instrumentally as means to an end versus intrinsic value that is appreciated for its own sake) and Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, four themes (i.e., second-order constructs) were identified to represent aggregations of these 10 first-order constructs: spiritual value, personal integrative value, personal identified value, and hedonic value (see Column 5 of Table 1).

Spiritual value. Spiritual value is an individual’s experience in obtaining a positive cognitive style, strong spiritual energy, and spiritual growth based on a set of mental assumptions and social support/ esteem leading to better well-being (Zhang & Yu, 2014). It is viewed as a self-justifying end-in-itself and pure psychological satisfaction with mythological appeals (Ranjan & Read, 2016). Spirituality, emotional support, and altruistic value represent diferent aspects of spiritual ecstasy (Holbrook, 2006) and thus are proposed as first-order constructs forming spiritual value.

Personal integrative value. Personal integrative value refers to a sense of being efective in one’s interactions with the social environment and being able to experience opportunities to exercise and exhibit one’s capacities (James et al., 2019). It represents an individual’s tendency to bolster the self-concept and personal achievement to a higher level. In a social mediaenabled co-creation context, customers interact with other actors to integrate digitised resources, combine or exchange knowledge, and build inimitable capabilities to create superior value and synergies (Mandrella et al., 2020). A customer’s knowledge and selfenhancement are fully assimilated into the customer self-actualisation practices and serve instrumentally as the operant resources to facilitate value co-creation (Ranjan & Read, 2016). Thus, knowledge and selfenhancement are proposed as first-order constructs forming personal integrative value.

Personal identified value. Personal identified value is a customer’s experience in obtaining his/her important objectives (James et al., 2019). Economic value and social value as the basic human needs are accepted and owned as personally important (Harsanyi, 1969). This argument is supported by some researchers statements such as “people’s behavior can largely be explained in terms of two dominant interests: economic gain and social acceptance” (Harsanyi, 1969, p. 127). Thus, economic value and social value are proposed as first-order constructs forming personal identified value.

Hedonic value. Enjoyment, aesthetic value, and novelty value represent a good feeling (Füller et al., 2009), a sensory and cultivated response to beauty and artistic forms/expressions (Kelleher et al., 2019), and an intellectual stimulation (Williams & Soutar, 2009). Such subconscious and visceral appraisal is experienced for its own sake and as an end. Since enjoyment, aesthetic value, and novelty value capture the essential meaning of hedonic value that is defined as a customer’s perceived value derived from mentally stimulating, entertaining, pleasant, and enjoyable experience (Kohler et al., 2011), we conceptualise hedonic value as a second-order construct consisting of these three first-order constructs.

Throughout the axial and selective coding phases, a constant comparison among the construct conceptualisation, review data, and the existing literature was employed to iteratively refine the typology of CCEV and the definitions of each dimension and theme. We asked two IS researchers who were unfamiliar with the research domain to provide unbiased feedback on this initial conceptualisation. The iterative process ended when a coding consensus was reached and the theoretical saturation occurred. We also considered if these four second-order constructs could be represented by a third-order construct. However, given that each second-order construct represents a unique value quadrant, we concluded that these second-order constructs could not be abstracted into a higher-level aggregation (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). ## 4. Instrument Development of CCEV

Drawing on our specified construct domain and conceptualisation, we develop a valid and reliable instrument of CCEV. First, we create a comprehensive item pool of CCEV and assess the content validity. Then, we iteratively validate the reliability and validity of the measurement instrument. Finally, we examine the nomological validity of CCEV in its theoretical network.

## 4.1. Item Generation

During the item creation process, we leveraged the findings derived from the open coding to generate the item pool. We also searched the extant literature that examines similar constructs and reviewed the relevant items to identify appropriate items and adapted and added them to the item pool. In addition, we conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews of 10 customers participating in value co-creation activities to provide additional insights to complement and cross-validate the items generated from the literature review and the grounded research, thus assuring the completeness and appropriateness of the item pool. We employed a purposive random sampling method on Xiaomi’ online community to identify eligible subjects for the interview and selected 10 customers who frequently participated in various value co-creation activities on this site (at least once every day) in the last one year. These active customers are believed to have rich opinions and reasonable perspectives about CCEV. The respondents were requested to report their interaction behaviour on this site, and describe how they feel and what they value regarding those interaction activities. We imbibed their narratives into the item pool. Thus, based on systematic literature review, exploratory grounded research, and in-depth interviews, an initial pool of 52 items was generated to capture essential meanings of the 10 constructs outlined in Table 1.

Then, we invited five experts in the IS and marketing fields to evaluate these 52 items. After reading the definitions of 10 first-order constructs and their items, they were requested to rate the face validity of these items and how well each item reflects the corresponding construct by assigning three types of evaluations: “clearly representative”, “somewhat representative”, or “not at all representative” (Anderson & Gerbing, 1991; Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). For these 10 first-order constructs, we retained only items that were evaluated as “clearly representative” by at least four of these experts. This process screened out 8 items and we modified certain unclearly worded items, resulting in 44 items. As shown in Table B1 of Appendix B, 14 items are unique from the grounded research, 28 items are recognised in both the grounded research and the literature, and 2 items are only identified from the literature.

## 4.2. Development of Measurement Model

## 4.2.1. Item Purification and Refinement

To determine the factor structure of these 10 firstorder constructs and purify their instrument, we first performed an exploratory factor analysis. The data were collected through an online survey in July 2019. The survey instrument of 44 items was measured on a 7-point Likert scale, which anchored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). A survey hyperlink was created and posted on the forums in Xiaomi community to recruit those who had participated in value co-creation. We tailored all items to the Xiaomi community context to ensure that all participants had well-established thoughts to make meaningful responses. To encourage participation, we ofered a reward of 8 RMB in the form of virtual cash for each response. Three filter questions were posed at the beginning of the survey requesting respondents to briefly describe their value cocreation activities, the related products/services, and their perceptions, respectively. Only those that had completed these three questions were regarded as actual value co-creators and were displayed the rest of the survey. To ensure that each respondent submitted only one response, each participant’s Internet Protocol address and demographic information were tracked and examined. A total of 254 responses were received over a three-week period. Finally, 234 valid responses remained for subsequent analysis after removing incomplete or invalid data. The respondents were relatively balanced in gender distribution, and the majority (85.9%) were between 20 and 39 years of age. Further, 68.8% of the respondents had undergraduate or higher education, 74.3% were employed, and 75.2% visited Xiaomi community at least once weekly (see Table B2 in Appendix B).

We performed an exploratory factor analysis using SPSS 25.0. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) statistic was 0.945, indicating that the data are amenable for factor analysis (Kaiser, 1974). The principal components analysis with varimax rotation extracted 11 factors with eigenvalues greater than one. Since eight items corresponding to the self-enhancement dimension loaded on two factors, we scrutinised the data and these eight items. We found that the four items that loaded on the first factor and the other four items for the second factor reflect two diferent aspects of selfenhancement, namely self-enhancement from the competency perspective and from the empowerment perspective. Thus, we divided them into two dimensions: competency and empowerment. All items loaded cleanly onto the expected factors (factor loadings > 0.6) except for one item of economic value that was dropped. Next, we performed another round of principal components factor analysis with varimax rotation on the remaining 43 items (the results are showed in Table B3 of Appendix B). These 43 items together explained 86.2% of the total variance. Table 2 presents the final items retained for the reliability assessment and construct validation.

## 4.2.3. Test of Hierarchical Factor Structure

Drawing on the results from the grounded research, CCEV is assessed by four components (spiritual value, personal integrative value, personal identified value, and hedonic value) and their corresponding dimensions, namely 4 second-order constructs consisting of 11 first-order constructs. Our proposed measurement model (a second-order factor model, reflective-formative type) is shown in Figure 4, in which the construct specification is the choice of the author team. According to the decision rules presented by Jarvis et al. (2003), these four secondorder constructs could only be modelled as formative. First, the direction of causality is from the firstorder construct to its intended second-order construct. For example, an increase of spirituality can improve the level of spiritual value but the converse is not necessarily true. Second, each first-order construct represents a unique part of social mediaenabled CCEV. These 11 first-order constructs adequately paint the overall picture, and they are thus not interchangeable. Third, all the correlations between these 11 constructs were below 0.2 (as shown in Table 4), indicating a lack of covariation among them. As for the first-order constructs, the measurement items of every construct are interchangeable and are influenced by their corresponding construct. Thus, these 11 first-order constructs are modelled as reflective.

## 4.3. Nomological Validity Validation

To assess the nomological validity of a construct, it is important to explore the relationships of the focal construct to other constructs in a theoretical structure (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015). Thus, we drew on the social exchange theory to develop the theoretical relationships related to CCEV and examined them.

## 4.3.1. Social Exchange Theory and Theoretical Relationships Development

Social exchange theory, which originated from the neoclassical economics paradigms, grows out of the intersection of economics, psychology, and sociology (Blau, 1964). This canonical theory ofers a meta-theoretical foundation to establish the nomological network of CCEV. Social exchange theory posits that social behaviour is the upshot of a strategic reward-cost calculation process, in which individuals navigating in social contexts select behaviours that maximise their returns (Shi et al., 2014). If the benefits derived from social interactions outweigh the relevant costs, they will continue the behaviour and cultivate the ensuing relationship, or else they will avoid such involvement. Customer value cocreation in social media can be construed as an enacted social exchange process, in which customers endeavour to maximise their co-creation experience value. Thus, drawing on the social exchange theory, we stipulate that a customer’s experienced value during the cocreation process (CCEV) drives his/her ongoing cocreation behaviour.

Recognising customer value co-creation behaviour as a multi-dimensional concept that needs to be specified with more granularity (Yi & Gong, 2013), we articulate specific customer co-creation behaviours that apply in this context. Prior research (Fang, 2008; Saldanha et al., 2017) has identified two types of customer co-creation behaviours: information-intensive and co-development-focused. The former involves information or knowledge sharing activities, while the latter refers to the extent to which the customers participate in co-developing products/services. Because information-intensive behaviour reflects the upstream or input side of value co-creation activities and such behaviour is more prominent and visible in social media (Saldanha et al., 2017), our study focuses on information-intensive behaviour, namely knowledge sharing and information feedback. Knowledge sharing is defined here as a kind of proactive contribution behaviour that customers actively post creative ideas, novel perspectives, unmet needs, valuable solutions or opinions related to a firm’s products/services on a firm-initiated social media site. Information feedback manifests as the responsive contribution that customers provide solicited and unsolicited product/ service usage-related information on a firm-initiated social media site.

## 4.3.2. Measurement and Survey Collection

Eleven first-order reflective constructs of the CCEV scale were measured using the same instrument that was used in Section 4.2.2. The two dependent variables (knowledge sharing and information feedback) were measured with the number of postings by the customer and the number of posts answering peer customers’ product-related queries in the forum in the one month after the completion of the survey, respectively (Nambisan & Baron, 2007; Wu et al., 2015).

The research subjects were Haier Inc.’s customers who were members of the firm’s online community (https://bbs.haier.com/forum). Haier is one of the largest consumer appliances manufacturers in China. It seeks to provide innovative and premium products reflecting modern lifestyles by continually sensing and rapidly responding to evolving market conditions and customer preferences. The predominant nature of social interactions in Haier’s online community involves customers posting ideas on smart home, sharing product-related knowledge and usage experience, ofering suggestions or opinions to peer customers and the firm, as well as answering peer customers product-related queries. Following the closed loop analytical social survey (CLASS) approach proposed by Gunarathne et al. (2017), we collected data in Autumn 2019. First, we employed the same market research firm to conduct the third-round survey on randomly selected members. A total of 738 responses were received between August and September of 2019. After removing incomplete or invalid responses, 615 responses were used to validate the nomological validity of CCEV. Table B2 of Appendix B summarises the respondent demographics on three rounds of surveys. Next, the research firm matched the respondents with their profiles on Hair community and Web scraped their participant behaviour data on October 29, 2019. Thus, the objective data, i.e., the number of postings and answers made by each respondent, were obtained. The correlations of all research variables are presented in Table B4 of Appendix B.

## 4.4. Alternative Model Testing

To further verify the factor structure of our proposed CCEV scale, we conducted a robustness check to compare the nomological and construct validity of CCEV with that of three alternative measurement models (a first-order factor model with 11 reflective constructs, a second-order factor model including 11 first-order reflective constructs and one second-order formative construct, and a third-order factor model with 11 first-order reflective constructs, four secondorder formative constructs, and one third-order formative construct). The results show that the path coeficients from 11 constructs of this first-order factor model to knowledge sharing and information feedback ranged from 0.018 to 0.330, of which two relationships were insignificant. In the second-order and third-order factor models, both the second-order and third-order constructs had a strong influence on knowledge sharing $( \beta = 0 . 7 7 2 , 0 . 7 6 4 )$ and information feedback $( \beta = 0 . 7 1 9 , 0 . 7 1 4 )$ . This infers that our CCEV scale is more strongly related to customer value cocreation behaviour than the first-order model and reveals richer information than the second-order and third-order models. We also statistically compared the three models with our CCEV scale. As Table 7 indicates, the fit statistics and explanatory power of our model were superior to those of these three competing models, and one indicator weight in the second-order factor model was insignificant. These model comparison results suggest that the CCEV scale is preferred over the alternative models, thus further confirming the factor structure of CCEV.

## 5. Discussion

This study develops and validates an integrative conceptualisation and survey instrument of CCEV. Grounded on a systematic literature review and a partial GTM process, we theoretically and empirically propose a precise and context-specific conceptualisation – 4 themes comprising 10 dimensions. Based on exploratory grounded research coupled with literature analysis and semi-structured interviews, we create the initial items and employ a face and content validity assessment to refine them. Once the items are generated, we collect three waves of data to develop and validate our proposed measurement model. The measurement model is found to have a good scale reliability, construct validity, and nomological and predictive validity.

## 5.1. Contributions to Research and Theory

Despite CCEV being an important concept in the IS and marketing disciplines, our literature review reveals a lack of theoretical and methodological clarity on this construct. Our work addresses the research gaps by ofering a context-specific and fine-grained conceptualisation of CCEV and a reliable and valid measurement instrument. This advances the existing knowledge base in the following three ways.

First, our study contributes to a broad value research stream. Value as an elusive concept has been heavily discussed and debated since Aristotle (Vargo et al., 2008). The dominant theoretical archetypes of value co-creation – S-D logic, service logic, customer-dominant logic, customer experience, service system, and service ecosystem reflect on the inner meanings of value and value creation and present three diferent views of value: value-in-use, valuein-experience, and value-in-context (Helkkula et al., 2018). This study orchestrates the fundamental thoughts of value-in-use, value-in-experience, and value-in-context to paint the overall landscape of CCEV (i.e., the conceptual framework) from a perspective of social media-enabled service ecosystem, and elaborate its definition and assumptions from both individual experience and social constructivism perspectives. Furthermore, this study moves beyond the conventional theoretical boundaries to explore the ontological and epistemological meaning of this construct, and systematically develops and presents a clear characterisation and comprehensive instrument on measuring it. The meticulous conceptualisation and comprehensive assessment capture the synchronous, rich, and personalised interactive nature of social media-enabled customer value co-creation and herald the emergence of informed, empowered, and connected “smart customers” who proactively engage and are competent to participate in value cocreation activities and seek higher levels of value (Roy et al., 2019) – spiritual value and personal integrative value. Thus, the new conceptualisation and assessment add novel knowledge to the conventional value theories.

Second, our work contributes to the value cocreation research in three important aspects. The literature review suggests that previous studies in this stream have developed the conceptual models of CCEV and investigated its antecedents/consequents. Although these studies serve as a valuable starting point and contribute to the research domain, they do not provide a novel view of CCEV in social media. Our results from the exploratory grounded research (see Table 1) provide rich and phenomenological descriptions of customer experience value and its genesis across the social media-enabled co-creation journey, which ofer early novel insights into this emerging area of IT-enabled value co-creation and serve as sources of new domain knowledge. In addition, our study makes two contributions to future theory building in the field of value co-creation: (1) our proposed typology of CCEV with 4 themes and 10 dimensions stimulates a more nuanced understanding of this construct and thus motivates the development of models and the ories. We phenomenologically discover 10 value categories grounded in the customer review data, clearly define them, and theoretically consolidate them into four value themes expressing diferent need positions. In this way, our study acts as a critical springboard on which to develop theories in the value co-creation research (Wiesche et al., 2017); and (2) Our measurement model of CCEV facilitates researchers to develop a coherent and cumulative research tradition in this emerging area. It “can be the basis of new theories or can be reframed as theories if other researchers draw on this model to specify and test theories” (Wiesche et al., 2017, p. 694). Furthermore, drawing on the social exchange theory, this study examines the relationship between CCEV and customer value cocreation behaviour. Our results show that customers with diferent experience values during social mediabased co-creation exhibit unique behaviour profiles. The findings reveal a positive value co-creation cycle, with more self-determined value co-creation behaviour promoting the better fulfilment of individual needs and spawning co-created experience value, which in turn, form the basis for further value creation and ultimately lead to a vibrant, adaptative, and balanced ecosystem (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015).

Finally, the mixed-methods paradigm in the development of this CCEV construct makes one contribution to methodology. Our paradigm is integrative in four important aspects. First, we attach considerable importance to the pioneering work (e.g., Helkkula et al., 2012; Holbrook, 2006; Kelleher et al., 2019; Kohler et al., 2011; Nambisan & Baron, 2007; Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004; Ranjan & Read, 2016) and imbibe valuable thoughts into the theoretical domain of CCEV. Second, our data from three social media sites are preferable in richness and completeness for an exploratory qualitative analysis (data-driven inductive research) to compile customers’ digital footprints into a comprehensive picture of customer experience value and thus develop an empirically grounded theoretical understanding of CCEV and enrich the theoretical repertoire. Third, we collect the first-round survey data to purify and refine the initial items and determine their first-order dimensions through an exploratory factor analysis. Fourth, our measurement model is validated by two steps: the second-round survey data are first collected to perform the reliabilityvalidity-hierarchy examination, and then the fourthround data (the third-round survey data and behaviour data) collection is conducted following the CLASS approach to evaluate the nomological validity in conjunction with the reliability and validity of first-order reflective constructs and the validity of second-order formative constructs. Thus, our study simultaneously creates a novel methodological combination for induc tive conceptualisation from an interpretive perspective (epistemological) and deductive instrument development from a positivist perspective (ontological) and ensures methodological coherence. This is in response to IS researchers’ frequent calls for methodological pluralism – mixed research methods and multiple data sources.

## 5.2. Implications for Practice

This study unveils important practical implications for the evolutionary design and implementation of the business model and value co-creation strategies by developing a collection of robust measurement metrics of CCEV.

First, our proposed typology of CCEV infers an overarching implication that practitioners should expand their visions to take a holistic view of customer experience value in social media-enabled value cocreation. Further, our findings suggest that customer experience value is intersubjectively co-created and shared by customers and other actors in a dynamic and interactive co-creation context. This means that companies need to observe their customers in a broad context so as to develop a more penetrative understanding of customer value co-creation practices and how these practices enable value (Kelleher et al., 2019). For example, managers may dive into customers social situations to find out how customers use and experience oferings, uncover the interaction activities that customers are involved in with peer customers, companies or social media platforms, and identify the value that emerges from these co-creation practices and experiences. The immersive in-depth knowledge of customers’ scripts will inform practitioners how to embed products/services in customers’ existing and future contexts and activities, how to facilitate and enhance these customer activities that lead to value emerging and unfolding, and how to fuse the value proposition with customers’ lives, objectives, and aspirations (Heinonen et al., 2010).

Second, the measurement model of CCEV presented by this study assists practitioners in designing concrete value co-creation strategies to create and maintain a compelling customer experience value in all phases of the customer-firm value co creation processes. During the co-creation opportunities analysis phase, our hierarchical model could be used to investigate individualised and diferentiated customer experiential preferences. Our findings suggest that spiritual value, personal integrative value, personal identified value, and hedonic value align with four diferent customer groups: self-transcending and intrinsic-motivated, self-eficiency-focused and extrinsic-motivated, personal-benefits-directed and extrinsic-motivated, and feeling-dominant and intrinsic-motivated. Practitioners may delve into the propensity diferences in technology-driven value co-creation to target the right customer segments and unearth the unmet customer requirements. During the planning phase, our value matrix could serve as a checklist to help managers to identify critical experience value elements that conform with the scope of a firm-initiated co-creation site. For example, if the hedonic value is the espoused value proposition, companies should concentrate on the enjoyment, aesthetic value, and novelty value dimension. During the implementation phase, our comprehensive instrument could be leveraged to guide practitioners on how to improve the desirable experience values. Specifically, our findings reveal an especially promising value component – spiritual value, and an actionable routine to increase it at a fine granularity. During the evaluation phase, practitioners may adapt our commensurable metrics to measure, monitor, and improve the performance of customer value co-creation. For example, practitioners could survey customers regarding their actual experiences to obtain their opinions, existing and potential expectations, or suggestions and incorporate the customer-sourced information into this iteratively tuning co-creation design.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The results of our research should be interpreted with the following limitations in mind. First, this study anchors the conceptualisation and survey instrument of CCEV within a social media setting. Such contextualisation may limit the generalisability of the research findings. However, given the nascent state of building the CCEV construct and the ubiquitous penetration of social media in customer value co-creation (Zhang et al., 2015), we believe that it is important to explore the theoretical domain of this new construct in social media and then in the future extend our theoretical abstractions to other contexts. We invite future research to investigate other sociotechnical contexts to confirm whether our identified typology holds across diferent contexts and/or reveal other meaningful value categories. Second, because of our focus on customer-centric interactions, our conceptualisation from only the customer perspective may not be suficient in a broader sociomaterial context, such as IoT (Hofman et al., 2017). We believe that this represents an opportunity for future research. It would be interesting to explore the meaning of CCEV through the eyes of other actors. For example, researchers may interview enterprise representatives or conduct participant observation to obtain additional opinions and examine how these varied sourced insights complement or overlap with each other. In particular, it may be insightful to consider both human and nonhuman entities on equal ontological footing to conceptualise CCEV based on the customer-centric and noncustomer-centric interactions at a wide range of spatiotemporal depths (Hofman et al., 2017). Third, we recognise two empirical limitations associated with the boundaries of our instrument development. Since we focus on a comprehensive instrument development, we do not collect longitudinal data to examine the time-dependent dynamics of CCEV. An interesting avenue for future research will be to perform a long-term analysis to understand how customer experience value evolves with the unfolding of co-creation action. In addition, our survey instrument as a conventional approach in itself has its limitation. Future studies may employ appropriate procedural techniques in questionnaire design and data collection to control the response biases or other measurement methods to complement the self-reported survey mechanism. Finally, although our second-order factor model has a better explanatory power and model fit than the third-order model, the results are approximate. This infers that a third-order construct may be a better alternative when validating more complex models as a result of its parsimony in a nomological network. Future research could operationalise CCEV as a third-order construct to examine more complex relationships, while our second-order model is preferable when examining the multi-dimensional efects of CCEV.
