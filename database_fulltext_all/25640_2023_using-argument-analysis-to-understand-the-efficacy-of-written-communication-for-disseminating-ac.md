# Using argument analysis to understand the eficacy of written communication for disseminating academic findings to practitioners

Nan Liang<sup>a</sup>, Rudy Hirschheim<sup>a</sup> and Danli Chen<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Stephenson Department of Entrepreneurship & Information Systems, Louisiana State University, 2222 Business Education Complex South, 501 South Quad Drive, Baton Rouge, LA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Marketing, Louisiana State University, 2114-J Business Education Complex South, 501 South Quad Drive, Baton Rouge, LA

## ABSTRACT

While the IS field has devoted significant efort to improving written communication to disseminate academic research findings, little attention has been given to the issue of soliciting feedback from practitioners about these communications. This paper explores the question of how to engage practitioners with written communication that aims to disseminate academic findings. Three empirical studies are undertaken, each analysing various types of written communication using the lens of argument. Our results suggest two archetypes for supporting arguments: a warrant-using approach and a warrant-establishing approach, each of which ofers diferent ways to conceive of engaging with practitioner readers. Several proposals are ofered as potential avenues for better engaging practitioners via written communication.

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 3 July 2020 Accepted 3 December 2021

## KEYWORDS

Argument mapping; argument theory; rigour vs. relevance; academic vs. practitioner communication; disseminating academic findings; toulmin

## 1. Introduction

To better inform practice, a number of leading IS journals have implemented various initiatives to disseminate academic findings to practitioner-oriented readers. These include MISQ’s and ISR’s joint initiatives with the MIT Sloan Management Review (Gupta, 2017; Rai, 2017) and EJIS’s Science2Practice (Te’eni et al., 2017). Even though the end products of these initiatives difer, they all strive to communicate published or accepted academic articles to practitioners.

We argue that these eforts to engage practitioners could be valuable for three reasons. First, developing written communication based on published academic papers does not imply that practitioners should not be involved in the activities of conducting academic research. Instead, these communications could be a valuable complement to other approaches, e.g., research with practice (Rai, 2019), by informing other practitioners who are not immediately involved in the research project. Second, academic publications are institutionalised communication vehicles in the academic community through which scientific system practices self-reproduce (Kieser & Leiner, 2009). Therefore, it is of critical importance to investigate how to communicate this “basic communication element” (Kieser & Leiner, 2009, p. 521) to practitioner readers. Finally, developing written communications based on published academic papers has the potential to equip the academic community with the means to quickly and eficiently broadcast academic findings to practising professionals and the general public. This potential to communicate knowledge quickly and eficiently has merits, especially when narratives from alternative sources, e.g., fake news from social media, are prevalent in society (Allcott & Gentzkow, 2017).

Unfortunately, we do not see any critical systematic analysis in the IS field regarding how to engage practitioners using this specific type of interaction. A lack of attention to this issue leaves important questions unanswered. For example, is it possible to engage practitioners with written communication? If no, why not? If yes, what are the potential barriers to engagement, and how can we facilitate such engagement? Without answers to these questions, we argue that the current endeavours of practitioner communication are at best “fire-and-wait eforts” where we produce communications for the practitioner audience and hope they might respond.

Therefore, we argue that although the academic community could not and should not solely count on developing written communication to inform practice, the efective use of this approach is still worthy of closer examination. Given this purpose, the current article investigates the overarching research question:

RQ: How can practitioners be engaged with written communication that aims to disseminate academic findings?

Note that this research question is broad in nature. It is broad in the sense that concepts used to frame this question are not yet precisely defined. Therefore, in the rest of this paper, we first review the literature and introduce a theoretical lens to investigate written communication, which then leads to redefining the research question. We then present our research design, which consists of three studies. For each study, we discuss our data and findings. Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings.

## 2. Theoretical background

In this section, we first briefly review the literature on the dificulties of developing written communication for practitioner-oriented readers. Then, we introduce Toulmin’s (2003) argument framework as a set of analytical tools to analyse this problem. Finally, we refine the research question into two interrelated problems, i.e., how to develop written communications and how to engage practitioners with these communications.

## 2.1. Dificulties of communicating academic findings

Citing Lang’s (2003) work, Te’eni et al. (2017) summarise the trouble of communicating academic research to practitioners into three major dificulties. First, practitioners do not read academic journals. Second, practitioners and researchers speak diferent languages, e.g., academic jargon vs. professional terms. Third, because of the diferent, or even polarised, environments and worldviews in which practitioners and academicians operate, practitioners usually view academic research as useless and untrustworthy. In the IS field, eforts to disseminate academic findings with written communication typically aim to overcome these three challenges. First, various journals either start new channels, $\mathrm { e . g . , }$ Science2Practice, or use existing channels, e.g., SMR, as alternative outlets for communication with practitioners. Second, academic findings are presented in straightforward language and illustrated with real-world examples (Piccoli,

2019; Te’eni et al., 2017). Finally, to be useful and relevant to practitioners, actionable suggestions are ofered (Piccoli, 2019).

However, we believe these eforts are not enough for two reasons. First, individuals’ understanding of an instance of communication goes beyond the literal comprehension of the text used in the communication. Second, these suggestions focus on how to communicate to practitioners but say nothing about how to engage them with these communications. In the next section, we first introduce Toulmin’s argument framework and then discuss how this framework could address these two gaps.

## 2.2. Toulmin’s argument framework

Toulmin’s argument framework states that whilst reasonable arguments in diferent fields vary significantly with respect to their contents, they nevertheless share structural commonalities: they all have certain components, including grounds, claims, warrants, backings, qualifiers and rebuttals. An argument is a claim that is built on evidence, which is referred to as grounds. Grounds and claims are linked by a bridgelike statement called a warrant. Backings endorse the credibility of the warrant. The validity of the argument is subject to rejection under the condition specified by rebuttals, and the practical force of the argument is implied by qualifier terms such as “possible” and “must” (Toulmin, 2003). Figure 1 illustrates this argument framework and presents an example.

From this perspective, an audience’s acceptance of the argument depends more on the understanding of how the argument’s structural components support the claim rather than the literal comprehension of the text used to present these components. Therefore, approaches to communicate academic findings should go beyond simple means for comprehension of the text used in the communication to include how to present arguments for understanding the claim raised by the argument. In the next section, we apply this theoretical lens to the tasks of communicating academic findings and to refining our research question.

## 2.3. Refining research question

Note that Toulmin’s argument framework does not focus on how to craft arguments that are trustworthy to the receiver; instead, this theoretical perspective illustrates how the structural components of various arguments could aford the potential to solicit meaningful feedback. With this purpose, we conceptualise the task of communicating academic findings to engage practitioners as follows:

Suppose academic findings take the form of a Relationship, i.e., A accounts for B, which is supported by scientific evidence. Then, the task of engaging practitioners with written communication involves two tasks:

Task 1: Presenting arguments to propose action items based on the research findings.

Task 2: Using proposed action items and supporting evidence to engage the practitioners.

With this conceptualisation, we assume that the purpose of communicating academic findings is to provide action items instead of presenting the findings that contribute to academic knowledge. We acknowledge that academic research could also bring to light “false consciousness and inappropriate work relationships” (Lee, 1999, p. 31) prevalent in practice. However, our assumption is still appropriate for two reasons. First, this instrumental view of communicating academic findings is consistent with the purpose of various communications eforts in the IS community. For example, Piccoli (2019) stresses that actionability should be a major focus of manuscripts submitted to MISQ Executive. Second, this instrumental view could serve as a starting point to investigate complex and dynamic issues, as in the case of engaging practitioners.

Regarding these two tasks, we now refine our original research question into three specific ones:

In written communications, considering the proposed action items as arguments in the form of “if we do A (Grounds), B will be achieved (Claim)”:

RQ1: How do current initiatives craft evidence to support these arguments?

RQ2: Are there diferent ways to support these arguments to propose action items?

RQ3: If there are diferent ways to support these arguments to propose action items, how are these diferent ways associated with diferent approaches to engage the audience?

In summary, we assert that engaging practitioners not only requires that the communications are comprehensible but also that these communications have the potential to solicit meaningful feedback, which could be integrated into future research (Perkmann et al., 2021). Therefore, the task of developing communication should focus not only on how to rewrite the paper with straightforward language but also on putting forth the argument of the communication into a format that afords meaningful feedback. Following this logic, we discuss the study design in the next section.

## 3. Research design and findings

Using Toulmin’s framework, we explore the three research questions via three studies. Studies 1 and 2 aim to compare diferent ways to develop written communications (Task 1). Study 3 attempts to explore how this diference could afect the eficacy of engaging practitioners (Task 2). Table 1 presents an overview of the three studies in this paper.

## 3.1. Study 1: how current initiatives present evidence to support the proposed actions

In Study 1, we analyse 7 articles at SMR that are rewritten from published MISQ and ISR articles and 34 Insights that are published at Science2Practice. Note that we do not claim that this is a statistically representative sample of communications to practitioners; instead, we have chosen them because they represent current institutional eforts to rewrite published IS research to inform and engage practitioners. It is this institutional issue that we analyse.

## 3.1.1. Coding procedure and results

We follow a three-step coding procedure, modified from the method proposed by Fletcher and Huf (1990), to identify the arguments that propose action items and whether the warrants or backings of these arguments are presented in the rest of the communication. Two coders first jointly identify action items in these communications. Then, two coders jointly identified the main claims and grounds of these suggestions. Finally, two coders jointly judge whether the warrants or backings of the identified arguments are presented in the rest of the communication. Two coders agree on all coding decisions.

## 3.1.2. Findings of study 1

We identified 121 action items in total. After coding all action items into arguments in the form of “If you do A, B will be achieved”, we found that all arguments are supported by warrants or backings that are based on the evidence presented in the corresponding academic paper. For example, Kim et al. (2018) found that the relationship between the level of piracy and producer welfare exhibits a curvilinear correlation. Based on this finding, Kim et al. (2019) propose that companies should only focus on high-profile piracy, not minor piracy (grounds), to achieve higher profit (claim) because of the curvilinear correlations identified in Kim et al. (2018) (warrant).

## 3.1.3. Discussion of study 1

The findings of Study 1 are interesting because they indicate that although the two formats of communications vary significantly – the Science2Practice initiative summarises research articles into bullet points, while MISQ’s and ISR’s initiatives rewrite academic papers for publication in SMR – they both follow the same pattern from an argument framework perspective, i.e., all arguments are supported by warrants or backings that are based on the evidence presented in the academic paper. Figure 2 illustrates this pattern.

## 3.2. Study 2: are there diferent ways to support the action items

In Study 2, we expand the subjects of the study to articles that are not developed by IS journal initiatives. The purpose of Study 2 is to explore whether there exist diferent ways to support the proposed action items in addition to the pattern we identified in Study 1. With this purpose in mind, we collected all articles published in SMR in a six-month period, from July 2020 to December 2020, under the Managing Technology category (Sloan Management Review, 2020). After excluding webinars and interview transcripts, 22 articles remained. We then applied the same procedure as that of Study 1 to this dataset.

## 3.2.1. Counterintuitive findings

Our speculative interpretation of the findings in Study 1 was that because academic findings are indeed supported by scientific evidence, communications developed per these findings would reflect this evidence. According to this initial interpretation, we raise the question that, in the case of a communication that aims to disseminate “nonacademic” findings, e.g., a successful business case or personal experience, which are not supported by rigorously applied scientific methods, how does this instance of communication thematise the “nonscientific” evidence to support the proposed action items?

Our initial speculation was that “nonscientific” evidence, e.g., personal experience and successful cases, would be thematised diferently than scientific evidence. However, after we coded the articles collected for Study 2, we found that the results were qualitatively diferent from what we had expected. What we found is that instead of putting forward warrants or backings in a diferent way, many of the articles do not contain warrants or backings for the proposed action items other than some brief elaborations. For example, Dutt (2020) proposed three approaches to combat what he termed “digital pollution”, viz. having a clear vision, being sensitive to vision compromise, and taking social responsibility. However, for the most part the article does not mention how these three actions will indeed contribute to reducing digital pollution, i.e., warrants and backings for these arguments are completely missing. Instead, they present three major problems: digital pollution erodes public trust, polarises people, and causes mental illness. In light of this surprising result, we first discuss this counterintuitive finding and the changed procedure to analyse the data.

## 3.2.2. Warrants vs. refutations of potential rebuttals

For Toulmin (2003), statements in the form of “Given specific grounds, because of A, so claim B” is a misnomer, because A could either serve as a warrant linking the grounds to claims, or as a refutation of potential rebuttals claiming there are no exceptions. For example, suppose one person came into a room, and a second person came into the same room later. Then, regarding the question of how many people are in the room, the reasons supporting the claim that “there are two persons” could be a warrant, e.g., “Because 1 + 1 = 2, so there are two persons”, or a refutation of potential rebuttals that exclude exceptional scenarios when the warrant $^ { \mathfrak { a } } 1 + 1 = 2 ^ { \mathfrak { n } }$ should not apply. For example, the statement “Because the room was empty before the first person came in, so there are two persons” supports the claim that there are two persons by arguing that $\mathbf { \dot { \bar { 1 } } } + 1 = 2 ^ { \prime }$ , not $\displaystyle { ^ c } 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 { ^ \prime } ,$ , and is the correct warrant that should be used to calculate the number of people in the room.

Using Dutt (2020) as an example, indeed, statements such as “digital pollution polarises people” do not address whether the proposed action items will indeed lead to the mitigation of digital pollution. However, they do support the action items by guarding them against the sorts of rebuttals that claim digital pollution is not important at all. For example, by describing how social media platforms polarise people, Dutt (2020) rebuts potential rebuttals such as “companies should focus on creating jobs, not solving digital pollution”. With this insight, we next analyse and compare the two datasets, i.e., the articles that are written from MISQ and ISR publications (referred to as initiative articles) and the SMR Managing Technology articles.

## 3.2.3. Coding procedure and results

Compared to the coding procedure in Study 1, the first two steps of the new procedure are the same. However, in the final step, we expand our search to include not only warrants and backings for these arguments but also refutations of potential rebuttals. Two coders agreed on all coding decisions.

## 3.2.4. Findings of study 2

For the 22 action items identified in the IS journal initiative articles, all warrants or backings were presented to support the arguments proposing action items. However, for 96 action items identified from the articles in Managing Technology, 82 of them have only refutations of potential rebuttals but no warrants or backings. Table 2 summarises the findings of Study 2.

## 3.2.5. Discussion of study 2

From an argument perspective, although Managing Technologies articles do not focus on warrants, they explicitly argue that the problem at hand is “covered” (Toulmin, 2003, p. 99) by the warrant. Regarding an argument in the form of “if we do A, B will be achieved”, stating that a problem is “covered” by the argument could be interpreted as either claiming that there is no better choice than A, i.e., so the problem is “covered” by the proposed action item, or stating that there is no more important thing than B, i.e., so the problem is “covered” by the results of the proposed actions. For example, by arguing that digital pollution causes the loss of public trust, polarisation of people and mental illness, these societal problems are “covered” by arguments such as “If we do A, digital pollution will be solved” (Dutt, 2020).

Reflecting on the distinct functions of warrants and rebuttals, we construe the argument for action items as a two-stage process. The first stage is a rebuttal stage, during which the arguers position the proposed actions to the problem by presenting refutations of potential rebuttals. The second stage is a warrant stage, during which the arguers present warrants and backings to demonstrate the eficacy of the proposed actions. With this two-stage conceptualisation process, we propose two diferent patterns to propose action items, as shown in Table 3. Next, we use two examples to illustrate these two patterns.

Dutt (2020) is used as an example to illustrate the first pattern, which explicitly argues in the rebuttal stage but implicitly assumes in the warrant stage:

Rebuttal Stage: The arguer explicitly argues that the problem on hand, i.e., loss of public trust, polarisation, and mental illnesses is “covered” by the suggested action to mitigate digital pollution because digital pollution erodes public trust, polarises people, and causes mental illness.

Warrant Stage: The arguer implicitly assumes the eficacy of the proposed action items by assuming the warrant “if the leader has a clear vision, then the company would contribute to the mitigation of digital pollution” as valid, without demonstrating why a clear vision actually leads to the claimed mitigation.

Similarly, Kim et al. (2019) illustrate the second pattern, which implicitly assumes in the rebuttal stage but explicitly argues in the warrant stage:

Rebuttal Stage: The arguer implicitly assumes that the problem on hand, i.e., the various problems that companies face, as “covered” by the argument “if the company handles piracy properly, then better welfare will be achieved”, without demonstrating either why there is no better choice than handling piracy to achieve welfare or why welfare is more important than other goals.

Warrant Stage: The arguer explicitly argues why the argument the “median level of piracy actually leads to the welfare of the producer” is valid by presenting evidence that backs up this relationship as the warrant and backings of arguments proposing action items.

What this implies is that although neither Dutt (2020) nor Kim et al. (2019) is ideal in the sense they both implicitly assume something without explicitly arguing it, they serve their purpose well if we consider the purpose of the former (Dutt, 2020) as warrantusing and the latter (Kim et al., 2019) as warrantestablishing. Specifically, by describing the harms of digital pollution, Dutt (2020) justifies the argument that we need to do something about the digital problem, i.e., linking the results of the proposed actions to the problem. Similarly, by presenting evidence regarding the relationship between the level of piracy and producer welfare, Kim et al. (2019) justify the argument that if companies handled piracy properly, then they would achieve better profits, i.e., proving the link between the proposed actions and results.

We can see that the classification schema presented in Table 3 does not use the action items per se to classify them. Instead, it considers how reasons support the proposed actions, i.e., either as a refutation of potential rebuttals or as warrants and their backings. In the next study, we investigate how these two patterns of supporting arguments are associated with diferent types of feedback they could possibly trigger.

## 3.3. Study 3: are these diferent types of arguments associated with corresponding types of feedbacks?

We speculate that the types of feedback triggered by the arguments might match the type of arguments. Specifically, if the action items are put forward in a warrant-using style that focuses on the description of the problem to justify the use of an action, then it is likely that feedback would also engage with these descriptions of the problem. On the other hand, if the action items are put forward in a warrant-establishing style that stresses the eficacy of the actions, then the feedback would likely focus on the actions per se.

To test this belief, Study 3 compares two wellknown argumentation bases, viz. Carr’s “IT Doesn’t Matter” and associated commentaries in the Harvard Business Review (HBR); and MISQ’s special issue on “IS research relevance”. In this section, we first discuss the rationale for choosing these two debates. Then, we present the coding procedure and findings.

## 3.3.1. Carr’s (2003) vs. Benbasat and Zmud (1999): Two Diferent ways to propose action items

The main reason we choose these two debates is that they are consistent with the two patterns shown in Table 3. Specifically, Carr (2003) first discusses IT’s disappearing potential to gain competitive advantage, e.g., IT is near the end of its buildout. Based on this description of the problem, Carr proposes action items regarding the use of IT, e.g., companies should spend less on IT; otherwise, they may end up at a cost disadvantage.

Using the framework in Table 3, Carr’s presentation of the reasons does not support the eficacy of the action items, only their use. Thus, we cannot judge the eficacy of these actions based on Carr’s article because the reasons to support the proposed actions are absent.

For example, even if it is true that IT is near its buildout, is a commodity, and is standardised, does less spending on IT truly benefit the company, i.e., action item 1?

In contrast, Benbasat and Zmud’s (1999) article represents a diferent way to support the proposed action items. For example, they described one problem of the IS field as placing too much emphasis on rigour over relevance. Therefore, they suggest that IS researchers should select topics that are relevant to practice. In this case, the description of the problem indeed supports the eficacy of the action. Interestingly, Benbasat and Zmud (1999) do not extensively discuss the necessity of their recommendations, namely, is it necessary to improve the relevance of IS research? IS researchers might agree. However, how about IS practitioners who have their own rules of practice and do not rely on the IS research community (Lang, 2003)? Additionally, researchers in other disciplines, e.g., computer science, might be indiferent to the relevance of IS research. In this sense, Benbasat and Zmud (1999) implicitly assume the necessity of their recommendations to improve the relevance of IS research without explicitly arguing for it.

## 3.3.2. Coding procedure and results

Following the coding procedure proposed by Fletcher and Huf (1990), we first identify arguments proposing action items in Carr (2003) and Benbasat and Zmud (1999). We then identify counterarguments appearing in corresponding commentary articles. Finally, two coders decide how the counterarguments challenge those arguments that propose action items, i.e., either as challenges to warrants or as rebuttals. Two coders agreed on all coding decisions.

## 3.3.3. Findings of study 3

We identified 34 counterarguments in the commentaries responding to Carr (2003). None challenges the warrants of the proposed actions. Instead, they all challenge Carr’s arguments regarding why IT could no longer be used to gain competitive advantage, i.e., challenging the proposed actions as rebuttals.

We identified 19 counterarguments in three articles responding to Benbasat and Zmud (1999). Only 2 of them argue that there is no strong evidence showing IS research lacks relevance, so the actions Benbasat and Zmud proposed may not “cover” the problem at hand, i.e., challenging the action items as rebuttals. All other 17 counterarguments directly debate whether the actions proposed lead to the relevance of IS research, i.e., challenging the warrants of the proposed actions.

## 3.3.4. Discussion of study 3

Although there are various perspectives to interpret the diferences between the two debates, from the argument framework perspective, we interpret the findings of Study 3 as follows: How an audience engages with an argument not only depends on the characteristics of the texts used to present the argument, e.g., academic jargon or straightforward language, but is also associated with how reasons are thematised to support the arguments. Specifically, if reasons are presented as refutations of potential rebuttals to position the action items to a problem, the audience tends to engage with the discussion of this positioning. Similarly, if reasons are presented as warrants and backings to support the eficacy of the actions per se, the audience also tends to engage accordingly. Table 4 summarises how the two debates reflect this match.

These matched argument-feedback pairs represent, we argue, that the arguer and audience need to share a consensus regarding what should be taken for granted and what should be argued before the communication. This consensus makes it possible that the audience’s feedback has the potential to be integrated into the arguer’s argument; therefore, communication could possibly lead to a two-way dialogue. We next discuss how mismatches between arguers and audiences might contribute to the failure to engage the audience.

3.3.4.1. Intention of the arguer and expectation of the audience. We classify how arguers argue and what audiences expect, according to what is explicitly argued and what is implicitly assumed, as a 2 × 2 matrix shown in Figure 3. Using this framework, the two debates analysed in Study 3 represent situations in which the audience’s expectations match the arguers intentions, i.e., I and III. 3.3.4.2. Arguers intend to establish warrants, whereas audiences expect to use warrants. For a proposed suggestion in the form of “if we do A, B will be achieved”, if the arguers explicitly argue for the suggestion per se, without positioning A or B relative to the problem on hand, then those audiences who expect an explicit argument for the positioning would be confused. Corley and Gioia (2011, p. 21) present an anecdote that is quite telling about this mismatch: In the early 1980s, an oficer of the Academy of Management (AoM) hosted a number of high-level corporate managers at an AoM meeting. At the end of the day, the entourage members were asked what they thought about academic work. After an “uncomfortable silence”, “One of the executives thought it over for a few pregnant seconds, and said simply, ‘You people talk funny’”.

Using the framework in Figure 3 to interpret this anecdote, we believe that practitioners feel “You people talk funny” not necessarily because these academic sessions are filled with academic jargon but because academics explicitly use all kinds of evidence and reasons, attempting to establish warrants that those practitioners have already taken for granted. For example, suppose a group of practitioners attend a research symposium, during which various studies are presented aiming to explore factors afecting the outcome of software development teams. Would we be surprised if a conversation like the following occurred among these practitioners: “At first it was too hard to understand: all sorts of equations, tables, and jargon that I never heard of. However, I picked up their points before I almost fell asleep: more and better customer involvement lead to better software.<sup>1</sup> This is hilarious, who doesn’t already know this”.

In summary, this mismatch presents the scenario in which arguers present rigorous reasoning and strong evidence to support suggested actions, i.e., warrantestablishing; however, the suggested actions are not compared or positioned with their existing counterparts, especially those that are prevalent in the audience’s community. In this sense, although communication is elegant, it is not rooted in the current practices of the audience. This is the so-called “Ivory Tower” mismatch.

3.3.4.3. Arguers intend to use warrants, whereas audiences expect to establish warrants. For a proposed suggestion in the form of “if we do A, B will be achieved”, if arguers explicitly position either A or B relative to other alternative solutions and goals, respectively, without proving why A actually leads to B, then those audiences who expect explicit evidence endorsing this relationship would be sceptical. An example of this could be when an academic reviews a consultant report, e.g., Gartner Magic Quadrant “Research” (Gartner, 2021). The Magic Quadrant proposes that two dimensions, i.e., completeness of vision and ability to execute, could be used to select leaders for diferent tasks (Gartner, 2021). According to the Magic Quadrant, individuals who are high on both dimensions should be placed on tasks positioned for tomorrow, i.e., “high completeness of vision and high ability to execute → high performance of tasks positioned for tomorrow”. By explicitly discussing why individuals’ vision and ability to execute are important features, the Magic Quadrant “Research” indeed links the grounds of this suggestion, i.e., an individual’s high completeness of vision and high ability to execute, to an equivocal problem, e.g., how to select leaders for a technology market. However, it does not provide any evidence regarding why the grounds actually lead to the claim. Therefore, if the audience expects to know the validity of this relationship per se, they will be sceptical due to the simple fact that the Magic Quadrant does not bother to prove this relationship. We thus refer to this mismatch as one in which audiences criticise arguers as building a “Sandcastle” that has no solid base.

3.3.4.4. Dificulties to engage practitioners with written communication. According to the framework presented in Figure 3, we believe that the fundamental dificulties in engaging practitioners with written communications are not so much that practitioners disagree with the academic findings or proposed actions. In contrast, if arguers’ intentions are aligned with audiences’ expectations, then this disagreement could lead to meaningful feedback and, eventually, mutual learning (Habermas, 1984).

Additionally, although the dificulties of engaging practitioners are often construed as practitioners inability, or indiference, to comprehend academic jargon and esoteric language (Te’eni et al., 2017), approaches such as rewriting academic articles using straightforward language are insuficient to engage practitioners. As Figure 3 implies, even if audiences have the motivation to engage with arguers and the ability to literally comprehend arguers’ arguments, the mismatches between arguers’ intentions and audiences’ expectations prohibit the possibility of meaningful engagement. This is because audiences will only be engaged on subjects that they expect to be explicitly argued; however, if these subjects are only implicitly assumed by the arguers, the audience and arguers thus lose the common ground on which to engage.

In sum, the findings of our three studies answer the following three research questions:

In written communications, considering the proposed action items as arguments in the form of “if we do A, B will be achieved”:

RQ1: How do current initiatives craft evidence to support these arguments?

A1: Current initiatives rewrite or illustrate scientific evidence into simple language or real-world examples. This processed evidence is used as warrants and backings to support the proposed action items in the form of “if we do A, B will be achieved”.

RQ2: Are there diferent ways to support these arguments to propose action items?

A2: Yes, there exists another way to support the proposed action items in the form of “if we do A, B will be achieved” by providing evidence or reasons that function as refutations of potential rebuttals of the argument.

RQ3: How are these diferent ways associated with diferent approaches to engage the audience?

A3: Engagement is most likely to happen when arguers’ intentions, i.e., warrant-using or warrantestablishing, match audiences’ expectations, i.e., warrant-using or warrant-establishing.

## 4. Discussion and implications

There is no doubt that written communications to practitioners should be based on credible and scientific evidence. However, our findings imply that, in the context of warrant-using arguments, practitioners do not bother to confirm or falsify the warrant because the validity of the warrant is already presumed in this type of communication. In light of this, we propose two possible ways to engage practitioners while developing warrant-using communications.

## 4.1. From “developing communications for practitioners” to “developing communications with practitioners”

Our findings suggest that the key to engaging practitioners with warrant-using arguments is to position academic findings within the prevalent industrial practices and views. However, this is easier said than done, as it is the practitioners who possess the wisdom to choose among possible actions to act on regarding an equivocal phenomenon. Therefore, if an academic is not at the same time a practitioner, they are unlikely to have the proficiency to position the findings. In this sense, the current model of developing communications, i.e., academics are primarily responsible for crafting communications for practitioners, hampers the chance that these communications could engage practitioners from the very beginning.

Therefore, we suggest that academics should no longer only consider practitioners as the audience but also as coproducers of the communications, i.e., developing communications with practitioners. The inputs sought by academics in this collaboration should not be limited to learning professional terms or real-world examples but also practitioners’ opinions and expertise regarding how the proposed actions rebut current industrial practices or are rebutted by them.

## 4.2. From “translating scientific evidence to support the action items” to “translating the theorized storyline to position the action items”

Although the proposal of “Developing Communications with Practitioners” sounds appealing, the reality is that academics and practitioners belong to two diferent Communities of Practice (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003). Therefore, it might be unrealistic to reach out and involve the right practitioners in every case. We thereby propose an alternative approach that could possibly be conducted by academics alone.

An alternative to developing warrant-using communications is to rewrite the “theorised storyline” (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 2006, p. 25). For example, the theoretical background sections of academic papers, instead of presenting scientific evidence, e.g., the research design and data analysis sections, could communicate academic findings. Using TAM (Davis et al., 1989) as an example, instead of presenting reasons and examples demonstrating how ease of use and usefulness lead to IT acceptance, we could possibly develop stories as follows:

Research: Davis et al. (1989) compared the Theory of Reasoned Action and Technology Acceptance Model in the Theoretical Background, arguing that TAM would be a better model to explain users’ acceptance of IT.

Proposed Storyline: While thinking about important questions such as which car to buy, on the one hand, we want the car we love (Reflecting the attitude construct in TRA). On the other hand, sometimes we also consider how others see us in our cars (Reflecting the subjective norm construct in TRA). Now consider another example: most people neither love nor hate their hammers, nor do they care about what hammers others might use (rebutting attitude and subjective norm construct in TRA. Instead, we apply two simple criteria while choosing a hammer: first, is it easy to use? Second, is it useful to me? When thinking about an IT investment, the first question companies should consider is the following: What is IT to your company: your beloved car or just a tool to do its job, like a hammer?

This example illustrates the possibility of developing warrant-using communications, not based on the scientific evidence of academic findings but on the theorised storyline. This task might be easier for academics to perform because the entry barriers for academics to write communications such as this will be lower than the one proposed in the previous section, which requires intensive involvement of practitioners during the process of developing communications.

## 5. Limitations and future research

One limitation of this paper is that it does not explicitly position the findings and discussions in the literature base regarding the relevance of academic research. Such literature includes but is not limited to engaged scholarship (Van de Ven, 2007), design science (Baskerville et al., 2018), action research (Avison et al., 2017) and clinical research (European Journal of Information Systems, 2020). While such literature is valuable, our paper is diferent in that it focuses on how to engage practitioners with written communication without assuming practitioners will automatically respond to the communications that academics develop. As such, there is not a wealth of prior work that applies the argument perspective, within which this research could be positioned

This limitation concomitantly presents directions for future research. For future research, we plan to investigate how the argument framework could be applied to other scenarios when academics collaborate with practitioners, e.g., action research. We believe this direction is feasible and likely fruitful because the analysis conducted in this paper reflects a central question regarding how reasons are raised and exchanged among diferent parties in a way that could lead to mutual learning. In this sense, we believe the findings in this paper could add meaning to other practices for engaging practitioners.

## 6. Conclusion: Beyond Written Communication

In this article, we analysed the written communication developed by major IS journals to engage practitioners from an argument perspective. To conclude, we extend the discussion beyond written communication to briefly reflect on how untested claims could possibly sneak into the warrant-using discourse in credible clothing. Note that because our data analysis focuses on the patterns of arguments in written communication, our discussion here is only informed speculation, which intends to provoke future discussion, instead of drawing decisive conclusions.

Consider the case where some public figure states that no one knows more than he does, and then suggests people should inject disinfectant into their bodies to cure COVID-19. If we consider this suggestion using the framework proposed in Table 3, we can see that it indeed follows the pattern of a warrantusing argument: At the rebuttal stage, this hypothetical arguer explicitly argues that no one knows more than he does, with the purpose of rebutting other treatments of COVID-19. If this hypothetical arguer achieved numerous material trophies in the past, e.g., money, celebrity, etc., then someone might actually believe the assertation that “no one knows more than he does”. Then, in the warrant stage, he claims the eficacy of a dangerous suggestion, i.e., injecting disinfectant, to solve the problem at hand, i.e., curing COVID-19. Unfortunately, the eficacy of this nearly murderous suggestion is implicitly assumed in the context of a warrant-using argument. Furthermore, if the audience also expects a warrant-using argument, e.g., we should do something to cure COVID-19, they would actually take the eficacy of this suggestion for granted.

It is not our intention to relate this hypothetical arguer to any real person. Instead, we use this example to demonstrate that the fundamental problem of warrant-using arguments is that warrants and backings are taken for granted without being tested. Additionally, this problem, we argue, might not be solved by another warrant-using argument, e.g., arguing that this hypothetical arguer actually knows nothing about medical science, but only by warrantestablishing arguments that actually test the warrant. Although the test result is absolutely predictable and we suspect it is inhuman to test it even on animals, it might be necessary to illustrate how ridiculous this warrant is to those who propose it in the first place!
