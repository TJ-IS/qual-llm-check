# An empirical study of community cohesiveness, community attachment, and their roles in virtual community participation

Dan J. Kim<sup>a</sup>, Mark Salvacion<sup>b</sup>, Mohammad Salehan<sup>c</sup> and Dae Wan Kim<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Technology & Decision Sciences, G. Brint Ryan College of Business, University of North Texas, Texas, U.S.A; <sup>b</sup>School of Business, Yeungnam University, 280 Daehak-ro, Gyeongsan, Gyeongbuk, Republic of Korea; <sup>c</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, College of Business Administration, California State Polytechnic University, Pomona, CA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Although active participation of members is a critical success factor for virtual communities, few members actively participate in activities in virtual communities (e.g., posting, commenting, sharing, etc.). This study explores the diferences between passive and active community participation from community cohesiveness and community attachment perspectives. Drawing upon a context-specific cohesiveness-attachment-participation framework based on cognition-afection-conation traits of human psychology, we propose a research model and empirically test the roles of community cohesiveness and attachment in virtual community participation. The results suggest that community members’ active participation is positively influenced by both community cohesiveness and community attachment. Visitation, as the passive mode of participation, is influenced by community attachment only. The findings not only inspire future IS research to consider community cohesiveness, attachment, and participation as vital research constructs in other social contexts, but it also helps managers build managerial actions, such as prioritising their limited business resources to increase community members’ active participation, for the success of virtual communities.

## KEYWORDS

Virtual communities; community attachment; community cohesiveness; visitation; active participation; identity; prominence; dependence

## 1. Introduction

Virtual communities are virtual spaces where “people come together with others to converse, exchange information or other resources, learn, play, or just be with each other” (Kraut & Resnick, 2012, p. 1).<sup>1</sup> With their rapid proliferation and availability, virtual communities have become popular places for people to hang out, socialise, and interact with others (Jarvenpaa & Tuunainen, 2012; Leidner et al., 2018). Businesses are increasingly aware of the importance of virtual communities. Virtual communities create value for their participants by serving as collective spaces for the flow of (often tacit) knowledge (Faraj et al., 2016). For example, online brand communities reduce customer support cost, boost brand exposure and credibility, provide insights into new products, services, or features, serve as a channel through which new products and services are presented before oficial launch (MarketingInsiderGroup.com, 2019), and improve customer retention (Adjei et al., 2010).

Community members’ participation is crucial to the success of any community (Ren et al., 2012). Participation represents community members’ involvement and engagement in community activities, making the community more sustainable (Tsai et al., 2012). A popular rule of thumb known as the 90-9-1 rule highlights the inequality of participation in online communities by suggesting that 90 percent of members of online communities are passive consumers of information (e.g., lurkers), while 9 percent occasionally participate, and only 1 percent actively contribute most of the contents (Nielsen, 2006). In virtual communities, participation can be classified into two types: passive and active (Kumar & Nayak, 2019; Ridings et al., 2006). Examples of active participation include posting and commenting. Such active behaviours are referred to as prosocial behaviours and are concerned with group preservation (Buck, 1999; MacLean, 1990). It has been suggested that the amount of posts and comments for each member or per active member reflects the level of prosocial participation for a community (Preece, 2001).

While previous research has extensively investigated predictors of active participation in virtual communities, little attention has been paid to passive participants. Passive participants (i.e., lurkers) are users that log in to read posts but rarely submit any posts or comment on posts submitted by other users. Although lurkers do not directly contribute to communities, they are not necessarily selfish freeriders (Preece et al., 2004). Rather, lurking should be viewed as a lost opportunity for collaboration. From a service provider’s perspective, both types of participation are important for the virtual community’s success: Active users are core content generators, while passive users contribute to trafic on servers and respond to advertisements. Thus, to understand the determinants of success in virtual communities, it is important to study both types of participation.

Previous research has attempted to identify determinants to participate in communities in general (e.g., Algesheimer et al., 2005; Beal et al., 2003; Brewer & Caporael, 1990; Brief & Motowidlo, 1986; Caporael et al., 1989) and online communities in particular (e.g., Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2006; Barker, 2009; A. L. Blanchard & Markus, 2004; Ma & Agarwal, 2007; Ray et al., 2014; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014). Many previous studies focused on intrinsic factors with applications of social theories (e.g., social exchange theory, social identity theory, and social capital theory) to explain the relationship between members and the community and how this relationship influences active participation (e.g., Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002, 2006; A. L. A. L. Blanchard et al., 2011; Chih et al., 2017; Chiu et al., 2006; Hsu et al., 2018; Ma & Agarwal, 2007; Shen et al., 2006; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). In this approach, passive participation is overlooked and the IT artefact (i.e., the website/platform) is not studied, although technology can be treated as a social actor, and thus, can be the target of afect (Shank, 2010). On the other hand, there is evidence suggesting that participation in virtual communities can stem from habit (Ray et al., 2014). However, previous research has not thoroughly investigated habitual motivation for participation. Thus, it is important for research on virtual communities to i) distinguish between active, prosocial participation and passive participation (i.e., mere visitation), ii) bring the IT artefact into virtual community research and study how feelings towards the community conflate with feelings towards the platform, and iii) take into account the habitual motivations for participation.

To address the first gap, we identify diferent types of participation based on the degree of engagement on a continuum of prosocial activities (Ridings et al., 2006). The second and third gaps are addressed by studying perceived community cohesiveness and community attachment as determinants of participation. Perception-oriented cognition and emotional sense of bond to a virtual community, namely community cohesiveness and community attachment, respectively, can enhance the quality of contribution (Preece, 2000; Ren et al., 2012). Community cohesiveness refers to the extent to which members are attracted to the community and to each other by common social goals or cultural obligations (Chidambaram, 1996). It has been viewed as a significant force for increasing members’ involvement which consequently influences outcomes such as participation, performance, and productivity (Beal et al., 2003; C. R. Evans & Dion, 1991). Attachment is a profound and persistent bond based on afection or need that links the user to a community (Bowlby, 1983). In this study, attachment is composed of 3 dimensions of identity, dependence, and prominence which represent the emotional, need-based, and habitual aspects of attachment, respectively. Among these three dimensions, prominence is particularly important because it captures the understudied habitual aspect of participation in virtual communities.

The next section reviews prior relevant studies and presents a cohesiveness-attachment-participation (CAP) framework as the theoretical background of the study to address the relationships among cohesiveness, attachment, and community participation in a virtual community context. The third section introduces the proposed research model and hypotheses. The following two sections present the research method including the data collection procedure and the results of data analysis. Then, we discuss the theoretical and practical contributions of the study as well as limitations and future directions. The final section concludes the paper.

## 2. Theoretical background and literature review

## 2.1. Previous studies

While early research viewed knowledge contribution in organisational settings as the only form of participation in virtual communities (e.g., Bateman et al., 2011; Kankanhalli et al., 2005), social media platforms have since added other opportunities for participation such as sharing and collaboration through posting, liking, commenting, and moderating discussion (Bateman et al., 2011; Salehan et al., 2017). For example, Salehan et al. (2017) modelled participation as a second-order construct with two dimensions of sharing and collaboration and showed that three types of motivation (i.e., utilitarian, hedonic, and social) influence participation in the context of social media.

Previous studies show that motivations for participation in online communities can be classified as either economically-driven extrinsic or psychologically-driven intrinsic (Faraj et al., 2016). Most studies focus on the latter and use social theories such as social exchange theory, social identity theory, social capital theory, and social cognitive theory to explain active participation in virtual communities (e.g., Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002, 2006; A. L. A. L. Blanchard et al., 2011; Chih et al., 2017; Chiu et al., 2006; Hsu et al., 2018; Ma & Agarwal, 2007; Shen et al., 2006; Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Social and intrinsic motivations for virtual community participation include identity (e.g., Bagozzi & Dholakia, 2002, 2006; A. L. Blanchard & Markus, 2004;

Few studies have used other theories to explain participation in virtual communities. Ray et al. (2014) defined engagement as a “heightened state of mind that fully energizes people to undertake prosocial tasks that benefit others in a group” (p. 529) and showed that the outcomes (e.g., knowledge contribution and spreading of positive word of mouth) are the result of antecedent beliefs which are partially mediated by satisfaction and engagement. Another perspective is that of commitment which is defined as “a psychological bond that stabilizes individual behavior under circumstances where the individual would otherwise be tempted to change that behavior” (Bateman et al., 2011, p. 842). Commitment reflects dedication and personal responsibility (Klein et al., 2012). A study on the efect of continuance commitment (i.e., desire to remain employed with the organisation), afective commitment (i.e., emotional attachment), and normative commitment (i.e., feeling of obligation to continue employment) on participation found that reading posts is associated with continuance community commitment; posting is influenced by afective commitment, and moderating forums is a result of both afective and normative commitment (Bateman et al., 2011).

Although the social interaction between virtual community members has been thoroughly studied, the IT artefact facilitating all of these interactions has received little attention. While we know that platform features such as virtual copresence (e.g., a feature showing who is online), self-presentation (e.g., username and avatar), and deep profiling (e.g., member reputation or ranking) influence knowledge contribution in virtual communities (Ma & Agarwal, 2007), it is important to note that the technology itself can be treated as a social actor, and thus, can be the target of afect (Shank, 2010). As an example, some people say that they love FinTwit. FinTwit, which stands for Financial Twitter, is the virtual community of people discussing finance and stock trading on Twitter. It is likely that the afect expressed in this example is targeted at both the platform and the community since neither of those can create the kind of value ofered by FinTwit independently. The users provide information regarding stock trading and the platform, through features such as push notifications on other users activity and search for the most recent tweets about a certain keyword, facilitates rapid dissemination of information. Thus, the emotions expressed towards a virtual community may conflate with one’s afect towards the platform.

Table 1 shows that prior studies are overwhelmingly focused on the efect of social motivations on active participation, while few studies have investigated the relationships among passive participation, extrinsic motivations, community cohesiveness, and habitual participation in the context of virtual communities.

## 3. Cohesiveness-attachment-participation framework

In behavioural psychology, theories on human attitude (e.g., the theory of reasoned action, cohesiveness theory, attachment theory, etc.) describe the relationship between attitudes and their formation to explain various intentions and behaviours. As a background theory, this study uses the cognition-afectionconation (CAC) framework. CAC conceptualises attitude by three correlated but distinct components and posits that cognitive traits (e.g., knowledge, thought, perception, etc.) as well as emotional afections (e.g., emotions, preferences, emotional sense of attachment, etc.) influence conations (e.g., behavioural intentions/ actions, sharing, participation, etc.; Farley & Stasson, 2003; Hilgard, 1980; Norman, 1975). Cognitive traits include thoughts and perceptions about an object formed by judging its favourability (Holbrook, 1978).

Afective traits represent the feelings and physical measures, such as blood pressure and heart rate, that an individual has towards an object. Since the CAC framework is a general theory to describe human psychology, based on the CAC framework as a background theory, we propose a context-specific framework in the context of online virtual communities, namely cohesivenessattachment-participation (CAP) framework to explain how community members’ attitudinal, psychological, and emotional factors such as community cohesiveness and community attachment, influence a conative action named community participation (see, Figure 1).

Cohesiveness gained attention in the 1940s as part of a research programme on social communication. An early perspective viewed it as “the total field of forces which act on members to remain in the group” (Festinger et al., 1950, pp. 164–165) and as the attractiveness of the group for its members. This definition turned out dificult to measure since there are many forces involved and groups are attractive to diferent people for diferent reasons (Gross & Martin, 1952). Another perspective views it as a group’s “stickingtogetherness” (Gross & Martin, 1952, p. 553) or soli darity (Bhattacharyya, 1995; Hechter, 1987) which emphasises the relational bonds among group members and the ability of the group to resist disruptive forces. Based on this definition, cohesiveness can be viewed as a measure of group health and survivability.

Community cohesiveness refers to its cohesion; it commonly portrays harmony among group members, a sense of belonging towards a certain group, and a possessing bond that ties them to one another and to the group. Cohesiveness is frequently referred to as situations in which people are drawn or joined together by common social and cultural obligations or goals (Ferlander & Timms, 1999). It is also characterised as an active process that mirrors the propensity of a group to stay united and remain together in the quest for its objectives and goals (Carron, 1982). Accordingly, it has been considered essential for understanding group performance and productivity (Beal et al., 2003; C. R. Evans & Dion, 1991). Some researchers have deemed it as the most important small-group construct and have shown that it has a mediating role in productivity, absenteeism, social support, and mental health among others (Lev-Wiesel, 2003).

In the context of consumer behaviour, research has demonstrated that consumers serve as advocates of a brand when they provide support to other community members (Algesheimer et al., 2005; McAlexander et al., 2002) and community members who identify with a brand community recommend the community to other consumers (Algesheimer et al., 2005). In social communities, social capital increases when members help each other through group activities such as information sharing. Virtual communities with high cohesiveness have limited liability because group member contributions are considered morally responsible, that is they are limited, voluntary, intentional, and specialised (Muniz & O’guinn, 2001). Since cohesiveness is the result of an evaluation of common social goals or cultural obligations (Bowlby, 1983; Chidambaram, 1996), it would fit into the CAP framework as a cognitive construct.

Community attachment refers to the relationship between an individual and the groups to which he/ she belongs. Ever since the rise of urbanisation and industrialisation during the late 19<sup>th</sup> century and the early 20<sup>th</sup> century, the emotional state that people develop with a place or with the people they know has long been a topic of interest in Sociology (Hummon, 1992; Kasarda & Janowitz, 1974; Long & Perkins, 2007; R. E. R. E. Park et al., 1925; Simmel, 2012; Wirth, 1938). These were times when many people started to leave their homes and families behind to seek better employment opportunities in emerging cities. Emotional distress during this period of separation has been documented in the literature (Bowlby, 1983; Brennan et al., 1998; C. Whan Park et al., 2010; Thomson et al., 2005).

Attachment is commonly described as the strength of the relationship a person builds with a certain entity such as group, marketplace, product, or service (C. Whan Park et al., 2010; Thomson et al., 2005). In Environmental Psychology, the concept of attachment to a place is usually referred to as an afective association caused by place identity and place dependence (Buta et al., 2014; Kyle, Bricker et al., 2004; Kyle, Graefe et al., 2004; Ramkissoon et al., 2012; Williams et al., 1992). Place dependence suggests that the value of a place is relative to its distinctive attributes and how well it serves personal goals compared to alternatives (Jorgensen & Stedman, 2001). Place identity, on the other hand, is commonly described as the emotional and symbolic representation of a place based on the individual’s characteristics, values, thoughts, beliefs, and behavioural tendencies towards the place (Kyle, Graefe et al., 2004; Williams et al., 1992). The idea that people can be attached to a certain location, such as parks and other types of recreational places, emphasises the emotional connections an individual can have with those specific settings or environments. Additionally, the greater these social connections to a particular place are, the higher the emotional attachment formed with that particular location will be (Kyle, Bricker et al., 2004).

This study stipulates the concept of attachment to virtual communities consisting of three dimensions of identity, dependence, and prominence. In the afective stage of community membership, virtual community attachment represents the persisting bond based on the afection that links a community member to other members (Bowlby, 1983; Chidambaram, 1996). Considering that attachment is characterised by persisting bond and separation anxiety, it would fit in the CAC framework as an afective construct. Identity is often described as“that part of an individual’s selfconcept which derives from his knowledge of his membership of a social group (or groups) together with the value and emotional significance attached to that membership” (Tajfel, 1978, p. 63). Identity-based attachment signals attachment to the community as a whole rather than to its individual members (Ren et al., 2007). Dependence is commonly referred to as the idea that the value of an object is related to its utility in achieving personal goals (Jorgensen & Stedman, 2001). Together, these two dimensions are predominantly considered to form attachment, which is characterised by the emotional distress that occurs whenever the possibility of losing something or someone precious occurs.

The third dimension, prominence, is described as the object-self connection that develops over a period of time and eventually becomes a part of the memory (C Whan Park et al., 2013; C. Whan, 2010). Positive memories about the attachment target are more prominent for people who show higher levels of attachment to an object than for those who show weak attachment (Collins, 1996; Mikulincer, 1998b). Prominence reflects the salience of the bond between individual and target of attachment and is characterised by ease and frequency with which thoughts and feelings about the target come to mind (C. Whan Park et al., 2010). Research on attachment shows that certain behaviours may stem from habits (Mikulincer, 1998a). Similarly, Ray et al. (2014) suspected that prosocial behaviour might contain a habitual component after finding out that visitation frequency influences knowledge contribution. Since prominence reflects the thoughts and feelings about the target of attachment that automatically come to mind, it should be recognised as the dimension capturing the habitual aspect of attachment.

Although diferent disciplines focus on diferent dimensions of attachment, we believe that all three dimensions are important in the context of virtual communities. Members use virtual communities for informational needs which leads them to visit their communities more often. Many members have a regular checking habit (Salehan & Negahban, 2013) which can be the result of automatic thoughts about the virtual community caused by the prominence dimension of attachment. Finally, community identity is an important motivation for virtual community use (Barker, 2009) which indicates the need for identity attachment to be studied in this context.

Thus, this study considers community attachment as a second-order formative construct with three firstorder constructs because it is determined as a combination of specific dimensional components; identity, prominence, and dependence.

Conation, the third component of the CAC framework, covers a tendency to act (Wilkie, 1994). Although both the cognitive and afective elements are predictors of behaviour, they do not necessarily lead to behaviour. Hence, conation is the likelihood that an individual will exhibit a behaviour (Wilkie, 1994). Conative behaviour is an expression of an individuals’ intentions that represent his or her willingness to act in a certain manner. In the context of virtual communities, members’ participation is viewed as a key conative behaviour related to community performance because virtual communities depend on sharing and user-generated content (Bateman et al., 2011; Ray et al., 2014). Community members are key players of online communities both in a passive way by simply reading posts or in an active way by posting, commenting, sharing information, etc. (N. Choi, 2013; Krishnamurthy, 2009; Nov & Rao, 2008; Piskorski, 2009). Thus, we classify community members’ participation into two types: type 1 – visitation as a passive mode of participation and type 2 – active participation.

When new members join a virtual community, they would often devote their actions to relational purposes, to learn the rules and norms of the community through visitation (M. Evans et al., 2001). Most novice members of a virtual community tend to participate passively prior to actively engaging in prosocial activities. Since the norms and standards of virtual communities are built upon interactions and knowledge sharing among members, members engaging solely in visitation are usually encouraged by other active members to engage in prosocial activities such as sharing, posting, etc. (i.e., active participation). For example, Stackoverflow.com assigns each user a reputation score based on the quality of the responses that the user has provided to other users’ questions. The more reputation a user earns, the more privileges he/she will gain and the more tools he/she will have access to (Stackoverflow.com, 2019). Similarly, the most popular Lego designs on Lego Ideas platform go on shelves and are awarded a percentage of product sales (MarketingInsiderGroup.com, 2019). Although it is expected that visitation is a normal type of participation, active prosocial participation has a greater impact on the success of communities (Nonnecke et al., 2006; Rood & Bruckman, 2009). Rau et al. (2008) suggest that the procedure, as well as the quality of user contribution, is of vital importance for the survival and success of virtual communities. For instance, it has been suggested that the amount of posts for each member, or per active member, reflects the value of the community for advertisement, and thus, active participation is considered a determinant of success for online communities (Preece, 2001).

## 4. Research model and hypotheses

Drawing upon the CAP framework as the theoretical background, we propose a research model in the context of virtual communities, which includes two focal antecedents of community participation: community cohesiveness and community attachment. Community attachment is modelled as a higherorder construct with three dimensions: identity, prominence, and dependence<sup>3</sup> (Buta et al., 2014; Kyle, Bricker et al., 2004; Kyle, Graefe et al., 2004; C. Whan Park et al., 2010; Ramkissoon et al., 2012; Williams et al., 1992). We categorise participation into two types: active participation and visitation as the passive mode of participation. Figure 2 presents the proposed research model and hypotheses. Although this study does not focus on the impact of demographic variables on participation, age, gender, income, education, and duration of experience with the community were added as demographic variables to test for any observable efects.

## 5. The Relationship between Community Cohesiveness and Community Attachment

In the context of virtual communities, community cohesiveness refers to the extent to which the group portrays harmony as a result of members’ good morale and commitment to their community, whereas community attachment reflects the afective relationship between a member and the community. In this study, we define community attachment as a profound and persisting personal bond to a virtual community based on the afection that links a member to the group or community that he/she belongs to.

In the context of virtual communities, we suggest that cognition (i.e., perceived community cohesiveness) precedes afection (i.e., attachment). Social identity theory (SIT) can be used to explain how cohesiveness precedes the identity dimension of attachment. SIT posits that group-identity is the result of a self-evaluative process that is determined by properties of the group, especially in comparison with other groups (Hogg, 2001) and it happens through a process called depersonalisation which converts members from individuals to matches to the group. Members are more likely to become afectively committed to positively-evaluated groups and they tend to distance themselves from negatively-evaluated communities (Ellemers et al., 1999). In other words, SIT suggests that in communities, cognitive evaluation of group attributes precedes identity development. Community cohesiveness is an important characteristic of a group because it is an important predictor of group success (Beal et al., 2003; C. R. Evans & Dion, 1991). Hence, when the perception of group cohesiveness of a member is high, he/she is more likely to rate the group highly compared to other groups and consequently develop an identity around it. An example is Harley-Davidson brand ethos of brotherhood which intends to improve community identity by strengthening the sense of cohesion (Fournier, 2009). Another example is Starbucks’ renaming of drink size from small, medium, and large to tall, grande, and venti. This initiative creates a sense of harmony within the group, diferentiates its members from those of competitors, and facilitates identity development (Piontek, 2015).

Cohesiveness has a similar impact on the dependence dimension of community attachment. Many users join virtual communities to receive support towards their goals. The support these users receive will then enhance their perception of the community. A cohesive community, consisting of people willing to work together towards a common goal, would induce in its members the sense that it has staying-power or longevity, and that they can depend on it, thus contributing to the dependence dimension of community attachment. An example is StackOverFlow.com where more than 90% of questions are answered with a mean time of 11 minutes (Mamykina et al., 2011). Finally, the positive experience resulting from membership in a cohesive community would create lasting memories and thus, it would contribute to the prominence dimension of attachment. Considering the above arguments, we propose that:

H1: Community cohesiveness is positively associated with community attachment.

## 6. The relationship between community cohesiveness and community participation

Since posting and lurking fall on a continuum (Ridings et al., 2006), one cannot simply categorise users into two groups of lurkers and posters. Instead, we classify user behaviour into two categories of active and passive participation. While sharing information, as a prosocial activity, has a significant impact on social capital of online communities, most novice members of online communities tend to lurk, prior to actively engaging in prosocial activities (Chiu et al., 2006; Nonnecke et al., 2006; Rood & Bruckman, 2009; Tonteri et al., 2011).

When group cohesiveness is high, members are more eager to work together, interact with one another often, and regularly coordinate and share input from all of the members (Huang, 2009; Kratzer et al., 2006). In the context of virtual communities, community cohesiveness refers to the extent to which the virtual community portrays harmony because of members’ good morale and commitment to the goals of their community. Members holding strong perceptions of cohesiveness of their community find the community efective in achieving its goals and, thus, would devote more efort to it because they are confident that their eforts will eventually pay of. A recent example is Wall Street Bets which is a Reddit forum dedicated to discussion of the stock market. In 2019, some members started a discussion about inflating the price of GameStop stock to execute a short squeeze. The idea slowly gained support from other members who started buying the stock and forced the stock price to soar 1800% in January 2021 (Kochkodin, 2021). As the group showed success in achieving its stated goals through a coordinated efort, sticking together, and withstanding the pressure to sell, membership and active participation exploded. The number of subscribers grew 11 fold from 777,000 in January 2020 to 8.69 million in February 2021 and the number of comments per day grew 36 fold from 9630 in January 262,020, to 352,000 in January 262,021 (Subredditstats.com, 2021).

Moreover, a cohesive group is focused on objectives, encourages participation, and is less likely to criticise members for their participation. Therefore, we expect that when the perception of community cohesiveness of a member is high, he/she becomes more active in the virtual community. While the above arguments suggest that perceived community cohesiveness is related to active participation, we fall short of finding reasons that suggest that cohesiveness is related to visitation as a passive mode of participation as well. In fact, we have reasons to suspect that the two are not related. Members interact with one another and regularly coordinate and share input when group cohesiveness is high (Huang, 2009; Kratzer et al., 2006). Visitation, on the other hand, does not require coordination and, thus, it is not impacted by group cohesion.<sup>4</sup> These arguments lead to the following hypothesis:

H2: Community cohesiveness is positively associated with active (type 2) participation

## 7. The relationship between community attachment and participation

There are several reasons to suspect that community attachment is related to both types of participation. Members who form a bond with their preferred communities are more likely to participate in more demanding activities and spend more resources such as time and energy on their communities (N. Choi, 2013). They also frequently feel an urge to sufice their social needs by interacting with other members, which leads to increased passive participation such as lurking, as well as active participation such as commenting and posting. (Koh et al., 2003). Below we discuss in more detail how attachment leads to each type of participation.

First, there are various reasons to argue that the identity dimension influences both types of participation. Community members who have a profound and persisting bond to a community, tend to get involved more when they believe that they have a unique identity within that particular community (Barki & Hartwick, 1994; McKeen et al., 1994; C. Whan Park et al., 2010). Members with a group-self connection generally assess the community more favourably, are concerned about its public image, remain in the community longer, get involved in broad community activities such as defending the group, and use more energy on its part than those who feel that they are less connected (Hogg, 1992; K. K. Lee & Allen, 2002).

Second, members with high levels of community attachment prominence would often have automatic thoughts and feelings about the community which would lead them to seek habitual comfort by logging into their communities’ website/mobile app more often than others. Thus, we expect that members with high levels of community attachment would frequently lurk on their community to reduce their separation anxiety (i.e., increased passive participation) as well as engage in more demanding activities such as posting and commenting if there is a chance to help their community members (i.e., increased active participation).

Finally, community dependence forces members to log in and seek information whenever they need to. The information might be readily available if a similar question has already been asked. If that is the case or if the goal is to learn about the most recent discussions, a simple visitation would be suficient. Otherwise, the member would have to post a question and engage with other members to receive information. Considering the above arguments, we posit the following hypotheses:

H3a: Community attachment is positively associated with visitation (type 1 participation).

H3b: Community attachment is positively associated with active (type 2) participation.

## 8. Research method

## 8.1. Measurement development

Social media platforms have become a popular place to form virtual communities (Golan & Babis, 2019; Hsu, 2019; Posey et al., 2010). For example, Facebook has declared the development of social infrastructure to help people build a global community as its most important objective (Zuckerberg, 2017). Through its “groups” feature, Facebook allows its users to build communities around specific topics. Thus, we focused on users of social media platforms.

Following the three-stage instrument development process (i.e., item creation, scale development, and instrument testing) suggested by Moore and Benbasat (1991), we developed measurement items for the research constructs of this study. In the stage of item creation, we reviewed the existing measurement items from previous research and modified them to fit the context of virtual communities. As recommended by Bentler and Chou (1987), each construct was measured by at least three measurement items. To measure perceived Community Identity (CID), we used three items adapted from Buta et al. (2014) and C. Whan Park et al. (2010). Perceived Community Prominence (CPR) was measured with three items adapted from N. Choi (2013) and C. Whan Park et al. (2010) while perceived Community Dependence (CDE) was measured with five items from Kyle, Bricker et al. (2004) and Williams and Vaske (2003). We measured perceived Community Cohesiveness (CC) with three items adapted from Chidambaram (1996), Hedayati Marzbali et al. (2014), and Ku et al. (2013). Visitation (VI) was measured with three items from Brennan et al. (1998) and C. Whan Park et al. (2010). For Active Participation (AP), we employed three items from J. Choi et al. (2013) and Nonnecke et al. (2006). The details of measurement items and their sources are presented in Appendix B.

For the second stage, scale development, a panel of experts reviewed the instrument to ensure the face validity of the measurement items and to identify ambiguous items of the instrument created in the first stage. To improve the readability of measurement items, we conducted a small-scale pre-test (i.e., a pilot test) with 15 student subjects. Based on the feedback from the pre-test, a few measurement items were revised to make sure that the measures are clear and easy to understand. For the stage of instrument testing, the last stage of instrument development, another small-scale pre-test, with 32 student subjects, was conducted prior to collecting data for the field test. To ensure the appropriateness of the research instrument, it was tested for reliability, content validity, and construct validity using the pre-test sample. The final version of the survey went through a review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of human subject research without any issues.

## 9. Data collection

For the field survey, an online survey form was created and hosted under Qualtrics, a well-known online private survey research company. The web-based survey questionnaire was structured into three parts (see Appendix C). In part one, the participants were asked seven general questions related to the demographic variables such as gender, age, education, income, and so on. This was then followed by part two where participants had to answer nine series of questions pertaining to their virtual community usage starting with a validation question of whether they are SNS members or not and which SNS do they mostly use. After that, the third and final part of the survey asked questions that measure the key constructs of this study using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). Altogether, answering all questions was estimated to take around ten minutes. After the necessary preparations, the survey website was then released online via a URL on February 5th, 2015.

The online survey participants were recruited through multiple channels: advertisements posted on social media sites, email invitations using personal contacts, and a non-discriminative snowball sampling method using personal social networks. For the snowball sampling method, the initial survey participation requests, including the URL, were distributed to the research team’s social networks through various online social communities including Facebook.com, Twitter, LinkedIn.com, Instagram, Google+, YouTube, Reddit.com, and so on. Early respondents provided multiple referrals and each new referral then provided more participants. The survey data collection period ended on March 30th, 2015 after almost two months of data gathering.

A total of 744 responses were collected. After a careful examination, 235 responses were eliminated due to incomplete responses, suspicious response patterns, or too many missing values. A total of 509 valid responses were used for data analysis. Among the 509 responses, 47% were male and 53% were female. Two-thirds of them (66%) were in their twenties or early thirties. The majority of the respondents (75.8%) had at least a bachelor’s degree. In terms of country of origin, the respondents represented a diverse sample. The survey was taken by people from seventy diferent countries from all over the world. However, the largest number of responses came from the United States (36.3%). The respondents were engaged in various occupations such as student (31.4%), salaried company employee (21.2%), professor/teacher (11.4%), and wage worker (10.0%). In terms of annual income, 24% of respondents earned below \$10,000, 31% earn between \$10,001 and \$30,000, 22% earned \$30,001 to \$50,000, and around 15% earned \$50,001 or more. 8% of respondents preferred not to report their income.

In terms of virtual community usage, the top five platforms were Facebook, YouTube, LinkedIn, Twitter, and Google+. Nearly all respondents (92%) were a member of Facebook, while 57% were a member of YouTube, followed by LinkedIn at 52%, and Twitter and Google+ tied at 50%. The top 5 mobile SNS that the respondents used the most were Facebook (62%), followed by Twitter at 7%, Instagram at 6%, KakaoTalk at 5%, and others at 20%. More than 85% of the respondents were long-time (more than two years) users of their preferred SNS via mobile devices. 39% of the respondents spent less than 1 hour daily on their SNS, 46% spent 1 to 3 hours per day, 11% spent 4 to 6 hours, while only 5% of the respondents spent more than 6 hours daily. ## 11. Data analysis

Since this research is mainly exploratory and primarily aims at developing theories that identify key driver constructs, Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) was used to examine both the measurement model and the structural model. PLS-SEM is a composite-based estimation approach that difers from the covariance-based structural equation modelling (CB-SEM). PLS-SEM analysis is chosen over other analytical techniques for several reasons. First, PLS-SEM is considered appropriate for exploratory research (Hair et al., 2017). PLS-SEM is considered to be the most appropriate when research is in its early stages, or when more emphasis is placed on identifying possible relationships between the constructs than on the magnitude of those relationships (Goodhue et al., 2012). Second, PLS-SEM is generally used to avoid precluded solutions and factor indeterminacy (Hair et al., 2017). Third, although PLS-SEM utilises composite-based estimation, it accommodates both reflective and formative indicators at the operation layer without identification issues (Sarstedt et al., 2016; Hair et al., 2017). Compared to CB-SEM, PLS-SEM is more appropriate for this study because the proposed research model contains both higher-order formative and first-order reflective constructs. In addition, PLS-SEM is robust and ensures convergence with fewer statistical identification issues (Gefen et al., 2011; Hair et al., 2017).

Statistical software application package SmartPLS v.3.2.7 was utilised for data analysis. SmartPLS simplifies the modelling of formative and reflective constructs at the same time and simultaneously tests both the measurement model and the structural model (Hair et al., 2017; Wixom & Watson, 2001). It reports composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) for content validity and discriminant validity. Composite reliability is an appropriate measure for assessing reliability in SEM because it takes into account the unequal weights of the items used to measure a construct (Bacon et al., 1995). We adopted a two-step systematic evaluation for the data analysis (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988; Hair et al., 2017). The first step involved assessing the measurement model, while the second step examined the structural relationships among the latent constructs. The purpose of the two-step evaluation is to establish reliability and validity of the measures before assessing the structural relationships of the model.

The structural model was evaluated in terms of collinearity, significance of path coeficients, coeficient of determination, and predictive relevance. For the first approach, we applied the detailed procedure suggested by Malhotra et al. (2006) and Lindell and Whitney (2001). A marker variable was selected based on three criteria: i) measured using Likert scale similar to the other variables, ii) not theoretically related to other constructs in this study, and iii) not highly correlated with other measurement items. The results summarised in Table E3 show that compared to the basic model, the model with marker variable does not show any significant change in the coeficients and R-squares. For the second approach, we followed the detailed procedure suggested by Liang et al. (Liang et al., 2007) to check the ratio of the two models (with and without LCMVF). The results are summarised in Table E4. The average substantively explained variance of the indicators (i.e., without LCMVF) is 67.89%, while the average method-based variance (i.e., with LCMVF) is 1.52%. The ratio of substantive variance to method variance is about 45:1, which is quite low. Based on the CMB results, it would be reasonable to conclude that common method bias is not a likely contaminant of the results of the study.

We also tested multicollinearity of the model using four sets. For each set, one of the constructs was considered as dependent and the other constructs served as its predictors. Then we calculated inner VIF values for each set. As a general rule of thumb in collinearity evaluation, each value of predictor construct’s variance inflation factor (VIF) must be lower than 5 (Sheather, 2009). ## 14. Post-hoc analyses

## 14.1. Mediation efect test and importance-performance map analysis

The mediating efects of community attachment between community cohesiveness and visitation and between community cohesiveness and active participation were tested. First, the path model without the interaction of the mediator was estimated via bootstrapping method. The result show that both direct paths from CC to VI $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and from CC to AP $( \beta = 0 . 4 1 , \gamma < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ are statistically significant. Thus, testing the mediating efect of CA is meaningful. To test the mediation efect, we used the procedure of computed bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals suggested by Preacher and Hayes (2008). The use of CA as the simple mediator enables us to estimate indirect efects by using bootstrapping methods (5,000 bootstraps). If the bootstrapped confidence interval of the specific indirect efects does not include 0 and the indirect efect is significant, then mediation is supported. The results (see, Table E7 in Appendix E)

show a significant mediating efect of CA. Overall, the results confirm the significant mediating efect of community attachment on the relationships between CC and VI and between CC and AP.

Since this study applies the PLS-SEM approach, the extraction of the latent variable scores for each construct was possible. With this technique, it was possible to extend the findings of the study by conducting the Importance-Performance map/ matrix analysis (IPMA) through the use of the structural model’s total efect (importance) values and the average values of the latent variable scores (performance; Hair et al., 2017). The IPMA is a widely used analytical technique in Marketing that allows for prioritisation eforts to improve a certain goal. The IPMA is particularly useful in practical studies that identify diferent impacts that certain constructs have on business phenomena, such as customer satisfaction and company reputation, allowing for prioritisation of limited resources (Ringle & Sarstedt, 2016). Two IPMA analyses were conducted using all predictors of the target constructs – i.e., active participation (AP) and visitation (VI).

The results of IPMA analyses are presented as Figure E1 and E2 in Appendix E. For both cases, attachment shows a similar level of performance (i.e., y-axis) while it has a relatively higher total efect (i.e., x-axis) than perceived community cohesiveness. This means that community attachment plays a more important role in active participation based on the higher value of total efect (i.e., x-axis). These results are consistent with those of the structural model analysis presented in Figure 3. The results of this analysis can help narrow down the potential improvement areas that managers can focus on. Thereby, when managers need to prioritise limited resources and efort to increase community members’ participation, it is more important to develop community features or marketing campaigns that enhance individual members’ attachment to the community.

## 14.2. Multi-group analysis to check the heterogeneity of diferent communities

Because we tested the proposed research model using a pool of data collected across diferent virtual communities (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, etc.), there is a possibility of heterogeneity across the groups, which threatens the validity of the analysed results. Thus, we checked if diferent groups difer in terms of the results of the proposed model testing, using the SmartPLS MGA by employing two diferent nonparametric procedures: bootstrap-based MGA (Henseler et al., 2009) and the permutation test (Chin & Dibbern, 2010).

One issue related to MGA is that when we calculate the estimated path coeficients of the structural model using SmartPLS, the sample size should be enough. If there are fewer observations than degrees of freedom, we cannot conduct MGA (Hair et al., 2017). As presented in Appendix D, the top 5 platforms that the respondents used the most were Facebook (316: 62%), followed by Twitter (36: 7%), Instagram (29: 6%), KakaoTalk (27: 5%), YouTube (17: 3%), and others (101: 20%). Thus, we can only conduct three MGAs (i.e., Facebook vs. Twitter, Facebook vs. Instagram, and Facebook vs. Others), because of the limited sample size of Kakao Talk and YouTube groups. After we confirmed that compositional invariances of mean values and variances across groups using the measurement invariance of composite models (MICOM) suggested by Henseler, Ringle and Sarstedt (2016), we conducted three MGAs and presented the results in Table E8 of Appendix E. The results show that none of the p-values are lower than .05 for both Henseler’s MGA and permutation method. Based on the results, we can confirm that there are no significant diferences in terms of heterogeneity across these platforms.

## 15. Discussion

This study distinguishes between two types of participation, namely, visitation and active participation in the context of a virtual community. We measured two types of participation using survey items and used a rigorous theoretical foundation to study how community cohesiveness and community attachment influence participation. Bateman et al. (2011) noticed that some people contribute to communities in a selfless way, or what they call normative community commitment. They suspected that there may be multiple motivations behind normative commitment including feeling a need to repay after having benefitted from the community in the past, believing strongly in the community, or feeling it is the right thing to do.

In this study, we find that community cohesiveness and community attachment are strongly related to active prosocial behaviour (i.e., active participation) in virtual communities. Especially, community attachment plays a critical role in influencing both visitation and active participation. This result confirms that, as community members form a profound and persisting bond with their community, they frequently visit their community and tend to participate in more active prosocial activities such as posting and commenting. The results also indicate that perceived community cohesiveness both directly and indirectly through community attachment afects community members active participation, but it does not show any significant direct efect on visitation as a passive mode of participation. Our interpretation of these results is that higher cohesiveness does not guarantee both higher visitation and active participation. When members have a higher degree of emotional community attachment – i.e., they feel that they emotionally belong to the community, they frequently visit the community and they are more likely to participle in community activities.

This result provides evidence that community cohesiveness is a critical determinant of community attachment and participation and that community attachment plays a critical role as a mediator between community cohesiveness and community participation. We also find a significant positive relationship between age and community cohesiveness. Similarly, age is an important predictor of the sense of community in neighbourhoods (Riger & Lavrakas, 1981). We also find that gender and experience are significantly related to community attachment indicating that female members and members with more experience are more likely to get attached to virtual communities.

## 16. Theoretical contributions

From a theoretical perspective, this study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, based on the cohesiveness-attachment-participation (CAP) framework as an overarching theory, this study proposes a research model that investigates the roles of community attachment and community cohesiveness as predictors of participation in virtual communities. Despite the importance of community cohesiveness and attachment on the quality of community contributions, empirical studies of their roles in enhancing community participation are largely absent in the virtual community context. By proposing and empirically testing the proposed research model using the cohesiveness-attachment-participation framework, this study is one of the first attempts to uncover the underlying theoretical relationships among these three predictors of the success of virtual communities. Although previous research has looked at predictors of participation, using the proposed CAP framework, this study better explains participation in virtual communities because the proposed framework and its underlying theory are capable of explaining both extrinsic and intrinsic motivations.

Second, drawing upon previous studies that identified several dimensions of attachment (N. Choi, 2013; C. Whan Park et al., 2010; e.g., Williams & Vaske, 2003), this study conceptualises and proposes community attachment as a higherorder construct with three dimensions: community identity, community prominence, and community dependence. The results not only confirm that the higher-order construct fits the model well, but also show that the conceptualisation and operationalisation of community attachment as a higher-order construct provide several theoretical benefits such as reduced model complexity and increased theoretical parsimony. The community prominence dimension is particularly important because it captures the understudied habitual dimension of community attachment. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to empirically investigate habitual participation in virtual communities. Future research can use the community attachment construct to further investigate attachment in general and habitual attachment in particular.

Third, this study distinguishes between two types of virtual community participation (i.e., visitation and active participation) and their relationships between community attachment and community cohesiveness as predictors of two types of participation. The results theoretically confirm the importance of distinguishing among community cohesiveness, community attachment, and two types of participation in the context of virtual communities and show that such a classification can be used by future studies to shed light on participation in virtual communities. Besides, this study shows how age, as a demographic variable, afects the type of user participation. This is important because it indicates that user participation varies based on user characteristics and a one-fits-all model is not eficient in improving user participation. This opens a new research stream that focuses on how user participation can be improved based on user characteristics. Future research can delve more deeply into this area by studying the efect of diferent user characteristics on participation and by developing recommender agents that customise the experience for each user.

Finally, the attachment construct used in this study captures the conflation between feelings towards community and the platform through different dimensions of attachment. Each dimension has a particular focus between the IT artefact and the community. The community identity dimension is focused on the sense of oneness with the community. Community prominence is focused on the IT artefact and captures the habitual attachment to technology use. Community dependence is a combination of both reflecting the reliance on the community which is made possible through the platform and the quality of communication media (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Thus, community attachment provides a link between the feelings towards both the community and the platform that was previously missing in the literature.

## 17. Managerial implications

This study contributes to practice by identifying two important constructs that significantly afect two types of virtual community participation: community attachment and community cohesiveness. We find that community attachment, which is composed of three dimensions, is positively related to both types of participation. Once users are attached to an object, it would be dificult for them to leave because separation would lead to feelings of uneasiness. Hence, making users get attached to the platform would be a great strategy not only to increase their participation but also to retain them.

Diferent types of community attachment are developed for diferent reasons. Community identity attachment can be reinforced by focusing on members’ attention on community and community activities (Ren et al., 2012). One approach is through increasing group homogeneity (Y.-T. Simon & Pettigrew, 1990; Y.-T. Lee & Ottati, 1995) such as Starbuck’s drink size initiative or Harley Davidson’s brotherhood. Intragroup competition, such as ranking diferent clans in online games, is another approach used by online platforms (Hogg & Turner, 1985; Postmes et al., 2006). Finally, depersonalisation can be used to strengthen identity in communities (Ren et al., 2012). Examples of depersonalisation include giving information about the group to members, considering individuals as a group, and downplaying the diferences among members. Furthermore, the community dependence dimension is about how much users depend on a community for their needs. The more useful a community is to its members, the more likely it is that the members get attached to it since it helps them with their needs. Finally, community prominence reflects habitual attachment and is likely to be closely related to checking habit which is reinforced by informational rewards (Oulasvirta et al., 2012). Thus, features that provide users with the constant flow of information, such as push notifications, are likely to lead to increased prominence attachment.

Community cohesiveness is also positively related to active participation. This indicates that users who have a high perception of cohesion of their virtual community are more likely to be active members. Since the number of active members is an important predictor of virtual community success, it is important to find methods that improve the sense of cohesiveness on their platform as well as avoid actions that diminish it. Expected length of community membership, satisfaction with the community (Glynn, 1981), strengths of interpersonal relationships (Ahlbrandt & Cunningham, 1979), and agreement with the values and beliefs of the community (Bachrach & Zautra, 1985) are some of the predictors of community cohesiveness. Improving the sense of cohesiveness can also be done by highlighting member contributions and by recognising active members. On the other hand, community cohesiveness is characterised by group conformity and some researchers have warned that individuals may escape a community to be able to express themselves (Chavis & Newbrough, 1986). Hence, while it is important for virtual communities to have a clear set of values, which is usually presented in the form of community guidelines, applying severe restrictions on speech may have a negative efect on membership.

We also find that age indirectly afects the type of participation of users. Older users have a greater sense of community cohesiveness which is positively related to active participation. This finding highlights that virtual community platforms need diferent strategies to attract users of diferent age groups. Recommender agents can be used to achieve this goal. Members in the visitation-only stage of participation (i.e., no active participation) usually need to spend less time in the community because the user does not intend to generate content. To encourage members in the visitation-only stage to become active members, virtual community platforms should optimise their recommender agents to select a large number of posts that might be of interest to the members in the visitationonly stage. For active members, the focus should be on finding posts that the user is likely to contribute to. Another implication is that as the millennials, who currently constitute the majority of virtual community users, grow older, the sense of community among them may increase. This would translate into more active participation if platforms such as Facebook retain their current user base in the future.

## 18. Limitations and future research

As with any study, this research has several limitations. First, although this study mainly focuses on virtual community platforms such as Facebook, YouTube, LinkedIn, Twitter, etc., these platforms vary slightly in terms of a business model and functionality. Some are described as virtual communities or social networking sites, while others are referred to as social networking services, mobile social networking platforms, and so on. In this study, we use virtual communities and social networking sites interchangeably. However, we must accept this conflation of terminologies as a limitation of the study.

Second, this study attempted to gather a suficiently large sample. However, it is dificult to tell if the sample size is large enough for this research’s inference purposes. Especially, the participants of the survey were not equally distributed among diferent countries. For instance, the majority of the respondents were from the United States. We suspect that national culture, especially the individualism/collectivism dimension (Hofstede, 2001), may influence the prosocial behaviour of users. Thus, limited sample diversity may reduce the generalisability of the results. It would be interesting to look at how the individualism/collectivism dimension of national culture interacts with proactive behaviours such as active participation. The fact that the online survey questionnaire was only available in English may have also posed problematic for non-English speaking participants. Moreover, some participants have expressed their concerns about how this study only focuses on people who use virtual communities through mobile devices. For instance, some of them had dificulty distinguishing if the device they are using is considered as a mobile device. Hence, this study did not account for virtual community users over stationary wired devices such as personal desktop computers.

Third, visitation and active participation are not mutually exclusive and can be done simultaneously by one user. While lurkers’ participation happens under a visitation-only mode, the prosocial activities of active participants depend on visitation as well. Active participants may need to read a large number of posts to find contribution opportunities. At the same time, active participants often read posts for their own informational needs. Thus, the visitation space contains both posters and lurkers, and the visitation construct cannot be directly used to distinguish between posters and lurkers although such a measure can be derived from our measures. The derived measure would distinguish between those who are high on visitation and low on active participation and those who are high on active participation. It would also be interesting to look at other factors as determinants of lurking as well as the factors that prevent lurkers from active participation in community activities. Considering that active users create more value for virtual communities, it would be important to study the process under which lurkers are converted to active users.

Another area of future research is the conflation between afect expressed towards the community and the platform. We showed that the attachment construct is capable of bridging the gap. However, afect towards the platform is an understudied area. It would be interesting for future research to look at which platform features are more likely to get the users attached to the community and which features are related to certain social motivations for participation such as trust, sense of community, and social presence.

Finally, research shows that the relationship between cohesiveness and outcomes is stronger when all constructs are measured at a group level (Beal et al., 2003). One future research direction is to replicate this study at the group level to examine how group-level outcomes difer from individual-level outcomes used in this study.

## 19. Conclusion

Virtual communities have become a great place for people to spend their time, socialise, and interact with other individuals. Motivated by a lack of understanding regarding the phenomenon of community attachment and its role in the success of online social communities, this study proposes community attachment and perceived community cohesiveness as central predictors of virtual community participation and empirically tests the hypothesised relationships. We expect that the study not only inspires future IS research to consider community attachment, community cohesiveness, and participation as vital research constructs in other social contexts such as virtual worlds and role-based social network games, but also it helps managers build managerial actions, such as prioritising their limited business resources to increase community members’ active participation, for the success of virtual communities.

1. Although this study mainly focuses on virtual communities including Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, YouTube, LinkedIn, etc., these platforms have diferent business models and functionalities. Even with their diferences, they all serve the same broader purpose of providing virtual places for members to hang out, socialise and interact with others for companionship, social support, sharing information and opinion, and others, Thus, this study uses social network services, social networking sites, online virtual communities, and virtual communities, interchangeably.

2. Sense of community is defined as “members’ feelings of identity, belonging, and attachment with each other” (A. L. Blanchard et al., 2011, p. 84) and is diferent from community cohesiveness used in this study.

4. Although we did not explicitly propose a hypothesis between community cohesiveness and visitation as a passive mode of participation, we included this path as part of structure model testing to compare the efect of community cohesiveness on both types of participation.
