# Digital-enabled strategic agility: it’s time we examine the sensing of weak signals

Alain Pinsonneault<sup>a</sup> and Inmyung Choi<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Quebec, Canada; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems and Quantitative Sciences Area, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX, USA

## ABSTRACT

Information Systems research provides significant insight into how information technologies afect the strategic agility of firms. Most research to date has studied the responding element of agility at the expense of sensing. Further, extant research has mainly focused on how the sensing of formal and strong signals might influence agility. With the highly turbulent and fastchanging competitive landscape of the digital age, we argue for the need to expand the focus of this research to include examining how managers identify and make sense of weak signals, and how the sensing of weak signals influences digital-enabled strategic agility. Drawing on social network theory, this paper presents two streams of research and several research avenues that can stimulate and potentially guide research eforts on this topic.

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 2 September 2021 Accepted 29 December 2021

KEYWORDS

IT agility; strategic agility; weak signals; social media; social network

## 1. Introduction

Today’s organisations compete in high clockspeed environments characterised by global markets; permeable industry boundaries; pervasive digitisation of products, services, operations, and business models; and volatile regulations. In such environments, digitalenabled strategic agility (i.e., the ability of firms to search/detect/make sense of signals in the environment (sensing) and respond quickly to both opportunities and threats (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Queiroz et al., 2018; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011)<sup>1</sup> is essential for the survival and prosperity of firms (D’Aveni et al., 2010; Ravichandran, 2018; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011; Weber & Tarba, 2014; Yoo et al., 2012). The role of information technologies (IT) in strategic agility has been a central topic in the information systems (IS) literature. Numerous papers have examined the relationship between IT and strategic agility, including strategic IT alignment (Liang et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), IT flexibility (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), IT ambidexterity (Lee et al., 2015), IT capability (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011), and IT application orchestration capability (Queiroz et al., 2018). Much of this research to date has focused on the responding element of agility, premised on the assumption that organisations can only react if they have already sensed or noticed environmental cues in a thoughtful, timely, and accurate manner (Starbuck & Milliken, 1988).

While we are not disputing this assumption, such an approach treats the sensing element of agility as a black box and does not provide insight into this fundamental element. Some research eforts have directly examined sensing (e.g., Chi et al., 2010; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011; Park et al., 2017; Roberts et al., 2016; Roberts & Grover, 2012). However, these research eforts have mainly focused on sensing strong signals in the main environments of firms through formal data centric approaches, such as big data analytics and data warehousing.

We argue, in this paper, for the need to examine how managers sense weak signals. In the digital age, strong formal signals (i.e., objective, verifiable, and mainstream information emanating from formal sources) have limited competitive value. These strong signals become stale and lose their competitive value as rapidly as the information gets difused widely and swiftly through an abundant number of sources. In a high clockspeed and permeable environment, the survival of organisations often depends on their managers’ ability to identify and make sense of weak signals and emergent phenomena, which often happen at the periphery or even outside of an organisation’s immediate environment or traditional core attentional focus. Companies are increasingly searching for and trying to read and interpret weak signals (i.e., informal, ambiguous, “noisy” cues often found outside the core of a company’s main environment or focus of attention). Some companies, such as Walmart, have sensed and responded relatively rapidly to weak signals about the changing nature of the competitive landscape in retail (Venkatraman, 2019). Disney, need to quickly determine what new technologies such as augmented reality might mean for their business. Manufacturers like Nike need to sense and rapidly determine how 3D printing might play out for them (Venkatraman, 2019).

Some companies have had dificulties in sensing for weak signals. Sometimes, it seems that developing expertise in sensing strong signals drives away the desire or ability to sense weak signals. For example, Procter & Gamble is well-known for its superior sensing abilities with IT-savvy practices. It uses Business Sphere, an internally developed decision-making platform based on data analytics, to sense the changes in its markets. P&G was able to dramatically enhance its responding capability to its retail partners, such as Walmart, using a Vendor Managed Inventory (VMI) system (Yu et al., 2013). However, according to a recent report, the market share of Gillette (a product of P&G) declined to around 54 % from 70 %, and even the entire shaving market is destined to shrink (McGrath, 2019). Although P&G keeps sensing and monitoring its main environment (i.e., customers, retail partners, and primary competitor – Schick) with IT systems, it was not able to detect a key changing trend of new generations: Young people do not have a strong need for clean shaving and hair removal. In addition, P&G has a taken-for-granted assumption that its products can be provided only through retail partners, such as Walmart. Meanwhile, newcomers that rely on digital channels (e.g., Dollar Shave Club and Harry’s) have penetrated the shaving market by providing a subscription-based business model (McGrath, 2019).

As these examples illustrate, the digitisation of businesses increases the clockspeed of industries, blurs the barriers between industries, and facilitates the entry of newcomers. In light of the need to identify opportunities and threats in a timely manner, managers are becoming increasingly aware of the importance of sensing of weak signals, which are often found outside the traditional and core attention foci of organisations (i.e., core business environment).

Sensing and sensemaking of weak signals is fraught with important challenges for both managers and researchers alike. Sensing is subjective and weak signals are ambiguous, incomplete, and often based on illdefined and contradictory bits of information. There may be a sense of urgency and a proclivity towards acting or reacting before credible, verifiable, and actionable signals can be properly vetted and fully interpreted (Weick, 1993). In the high clockspeed environment of the digital age, waiting for definitive and verifiable evidence and the confirmation of weak signals can pose a risk to the survival of organisations. Managers must face one of the most important conundrums of the digital age regarding the sensing of weak signals: Making the mistake of not doing something when they should have acted (Type 2 error; false negative) or doing something when they should have done nothing (Type 1 error; false positive).

In this paper, we argue that the sensing of weak signals is fundamental to compete in the digital age and that we should expand our research and examine how managers identify and make sense of these signals and how sensing of weak signals afect digital-enabled strategic agility. We fully recognise that this is a challenging task: How can we study sensing of weak signals and agility? What are the key research questions? What could be a sound theoretical foundation? How to approach such a complex and somewhat ill-defined topic? These are some of the questions we try to answer in the following section.

## 2. Researching weak signals in the digital age

Sensing for weak signals is likely to difer from sensing for strong signals because of diferences in the nature of the signals themselves. Table 1 summarises these diferences. Most IS research to date has examined sensing of the main environment by relying on strong signals through formal data-centric approaches, such as big data analytics and data warehousing (e.g., Chi et al., 2010; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011; Park et al., 2017; Roberts et al., 2016; Roberts & Grover, 2012). Data analytics tools (e.g., business intelligence, data mining, and visualisation tools) can improve a firm’s capability to obtain timely information and to take competitive actions (Chi et al., 2010; Park et al., 2017). They favour digitalenabled sensing that focuses on flagship products or services (Tallon, 2008), current market and existing customers (Y Chen et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2015; Roberts & Grover, 2012), and key competitors’ behaviours (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Chi et al., 2010).

As shown in Table 1, examining how the sensing of weak signals is related to digital-enabled strategic agility is likely to require that we adjust our research approach to understand a rather complex reality. Firms in which managers actively scan weak signals are more likely to be able to develop competitive strategies and achieve better performance. They can defuse problems before they become major crises, be first movers, take advantage of significant opportunities, and secure large market shares. However, the sensing of weak signals is complex and fraught with challenges that research can help understand. The ability to mine a broad array of weak signals that are often found at the periphery of an organisation’s focal environment and attentional focus requires us to examine how managers devote attention across many areas, and how they scan and monitor various information sources. The sensing of weak signals also often requires that a manager be able to put his/her deeply held beliefs and assumptions aside and be open to alternative perspectives (Cunha & Chia, 2007).

Further, studying how firms sense weak signals requires that researchers focus on issues that are new, infrequently encountered, and open-ended. In addition, weak signals are quite illusive (e.g., predictions, opinions, news, ideas, and even rumours), illdefined, ambiguous, fragmented, and spread over various sources in diverse formats, making it dificult to examine how managers sense weak signals and how this might afect digital-enabled strategic agility.

Scanning for weak signals is also likely to be difused throughout the organisation because it is typically best done by individuals who are close to the diferent contexts (McGrath, 2019). Managers who have the highest rank and central positions in organisations are often unable to easily recognise the emergence of threats and changes. Rather, people in market-facing roles are those more likely to be the first exposed to weak signals. This makes the interpretation and integration of weak signals into a coherent organisational peripheral vision challenging. In a sense, this is the conundrum of agility: People who sense weak signals may not be able to see the big picture of what those signals mean, but managers (at higher levels in the organisation) who can see the big picture are less likely to face and be able to sense weak signals. Further, the challenge of sensing of weak signals is compounded by the fact that managers often become so attuned to the world around them that they can neither decipher nor believe weak signals. They can develop habits that “teach” them what to expect and look for. Routines can lead managers to expect that signals (i.e., strong signals) will appear in a certain way, which can lead them to dismiss or miss out on weak signals because these signals tend to manifest in unusual ways (see, Table 1).

## 3. Weak signals, social media, and digital-enabled agility

Recently, managers have increasingly relied on usergenerated information and on accessing complex and ambiguous information through colleagues and personal contacts to scan environments (Chen et al., 2012). Probing for weak signals frequently requires a mindful search into new and oftentimes unexpected information spaces. It requires searching for ambiguous and illstructured information. The nature of the information of weak signals makes it dificult to code, store, and process via formal information systems (Peterson, 2004). In addition, the large amount of information from diverse sources may also overwhelm managers and make interpretation dificult (Edmunds & Morris, 2000). The information that managers hear can be contradictory, but the information provides a comprehensive view when it is aggregated. Managers often rely on their colleagues and personal contacts because the information obtained through impersonal sources (e.g., databases, data warehousing) is often not rich enough to allow them to learn about what is truly happening or likely to happen (Auster & Choo, 1993; Galavan et al., 2008; Menon & Tomkins, 2004; Peterson, 2004). When sensing and detecting weak signals, managers need to amplify the weak signals and seek to construct their understanding of a wide range of illstructured information (Brown & Duguid, 1991). Scholars have recently pointed to the importance of an individual’s social/informational network that gives managers a greater ability to generate ideas (e.g., Burt, 2005), seek information from close and distant colleagues (e.g., Borgatti & Cross, 2003), communicate and share information (e.g., Haythornthwaite, 2005), and share and integrate knowledge (e.g., Robert et al., 2008). Consequently, the value of IT in enhancing the sensing of weak signals does not seem to be so much in its information processing capability, but rather in its capacity to facilitate the process through which managers identify, interpret, and make sense of weak signals, often coming from the periphery of firms’ environments, through their social/informational networks.

## 4. Theoretical background

Since sensing of weak signals tends to rely on usergenerated data and social media, we draw on social network theory and propose research avenues that can serve as a guide for research on this topic. The key premise of social network theory is that whom one knows afects, to a great extent, what one knows (Burt, 1992; Granovetter, 1973; McGrath, 2019).<sup>2</sup> There are resources embedded in the social structure that can be more easily accessed by members, depending on their positioning in the network (Burt, 1992; Coleman, 1988).

The advantage inherent in the network of relationships is commonly referred to as social capital (Coleman, 1988). Two key types of social capital are relevant to our examination of the sensing of weak signals: structural and relational. Structural social capital is derived from the overall pattern of connections between actors – that is, who a manager can reach, how he/she can reach them, and where they can reach them. In particular, we might ask whether the contacts are within a closed network or across networks (Burt, 1992).<sup>3</sup> The benefits occur through access to a wider diversity of information, early access to timely information, and control over information difusion (Burt, 2005).

Relational social capital describes the properties of the relationships people developed with each other through a history of interactions (Granovetter, 1992). The benefits lie in better access to information, improved communication between actors, and the fostering of trusting relationships among members (Coleman, 1988). Therefore, individual actors can increase their social capital by brokering two otherwise disconnected groups (Burt, 2005) or by building embedded ties in their networks (Uzzi, 1999). Social media are helpful for developing structural social capital and relational social capital by supporting the filtering, aggregating, flagging, promotion, or dismissing of signals.

## 5. Two research streams

As illustrated in Figure 1, overall, we suggest that social media can impact a manager’s network and, therefore, his/her ability to sense weak signals, which in turn can afect digital-enabled strategic agility through two pathways: Structural Social Capital and Relational Social Capital.

## 5.1. Social media and structural social capital

Social media can enhance peripheral vision through explorative uses that increase the chance of building bridging ties across communities and thus having access to distant people (McEvily & Zaheer, 1999; Tiwana, 2008). Social media allows managers to access a large number of social/informational referents with diverse mental schemas, which increases the chance of encountering new information, thereby opening up the scope of observation in the periphery. For instance, social media provide abundant opportunities for social interactions that facilitate the formation of new weak social ties outside managers’ daily social circle. In particular, managers read comments and opinions from customer-facing experts (e.g., sales, customer support, and industry commenters) who might sense weak-signals in the market or new opportunities from technology. Other IT applications – such as company white-pages, electronic bulletin boards, blogs, and special interest forums – can extend managers’ reach to relevant individuals who are external to their business units or organisations, as well as to their expertise during the information seeking process.

Accessing these weak electronic ties across communities (i.e., bridging ties) could enhance a manager’s sensing ability in three ways. First, since information circulates more within groups than between groups (Burt, 2017), bridging ties provide access to nonredundant sources of information external to local communities. Hence, these ties increase the exposure of managers to multiple bodies of knowledge and perspectives. Such exposure can help managers uncover weak signals beyond the borders of their organisations (Prahalad, 2004), discover discrepancies or inconsistencies more easily (Moran, 2005), better understand diferent parts of the confusing picture from the periphery (Day & Schoemaker, 2006), stimulate the synthesis of new understandings (Burt, 2005), and spot novel insights (Ilmola & Kuusi, 2006).

Second, bridges are the shortest inter-group paths. People with more bridging ties have faster access to more timely information and knowledge from other groups than those who do not (Hansen, 2002). The timeliness of information access positively afects sensing ability because, in a dynamic environment where changes happen fast, managers have to identify weak signals and early signs of change as fast as possible, before they become widely known (Ilmola & Kuusi, 2006).

Third, bridging ties provide access to a large base of referrals introduced by key contacts in external networks (Burt, 2005). Having access to information provided by referrals of key contacts can allow managers to enlarge their sensing of opportunities by providing large bases of information and knowledge. Therefore, managers who have wider social/informational networks through bridging ties (and weak ties) are likely to identify more ideas and recognise more opportunities than those with less extensive networks (Ozgen, 2003). Ultimately, more ideas and opportunities can be an input for timely strategic action, which in turn potentially increases the strategic agility of firms.

## 5.2. Social media and relational social capital

Structural capital focuses on how the linkages between people afect a manager’s sensing ability for weak signals. Structural capital needs to be completed by the nature or characteristics of the personal relationships people develop with each other through a history of interactions (Fleming et al., 2007). People may have communication standards, protocols, and expectations shared by the history of interactions, which can help in the transmission of relevant weak signals. Social media can not only create structural social capital, it can also be used to enhance the strength of a manager’s ties and, therefore, the degree of social embeddedness he/she has in the network (Hampton, 2002; Pickering & King, 1995). IT helps to build strong relationships that facilitate information sharing and the transfer of tacit knowledge crucial for making sense of ambiguous weak signals and for agility. IT can be used to create relational embeddedness, establishing trusting relationships and mutual understanding.

Strong ties can improve sensing for weak signals in three main ways. First, strong ties are maintained through frequent and emotionally intense communications (Pickering & King, 1995). Therefore, they allow two parties to develop shared language and mental schema – which are essential for externally scanned fuzzy and ill-defined information to be interpreted and understood. Since mental schemas function as the knowledge structures against which new information is tested for relevance, information obtained through strong ties is likely to be relevant to managers. Information relevance for a given organisational context (e.g., its strategic objectives, customers, competitors, and suppliers) is important in environmental scanning (Morrison & Wilson,

1996; Tallon & Kraemer, 2007). Having relevant information is critical during the cognition process involved in scanning and sensemaking of weak signals. Therefore, IT can be used to strengthen a manager’s ties in a network, which is likely to improve the relevance of the information and sharpen a manager’s sensing ability, and in turn agility.

Second, relational embeddedness enables access to timely information because shared mental schemas increase the efectiveness of communications and of information exchange (Towry, 2003; Weimann, 1982). Individuals who share mental schemas are better able to understand each other’s needs and requirements, and are better able to provide fresh information that fits with the information seeker’s requests (Towry, 2003). Thus, managers who are embedded in their social networks should have faster access to more timely information.

Third, relational embeddedness is also likely to moderate the relationship between the diversity of information and sensing ability. Strong social ties can help a manager make sense of and interpret diverse information coming from a variety of sources. Relational embeddedness constitutes a basis of trust that can increase manager’s acceptance of new ideas from external sources (Cross & Sproul, 2004). Also, relational embeddedness can yield richer and more in-depth information-sharing since a close contact is generally more willing to take the time to carefully explain, detail, and craft the information he/she sends. Overall, relational embeddedness is expected to facilitate the sensing of weak signals, which in turn is likely to lead to higher agility.

## 6. Implications and avenues for research

Studying the sensing of weak signals and digitalenabled strategic agility from a social network perspective, as suggested above, has implications for future research and provides interesting research avenues. This perspective helps strategic agility researchers to look across levels of analysis by considering the individual level of analysis (i.e., social networks of individual managers).<sup>4</sup> IS scholars have predominantly examined the relationship between strategic agility and IT from a macro perspective (i.e., firm-level) rather than from a micro perspective (i.e., individual-level). Existing literature focuses on how firms sense and respond to environmental changes, and pays relatively little attention to managers who actually identify and make sense of data on customers, competitors, and markets, and who make strategic decisions. More precisely, examining managers’ social connections can allow us to develop an understanding of how managers acquire, interpret, and distribute information as well as how they maintain strategic agility. We suggest that managers with bridging ties in their social network have advantages in accessing the diverse set of information in a timely manner with referrals, which leads to a superior sensing ability and strategic agility.

It would also be useful to discover how to bridge these two levels and examine how the sensing of weak signals by individuals and distributed sensemaking can be accumulated, aggregated, and integrated to form a consensus around perceptions of environments at the organisational level.<sup>5</sup> Figuring out how to bridge these levels might be challenging as individuals tend to respond to early-stage signals while groups/organisations tend to respond at later stages in the innovation cycle, where validation and resource allocation are the focus. The sensing of weak signals is often based on a massive influx of fuzzy data, sometimes with a high noise ratio, that managers are often unable to fully interpret and figure out. Making sense of such data is highly contextual and might be dificult to communicate eficiently and efectively to others. In other words, managers might have a keen sensing ability and be aware of threats, but they may fail to convince others to take appropriate action on those threats. In a sense, bridging individuals’ sensing and sensemaking and organisational sensemaking might be similar to Cassandra in Greek mythology – she who is never to be believed – no matter how good her vision might be.<sup>6</sup>

It would be interesting to examine how this issue can be resolved. It could be argued that high relational capital among managers of an organisation might reduce this tension. Managers who have frequent interactions with each other might develop shared languages and mental models, which could help them more easily make sense of ill-defined information, develop a common sensemaking of weak signals, and weed out misinformation.

It would also be interesting to examine how social media and IT in general are used by managers for the sensing of weak signals. Most existing research to date studies how firms reap benefits from “formal”, data-centric IT systems for strategic agility. In this paper, we suggest that social media might afect the sensing of weak signals. Recent advancements in business analytics can also help in identifying weak peripheral signals. For example, Pant and Sheng (2015) developed predictive models for competitor identification using website information. Shi et al. (2016) introduced an algorithm to identify potential rivals in the product, market, and technology space. Similarly, Pan et al. (2019) suggested a text analysis approach for sensing new entry threats into an industry. Along this line, interpreting weak peripheral signals might be supported by open, online communities in an approach in which firms combine external and internal ideas to make a pathway to market (Gómez et al., 2017). For example, a website platform, InnovationNet, supports the acquisition of information among external partners and helps to connect employees of diferent firms (Ravichandran et al., 2017).<sup>7</sup>

Finally, it could be interesting to examine the unintended consequences of relying on social media to sense weak signals. As a weak signal enters the network through social media, it could be considerably amplified, sometimes with far-reaching and not always desirable consequences (Jia et al., 2020). For example, misinformation could prove hazardous. If managers accept misinformation and take hasty actions, they will incur substantial costs and threats to their firms (Type II error). Rumours probably enter social media through weak signals. As such, there is a risk that firms might react to something that turns out to be completely false. On the other hand, managers may miss and wait for the correct information only to find they failed to react in time (Type I error).

## 7. Conclusion

The extant research on digital-enabled strategic agility has provided significant insight into this phenomenon. We argue that we now need to examine how managers identify and make sense of weak signals and how such signals influence the strategic agility of firms. We draw on the concept of weak signals and on social network theory to present a fresh perspective on digital-enabled strategic agility research and develop a research agenda. We hope that the current paper, which raises as many questions as it provides answers, will stimulate research on this important topic and will serve as the theoretical foundation for future work on this topic.
