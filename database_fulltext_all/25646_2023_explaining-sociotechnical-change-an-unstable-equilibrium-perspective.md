# Explaining sociotechnical change: an unstable equilibrium perspective

Louise Harder Fischer <sup>a</sup> and Richard Baskerville<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>IT-University of Copenhagen; <sup>b</sup>Georgia State University

## ABSTRACT

Within the domain of Information Systems, assumptions around sociotechnical change cohesively build on establishing a stable and balanced relationship between the social and technical structures. When these structures become too rigid, revolutionary moments that punctuate the status quo are required for the structures to accommodate to a new situation. Approaching sociotechnical change with “punctuation of equilibrium” is becoming increasingly inefective. In line with rising instability and rapid digital development, we argue that new sociotechnical change approaches are needed. In this paper we investigate how individual knowledge workers respond to the increasingly complex challenges they face when orchestrating social and technical structures to support eficient and creative knowledge work. We suggest a new perspective that covers an unstable equilibrium emerging from the interplay between individuals and their usage of “the collection of rigid and flexible workplace technologies”. Our study is based on data from interviews made with 40 knowledge professionals over a period of three years, inquiring into individual experiences with sociotechnical change. Our study reveals contradictory patterns of both technological-individualisation and -socialisation. A universal generative mechanism emerges when these patterns complement each other and generates continuous change that seems to diminish inertia from rigid structures while also intensifying agile responses.

## 1. Introduction

One of the most valuable perspectives on sociotechnical change in information systems (IS) is provided by the punctuated equilibrium model. This model explains a process in which the IS resides in a state of equilibrium and stability between its social and technical components, for extended periods of time. When transformational events occur, the system endures a brief state of disequilibrium and revolution, until reaching a new state of equilibrium (Gersick, 1991; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). In this paper we inquire into the present nature of sociotechnical change and ask if the notion of change underlying this model is shifting qualitatively. The work from which the punctuated equilibrium model evolved has always recognised periods of equilibrium as being periods of relatively greater stability than periods of disequilibrium. Small changes and minor fluctuations in stability will, however, always be present, even in periods of equilibrium (cf., Gersick, 1991; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). The pursuit of agility and the accompanying prevalence of fast-changing technologies have led, in the past decade, to increasingly unstable environments. As a result, we suspect that substantial periods of relative equilibrium in IS have grown shorter, less frequent, and/or unreliable.

Understanding the shift in the nature of change and its implication for efective IS requires us to parse the concepts of stable, equilibrium, stable equilibrium, unstable, and unstable equilibrium. In simple English parlance (OED<sup>1</sup>), stable means “not likely to change or fail; firmly established”, equilibrium means “a state in which opposing forces or influences are balanced”, or “a process and its reverse are occurring at equal rates so no overall change is taking place” and stable equilibrium means “a state in which a body tends to return to its original position after being disturbed”. Unstable means ‘not remaining steadily in the same place; apt to move or be moved about. An unstable equilibrium describes a system that is so dynamic that “stability can never be more than temporary”, such a system is in “perpetual flux, moving through periods of destabilization and stabilization, but never achieving a stable equilibrium” (Empson & Alvehus, 2020, p. 1247).

To illustrate these diferences, imagine a platform supplier who updates its email service and changes how emails are categorised and shown in in-boxes. One employee then uses the new changed system to delay receipt of all emails to end-of-day, so setting aside time to respond once a day. Another employee uses filters so that only emails from a small network of close colleagues are shown during the day, the receipt of all other emails being deferred until the end of the week, setting aside time once a week to respond. The claim that the system is stable is not one that is easy to make, as clear changes were made to the default settings and the prior work-mode. The claim that the system is in equilibrium is, however, one that is easy to make, the system changes made being ofsetting, and the technological opportunities provided being used to improve an essential work-activity. The claim that the system is in stable equilibrium also cannot be easily made, as it did not return to its original state (two processes were changed). The only state that is therefore left is unstable equilibrium.

We find it important to discuss the notion of unstable equilibrium in the context of IS change because, without this understanding, we could potentially believe that IS change is one of an inevitable descent into a more chaotic, continuous state of nonequilibrium (Licker, 2004). Influenced by such a belief, IS managers could mistakenly respond with eforts to stabilise equilibrium and as such replace flexibility with rigidity. Continuing with the platform supplier example from above, managers might reason that the diferent approaches to email usage were a descent into chaos. With such a conclusion, a reasonable manager would invoke stricter email policies for all employees. As a result, the organisation would lose any new opportunities from the flexibility provided by the email supplier that would have fit work-practice to individual preferences. The recognition of an unstable equilibrium could provide a novel perspective and help to avoid a misperception that digital transformation projects are in disarray (Bayern, 2019). It may even help prevent viable projects being consigned to our persistently large failure bin (Dwivedi et al., 2014).

Our interest and focus in this paper is in identifying generative mechanisms i.e., the trans-empirical real entities (Blom & Morén, 2011), that explain equilibrium changes in the current knowledge-intensive workplace. Non-routine tasks and cognitive work are still accelerating, despite the threat from automation (Willcocks, 2020). The knowledge professionals who undertake these tasks also account for the largest rise in the job market that we have seen in decades (Zumbrun, 2016). We consider this changing workplace to be a very relevant research setting because the pace of change is accelerating. Such acceleration is due to rapid environmental developments and technologies that are both emerging and converging (Urbach & Röglinger, 2018; Warner & Wäger, 2019). Complexity and unpredictability are therefore transforming workplaces into a state of constant flux (Ang, 2011). A typical response to this flux is the introduction of agile frameworks (Maruping & Matook, 2020); with agility becoming one of the most important characteristics of the modern organisation (Birkinshaw, 2018; Warner & Wäger, 2019). Continuous and transformative social and technical changes are therefore now taking place simultaneously (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010; Bygstad, 2017, Fischer & Baskerville, 2018). Our study, through exploring sociotechnical change and the changing nature of equilibria in these important work settings, therefore aims to provide a more current explanation of sociotechnical change. Our work furthermore focuses on autonomous individuals with fast-changing technologies in unstable environments acting to achieve change. Often these events are framed as an organisational struggle with shadow IT systems, which are systems that are developed by users or used without the knowledge of IT departments (Raković et al., 2020). Shadow IT systems can satisfy individual needs to serve the organisation, but also destabilise organisational IT systems due to their operating beyond the control of the IT department.

We have studied changes over a period of three years (Fischer & Baskerville, 2018, Fischer & Baskerville, 2020), our suspicion from this being that these dynamics will prevail. That managers and researchers understand these changes and dynamics therefore has become important. Our work aims to clarify generative mechanisms that extends the punctuated equilibrium model and provides an novel perspective of the nature of change. This new perspective explains how newer sociotechnical change emerges. It also allows IS managers and researchers to better promote an IS that remains in unstable equilibrium for extended periods of time. Our research therefore seeks to contribute to both theory and practice.

Our research approach is inspired by Rowe (2018), which difers from the approaches used in traditional studies of sociotechnical change. In our study, we examine the implications of the interaction between a collection of technologies and individual knowledge professionals in a representative subset of current knowledge and technology-based organisations. Our aim is to find out whether changes in equilibrium occur in other ways than that described in previous theories, and the implications of individual actions with technology upon sociotechnical change.

The following question guided our research. How do individual actions with work-place technology change sociotechnical equilibrium?

We first present the theoretical background from the domain of sociotechnical change and equilibrium, and then discuss the importance of understanding the underlying mechanisms that trigger change. Then we explain our research approach, the philosophical underpinnings of critical realism, and our research setting. The findings of the analysis of the interview data from 40 knowledge professionals (collected in three rounds over a 3-year period) are then presented, the empirical data describing the events and outcomes of individuals engaging with technology that trigger equilibrium changes. We furthermore infer, as a subset of our findings, dynamics that illuminate generative mechanisms to reach an understanding of how actual social-technical change emerges using a critical realist script for creative theorising in information systems (Williams et al., 2018). Lastly, we discuss our contributions to both practice and theory, and ofer conclusions.

## 1.1. Theoretical background

We will first review the sociotechnical change and equilibrium theories and perspectives premised by past IS-research. Then we will explain the qualitative shift in sociotechnical change mechanisms.

## 1.2. Sociotechnical change

In Table 1, we summarise the objectives and focal aspects described in the research literature on sociotechnical change. Much of this work provides a focus on change objectives such as routines, processes, practice, and structure improvements. This research shares the common aspiration that sociotechnical arrangements are achieved in a way that ensures an optima balance between the technology’s structural and institutional purpose, and the social system’s adaptation to new ways of carrying out work. The usual aim of this work is that stability is achieved through repetitive routines, regularities, patterns, and deep structures. These views also assume that a balanced integration between the social and the technical sub-systems in the IS can be achieved through mutual adjustment, as manifested in organisational routines, processes, practices, or sociotechnical structures.

Theories on sociotechnical change that are predominantly aimed at stabilising the setting for longer periods, seem to be inadequate responses to the current complex and unpredictable environment. We subscribe to the position that sociotechnical change theories and models are important because, without them, organisations can be pulled in what seem to be conflicting directions (Mumford, 2006; Sarker et al., 2019). Tackling complexity requires flexibility and diversity. Profit generation, however, requires eficiency and control. We often see sociotechnical systems biased towards the economic objectives of efectiveness and stability (Sarker et al., 2019), eventually exhibiting rigidity, inertia, and stickiness (Baptista et al., 2010; Besson & Rowe, 2012; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008; Tushman & Romanelli, 1985). These are characteristics that make adapting to the pace of change even more dificult. It is therefore important to develop sociotechnical change models that promote a balance between flexibility and stability (Eason, 2008) and economic and humanistic objectives (Sarker et al., 2019).

## 1.3. Equilibrium

Equilibrium is a core tenet of social-technical approaches (Eason, 2008; Lyytinen & Newman, 2006; Mumford, 2006), and is central to what we define as being a well-functioning IS (Besson & Rowe, 2012). Equilibrium is where “the social components of an organization are combined with the technical components in an attempt to create a balanced and synergistic relationship” (Grifith & Dougherty, 2002, p. 219).

In Table 2, we position sociotechnical change theories in their views of equilibrium, these being positioned in terms of whether they hold an evolutionary or revolutionary view of change (Tushman & Romanelli, 1985).

The notion of equilibrium in sociotechnical change theories associate change and equilibrium with deep structures, so manifesting the meaning of the real world that the information system intends to model. Deep structure properties are robust and stable (Wand & Weber, 1995) for two reasons: “1) The trail of choices made by a system rules many options out, at the same time as it rules mutually contingent options. 2) The activity patterns of a system’s deep structure reinforce the system through mutual feedback loops” (Gersick, 1991, p. 17). Our review suggests that sociotechnical change theories adopt two related perspectives – stable equilibrium serving as an evolutionary perspective, and punctuated equilibrium serving as a revolutionary narrative (Besson & Rowe, 2012; Silva & Hirschheim, 2007). In the stable systems model, reciprocal adjustments continuously restore equilibrium back to the original position. In the punctuated model, the system enters a state of disequilibrium when transformational change occurs, until a new state of equilibrium is achieved (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008). This describes a process of revolutionary change through the IS residing in states of equilibrium.

Deep structures inhabit information systems. Surface structures also inhabit information systems and are the phenomenon that encompass the facilities made available in the IS and that allow users to interact with the system, so manifesting the nature of the interface between the information system, its users, and the organisational environment (Wand & Weber, 1995). Surface structures, in a technological sense, define the way interface applications and devices deliver user and IT system interactions, user interaction with deep structures being through surface structures (Truex & Baskerville, 1998). Surface structures therefore depend on the deep structure, and so provide a representation of the system (Wand & Weber, 1995).

Sociotechnical change theories do not, however, consider surface structures to be a separate component of change. Unstable equilibrium is likewise unchartered territory. This equilibrium state is momentary, a continuous dynamic moving the system from one brief equilibrium to another. We use a metaphor of the walking moai of Easter Island as a simple visualisation of this form of equilibrium. Lipo et al. (2013) showed how small opposing forces (ropes) can rock large stone statues such that they move forward from one position to the next. See, Figure 1 below.<sup>2</sup>

## 1.4. Sociotechnical mechanisms

Sociotechnical theories predominantly use mechanical causal mechanisms to explain the nature of a change process. Ropohl (1999) explains change in terms of two distinct causal mechanisms. The first is the mechanism of technological institutionalisation that describes the process of trans-individualising a task or an invention to the whole work-system. The second is the mechanism of technological socialisation that describes the process of shared beliefs and social norms that emerge around carrying out work using new technology. Our view is that sociotechnical change as described, and the diferent positions on objectives (Table 1) and how equilibrium is maintained (Table 2), implicitly and explicitly subscribe to these basic mechanisms. Gill and Bhattacherjee (2009), for example, suggest that technologies move from novelty to institutionalisation. Bygstad (2017) refers to the process of integration when surface technologies (i.e., lightweight IT solutions) become stabilised and more pervasive, and then subject to standardisation and centralised governance. Robey et al. (2013), Baptista et al. (2010), Avgerou (2013), and Besson and Rowe (2012) likewise refer to institutionalisation efects, and to the risk of rigidity and inertia as a dilemma of deep structures. Robey et al. (2013) furthermore describe routinisation as the act of developing a procedure into a regular and customary process, a process that is similar to technological socialisation.

Avgerou (2013), however, argues that causal relations cannot account for the interpretive flexibility of actors encountering technology, nor for the processes shaping the IS phenomena that lead to dynamic and largely unpredictable consequences. We therefore introduce an alternative explanatory account of the present dynamic in the light of the critical realism in which this study is placed. Mechanisms are understood, in the philosophy of critical realism, as being nonmechanical and as difering from causal mechanisms in three important ways. Firstly, a generative mechanism cannot predict specific outcomes, but can account for a specific event and observation in hindsight. Secondly, generative mechanisms are relational, and have the power to change the structural relationship between individual actions and social relations, so allowing the generative mechanisms to be inferred by studying the relational character between social relations and individual actions. Thirdly, generative mechanisms are universal, and are either latent or active (Donati, 2015). In this study, we look for these certain types of generative mechanisms that account for equilibrium outcomes. These mechanisms work as a dynamic force that operates in a continuum of a set of contradictions (Donati, 2015). The relational logic is a combinatory logic that is diferent from the mechanical and causal logic found in the sociotechnical change theories. It is also diferent from the previous theorising of generative mechanisms in IS (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Strong & Volkof, 2010; Zamani & Pouloudi, 2020). We assume that this logic of mechanisms better mirror the driving force of change, a logic being based upon interaction, on a contradictory complementarity between two opposite realities at the surface and deep levels, and the acceptance of paradoxical situations, without conflating them (Donati, 2015). We will therefore continue with an explanation of sociotechnical change that can be complementary to existent theories.

## 2. Research approach

In this section, we describe the approach we adopted in this study to determine how the actions of individual knowledge professionals with concomitant workplace technology, change sociotechnical equilibrium, and therefore why unstable equilibrium define the notion of sociotechnical change in knowledge intensive settings.

## 2.1. Philosophy

In this study, we adopted a critical realist philosophical approach. Critical realists see the world in two dimensions: a transitive- and an intransitive dimension. The intransitive dimension exists outside of humans and is efective. Critical realists explain that this external world consists of generative mechanisms, and that these mechanisms are the causal structure or process that explain the essence of why and how the social world changes. The transitive dimension is accessible and observable through the conscious construction of social reality being reported and experienced by individuals uncovered in a social constructivist philosophy of science. The intransitive dimension represents a more positivistic view of reality. As researchers, we can, however, by explaining the generative mechanisms that constitute the intransitive domain, infer a more generalisable and objective explanation of why change (experienced in the transitive dimension) occurs (Archer, 2015; Bhaskar, 2008; Bygstad, 2010; Dobson, 2002; Hofkirchner, 2014). Our aim therefore is to develop a better understanding and explanation of these enduring generative mechanisms, through adopting a critical realist philosophy (Dobson, 2002).

Our philosophical perspective provides the study with the opportunity to focus on identifying the relational character of generative mechanisms, when studied at diferent levels of structures. The relational character, if activated, can explain how (and which type of) equilibrium and ultimately how sociotechnical change occurs when individuals interact with a collection of technology at their workplaces, where a deep level and surface level perspective is included.

Wolfgang Hofkirchner theorises, in Information Science, such a non-mechanical, relational, generative mechanism. He describes (Hofkirchner, 2014) how individualisation and socialisation are processes that occur simultaneously at diferent levels in a socia system, at the individual actions and social relations levels. Hofkirchner (2014) furthermore describes the interaction between socialisation and individualisation through the emerging relationship between individuated individual’s behaviour and new social forms. When we use the term individuated individuals, we mean knowledge professionals who are social actors, but who act with a self-oriented autonomy that is less anchored in social norms (Castells, 2014; Fischer & Baskerville, 2020). This is, according to Castells, the process of individualisation, and is an information societal phenomenon that has efectively changed how people in general defy firm institutionalisation and rigid social norms, so taking responsibility for their own lives and actions (Castells, 2014). However, individualisation without the simultaneous process of socialisation would end in individualism, egoism, and a withdrawal from the social setting. The generative mechanism of individualisation and socialisation, where not halted, will therefore lead to individuated individuals acting and interacting independently while forming social relations that condition the generation and utilisation of a common good. Caring for a common good or sharing a resource allows individuals to diferentiate and become individualised, Hofkirchner (2014) believing that the more individuals are “individualized”, the more they are enabled to contribute to the common good. The stronger the socialisation of such a common good, the more individuals can become individuated (Hofkirchner, 2014).

We therefore suggest transporting Hofkirchner’s universal, relational generative mechanism to the current sociotechnical reality of knowledge professionals, as we believe it will provide us with a lens that will allow us to explain how sociotechnical change emerges at the individual-level, which is observable through their actions with technology at the surface, as they form social relations and engage with institutional scripts from technology at the deeper social-level in their work-systems. Exploring change using this lens fits well with the study of how the surface level of structures becomes an important element of change. Hofkirchner (2015) also explains how this leads to the emergence of a new quality of the interaction between single entities, which we refer to as downward and upward causation between the surface and deep structures.

## 2.2. Research-setting

Our empirical focus is to identify a suficient number of occurrences of change dynamics in contemporary workplaces. The contemporary workplace of knowledge professionals, where this contains a suficient level of autonomy, accountability, status and IT-selfeficacy, constitutes the research setting and the conditioning context. Our aim therefore is to, through acquiring the experiences knowledge professionals report when reflecting upon their past, present and future practices of interacting with available technology and working in jobs of constant changes, obtain a more universal understanding of sociotechnical change and how it proceeds.

We interviewed in total 40 knowledge professionals, who worked in professional settings in organisations of diferent sizes, of diferent ages and from diferent industries. They all held similar jobs and positions, all requiring a great deal of complex and random human interaction with others and with current technology. The interviewees are knowledge professionals and have a high level of autonomy in the carrying out of their work. They, however, are dependent on others and participate in diferent groups and collaborative settings to solve problems and arrive at good decisions. They use a wide range of functional, network, and enterprise technologies in the execution of their work.

We wanted a convenient yet random sample of knowledge professionals. We therefore posted a “call for research on the changing nature of knowledge work” on one of the authors’ LinkedIn profiles (1600 contacts). LinkedIn is a network of professionals and fits well with our sample strategy. We wanted to collect a representative sample. We therefore endeavoured to achieve a variance in gender, age, education, industry and professions and invariance in the usage of a wide yet similar range of work-place technologies.

We included three criteria in participant selection: 1. Hold positions with a specific level of responsibility and status i.e., project managers, managers, and senior consultants. We also wanted respondents to have a reasonable amount of autonomy and accountability. 2. Curious about the possibilities and challenges of technology and workplace performance. We wanted respondents with a reasonable amount of IT selfeficacy. 3. Work in settings characterised by frequent changes. We wanted respondents who are used to adapting to or to promoting changes in work related issues. After emailing and messaging back and forth, we selected 18 people for interviews in the first study in 2016. We adopted a group interview format for the second study in 2017. We furthermore selected 11 new participants, repeating this approach in round three in early 2019. See, appendix A for a list of respondents.

## 2.4. Analytical approach

In this section, we explain our critical realist analysis and interpretation of our empirical data. We followed the steps provided by Williams et al. (2018). First, we developed a theoretical understanding by identifying essential concepts of sociotechnical change, equilibrium, and mechanisms identified in the background literature. These concepts served as initial codes for analysis. (1) Using these codes, we coded the empirics from round one and two to familiarise our-selves with the data. (2) Then we framed the experiences ontologically in terms of abstracted patterns and events in the surface and deep structures, and the relational character between these structures. We identified events and aspects that related to how the individuated individual acts with technology at the surface and at the deeper level and coded for events and aspects that relate to how social relations form in interaction with technology and social structures. Then we subjectedout experiences of a relational character and applied codes that relate to downward and upward causation. (3) Following on from this, we hypothesised the existence and enactment of a set of patterns underlying a universal generative mechanism that could account for the phenomenon and (4) proceeded from here with a new round of data collection to corroborate the existence and operation of the hypothesised patterns from step 3, by repeating a third round of data-collection. Table 4 describes in detail the data-analysis process.

## 3. Findings: the unstable equilibrium in sociotechnical change

Organising manner. We identified the influential power of the universal generative mechanism of “individualization and socialization” as described by Hofkirchner (2014), translating it into the sociotechnical realm as technological-individualisation and - socialisation. This translation explains how the state of an unstable equilibrium can lead to powerful sociotechnical change. At the surface we identified a pattern of autonomous actions relating to the design of individual interaction in the form of practices and flexible routines. Thus, we found evidence of the activation of one aspect of the generative mechanism, i.e., technological individualisation (in this context). We observed how the pattern of experimentation with malleable and mobile technologies halted/complemented the institutionalisation from technology at the deep structure level, thus rendering the setting less rigid (where allowed). At the deeper level we identified a pattern of social adoption of platform-technologies into shared ways of doing things along with shared ways of understanding such common actions. We thus found evidence of the activation of the other aspect of the generative mechanism, i.e., technological socialisation at the deeper level. Technological-socialisation follows from the institutionalising power of platformtechnology as deep structures, i.e., the transindividualisation of a task. The generalisable patterns at the surface and at the deeper level seem contradictory in nature. However, what happens at the surface and the deeper levels in the various settings triggers a re-balancing pattern induced through the caring for data when experienced as a shared resource. Data and information is usually seen as a relational commons (Hess, & Ostrom, 2007) and we observed how data is perceived to be a common good, as described by Hofkirchner (2014).

## 3.1. Pattern at the surface of structures

We found evidence that innovation and numerous changes occur at the surface of structures. Individuals improve how to provide and use data when they digitise information and design new routines. When acting as team-members they push for more easy and informal sharing of knowledge, which in turn improves individual and team productivity and enables a move to the cloud for mobility and flexibility. This behaviour pattern provides constant change to structures at the surface. The objective of this change is increased productivity as individuals with their own technology align with other individuals with their own technology.

Digitising information and designing new routines: When autonomy is provided at the surface, individuals improve how they provide and use data and improve how they remain productive. By designing new personal routines and customised work practices, such improvements follow. As one respondent told us:

“I have started to use OneNote extensively in my work. Rather than use my treasured Moleskin notebooks, I made the switch to digital and to taking most of my notes in OneNote. Main motivation was to enable sharing of the notes with others. An unexpected side efect was search and pictures. It turns out that searching through notes is so efective that I actually don’t remember where in my OneNote I placed a note; I simply search for it. It has forced me to be a bit more diligent in titling my notes. [.] It turns out that I can take pictures from within OneNote on my iPhone so I have both the notes and pics from whiteboards in the same place. Problem solved. And when the OneNote notebook is shared with a team, then the entire team has access to both notes and pics within a few minutes. Super efective”, (Respondent 24)

Enable easy and informal knowledge sharing: easy and informal applications allow flexibility in participation. This flexibility is important for individuals who need better and easier instant knowledge sharing with team members. As two respondents told us.<sup>.</sup>

‘We use Slack now. [My team of 5 people] wanted a common place where everyone was able to follow questions/answers to increase knowledge sharing across organizational and geographical boundaries in our team’. ‘We [. . .] create a culture of asking/answering which has limited the numbers of emails and made the discussions less formal’ (Respondent 7).

“In an attempt to revitalize our meeting culture [in my team], and make a flatter structure, we have begun using the tool Trello for planning open staf meetings. Everybody can set-up a meeting, everybody can attend and if you cannot attend, but have something to say on the subject, you can comment on the meeting card” (Respondent 33).

Improving individual and team productivity: Individuals constantly improvise with technology, improving their own productivity by introducing apps and productivity hacks. As two respondents told us:

“I have used the ofice application MyAnalytics to track my productivity by monitoring my Outlook products. My Analytics would provide me with data that e.g., every time there was a Monday morning statusmeeting, I spent 20 % of the time reading emails - was it then eficient for me to participate in those meetings” (Respondent 34).

“The way I use technology. It is so structured. I have some specific set of rules [. . .] No email notifications. When I go into my inbox in the morning. I simply get a calendar overview, so I do not get these 200 emails during night from all sorts of places. They do not pop-up right in my face. The first half hour I’m at work, I go through e-mails and convert them to tasks” (Respondent 2)

Work moves to the cloud: Individuals push towards cloud-solutions, not only to store and access their own information, but also to share information. In this way, their work becomes more mobile and flexible. As two respondents told us:

“[I’m more] mobile in my work style. I’m able to access my files and tools needed from anywhere. I can work from anywhere [. . .] can still get access to all the needed apps from any device as they are all in the cloud” (Respondent 26).

“Now we work 99% without paper and all is stored in the cloud [. . .] This change gives me the opportunity to follow and monitor trends in a snapshot of time” (Respondent 25).

## 3.2. Pattern at the deeper level of structures

We found evidence that the deeper level of structures comes from a move towards centralising data to accommodate the need for organisational transparency. This, in turn, is experienced as an increasingly greater and greater demand for compliance with new standard processes and workflows that arise from enterprisewide platforms and systems. Unlike at the surface level, where the objectives of change are increased productivity, the change objectives at the deeper level of the organisation are the eficiency and transparency of clear business processes and technical structures. Consequently, there are changes to information structures to support these change objectives. Platforms provide transparency in the technical structures. This behaviour pattern provides up-to-date information, and an alignment between people and technology.

Centralisation of data delivers organisational transparency: Data is important and involves social alignment i.e., workflows and work modes with enterprise IT. One outcome is the importance of organisational transparency, which is delivered by cloud solutions that centralise and integrate data. As explained by two respondents:

“We wanted . . . transparency and focus on the most important KPI’s, so everybody in our organization is aligned when we are talking about numbers, targets etc. All employees and investors have installed the Geckoboard app on their iPhones, which give us all live data and statistics. This has given us a full focus and common understanding of the importance of numbers and KPIs” (Respondent 28).

“We have a week plan. There are a lot of administrative things I must report on specific dates and in specific systems, to provide the upper-level managers with economic numbers and so forth. This has to be done, so that my manager can have a pre-meeting around resources before it goes further up” (Respondent 10).

Standardised workflows and processes are needed: Standard data input is essential. Such standards provide better transparency and thereby enables instant access to data. Various kinds of platforms institutionalise some processes. By explicitly promoting standardised workflows from enterprisewide platforms, work becomes optimised and accelerated. Social structures also become visible through specific work-processes. As explained by two respondents.<sup>.</sup>

“In order to be aligned and to structure project/product development, we are forced to use the same software and to use it in the same way. An example is our use of Asana (project management tool), where we manage all our projects, product development on all our platforms (web, app, mobile, design, communication). To work optimally, everybody needs to use it in exactly the same way. This means that all stakeholders communicate in common language, which of course limits misunderstandings and powers up the speed of our development” (Respondent 22).

## 3.3. Relational dynamic between surface and deep structures

In addition to the two patterns above, we also found evidence of a relational dynamic between these patterns at the surface and deep structures. Individual actions supervene in a way that depends on how the relational structuring of the deeper and surface structures progress. Actualisation with technology is a compound relationship: simultaneously two intertwined and inseparable relationships. The relationship between being social and being individual is also a relationship at the organisation’s deep and surface structure level. The relational character between actions at the surface and deep level triggers a constant re-balancing activity between four sets of complementary contradictions. Below we report a set of distinct relational triggers of upward and downward causation, as illustrative examples of how this relationship advances.

Individual autonomy at the surface triggers structured behaviour: Individual autonomy at the surface triggers a great deal of planning and organising; When members are granted full freedom in how they work, then working together requires a lot of structuring. As experienced by one respondent:

“I use 20% of my time on planning actual interaction. There is this asynchronous move in how we live and work $\left[ \ldots \right]$ working virtual like I do, takes a lot of planning [. . .] spontaneous calls don’t work, people don’t answer. So, to get the ship sailing, you need to know when you [can] have a 1:1 with people” (Respondent 18).

On the other hand, when work becomes too strict, actions become more egoistic. In extreme cases people quit.

“I enjoy the strict sales processes if they work. But they don’t work for me. And I would like to have much more autonomy over my tools, techniques, and teams. But I get a totally diferent balance when I start on my own on 1 Oct” (Respondent 26).

Strict processes trigger a need for freer workflows. When autonomous and accountable members experience strict and narrow processes, they counter by navigating a free and more creative workflow to fulfil their jobs. Sometimes it feels as if it is a struggle to navigate. As one respondent states:

‘So, as the processes become more and more fixed and rigorous, there is an opposite pool of people who need to work across departments and outside the strict or tight processes. So, you could say that sometimes, I think, maybe there is a tendency to hire people into the process, and those are the types of people the company wants to cultivate. Yet, the proportion of those who have such a more personal commitment and ownership and need to navigate more broadly in the organization, unknowingly ‘fall out’ because we are constantly struggling to be outside the established processes in some way’ (Respondent 4).

Continuous innovation with technology at the surface triggers establishing shared norms at the deeper levels. The freedom to choose technology will in turn trigger compromises and agreements. Autonomy, accountability, and the possibility of continuous innovation at the surface creates issues in establishing shared norms. The more access to data needed, the more people must adjust their work-modes. Compromises are needed; freedom is limited. As one respondent states:

‘Yesterday we had a series of meetings about starting closer cooperation with a customer. [. . .] at the end of the day you have been asked to join four diferent online groups. Some use Microsoft Groups, someone has made a Sharepoint site, someone made a Microsoft Teams site. It is a great example [of] that there are many diferent channels and people have diferent preferences. When you are fifteentwenty people, there is a need to agree on a platform. [. . .] So, the channel that fits best in the set of channels I already have, [is what] I usually chose. All those lying outside I simply ignore. Not because I do not rationally know that they are important, but they don’t fit into the limited channel bandwidth I have. I think there are two levels in it. I think the first is personal preferences and freedom to choose, the second is if there are enough people who kind of agree that now it is this platform that is being used, then the value of it can get so high that the tolerance for having to take something extra in increases. [. . .]. Occasionally, I know I am simply going to have to be on this Sharepoint Site [. . .] it annoys me that I must do it, but the value in what’s in there is big enough for me, to exceed my pain threshold’ (Respondent 1).

Company control of platform in turn triggering creative work styles at the surface. Proper company control of data and platforms provides people with a lot of freedom to experiment with work styles. However, it can also become either too uncoordinated or too strict. As one respondent tells us:

” We have a very structured setup, but we look at services and solutions that actually set the work style free. We use the same hardware package for all, but let people explore all aspects of the suites and workstyles they get in the digital workspace. One example is that we eliminate solutions like Slack and Google Docs and implement the same solutions in Teams and Ofice 365. It enables us to have control of data and security but empower people to explore new ways of working together and from all locations and devices. And still have an Enterprise Architecture that we can aford and control” (Respondent 36).

Without this enabling pattern, it is dificult to nurture knowledge and data. Data is observed as being an important resource (a relational common) benefitting everyone.

Access to data from everywhere: Individuated individuals increasingly introduce apps and devices into their own routines and new practices to access and share data. As explained by one respondent.<sup>.</sup>

“Data synchronization has changed the way I work I’m a sucker for technology and I have an enormous number of devices. I have exactly the same access to everything across all platforms. I can work from everywhere with the same eficiency” (Respondent 28).

## 3.5. The unstable equilibrium model

Our interpretation of the evidence suggests that a “individual pattern of actions at the surface”, “a collective pattern of actions at the deeper level” and the “dynamic relational pattern between these actions”, jointly enable a state of continuous re-balancing that deliver an unstable equilibrium, and that such a state is now characteristic of knowledge intensive settings. The unstable equilibrium is the outcome of seemingly contradictory patterns at diferent levels, working together, collectively activating a generative mechanism of “technological individualization and socialization”. This mechanism now inhabits organisational settings when enabled through caring for data and when experienced as a relational commons. In Figure 6 we use the metaphor of the Moai to illustrate how sociotechnical change occurs in the knowledge intensive settings that constitute the conditioning context of our study. The influential power from the generative mechanism is illustrated as individuals pulling ropes that tilt the Moai from side-toside, with the result that it moves forward

The generative mechanism penetrates the structures to the core, technological -individualisation and - socialisation continuously acting as contradictory forces that become complementary in keeping the structures adapting and moving. Agility inhabits the surface structures due to the array of apps as platform interfaces, permitting interoperability with a wide variety of “plugin” technologies at the surface level. Constant changes and innovation occur in the surface structures, organisational IT existing in a continuous state of an unstable equilibrium based on individual, social and technical preferences. The instability arises from individual actions that sometimes even include unplugging from the setting. The rear team pulling ropes represent the relational commons manifest in shared data structures. This relational commons fosters and promotes certain shared ways of thinking and working that keep the Moai upright, but tilted forward so it will “walk”; continuously moving further away from its original position. In other words, the shared commons keeps the sociotechnical system from falling flat on its face because of the unstable equilibrium. The socialisation adapts to the platform and the individualisation arises from within this socialisation in the choice of surface technologies (i.e., flexible and mobile applications). As a result, the surface is largely unstable, this scenario yielding an unstable equilibrium between the deeper structures and the surface structures. In Figure 6, data is the centre-of-gravity in the Moai, and is the point of the sociotechnical arrangement that must be kept true. Holding the space of unstable equilibrium requires an activation of the generative sociotechnical mechanism of “technological individualization and - socialisation”. The contradictory complementary nature of these processes keeps the centre of gravity, the relational commons, correctly positioned to rock progressively forward.

## 4. Discussion

Our findings suggest that the current pace of technological development, and the frequent multiple ofsetting of technical and social changes, renders long periods of stability less likely. In a context of instability and fast technological development, deep structures are now anchored to technologies such as digital platforms that are more changeable and adaptive. At the same time in this context, deep structures are also afected by social and individual practices that are more agile and flexible, so reducing their tendency to dampen equilibria disturbances. This inherent emergence suggests two possible adjustments to the punctuated equilibrium model. Firstly, we cannot define radical or revolutionary change in a punctuated unstable equilibrium model through distinguishing change in deep structures. This change must be defined in surface structures. Both structures (and possibly others) may be involved in the ofsetting changes that comprise periods of unstable equilibrium. What we previously considered radical or revolutionary change has become more common and ordinary. Evolutionary change cannot be confused with the stable equilibrium model that describes systems settling back to their original positions. Secondly, the (agile) organisational ability to easily make ofsetting adjustments to its structures and to adapt to changes in its environment, will tend to create much longer periods between punctuations. A setting characterised by agility, flexibility and freedom to change will find much less frequent need to recreate itself or completely reorient itself.

Therefore, this study suggests several original contributions to the domain of sociotechnical change that are relevant for both managers and scholars.

First, we reveal an emerging and novel type of equilibrium i.e., unstable equilibrium at play across the settings of knowledge professionals. The principle of an unstable equilibrium denotes a state of equilibrium in a setting, such that when the status quo is slightly displaced, it departs from its original position, and continues to do so. Recent models of sociotechnical change focus on restoring stable equilibrium from non-equilibrium conditions or on forcing an episodic revolutionary change to its deep structures. Primarily we found that the concept of unstable equilibrium helps explain how sociotechnical change is unfolding in today’s knowledge intensive workplaces inhabited by knowledge professionals. The unstable equilibrium is an original contribution with roots in previous research into punctuated equilibria (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008; Silva & Hirschheim, 2007; Tushman & Romanelli, 1985). This idea also has roots in the work on the fragility of an equilibrium in current organisation dynamics (Empson & Alvehus, 2020). Aside from making an original contribution on its own, the unstable equilibrium also extends prior literature on change, particularly in elaborating the conceptualisation of contradictory complementarity as a social dimension of an unstable equilibrium. This aspect extends previous research by ofering a more elaborate explanation of events at the surface level in relation to events at the deep structure level (e.g., Besson & Rowe, 2012; Gersick, 1991; Lyytinen & Newman, 2008).

Second, we extend the knowledge of how generative mechanisms influence the sociotechnical reality of knowledge professionals in contemporary settings. Inspired by a universal generative mechanism of individualisation and socialisation, we theorise ofsetting contradictory yet complementary movements (Hofkirchner, 2014). Looking at individual and social behaviour patterns at the surface and at the deeper levels in relation to “the usage of the collection of work-place technologies”, we can explain the simultaneous interplay between technological-individualisation and -socialisation as a dualdimensional mechanism that re-balances the IS, yet constitutes an unstable equilibrium (Empson & Alvehus, 2020). This is a novel contribution to IS-research theorising of mechanisms. Prior sociotechnical studies imply assumptions of a mechanical causal mechanism (Avgerou, 2013), while more recent critical realist studies theorise generative mechanisms and how they contingently combine or halt each-other in relation to one specific technology or device (Bygstad, 2010; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; McGrath, 2013; Strong & Volkof, 2010; Zamani & Pouloudi, 2020).

Third, our study indicates that sociotechnical change models must now include surface structures in which continuous changes from individuated individual’s actions with new and fast-changing technologies become an influential component of actual change. Recent models focus on deep structures, as the axis of change. Change in surface structures is assumed, in much research, to be dependent on change in deep structures (Lyytinen & Newman, 2006, 2008; Strong & Volkof, 2010; Wand & Weber, 1995), insignificant or eventually becoming deep structures (Bygstad, 2017). We challenge assumptions that disequilibrium is a matter of disruptions in the deep structures. Such assumptions unnecessarily inhabit the punctuated equilibrium model. We ofer an elaboration path for the punctuated equilibrium model that considers surface structures as the centre of emergence. We extend the fundamental work on surface structures and deep structures in suggesting that instability in the surface structures can drive change in those surface structures to a more independent degree however, with downward and upward causation from the deeper structures, from which stability become less and less significant. The whole settings rest in unstable equilibrium and provide both well-being and eficiency (Sarker et al., 2019). Thus, we suggest another perspective of equilibrium than Lyytinen and Newman (2008), Lee (2010), and Swanson (2017) and an approach that recommits to developing the sociotechnical perspective (Sarker et al., 2019). Central to our findings is the concept of “a relational commons” as an important and significant component of an unstable equilibrium and as a trigger of the universal generative mechanism, enabling the dynamic movement forward i.e., the direction of the black dotted arrows in Figure 6. A relational commons is something people care about and are dependent on. Data and information serves, in our research, as a relational commons (Hess & Ostrom, 2007) that people care about. The relational commons is a new concept in relation to sociotechnical change. While information is at the core of the IS-discipline, it has long been pushed to the backstage (Sarker et al., 2019). By promoting data and information as a commons and as a new component of change in the IS, we can move it to the foreground. In the data-driven future, we suspect that enterprises wil look for IS managers that can deliver on the agenda of unstable equilibrium and to approach data as a relational commons.

For future studies, we suspect that knowledge intensive settings inhabit possibilities of enforcing deeper organisational data structures, while simultaneously catering to the individuated individual’s autonomy at the surface. This eventually involves further examination of the concept of data and information conceptualised as a commons (Hess & Ostrom, 2007) and as a common good (Hofkirchner, 2014) and the mutual relation to individualisation (Castells, 2014). This involves more knowledge of how to trigger the generative mechanism of technological-individualisation and -socialisation properly, without causing breakdowns (Hofkirchner, 2014) or tipping surface structures towards deep structures (Bygstad, 2017).

Our study is based on qualitative data from individuals across settings. Future longitudal context- and case-specific studies could potentially reveal situations in which the universal generative mechanism is halted momentarily, thereby not producing unstable equilibrium. Future research is needed to identify these situations and settings. Future quantitative research is likewise required to investigate whether the unstable equilibrium can be detected within larger, normal, and more random datasets.

## 5. Conclusion

From background literature of sociotechnical change, we found that the objective was to usually reach a state of stability through the enablement of routines, practices, processes, and structures, the deeper structures of an organisation being through this solidified. These theories assumed that sociotechnical settings exist for long periods of equilibrium through the continuous stabilisation of deeper structures or the setting of a new stable state by managers’ revolutionary interventions. In general, the causal mechanism of technological-institutionalisation and technologicalsocialisation explained this stabilisation outcome. We find that the characteristics described in our research may be suficient to define a new dynamic. The essence of this dynamic is continuous sociotechnical change. This continuous change occurs in surface structures where individuated individual’s actions with new and fast-changing technologies become an influential component of actual change. This dynamic is characterised by an unstable equilibrium for long periods due to the relational character of the universal generative sociotechnical mechanism of technological individualisation and -socialisation. This new dynamic may be growing prevalent today. With this insight, IS managers and researchers are better prepared to operate with an IS that remains in unstable equilibrium thereby increasing the possibility of a faster response to changing environments.
