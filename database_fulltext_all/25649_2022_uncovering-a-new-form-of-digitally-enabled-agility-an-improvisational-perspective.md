# Uncovering a new form of digitally-enabled agility: an improvisational perspective

Nadège Levallet <sup>a</sup> and Yolande Chan<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Maine Business School, University of Maine System, Orono, CA USA; <sup>b</sup>Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Canada

## ABSTRACT

Agile organisations address changes with ease and speed. These changes are tackled through the concept of ongoing agility, using a sense-respond process, composed of sensing, decisionmaking, and acting. Increasingly, however, unexpected events require a diferent agility response. In this study, we propose and empirically examine how improvisational agility – conceptualised as organisation improvisational capability, the ability to sense and respond to unexpected events with speed and creativity – is a type of agility better suited for unanticipated events. Specifically, we focus on the improvisational agility sense-respond process. Using four exploratory cases, we find that organisations can leverage existing ongoing agility mechanisms for improvisational agility and, through learning, can leverage improvisational agility in ongoing agility. We also explain how diferent components of information management capability support improvisational agility, depending on the unexpected events and the need for speed or creativity.

## KEYWORDS

Organisational agility; organisational improvisation capability; ongoing agility; improvisational agility; sense-respond process; information management capability; unexpected events; organisational learning; case study

## 1. Introduction

In 2016, Mastermind Toys, a Canadian speciality toy retailer, identified a need to improve its customers online experience. In 2018, the company launched services such as a loyalty programme and an in-store clickand-collect option (Patterson, 2018). The organisation saw itself as a trendsetter, worked in close collaboration with its suppliers to identify new products, and received a prestigious toy retail innovation award (Bouw, 2021). In January 2020, the organisation recognised the need to further embark on a complete digital transformation journey when the new chief executive oficer (CEO) Sarah Jordan joined the company: “I knew that we needed to build out our digital experience and bring the same wonder kids feel in our stores to our website” (Dallaire, 2020). The revamped online platform was launched in 2020, using Shopify Plus platform technology (Grigoruk, 2020).

With these changes, Mastermind Toys moved ahead of its competitors with their online presence. The organisation had detected a trend towards digital experience, interpreted it as an opportunity to diferentiate the company, and proactively implemented an action plan that leveraged the organisation’s capabilities. This illustrates ongoing organisational agility, where organisations detect and respond to changes with ease, speed, and dexterity (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Organizations use a sense-respond process, where they continuously sense events and changes in the environment, interpret these, and decide on a plan that they then act on (Park et al., 2017).

At the same time, a diferent type of agility occurred at Mastermind Toys. When the COVID-19 pandemic hit Canada in March 2020 and all non-essential businesses like Mastermind Toys had to close their doors, Jordan knew it was urgent to react: “We almost immediately pivoted to accelerate that digital strategy. While we were already working on the overhaul of our website, we quickly had to introduce curbside pickup”.

This unexpected event (the COVID-19 imposed lockdown) triggered a quick and creative response from the organisation on a number of initiatives, such as ofering curbside pick-up within days. There was not much time to design action plans; rather decisions and reconfigurations of resources and capabilities in the organisation happened concurrently. The organisation had to rapidly design new processes and modify its online platform – leveraging its in-store click-and-collect programme experience and its knowledge of parents’ needs – to let customers pick up their purchase outside the store. This meant developing new functionality on the website, and creating contactless pickup procedures for employees and customers. According to Jordan, the pandemic forced the organisation to decide and act fast: “For me, it was an acceleration of our strategy . . . We had to move faster”

(Bouw, 2021). This type of agility, which we refer to as improvisational agility, is in contrast to the ongoing agility mentioned above and is the focus of this study.

In the information systems (IS) field, agility literature is generally focused on ongoing agility, where organisations use a continuous sense-respond process (Holmqvist & Pessi, 2006; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011; Ngai et al., 2011; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Ideally, agile organisations act ahead of their competition, meaning that they see what others could not and act on an opportunity or threat before anyone else. Digital resources and capabilities are key in facilitating ongoing agility, because they allow for timely and efective information management in sensing (e.g., scanning capabilities), decision-making (e.g., communication tools), and acting (e.g., enterprise systems).

Ongoing agility is useful for events that can be predicted or proactively detected in the environment, that is before they occur. The Mastermind Toys example does remind us that other events – like the COVID-19 lockdown – call for a diferent type of agility response. In these cases, ongoing scanning of the environment cannot anticipate or detect the event, and a more reactive agility response is needed. The underlying sense-respond process components – i.e., sensing, decision-making, and acting (Park et al., 2017) – are similar for both ongoing and improvisational agility. However, we argue that their specific roles and purposes difer, and that improvisational agility is developed diferently from ongoing agility. Consequently, we use a capability lens to guide our theorising and ask the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1: How does the improvisational agility senserespond process compare with the ongoing agility sense-respond process?

RQ2: How is the improvisational agility senserespond process built?

To address these questions, we leverage the improvisation literature, and specifically the organisational improvisation capability (OIC) literature, to examine how the sense-respond process components vary between ongoing agility for predictable events and improvisational agility for unexpected events, and how these forms of agility are related. We also examine the specific role of digitally-enabled information management capability (IMC) to address RQ2. As noted above, digital resources and capabilities for information management are key in agility. Digitally-enabled IMC is defined as an organisation’s ability to leverage digital infrastructure and information management (IM) processes to provide quality information for decision-making (Marchand et al., 2000; Mithas et al., 2011). While the OIC literature informs us on how IMC might enable improvisational agility (Levallet & Chan, 2018; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010), we know little about its specific role during the senserespond process.

We focus our study on small and medium enterprises (SMEs) to address the call by Park et al. (2017) to examine agility and the role of IMC in SMEs, especially in fast and unpredictable environments. We study whether improvisational agility is more applicable and visible in these contexts. With increasing numbers of unexpected events, improvisational agility is bound to become a prominent area of agility research. With this study, we contribute to the next frontier in agility research by diferentiating between ongoing agility and the improvisational agility needed for unexpected events in conditions of extreme time pressure and uncertainty, and by highlighting the important role of creativity for improvisational agility in these contexts.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Organisational agility

Definitions of organisational agility vary (Tallon et al., 2019), but studies in the IS literature converge to define agility as a dynamic capability. Dynamic capabilities are often defined as an organisation’s “ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments” (Teece et al., 1997, p. 516), through a senseseize-reconfigure process (Teece, 2007).

The view of organisational agility as a dynamic capability was discussed early on in the agility literature. Agility serves as the mechanism to identify opportunities and exploit them through the ongoing reconfiguration of operational capabilities (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Recent research has further anchored agility as a dynamic capability, defining it as “an organization’s ability to quickly sense and respond to environmental changes in order to quickly seize market opportunities” (Park et al., 2017, p. 649) and the “degree to which a firm is able to sense and respond quickly to customer-based opportunities for innovation and competitive action” (Roberts & Grover, 2012, p. 232). Agility is especially useful to support “strategic tasks of sensing and responding to internal and external business events of environmental changes” (Park et al., 2017, p. 650). When organisations have developed an agility capability, they can continuously sense and respond to environmental changes through the reconfiguration of organisational resources and capabilities (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011).

Our review of the agility literature<sup>1</sup> supports definitions of agility as a capability emphasising a senserespond process to address environmental events (Overby et al., 2006; Sambamurthy et al., 2003;

Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Appendix A shows that most studies discuss the sensing, decision-making, and acting components of agility – sometimes implicitly.

Importantly, this sense-respond process suggests that organisations should continually be on the lookout for changes that might impact them, using formal monitoring capabilities. Ideally, agile organisations detect changes ahead of others and interpret these as opportunities or threats as part of their decisionmaking processes; to do so, they leverage past knowledge and current information at their disposal (Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012; Park et al., 2017; Teece et al., 2016). Before acting, organisations often leverage decision-making capabilities and mechanisms such as scenarios and experiments to help devise an action plan (Park et al., 2017; Teece et al., 2016). Execution of the action plan involves the speedy reallocation of organisational resources and capabilities, in part thanks to flexible digitally-enabled operational processes (Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012; Roberts & Grover, 2012). The sensing, decision-making, and acting components of organisational agility need to work well together for the organisation to be agile (Park et al., 2017).

## 2.2. Ongoing agility and improvisational agility

We note three key challenges in the agility literature related to events and associated sense-respond processes. A first challenge lies in the frequent lack of discussion concerning how the sense-respond process varies depending on diferent environmental changes. These changes or events tend to be categorised in terms of customer needs, partner needs, competitor moves, or technological changes (Holmqvist & Pessi, 2006; Park et al., 2017; Ravichandran, 2018; Richardson et al., 2014; Roberts & Grover, 2012; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). In many studies, the environment is described based on turbulence level or the speed and rate of change (Park et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Events are sometimes characterised based on uncertainty or predictability, or their level of unexpectedness (Vervest et al., 2004). Yet, except for a few studies (e.g., Chakravarty et al., 2013), it is assumed that the agility sense-respond process does not vary, regardless of the event. In quantitative studies, which represent most empirical agility studies, environmental dynamism tends to be used as a control variable. Only a few studies (e.g., Park et al., 2017) explicitly examine the specific nature of events for agility. As a result, we know little about whether the agility sense-respond process difers based on diferent environmental events.

Second, an assumption in prior agility research is made that, with the right digital resources and capabilities to scan the environment, events can be detected on an ongoing basis, which triggers a proactive agile response by organisations, ideally ahead of the competition (Teece et al., 2016). However, over time, organisations may favour sensing mechanisms that have proven useful in the past and thus prioritise and invest in these capabilities over others, potentially leaving other types of events undetected (Winter, 2004).

Therefore, a third challenge exists to determine what happens when these events are not detected proactively but rather sensed reactively (Chakravarty et al., 2013) and still require an agile response. It is possible that the sense-respond process might difer from the “regular” continuous sense-respond scenario discussed in the agility literature. In addition, most reviewed studies, with a few notable exceptions (Holmqvist & Pessi, 2006; Huang et al., 2014; Park et al., 2017), operationalise sensing, decision-making, and acting using a single combined scale, sometimes with only implicit distinctions among them. We need to examine whether all components of the senserespond process always matter in agility and the roles they play depending on the type of events (Park et al., 2017).

It is increasingly important to challenge assumptions suggesting that events are similar in nature and can be detected proactively, and that the senseresponse process is a one-size-fits-all approach for any event. Organizations today face events that cannot be proactively detected. Unexpected events are those that happen all of a sudden or take an unexpected turn (Bechky & Okhuysen, 2011; Cunha et al., 2006). They cannot be proactively detected by typical sensing capabilities but are nonetheless sensed reactively (after the fact). These events often require a swift response because of their likely immediate impact on the organisation.

Two categories of unexpected events have been discussed in past research: global events with a potential major impact; and “ordinary” day-to-day events that may only afect one or a few organisations. Global events such as climate-related catastrophes (hurricanes, floods, droughts, wildfires, etc.), pandemics like COVID-19, social movements, and political events (e.g., Brexit) are on the rise (Bernstein, 2020; Erickson, 2020; UNESCO, 2020; USGS, n.d.). The advent of social media, big data, and information ubiquity and immediacy create heightened expectations for immediate organisational actions in response to environmental events (Van Knippenberg et al., 2015).

Unexpected ordinary events afect the focal organisation or a few organisations. These events can be unexpected market disruptions, customer requests, or breaking news stories (Ciuchta et al., 2021; O’Toole et al., 2020). Similarly, organisations in high clockspeed industries such as telecommunications, electronics, and media and entertainment are forced to innovate faster or run the risk of being displaced by disruptive innovations (BCG Global, 2020; Chan et al., 2019).

Unexpected events also range in levels of uncertainty and time pressure. Events with high uncertainty have many “unknown unknowns” (Teece et al., 2016) and require organisational responses to be creative and deviate from regular processes and routines. In contrast, unexpected events with low uncertainty might also require deviations from regular processes but in more limited ways. Events with high time pressure require urgent organisational responses, in contrast to events with more limited time pressure (Crossan et al., 2005).

Agile organisations can devise plans to try to anticipate and recover from unexpected events, for instance, with disaster recovery plans or succession planning. Indeed, some organisations had “detected” the risks linked to a potential global pandemic and developed plans to address these risks. Still, when governments around the world ordered their countries into full lockdowns, these organisations needed to move from their “regular” ongoing agility mode to an improvisational agility mode to address this sudden, unexpected event escalation. While organisations may have been prepared, the eventual occurrence of the event did not necessarily mean they were efective in responding to the threat using ongoing agility.

## 2.3. Improvisational agility conceptualised as organisational improvisation capability

The agility literature is mainly focused on ongoing agility, which allows organisations to sense and respond to mainly detectable events with a more limited time pressure. To enhance our understanding of agility in other settings, we turn to the improvisation literature, more specifically the literature on OIC. Building from the tendency to improvise through “the deliberate fusion of the design and execution of a novel production’ (Cunha et al., 2017: 560; Miner et al., 2001: 314)” (Ciuchta et al., 2021, p. 290), OIC refers to the ability to improvise efectively. As such, OIC has been defined as the organisational ability to address unexpected events by leveraging organisational resources and capabilities in a speedy and creative way (Kung & Kung, 2019; Levallet & Chan, 2018; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010; Vera et al., 2016). OIC’s impact varies in terms of degrees or levels, suggesting that OIC’s responses may vary from limited to extensive deviation from existing processes, requiring limited or extensive reconfiguration of resources and capabilities, and producing slightly modified or brand new product and services (Crossan et al., 2005; Miner et al., 2001). We argue below that in unexpected, uncertain, high-pressure settings, the agility we see is improvisational agility which can be conceptualised as OIC, because like ongoing agility: (1) OIC is a dynamic capability<sup>2</sup> that organisations can develop; (2) OIC leverages the sense-response process to address unexpected events; and (3) the nomological net of OIC and ongoing agility is similar.

First, similar to ongoing agility, OIC is a type of dynamic capability. In their recent review of the improvisation literature, Ciuchta et al. (2021) note that some of the improvisation research has been focused on studying the development of improvisational capabilities through repeated patterns of improvisational actions (Baker et al., 2003; Kung & Kung, 2019; O’Toole et al., 2020; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010; Vera et al., 2016). The dynamic aspect of OIC is related to the need for organisations to learn how to best reconfigure existing resources and capabilities given each specific unexpected event requiring an improvisational response (Vera et al., 2016). In their study of improvisation in research and development teams, Vera et al. (2016) define improvisation as a dynamic capability. As a dynamic capability, OIC allows the organisation to “alter how it ‘earns a living (Winter, 2003)” (Vera et al., 2016, pp. 1877–78), leading to changes in processes, operational capabilities, and products and services ofered.<sup>3</sup>

Second, the sense-respond process that characterises ongoing agility is also present in OIC. OIC is partly defined by its relationship to time, notably in the convergence between planning to improvise (decision-making) and execution of improvised actions (acting; Baker et al., 2003; Crossan et al., 2005; Moorman & Miner, 1998). This requires both quick actions with limited or no time to plan, and creativity to be able to deviate slightly or significantly from existing routines and processes. Past literature suggests that sensing-deciding-acting components are present in OIC (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010). As noted in Appendix B, a review of the OIC literature indicates that the sense-respond process is centred on decisionmaking and acting components. Because of the unexpected nature of events triggering the need for an improvisational response, and therefore the dificulties associated with proactive detection of these events, sensing is present but is not the most significant aspect of OIC. Instead, when an organisation faces an unexpected event, sensing is reactive and often limited to its recognition. What matters most is the leveraging of information and knowledge (past and present) to make decisions and act rapidly and creatively. Because of the time crunch generally associated with an unexpected event, organisations often interpret, design (decide), and execute (act) their responses at the same time, in a swift, iterative, creative way. Therefore, from a sense-response process view, decision-making and acting should play a prominent role in the conceptualisation and measure of OIC. Last, while sensing has not been explicitly conceptualised or operationalised in most improvisation studies, calls have been made to investigate the overall senserespond process of OIC (Vera et al., 2016, p. 1901).

Figure 1 visually represents our literature-based interpretation of both ongoing agility and OIC and highlights the diferent levels of importance of sensing, decision-making, and acting components in ongoing agility vs. improvisational agility, conceptualised as OIC. We also show that ongoing agility is best suited for events that are like “waves” in the environment, compared to improvisational agility that is best suited for “storms” (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010).

Third, the OIC’s nomological net is also similar to ongoing agility’s nomological net, where the important role of knowledge resources, enabling digital technology capabilities, and other organisational capabilities has been noted. Appendix C provides an overview of the similarities between ongoing agility and OIC, as noted in the literature.<sup>4</sup> From this, we see that in terms of organisational context, both OIC and ongoing agility benefit from top management energy and expertise (Crossan et al., 2005, p. 200; Park et al., 2017), a limited organisational structure and an experimental culture (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1997; Davis et al., 2009; Ravichandran, 2018; Teece et al., 2016), and access to timely or real-time information and knowledge through IMC (Eisenhardt & Tabrizi, 1995; Miner et al., 2001; Park et al., 2017). The role of digital technologies to support ongoing agility and OIC is also noted, for instance, through the use of specific digital tools such as business intelligence and communications technologies, or more generally through digital capabilities (Levallet & Chan, 2018; Park et al., 2017; Ravichandran, 2018; Tallon et al., 2019). Last, when viewed as a means to an end (Tallon et al., 2019), the organisational impact of both OIC and ongoing agility includes overall organisational performance (Richardson et al., 2014), innovation and new product development capability (Pavlou &

El Sawy, 2010; Ravichandran, 2018; Vera et al., 2016), and overall responsiveness or flexibility (Moorman & Miner, 1998; Ngai et al., 2011). In sum, as the core definitional and nomological elements of OIC and ongoing agility are the same, we conceptualise improvisational agility as OIC.

## 2.4. Agility in small and medium enterprises

Our review of the ongoing agility literature suggests that, aside from a few notable exceptions (e.g., Chan et al., 2019; Ngai et al., 2011), the majority of empirical agility studies have focused on large businesses, or have not distinguished between large and small businesses in their studies. However, developing agility may be diferent in SMEs than in large organisations. Park et al. (2017) recently called for further study of the agility sense-respond process in SMEs.

It may be more dificult for SMEs to be agile (Chan et al., 2019; O’Toole et al., 2020). Many SMEs face more financial constraints than larger firms, which may limit their ability to develop the digital tools and expertise needed to sense the environment, or the slack resources that could be easily reconfigured to address a specific event. In addition, SMEs may encounter dificulties when rapidly coordinating decisions and actions, because of the heavy demands usually placed solely on the top management team (O’Toole et al., 2020). Despite this, SMEs generally face less dificulty in terms of information flow than larger organisations (Park et al., 2017). Information management and decision-making capabilities are expected to be more developed in more mature SMEs than in younger ones (Arend, 2014). Additionally, the organisational structure of SMEs may not be as rigid as in larger organisations that tend to have more structured work environments. Consequently, smaller businesses may disseminate information, adapt processes, and learn more easily (Chan et al., 2019), suggesting that there is particular value in studying improvisational agility in SMEs.

We also argue that OIC is a form of agility, namely improvisational agility. As such, we aim to make explicit the sense-respond processes of improvisational agility in SMEs. ## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Multiple case studies of small and medium enterprises

We used an exploratory case study approach to understand how the improvisational agility sense-respond process compares to ongoing agility’s sense-respond process, and how improvisational agility is built (Yin, 2018). The case study research method is helpful to examine phenomena within their organisational contexts, allowing researchers to develop deeper insights (Yin, 2018). Multiple retrospective case studies constitute a widely-accepted research method used in research on ongoing agility (e.g., Chan et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2014; Ngai et al., 2011) and are well suited to address our questions.

We used a purposeful theoretical sampling method. In order to restrict the domains of interest and increase the chances that improvisational agility might be visible in our sample, we chose three theoretical criteria: environmental dynamism, organisational size, and experimental culture. First, for environmental dynamism, we selected organisations within service industries because they are characterised by innovation, change, as well as market and technology unpredictability (McGrath, 2013). Second, regarding organisation size, we chose to study SMEs of 50 to 500 employees (Government of Canada, 2021); this sector is under-researched, despite being a key economic driver. Third, organisations with an experimental culture are more likely to embrace change and deviate from regular routines in their responses to environmental changes, making them a prime setting for agility (Crossan et al., 2005; Ravichandran, 2018).

To select cases, we used a theoretical replication logic, which is helpful to identify contrasting but somewhat expected findings (Yin, 2018). While the selected organisations have common characteristics because they meet the stated theoretical criteria and we expected them to display agility, we still sought diversity in the type of organisations studied (e.g., industry, oferings, information technology (IT) strategy). This design ofered the opportunity to identify variability in organisations within these specific selection factors, making our results potentially more generalisable (Dubé & Paré, 2003). Following preliminary discussions to confirm eligibility to participate (fitting the theoretical criteria, interest from all top management team members), four of 11 organisations that met our criteria were selected to participate in the study.

## 3.2. Data collection

Data was collected using semi-structured retrospective interviews over a four-month period. We conducted one pilot study (data was not included in the main study) to refine the interview protocols. We interviewed IT and non-IT executives or senior managers in the organisations. The 20 interviews (averaging 51 minutes each) were recorded, transcribed (289 pages total; average of 14.5 pages per interview), and coded. Participants reviewed their interview transcripts for accuracy. In addition, secondary data from online documents, websites, and internal documents (strategic plans, presentations, etc.) was collected and analysed. Following preliminary data analysis, we also conducted follow-up interviews with some participants to confirm our interpretation of the findings (Yin, 2018).

## 3.3. Data analysis

We used the organisation as our level of analysis but we also employed an embedded case analysis approach to analyse unexpected events faced by the four organisations. Embedded case analysis allowed us to examine the specific characteristics of the unexpected events (Miles et al., 2014; Yin, 2018).

Our research design was guided by a positivist approach, in that we sought to confirm the existence of specific constructs and relationships present in the data. However, we remained open to unanticipated findings. The lead author coded all transcripts in MAXQDA: first using descriptive coding to provide brief section descriptions and then using more detailed provisional coding, based on the list of predefined codes that we developed from the literature (Saldaña, 2016). Following this initial coding, the first author developed rich descriptive narratives for each of the cases. The authors met on a weekly basis to review the coding at the organisational and event levels, discuss the assessment of the organisational capabilities, and develop a logical chain of evidence for each case. We followed a theoretical propositions analysis strategy and used pattern matching as an analysis technique to compare the anticipated relationships developed from our literature review and the empirical data (Yin, 2018). Figure 2 shows the coding structure, and Appendix D gives more details on the case study data and coding examples.

All organisations compete in dynamic industries, perform well financially, and are growing. Leaders are experts in their field and energetic (Park et al., 2017). ## 4. Analysis of findings

We analysed our findings in two ways: (1) the ongoing agility and the improvisational agility within each organisation, which is helpful to identify how they relate to each other (RQ1); and (2) the unexpected events, the improvisational agility’s sense-respond process, and the role played by IMC (RQ2).

## 4.1. Ongoing agility and improvisational agility in case organisations

In our analysis of the case organisations, we identified various capabilities and mechanisms that support ongoing agility. Appendix F summarises these supporting capabilities for sensing, decision-making, and acting components. Based on this, we note that DS and CC have developed the most diverse supporting capabilities and mechanisms, especially for acting. ## 4.1.1. Ongoing agility

Below, we discuss the sense-respond process of ongoing agility. Sensing agility (the sensing component of agility) is well-developed in all organisations, except for MA which has more sensing challenges. Many supporting capabilities and mechanisms are used to sense environmental changes. The three organisations that show a well-developed sensing agility get timely information most frequently through networking, relationships with clients and partners, and efective internal (DS) or external (CC) information management. Most of these approaches are not formalised, but CC has formal mechanisms in place to monitor its competitive environment. The organisation has developed a survey to scan its competitive environment and identify potential acquisition targets, a key aspect of its growth strategy.

Decision-making agility (the decision-making component of agility) is more developed at DS and CC than at SS and MA (see Appendix F). While MA uses some decision-making supporting capabilities that leverage sensing capabilities (e.g., relationships with clients that provide data for scenario tools), these tools are often not used until late in the decisionmaking process. Similarly, at SS, the use of decisionmaking tools, like rapid testing, is sometimes ofset by “gut feel” executive decisions that overrule evidencebased ones. The other two organisations, DS and CC, commonly use scenarios, forecasting, and testing to facilitate agile decision-making. For example:

“If the use of phone is shrinking because of alternative technology, then how do you deal with that? So, we have to look at web-based scenarios. Also, we have to expand our product lines, find new technology and processes, and diversify.” Chief Financial Oficer (CFO), DS

Many decision-making mechanisms common to these two organisations leverage digitally-enabled information (key performance indicators, client data, and financial data).

Acting agility (the acting component of agility) is the most developed component, with all four organisations able to reallocate resources and capabilities or identify the need for additional ones. Acting agility is more developed at DS and CC. There, customer responsiveness and partnership agility appear to be enabled by the digital infrastructure, such as applications that facilitate remote work or secure access to files. Acting agility in these two organisations is also facilitated by internal expertise (rather than external partners) to enable quick responses, as reflected in the following comment: “External consultants do not live within the organization. They have less understanding of our needs, our challenges, and they cannot react as quick in a case of an emergency”. CIO, CC

While acting agility is important to these organisations, it can be impeded by legacy systems that are not fully integrated with the digital infrastructure (DS) or by a lack of decision-making agility preventing the organisation from assigning the right resources to a project (MA). In addition, acting agility can be emotionally draining for employees, as noted by participants at DS and MA, especially when acting agility is ad hoc (MA). Based on this analysis, since the sensing, decision-making, and acting components are key to developing ongoing agility, we conclude that DS and CC have well-developed ongoing agility, while SS and MA are agile on an ad hoc basis only, meaning that they have not developed an ongoing agility capability.

## 4.1.2. Improvisational agility

The four organisations exhibit varying levels of improvisational agility, ranging from well-developed (DS), emerging (SS), limited (MA), to ad hoc only (CC). Here, we describe these levels in the context of each organisation using recent organisational decisions and actions, related to the unexpected events described in the next section, data about each organisation’s structure, culture, strategic perspective, and our own onsite observations.

DS is the only organisation with fully developed improvisational agility. From each of a series of unexpected events, DS executives demonstrate that they learn, and improve their improvisational agility decisions and actions. For instance, the organisation’s actions were efective following a train disaster (see, Figure 3, event #1) based on its previous experience with other disasters, such as a hurricane (event #6). The organisation was able to quickly integrate its digital infrastructure with partners (call centres and afected client), which facilitated information sharing and collaboration. Furthermore, during the train disaster event (#1), executives rapidly learned from their mistakes, notably regarding data sharing, and adjusted their processes as the event unfolded.

At the other end of the development spectrum, CC’s improvisational agility can be described as ad hoc. Some of the unexpected events recounted by CC study participants were related to improvisational agility responses during specific client projects, with limited organisational impact. Other examples were key to the organisation, for instance, acquisitions, but these depended on the organisation’s ongoing agility, not improvisational agility. Overall, both organisational culture and structure, especially formal organisational procedures, made it dificult for CC to develop improvisational agility.

SS and MA lie somewhere between DS’s welldeveloped, and CC’s ad hoc, improvisational agility. Our findings indicate that SS’s improvisational agility is limited but that the organisation wants to develop it further. Specifically, SS has reflected on its decisions and actions, especially regarding the importance of having digital applications in place to facilitate rapid information sharing and use. An example of this was the implementation of a new customer relationship management (CRM) system following event #5 and the loss of a major client. This was done to increase the emerging improvisational agility. Like CC, MA’s improvisational agility is often ad hoc, where outcomes do not always lead to long-term alterations.

However, as we discuss below, responses to unexpected events that participants discussed were critical in terms of revenue generation and client acquisition. MA clearly values improvisational agility; part of the organisation’s culture is to jump on opportunities, as noted by the CFO: “We like to believe that we can meet any of our clients’ challenges. It is a ‘Bring it on, we can do it’ sort of attitude”. While the organisation is sometimes successful in its improvisational agility decisions and actions, it seldom addresses challenges from past responses, for example, those due to the lack of available information.

“I think that [the lack of IMC] impacts [the assessment of strategic opportunities and threats] in a pretty big way. Without the right information either historically about what you have done that has been a success, having data at your fingertips with insights, there can be a huge impact on the strategy of the programmes. Whether it is not asking the right person the right thing or not having that database of case studies at your disposal that you can easily search through.” Managing director, digital services, MA

Consequently, MA’s ability to fully develop improvisational agility is impeded by its inability to improve how it responds over time, in part because the organisation lacks the information it needs to reflect on past actions.

## 4.2. Improvisational agility’s sense-respond process

Having compared ongoing and improvisational agility, we now focus on 10 unexpected events that were addressed with improvisational agility. Our objective is to examine the sense-respond process of improvisational agility. Figure 3 graphs the unexpected events according to uncertainty and time pressure, while Appendix G provides details of the sense-respond process and outcomes for each event. The events are classified based on the level of time pressure and uncertainty leading to deviation from ongoing agility routines and processes. Three events are characterised by high time pressure but relatively limited uncertainty (events #1 to #3), two by relatively limited time pressure but high uncertainty (events #4 and #5), and the last five by both high time pressure and high uncertainty (events #6 to #10). We analyse these events by focusing on the sensing, decision-making, and acting components of improvisational agility. As outlined earlier, IMC components involve digital infrastructure, quality information, and IM processes. We highlight the role of these components for improvisational agility responses.

## 4.2.1. Sensing unexpected events

Although our case organisations make regular use of sensing capabilities for ongoing agility (as described above and in Appendix F), we note that unexpected events are sensed reactively and mainly through customer requests or executives’ networks. For example, with event #7 (see, Figure 3), MA sensed an opportunity to develop a Twitter-enabled vending machine during a brainstorming meeting with a client:

“We were at the auto show and we had pitched [our current client] in December the idea of taking the collectible cars that they wanted us to hand out to people, put them in a vending machine, and then having people Tweet at the vending machine and it would dispense a car. So, a Twitter activated vending machine. We built this in about 6 weeks.”, Managing director, digital services, MA

Information and knowledge sensed were not usually digitally enabled, except for two unexpected events (event #2 with the use of a CRM system and event #3 through IT monitoring systems).

## 4.2.2. Decision-making

Decision-making for unexpected events was mainly done during executive meetings and meetings with key organisational members when needed. Depending on the type of unexpected events, diferences were seen regarding the focus of decisionmaking between interpretation of information and devising an action plan. Interpretation and action plans were critically important for events that required creative decisions because of their significant deviation from ongoing agility (events #6-10).

During unexpected events with high time pressures but limited uncertainty (events #1-3), organisations must respond with speed but there is a limited need to be creative. Therefore, interpretation of the information gathered during the sensing phase is limited and decision-making is focused on devising an action plan. Limited case data suggests that there is value associated with the use of digital applications in decision-making during improvisational agility, as highlighted here during event #2:

“Those [CRM] tools were more valuable in this case than they have been in the past. That information is very valuable for informing the decisions about what we make, about what to do next, who to follow-up with, who to talk to and those kinds of things.” VP, financial services, SS

During unexpected events with limited time pressure and high uncertainty (events #4 and #5), improvisational agility decisions needed to be creative because the events deviated from ongoing agility, but speed was not as critical. DS displayed high levels of creativity during event #4, specifically by leveraging leaders expertise to design and develop a new digital platform within a few weeks. The organisation experimented with diferent scenarios while developing the platform, for instance, to test platform features. Importantly, the organisation leveraged its existing digital infrastructure to speed up actions and gather useful information for subsequent decision-making. In contrast, SS’s limited IM processes and available applications at the time of event #5 resulted in the loss of a major client. The organisation was unable to systematically collect and interpret information pertaining to its client’s change in IT management. As a consequence, SS could not devise a creative action plan to retain its client.

In the organisations (DS, MA, and CC) that responded to events characterised by high time pressure and high uncertainty (events #6 to #10), creative decisions extensively relied upon organisational expertise to interpret and extrapolate from the available information. For instance, DS’s team brainstormed solutions to address an urgent client’s request to devise a novel digital solution that helped reunite pets with their owners during a hurricane (event #6). During events #6, 9, and 10, the extreme time pressure to start acting forced the organisations to fuse decision-making and acting and proceed with improvisational action plans and adaptations, highlighting rapid back and forth movement between these two components. In contrast, inefective use of digital applications, as well as limited use of scenarios and lack of information (events #8 and #9) slowed down MA’s decision-making process and subsequent actions.

## 4.2.3. Acting

Acting was extensive. Depending on the type of unexpected events, diferences were seen regarding integrating internal resources and acquiring external resources, mainly in the form of human expertise. As noted above, events calling for both creative and speedy actions required constant re-evaluation of decisions and adaptation of actions in real time (events #6, 9 and 10).

During unexpected events with high time pressure and limited uncertainty (events #1 to #3), the organisations acted quickly. As Appendix G shows, actions taken involved resource alterations in the form of digital and knowledge resource integration (events #1 and #3). For instance, during event #1 (a train derailment), DS leveraged its existing expertise and infrastructure to quickly modify its processes and handle much larger volumes of donor calls than was typical for this type of disaster. In addition, addressing the events may require reallocating knowledge resources (transfer of expertise within the organisation) and acquiring digital and human resources (event #2: bank opportunity). By leveraging internal resources, especially digital infrastructure and applications at CC and DS, the organisations sped up their response to unexpected events (events #1 and #3).

During unexpected events with limited time pressure and high uncertainty (events #4 and #5), the organisations (DS and SS respectively) acted creatively. Our findings suggest two responses to unexpected events: first, when time pressure is not extreme, both organisations seek to acquire external resources that add to their internal competencies (event #4); and second, creativity is also needed when the outcome is negative and resources may need to be reconfigured or released. This happened when it became clear that SS would lose a large client (event #5), and the organisation reconfigured knowledge resources throughout the organisation to save as many jobs as possible. As a last resort, SS had to release human resources and dismiss some of its employees.

Events characterised by high time pressure and high uncertainty, such as events #6 to #10, required the organisations in this study to employ actions that are both creative and implemented quickly. When time pressure is high but not extreme (events #7 and #8), the acquisition of external resources helps develop a new product (MA). While MA possesses other critical resources such as digital marketing capability, it needed an engineering expert to build the product quickly, as MA’s CFO noted about event #7: “There is no way we could develop the competency in house. It requires an engineer. It is completely diferent”.

During unexpected events with extreme time pressure, for instance, in the aftermath of a hurricane (event #6), internal digital and human resources need to be quickly reconfigured, sometimes causing high levels of stress (event #9). This was somewhat alleviated at DS when the flexible digital infrastructure helped facilitate speedy actions (event #6).

## 5. Discussion

In this study, we set out to answer two research questions: one comparing the sense-respond processes of ongoing agility and improvisational agility, and the other examining how improvisational agility is built. Understanding the sense-respond process of improvisational agility during unexpected events is especially important because of the increasing frequency of these events and because their unexpected nature challenges the proactive sense-respond process that characterises ongoing agility. Table 4 summarises our research questions, associated findings, and related propositions. These are further discussed below. We conclude this section by discussing how knowledge gained from improvisational agility responses and subsequent learning may impact ongoing agility in the organisation.

## 5.1. Regarding research question 1: ongoing agility and improvisational agility

RQ1 compares ongoing agility and improvisational agility. In this study, two case organisations, DS and CC, developed an ongoing agility capability. The efective leveraging of the sense-respond process allows these organisations to respond to changes. In contrast to CC, DS has also developed an improvisational agility capability, allowing the organisation to efectively address unexpected events. The other organisations, SS and MA, have limited or ad hoc ongoing and improvisational agility. While these last two organisations are agile when needed, these responses are sometimes ineficient and they lack systematic capabilities for both types of agility. In our sample, organisations that efectively responded to unexpected events had already developed supporting capabilities for ongoing agility. The presence of ongoing agility without improvisational agility at CC also suggests that – as we would expect from past research – ongoing agility can be developed in organisations without improvisational agility. That is, some organisations are well versed in proactively detecting changes in the environment and efectively addressing them through the decision-making and acting components of the senserespond process. However, the reactive sense-respond process involved with unexpected events requires a diferent approach that not all organisations (e.g., CC) are efective at.

In addition, when examining the components of improvisational agility, our data shows that organisations tend to favour decision-making agility and acting agility when they are using improvisational agility. This was important during unexpected events with extreme time pressure to respond. During these events, the ability of some of the organisations to make and act on decisions swiftly was essential (as in events #1 and #6). In one case, MA was able to address an unexpected event with a positive outcome, but decision-making and acting agility were lacking, resulting in undue stress for organisational members (event #9). Sensing agility was not as critical for improvisational agility, although it is adequately developed in three of the organisations. We found limited evidence suggesting that high sensing agility was required to address unexpected events. This contrasts with the roles of decision-making and acting, which were much more prominent. Therefore, we propose<sup>5</sup>:

Proposition 1: The decision-making and acting agility components are critical for improvisational agility, while the sensing component is less critical.

We find that during events that require both very fast and creative decisions due to extreme time pressure and high uncertainty, the overlap between decision-making and acting is evident. Fusion between design (decision) and execution (acting) is often viewed as a definitional characteristic of improvisational agility conceptualised as OIC (Ciuchta et al., 2021). Our analysis indicates this is especially true for organisations under extreme time pressure to respond to events (DS, MA, and CC). During such events (see, Figure 3: events #6, #9, and #10), the organisations quickly moved back and forth from decision-making to acting. They used what they learned to iterate the deciding-acting process. Organizational expertise played a key role in facilitating improvisational agility decisions, especially during unexpected events requiring an extremely fast response. For instance, CC used scenarios to test hypotheses in response to labour unionisation (event #10) and learned from these experiments in real time. The organisation then followed up with new hypotheses and immediately tested them, all over a period of a few days. We note a similar approach in event #9, with MA’s response during a football event promotion and event #6 with DS’s response to a hurricane. Therefore, we propose:

Proposition 2: Under extreme time pressure, improvisational agility requires organisations to rapidly go back and forth between decision-making and acting.

We found that alterations to digital and knowledge resources are common improvisational agility actions for the organisations we examined, but they difered depending on the type of unexpected events and the actions that ensued. During unexpected events calling for high speed in the sense-respond process, organisations are more likely to focus on reorganising and reallocating their internal digital and knowledge resources. This is true for unexpected events with high uncertainty (such as event #6), but also for events with more limited uncertainty (such as event #1), suggesting that extreme time pressure forces organisations to focus on their internal resources and limits their ability to locate and integrate external resources, and this regardless of uncertainty levels. Whenever time pressure is high, resource alterations focus on integrating existing resources even if this integration has negative consequences for employees (event #9). In contrast, when organisations are facing events calling for creativity but with more limited time pressure, they tend to bring in external resources to complement their internal ones and respond to unexpected events. This was the case at MA when the organisation realised the need for specific engineering expertise to develop a Twitter-enabled vending machine (event #7). Therefore, we suggest that:

Proposition 3: When uncertainty is especially high, the acquisition of external resources facilitates creative actions for improvisational agility. In contrast, when time pressure is especially high, the reallocation of existing digital and knowledge resources facilitates rapid actions for improvisational agility.

## 5.2. Regarding research question 2: supporting the sense-respond process of improvisational agility

RQ2 explores more specifically how the sense-respond process of improvisational agility is built. While sensing, decision-making, and acting are key components of ongoing agility, past research suggests that sensing is limited for improvisational agility, because tools like formal scanning mechanisms (devised for ongoing agility) and proactive identification of trends ofer limited benefits for unexpected events. This was confirmed by our findings. We identified various sensing tools used by the organisations, some of which were related to IMC. However, there was limited use of these tools to detect unexpected events, regardless of the time pressure or uncertainty levels associated with these events. Sensing mechanisms afecting responses were those that provided information about the participants involved in the events. This type of quality information was collected and used in response to all 10 unexpected events. In a limited number of events, sensing was done through digital applications.

While past research on ongoing agility suggests that decision-making and acting may be clearly delineated and separated (Park et al., 2017), our current knowledge of the sense-respond process for improvisational agility is more limited (Vera et al., 2016). Through an in-depth investigation of various unexpected events calling for diferent improvisational agility responses, we found that organisations need creative decisions to respond to unexpected events that are highly uncertain, because these events force them to deviate from their typical ongoing agility response. In these cases, organisations extensively use internal expertise. When expertise is not efectively leveraged, outcomes are poor and although creative, the slow pace of decisions negatively influences subsequent actions (MA). To a lesser extent, IM processes and information are used but mainly to speed up decisions (DS).

Our findings also highlight the importance of improvisational actions when responding to unexpected events. This supports the suggestion from previous empirical research that an action-oriented operationalisation is needed (see for instance, Moorman & Miner, 1998; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010). During improvisational agility actions, organisations in this study relied upon IMC (especially digital infrastructure and applications) to speed up actions when faced with unexpected events characterised by high time pressure. For instance, DS leveraged its mostly cloud-based infrastructure heavily to scale up its processes quickly when responding to a train disaster (event #1). These were critical actions to contend with an overwhelming increase in donation calls within a few hours. Similarly, actions by the organisation were sped up during event #4 through DS’s digital infrastructure, which allowed the organisation to move quickly between analysis of platform use data and new experimental actions. Consequently, we propose:

Proposition 4: Improvisational agility is supported by IMC. Specifically, quality information, efective IM processes, and a flexible digital infrastructure are needed for sensing, decision-making, and acting respectively.

## 5.3. The cycle of learning between improvisational agility and ongoing agility

Although we did not set out to study the reciprocal efects of improvisational agility and ongoing agility, our findings suggest these are present. Organizational learning is a key component of improvisational and ongoing agility, as dynamic capabilities (Zollo & Winter, 2002). This learning cycle facilitates the evolution of these dynamic capabilities (Vera et al., 2016). First, as our findings indicate, improvisational agility benefits from knowledge accumulated from past ongoing agility responses. To varying degrees during improvisation agility responses, the organisations leveraged capabilities originally developed to support ongoing agility (see Appendices F and G).

Second, our findings also suggest that in organisations that learn from their improvisational responses to unexpected events (DS, and SS to some extent), this learning benefitted ongoing agility. For instance, following event #1, DS developed a new disaster management process that can be used to manage “typical” disasters. Following the loss of a client due to SS’s inability to gather and interpret information, the organisation implemented a CRM and a process to gather and use knowledge about their leads and clients. This proved helpful for event #2 and the acquisition of a new client. We note however that knowledge from improvisational responses is not always worth reusing or adapting for ongoing agility (e.g., event #10). It is also possible that an organisation cannot readily capture or retain new knowledge because of a lack of digitally-enabled IMC, a key component to prevent knowledge loss (Levallet & Chan, 2019).

## 6. Contributions to research

To our knowledge, this research represents a first attempt at examining some of the assumptions of the agility sense-respond process. We focus on the importance of the events that trigger improvisational agility responses. We show that when organisations face sudden, unexpected events (as opposed to predictable events), the agility sense-respond process difers and organisations rely on improvisational agility. We introduce the concept of improvisational agility and use the OIC lens to examine the sense-respond process of this form of agility. This study makes three main contributions to the agility literature. The first contribution is related to the nature of events that call for diferent agility responses. The second focuses on the role of creativity in agility, while the third discusses the importance of resources and capabilities like IMC for improvisational agility.

First, diferent organisational agility responses are needed depending on environmental changes (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Tallon et al., 2019). Defining improvisational agility for unexpected events by using an OIC lens in this study allowed us to highlight the growing importance of events that are not easily detected through formal sensing mechanisms, and the need to dedicate more attention to these phenomena in the agility research, traditionally focused on ongoing agility. To date, most agility research has examined the role of digital technologies for ongoing agility (Tallon et al., 2019), mainly when these organisations respond to events that can be sensed or detected. With this study, we expand the agility research beyond the current focus on ongoing agility to highlight the importance of improvisational agility for unexpected events. Improvisational agility is needed for events that cannot be easily detected and predicted and that require a diferent sense-respond approach. Considering the increasing occurrence of unexpected events with major impacts on organisational survival, we anticipate that organisations will increasingly have to rely on improvisational agility.

A second contribution is related to creativity in agility research. Past research has shown the facilitating role of digitally-enabled capabilities for process adaptability and adjustments (Chen et al., 2014; Huang et al., 2014; Park et al., 2017). Our study extends this to include the importance of IMC, notably efective IM processes, to creativity during improvisational agility. Creativity is especially critical during the decision-making part of improvisational agility and can be enhanced by the acquisition of external resources and capabilities to foster creative responses. However, SMEs dealing with resource constraints may have to limit their acquisition of external resources to highly uncertain events when there is enough time to make decisions and act. In other cases, SMEs may be pressured to focus on reconfiguring internal resources in extreme conditions, even when uncertainty is high and even when it is possible that internal resources might not sufice to address a specific event. Future research on creativity in improvisational agility should further examine the important role of resource alterations and orchestration to respond to varying environmental changes (Lovallo et al., 2020; Queiroz et al., 2018).

Third, our findings extend knowledge about the role that resources and capabilities like digitallyenabled IMC play for sensing, decision-making, and acting during improvisational agility. The question of how digital capabilities, such as IMC, provide value to organisations is an unresolved one with most, but not all, studies demonstrating a positive impact of digital capabilities on other capabilities, including agility (Mithas et al., 2011; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010; Ravichandran, 2018). We add to this research stream by answering calls to study digital capability configurations at a more granular level (Wang et al., 2012) and by showing how IMC components play diferent roles in improvisational agility, especially in SMEs. We increase our understanding of the processes for improvisational agility, i.e., how SMEs can efectively sense, decide, and act to address unexpected events, thereby refining the improvisational agility nomological net. Specifically, we find that flexible digital infrastructure, including applications, are critical for speedy actions, while IM processes and quality information and expertise are key for creative decisionmaking and sensing. The careful development of digitally-enabled IMC provides the base upon which SMEs can access real-time information and reconfigure existing capabilities.

## 7. Contributions to practice

Unexpected events, whether global or local, are on the rise and can have a detrimental impact on SMEs if not swiftly addressed. Because these events are not easily anticipated, addressing them with ongoing agility may not be possible. Instead, improvisational agility provides an important way for organisations to tackle them. In addition, ongoing agility can serve as a foundation on which to develop improvisational agility. In those organisations with ongoing agility, the move towards improvisational agility is more easily achieved than in less agile organisations. Improvisational agility can benefit from the various mechanisms leveraged by agile organisations, especially for decision-making and ensuing actions. We also show that improvisational agility can, through the learning cycle, improve ongoing agility. This is especially important in SMEs that may face resource constraints and need to make the best of their existing capabilities.

Organizations may not be distinguishing between improvisational agility and ongoing agility. Regardless, it is important for organisations to be ready to efectively respond proactively to predictable events and reactively to unexpected events by developing both ongoing agility and improvisational agility. As such, the role of digital capabilities as a complement to other capabilities for ongoing agility is equally critical for improvisational agility. Agile organisations interested in developing improvisational agility should determine whether their digital infrastructure and IM processes contribute only to speed, or also allow the organisation to be creative. As unexpected events continue to grow in importance and frequency, adding to the agility “arsenal” by moving beyond proactive responses will undoubtedly become more important. We propose in this paper that improvisational agility covers a small portion of organisational agility, but it is bound to grow rapidly. SMEs should equip themselves to survive and thrive during these increasingly uncertain times.

## 8. Limitations

As with any study, our study has several limitations, which ofer research opportunities. First, our focus on SMEs limits the generalisability of our findings, even though our study helps refine the overall organisational agility construct (Tallon et al., 2019) by introducing improvisational agility using an OIC lens. Larger organisations, often the focus of IS research, tend to have more complex information platforms that may slow down real-time information availability for improvisational agility (Park et al., 2017). Future research should investigate the role of digital capabilities for improvisational agility in larger organisational settings.

Second, this research only examined improvisational agility at the organisational and event levels, although improvisational agility impacts, and is impacted by, factors and processes at other levels in the organisation (Cunha et al., 1999). As we noted, some unexpected events and associated improvisational agility responses increased stress levels for employees, especially when the organisations had not fully developed their capability and did not have the digital technologies in place to facilitate improvisational responses. In these organisations, it is important to further develop the organisational capability but also to develop agility abilities at the individual level. For instance, improvisational agility training might help build employees’ self-confidence in tackling these unexpected events (Daly et al., 2009). Future research should investigate others ways in which organisations can help reduce individual stress that may be felt during improvisation events, including through the use of digitally-enabled processes.

Third, our study only recorded actual responses to unexpected events. We examined how organisations respond to unexpected events but it is possible that in some cases, being able to stop doing something negative or doing nothing would be more efective (Ciuchta et al., 2021). Future research could examine how improvisational agile actions, especially resource alterations, might difer when organisations choose to shut down an activity or to fail fast. In addition, while this was not intentional, most of the events we examined had a positive outcome. This has been a challenge in many improvisation studies (Ciuchta et al., 2021). Future research could compare the impact of improvisational agility on performance relative to the impact of ongoing agility.

## 9. Conclusion

By quickly and creatively reconfiguring its resources and capabilities at the start of the COVID-19 lockdown, Mastermind Toys was able to dramatically increase online sales to compensate for lost in-store revenue. During this period, the organisation has stayed true to its focus on customer value, for instance, by favouring permanent playful curbside pickup signage over temporary solutions. As a result of these and other digital and physical changes, Mastermind Toys online sales went up by almost 300% between 2020 and 2021 (Business Wire, 2021). The organisation’s learning cycle allowed them to leverage ongoing agility knowledge for improvisational agility, and vice-versa. This exemplifies the long-term benefits associated with rapid learning and successful improvisational agility.

The study of organisational agility needs to expand beyond responses to predictable changes. Unexpected events are growing more frequent and can have dire consequences when not addressed, as the COVID-19 pandemic reminds us. In this article, we demonstrate that improvisational agility can help organisations successfully tackle these events. We examine the senserespond process of improvisational agility. We also articulate the role played by digitally-enabled IMC for the development of improvisational agility during sensing, decision-making, and acting.

In this study, we did not focus specifically on organisational learning, even though IMC components play a key part in facilitating learning from improvisational agility. Our findings are promising in suggesting that improvisational agility and ongoing agility are connected by learning. We encourage researchers to examine how this organisational learning cycle occurs and under what circumstances knowledge gained from improvisational agility may impact ongoing agility, and vice-versa, in positive but also potentially in negative ways.

Finally, we hope that our findings will trigger research on improvisational agility using the OIC lens. While still under-recognised in IS research, improvisational agility is becoming increasingly critical to succeed in today’s volatile environments.

## Sample interview questions

(1) What is your current role within the organisation? How long have you been in this position?

(2) Could you please briefly describe your organisation and industry?

(3) For the next few questions, please think back to a recent time when you had to rapidly assess a strategic opportunity or a threat and act upon it. Could you please describe what strategic opportunity/threat it was and whether it applied to your unit/organisation? Could you describe how senior management responded to this strategic opportunity/threat? Who was involved in the decision making? How were decisions implemented?

(4) Still thinking about the same strategic opportunity/threat, what did you do? In your view, was it efective?

(5) Could you describe the various information systems you use? To what extent do you use business intelligence systems, ERP systems or other systems when you need to assess strategic business opportunities or threats?

(6) How would you rate your information systems in terms of scalability (e.g., ability to handle growth requirements)? Compatibility with other systems? Modularity (e.g., the ability to reuse software modules in new systems development)?

(7) Could you describe the various information systems platforms you use? To what extent does your IT infrastructure matter when you need to assess strategic business opportunities or threats?

(8) To what extent has your organisation formalised its information systems strategy? How would you describe this strategy and your technology plan?

(9) How would you describe the ability of your organisation to gather, transfer, and use information and knowledge? To what extent do you use information systems to gather and analyse data, information, and knowledge?

(10) How would you describe your organisation’s culture? Experimental/Risk averse? In other ways?

(11) How would you describe your business environment in terms of a) its predictability, b) the rapidity at which it changes?
