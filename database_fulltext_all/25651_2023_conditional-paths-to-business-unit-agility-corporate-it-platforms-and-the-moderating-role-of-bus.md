# Conditional paths to business unit agility: corporate IT platforms and the moderating role of business unit IT autonomy

Magno Queiroz <sup>a</sup>, Paul P. Tallon<sup>b</sup>, Tim Coltman<sup>c,d</sup> and Rajeev Sharma<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>College of Business, Florida Atlantic University, Boca Raton, Florida, US; <sup>b</sup>Sellinger School of Business, Loyola University Maryland, Baltimore, Maryland, US; <sup>c</sup>School of Business, University of Wollongong, NSW, Australia; <sup>d</sup>Waikato Management School, University of Waikato, Hamilton, New Zealand

## ABSTRACT

Agility has long been recognised as a key determinant of organisational performance. The pursuit of agility within business units (BUs) is challenging as IT executives in multi-business organisations (MBOs) are tasked with balancing a need for organisation-wide IT synergies against a desire among BUs for local IT solutions that enable agility in their end markets. MBOs are increasingly turning to corporate IT platforms to respond to their BUs’ IT needs but doing so presents a challenge to BU IT autonomy and, in turn, to BU agility. In this paper, we posit that BU agility is determined by resource synergies based on the interplay between corporate IT platforms and local, BU-controlled, IT applications. These applications lead to greater BU process digitisation and, in turn, to expanded BU agility. We evaluate our model using data from an international survey of IT executives in MBOs. Our findings provide support for our model and, in so doing, identify diferent paths that MBOs can take to enhance BU agility. Our contribution afirms the value of corporate IT platforms for BU agility but suggests that MBOs should tread carefully when developing corporate IT platforms that coincide with mandatory use of shared IT applications.

KEYWORDS Multi-business organisations; agility; business unit; corporate IT platform; IT autonomy; IT applications

## 1. Introduction

To survive and prosper in an increasingly competitive and unpredictable environment, organisations are expected to strive for greater agility – defined as the ability to respond to disruptive, sudden, and unexpected market events with ease, speed, and dexterity (Overby et al., 2006; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011; Tallon et al., 2019). In established multi-business organisations (MBOs) such as Bank of America and Stanley Black & Decker, the pursuit of agility is challenging due to their complex internal governance structures. For example, MBO executives are increasingly tasked with creating and maintaining organisation-wide information technology (IT) synergies. Eforts to realise IT synergies must, however, be tempered by a desire among diverse business units (BUs) for some degree of autonomy and control over local IT applications since they too face a need for greater agility (Kude et al., 2018; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). An often-overlooked question for IT executives seeking to resolve this conundrum is: whose agility should MBOs emphasise – the organisation as a whole or individual BUs?

The impacts of IT resources on organisational agility and the role of organisational agility as an antecedent of firm performance have been well established in prior studies (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Park et al., 2017; Ravichandran, 2018; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011).

We know far less about BU agility, however, even as a growing body of literature has begun to recognise that agility at the BU level is a key business imperative for MBOs (Kock & Gemünden, 2016; Swaford et al., 2008; Tallon et al., 2019). The argument driving the need for further study of BU agility is compelling: for MBOs to be agile, their BUs should be agile. It does not make sense to talk about an MBO being agile if we do not first ask whether its individual BUs – and particularly any flagship BU that represents the lion’s share of an MBO’s revenue or profitability – are agile. Accordingly, researchers have begun to explore the links between IT and BU agility (Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2021). This paper continues that line of inquiry by asking how should MBOs leverage IT to achieve BU agility.

The challenge, as prior research identifies, lies in the fact that MBOs face competing demands. On the one hand, there is a desire and perhaps even a shareholder expectation that MBOs will implement common systems across all BUs to deliver global eficiencies. On the other hand, any such desire for a onesize-fits-all solution must be balanced against the need for idiosyncratic IT solutions within BUs (Quaadgras et al., 2014; Queiroz et al., 2020; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). Managing competing IT demands between corporate parents and BUs is a perennial IT governance challenge that requires, “balancing tensions between autonomy and synergy” (Williams & Karahanna,

2013, p. 934). With an eye towards generating increased IT synergies, corporate parents often consolidate IT support for common processes (e.g., compliance, HR, procurement, and finance) under a corporate IT platform (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Shanks et al., 2018; Tanriverdi, 2006).<sup>1</sup> Their argument is that these processes are suficiently predictable and common across most, if not all, BUs to warrant consolidation onto a shared, corporate-sponsored IT platform. To the extent that these IT platforms align closely with the needs of BUs, they can be easily and quickly accessed and deployed by BUs in ways that directly enhance BU agility. Hence, MBO eforts to enhance corporate IT platform competence, defined here as the extent to which corporate IT platform capabilities are suficient to meet the BU’s IT needs, could enable improvements in BU agility.<sup>2</sup>

Notwithstanding the benefits of corporate IT platforms, BUs are typically the first to encounter changes in market needs or to feel pricing pressure from clients knowing that such efects can be localised within their markets rather than applying equally across all BUs. Hence, individual BUs may want to retain a formal degree of autonomy over customer service, supply chain operations, and IT decisions within their end markets. In this case, reliance on the corporate IT platform for some forms of IT support could give the BU an opportunity to divert local IT resources – over which it retains control – towards business processes that are unique to its markets (Roberts & Grover, 2012). Faced with the opportunity to use applications housed within the corporate IT platform, BUs may find it easier and financially more attractive to double down on digitising their core business processes (Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018; Westerman & Bonnet, 2015). Digitisation refers to the extent to which an organisation – in this case a BU – uses IT resources in various aspects of its oferings and business processes (Jabr & Zheng, 2020, p. 5). Greater process digitisation, particularly when supported by a corporate IT platform, makes it easier for a BU to enact IT-based initiatives and to adapt its processes to accommodate changing markets (Ravichandran, 2018). Thus, an increase in BU process digitisation, defined as the extent to which the BU uses IT applications to meet the needs of its core business processes, could enhance BU agility.

Yet, for MBOs and indeed for BUs, there is some debate as to whose IT – corporate-provided IT platforms or BU-controlled IT – is a better enabler of BU agility and under what conditions (i.e., when) might one source of IT support be better than another (Tallon et al., 2019). MBOs recognise the dangers of overly limiting the autonomy of BUs to the point where the BU has so little say in its own afairs that it struggles to maintain its competitive edge. At the same time, MBOs may be hesitant to allow BUs full reign to ignore a corporate IT platform and to instead focus on local IT solutions only. These are important considerations for IT executives seeking to leverage IT for BU agility. However, we know little about how and under what conditions corporate IT platforms and local BU IT resources influence BU agility. We address this gap in our understanding by focusing on the following research question: When and how do corporate IT platform competence and BU process digitisation influence BU agility?

To answer this question, we draw on multiple streams of literatures on IT governance, organisational agility, and IT business value (Kohli & Grover, 2008; Ravichandran, 2018; Williams & Karahanna, 2013). Prior research on IT business value argues that IT competences lead to intermediate outcomes such as better processes and an increase in value (Kohli & Grover, 2008), while research on IT governance draws attention to the challenges MBOs face when balancing tensions between central control and local autonomy (Kude et al., 2018; Williams & Karahanna, 2013). Drawing on this literature, we develop the idea that corporate IT platform competence afects BU agility through BU process digitisation. We theorise that these efects are contingent on BU IT autonomy, defined as the extent to which the IT needs of the BU’s core processes are met locally through BU IT applications rather than through corporate IT. We position our theory in the context of the relationship between corporate parents and key BUs.<sup>3</sup> To test our model, we use data gathered from an international survey of IT executives. The results provide support for the proposed theory. Our distinctive theoretical contribution to the agility literature is explicating the contingent role of BU IT autonomy on how corporate IT platforms and BU-controlled IT resources influence BU agility.

## 2. Theoretical development

Figure 1 shows our research model. The definitions of constructs in the model are summarised in Table 1. The logic underlying our model reflects insights from prior research within three research streams: organisational agility, IT business value, and IT governance. Extant literature argues that IT resources and IT capabilities are among the main predictors of agility (Tallon et al., 2019). For instance, research shows that web-based customer infrastructure (Roberts & Grover, 2012) and IT competencies (Chakravarty et al., 2013) can enhance agility (see Appendix A for a summary of the literature examining the impacts of IT on agility). Although this research stream has focused on studying organisational agility (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Liang et al., 2017; Ravichandran, 2018), the literature has partially examined agility at the BU level to understand how BUs respond to market events in their end markets (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011; Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2021; Roberts & Grover, 2012).

The use of corporate IT platforms in MBOs adds a potential wrinkle to how IT might impact BU agility since a BU’s IT support may come from a corporate IT platform and be largely outside its direct control. Corporate IT platforms are seen as organisation-wide digital infrastructure components that provide a foundation for business activities (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Tanriverdi, 2006).<sup>4</sup> Therefore, MBOs may require that BUs leverage these components in their products and markets with the expectation that benefits will accrue to the BU and eventually to the MBO as a whole (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015, p. 106). However, the literature on IT business value argues that the benefits of IT can also occur through mediating factors (Kohli & Grover, 2008; Wu et al., 2015). For instance, extant research argues that investments in IT resources (e.g., corporate IT platforms) lead to IT-based value (e.g., financial benefits or other output value such as agility) through mediating factors such as the use of IT to enable business processes (Kohli & Grover, 2008, p. 27). Put diferently, IT resources can lead to intermediate outcomes such as better processes and to an increase in IT-based value.<sup>5</sup> The path to IT-based value may present some challenges, however. Notably, in MBOs, the need to use IT to simultaneously achieve both corporate-wide and BU-specific goals may create a degree of tension between the corporate parent and key BUs, particularly when corporate IT platforms are perceived as a forced replacement for local IT resources (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Weill & Ross, 2004; Williams & Karahanna, 2013).

The MBO structure emerged as a response to problems of coordination and control within diversified corporations. It implies the decentralisation of some aspects of decision-making and resource control to BU managers, while at the same time emphasising the need for parental coordination to build a portfolio of BUs that can meet corporate-wide goals (Helfat & Eisenhardt, 2004; Hoskisson et al., 1993). The challenge for MBOs is to establish a balanced approach to IT governance that increases IT eficiency while still providing BUs with enough autonomy to address local priorities (Boonstra et al., 2017; Kude et al., 2018; Weill & Ross, 2005; Williams & Karahanna, 2013). While MBOs can gravitate towards extremes of either centralisation or decentralisation, inter-divisional exchanges implies that a fully centralised or decentralised structure is rarely feasible (Williams & Karahanna, 2013). In practice, corporate parents intervene in BUs not only to minimise coordination problems but also to guide and influence BU activities through the knowledge and skills they accumulated over time (Kownatzki et al., 2013; Poppo, 2003; Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999). At the same time, granting BUs more autonomy over critical resources (e.g., supply chain systems) puts decision making authority in the hands of those who are closest to target markets and who are most knowledgeable of local conditions, thus improving the ability of BUs to respond to market events (Davis et al., 1992; Ross & Beath, 2007b). Hence, BU IT autonomy can function as a facilitator of BU agility since it allows BUs to more easily and quickly leverage locally controlled IT resources within the BU to meet pressing business needs.

## 2.1. BU IT autonomy and BU agility

Although prior research shows that IT resources and capabilities can enhance agility (Park et al., 2017; Tallon et al., 2019), an unresolved issue in the literature concerns the role of BU IT autonomy in explaining variations in BU agility. Increased levels of IT autonomy allow BUs to develop local IT solutions with little corporate involvement. This, in turn, help BUs cope with localised market threats and opportunities. In practice, however, the relationship between corporate parents and BUs is often complex and can, on occasion, present barriers to agility (Kownatzki et al., 2013; Martin & Eisenhardt, 2010). For example, Kownatzki et al. (2013) argue that corporate parent interventions in BU afairs may limit BU strategic flexibility and autonomy. In addition, according to the resource dependence theory, increases in intrafirm resource dependence – e.g., when BUs become increasingly dependent on resources controlled by the corporate parent – can produce a power imbalance and ultimately influence organisational behaviour (Hillman et al., 2009). In MBOs, increases in BU dependence may occur due to corporate parent’s eforts to achieve synergies and economies of scale (Kude et al., 2018; Tanriverdi, 2006). While the imposition of shared IT capabilities on BUs can enable greater IT eficiency, it can equally undermine BUs’ IT autonomy. However, as we know from agency theory, information asymmetry (the fact that BUs know more about their specific markets than the corporate parent) and the associated costs of sharing information with the corporate parent may make some form of BU autonomy inevitable (Bosse & Phillips, 2016; Gaba & Joseph, 2013).

## 2.2. Corporate IT platforms, BU process digitisation, and the moderating role of BU IT autonomy

In recent years, MBOs have gone to great expense to develop far-reaching corporate IT platforms. These platforms are often based on scalable architectures and enterprise systems to standardise a set of shared IT capabilities that can be used across the entire organisation (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). These capabilities may be critical for some BUs but optional for others who have, at present, limited need for their deployment. Sambamurthy et al. (2003), Fichman (2004), and Ravichandran (2018) note that digital options – which corporate IT platforms often represent – can improve agility. In addition, corporate IT platforms facilitate digital reuse across the organisation (i.e., the use of existing IT capabilities to solve new business issues), which enhances time to market (Ross et al., 2006; Woerner et al., 2013). Thus, support from corporate IT platforms can lead to increased BU agility. For example, a BU looking for data analytics capabilities may avoid the cost and time needed to create local capabilities and instead leverage data analytics capabilities within the corporate IT platform. This was the approach used by Caesars Entertainment (Harrah’s) to create their firm-wide data analytics capabilities (Sigala, 2013). This approach is consistent with research that shows that corporate parent interventions can lead to performance benefits at the BU level (Bowman & Helfat, 2001; Parmigiani & Holloway, 2011).

Despite the benefits that corporate IT platforms provide, some BUs may prefer an IT support model that afords them more autonomy to devise and execute IT-enabled actions deemed necessary to compete in their end markets. To the extent that BUs operate in diferent market segments and face unexpected market discontinuities, it may be limiting for them to rely on the corporate parent to support all of their IT needs. Their ability to quickly adapt to market events can be impeded if IT capabilities that are needed to respond to those changes are not present in the corporate IT platform. Hence, a one-size-fits-all IT support model – where BUs have low IT autonomy and depend heavily on support from corporate IT – could be a limitation for BUs where a customised form of IT support is needed to drive agility. Conversely, the higher the BU’s IT autonomy, the lower its dependence on the corporate IT platform. Thus, under conditions of high BU IT autonomy, corporate IT platform competence becomes less critical to BU agility. In other words, while corporate IT platform competence enhances BU agility, this efect diminishes as BU IT autonomy increases. Accordingly, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): The relationship between corporate IT platform competence and BU agility is negatively moderated by BU IT autonomy.

Under conditions of high BU IT autonomy, the BU is free to develop and use IT applications and local support mechanisms that best meet its needs without having to directly limit its IT options to whatever is acceptable to the corporate parent. Since agility is tied to an ability to create and process data or insights about localised market events (Tallon et al., 2019), BUs are more likely to improve agility when they use IT applications to support or digitise their core processes (Roberts & Grover, 2012). We know from prior research that the use of IT to support core processes in the value chain may create spillover efects whereby the efects of IT use in one area of the value chain can spillover to downstream processes (Tallon, 2012). For example, IT use in the supply chain can, by making it easier to monitor inventory levels, help to improve manufacturing throughput. If the use of IT is inefective – particularly in an area that is at the heart of the BU strategy – it may have a negative efect on downstream processes within the value chain (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011; Tallon et al., 2016). Using our earlier example, a lack of IT use in supply chain processes could seriously limit manufacturing throughput and, ultimately, hurt BU agility. Thus, increased use of IT applications to digitise core BU processes can have a positive efect on BU agility (Roberts & Grover, 2012; Tallon et al., 2019).

Decisions concerning IT use within BUs do not, however, unfold in a vacuum (Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2021; Queiroz et al., 2020). For example, MBOs often employ IT governance mechanisms to improve corporate IT control and facilitate cross-unit coordination (Wu et al., 2015). Therefore, BU IT leadership may be required to check in with the corporate parent to ensure that they are adhering to company policies. IT expenditure cuts could be imposed by the corporate parent if overall firm performance is weak. Budget procedures could also be enacted to force BUs to seek approval from corporate for specific items of IT expenditure. In this context, BU IT autonomy can be the result of a negotiated process with the corporate parent. Low levels of IT autonomy – where BUs are forced to rely on support from corporate IT to meet their needs – leave less opportunity for local IT applications to directly shape BU agility. Conversely, high IT autonomy frees the BU to create its own IT agenda with less corporate involvement (Ross & Beath, 2007b). In this case, BUs can more easily and quickly change the pace of IT deployment, create tailored IT solutions to support their business activities, and reconfigure applications if needed to keep up with market change. Hence, under conditions of high BU IT autonomy, BU process digitisation can have a proportionately greater role in shaping BU agility. Accordingly:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): The relationship between BU process digitisation and BU agility is positively moderated by BU IT autonomy.

Corporate parents often build shared IT capabilities to support common BU needs (Tanriverdi, 2006). Individual BUs, however, seek to build a portfolio of applications to support their idiosyncratic business activities (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). The challenge for MBOs in this case is to find the right balance between corporate-wide eforts needed to create synergies and BU-specific eforts needed to create value in local markets (Montealegre et al., 2019; Williams & Karahanna, 2013). Such eforts bring into consideration the strategic actions of heterogeneous actors in the organisation and their preferences on modes of IT control related to change (Tilson et al., 2010). In this context, organisations’ eforts to exert IT control through corporate IT platforms can reduce the IT burden on their BUs, allowing individual BUs to focus less on what IT resources to use and more on how to use them to sense and respond to market change. For example, corporate IT platforms can reduce the complexity of local BU IT portfolios (Shpilberg et al., 2007), making it easier for BUs to renew their IT resource base (Queiroz et al., 2018), and facilitating the digitisation of business processes (Tallon et al., 2016).

Building on the arguments advanced previously in H2 as to the influence of BU process digitisation on BU agility, we propose that corporate IT platform competence will have a positive efect on BU agility, mediated through BU process digitisation. However, given that the relationship between BU process digitisation and BU agility is contingent on the degree of BU IT autonomy, this mediation efect can vary as a function of BU IT autonomy.<sup>6</sup> Under conditions of low BU IT autonomy, the corporate IT platform is needed to meet most BU needs. In this context, local BU IT plays a diminished role in shaping BU agility. On the other hand, under conditions of high BU IT autonomy, the BU is free to leverage the corporate IT platform to enable local IT eforts and support their core business processes. Thus, we propose that corporate IT platform competence enhances BU agility through BU process digitisation and that this relationship is stronger when BU IT autonomy is higher. Formally:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Corporate IT platform competence has a positive efect on BU agility through BU process digitisation, the magnitude of which is moderated by BU IT autonomy: the efect is stronger when BU IT autonomy is higher.

## 3. Research method

We studied the organisation’s self-identified key market-facing BU. This is in line with prior research that examined the performance of a key BU within MBOs (Gaba & Joseph, 2013). The focus on a key BU helps to control for extraneous factors that could influence assessments of agility in organisations competing in multiple market segments (Gaba & Joseph, 2013; Olson et al., 2005; Slater & Olson, 2001). While the emphasis on a key BU can limit generalisability, it ofers greater internal validity for theory testing (Setia et al., 2013). We chose a design that ofers greater internal validity, knowing that while firms may have several BUs, some may be more important than others.

## 3.1. Data collection

Data for the study were gathered from an internationa survey of 120 organisations. The population of interest includes MBOs in the U.S., Australia, and Germany. From this population, we developed a sampling frame of 1,200 organisations (800 randomly selected from the U.S. and 400 from Australia and Germany). The survey was administered in 2012, with follow up data collection eforts untill 2014 to gather archival performance data. We discuss the use of archival data to complement the self-reported data in a subsequent section. To identify the profile of an ideal informant for our study, we interviewed senior executives at three publicly traded MBOs. The executives interviewed held senior positions as Vice President of Global E-Commerce and Chief Information Oficer (CIO). In our discussions with these executives, we learned that CIOs would be appropriate informants for our study. CIOs have, by nature of their position, a need to work closely with peers in BUs to ensure an appropriate level of IT support for business activities, particularly the activities of key BUs. Moreover, in some firms the key BU has a divisional CIO or a similarly titled person that works closely with the corporate CIO on a regular basis. We identified a CIO familiar with the key BU as our informant. Our single informant approach is consistent with prior research that targeted CIOs to assess activities of the corporate IT unit and key BUs (e.g., Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018). Overall, 80% of respondents were corporate CIOs while 20% were BU CIOs. The average BU in our sample accounts for 40% of consolidated corporate revenues, confirming their importance to the performance of the MBO as a whole.

We received responses from 141 organisations, indicating an initial response rate of 12%. However, twenty-one responses were excluded due to missing data. Thus, we used 120 responses in our analysis, representing a final response rate of 10%. While low, this is on par with survey response rates reported elsewhere in the IS literature for large firms and where respondents are senior executives (Chau et al., 2020; Ravichandran, 2018; Wagner et al., 2014). Our assessment of non-response bias and common method variance reported in Appendix B indicates that these biases are unlikely to be a major concern. The participating organisations come from the U.S. (41.6%), Germany (40.1%), and Australia (18.3%). They represent several industries: financial services (19.1%), electronics (19.1%), manufacturing (16.6%), wholesale & retail (11.9%), energy (10.8%), and other industries (22.5%), including logistics and healthcare.

## 3.2. Assessment of informant competency

The average tenure of our informants was 12 years, indicating that they had participated in multiple annual planning and budgeting cycles of their firms. The results of a multivariate analysis of variance indicated no significant diferences between responses from the two CIO groups (Wilks’ lambda = 0.952; $\mathrm { ~ F ~ } = ~ 1 . 9 7 ; ~ p ~ > ~ 0 . 1 )$ . Further, in an earlier study, we sought to understand the leadership role of CIOs and their participation in the firm’s strategic planning process (Tallon 2014). We surveyed 133 business executives across 13 firms and asked them about the extent to which the CIO was an active participant in the strategic planning process. The results noted that CIOs were actively involved in strategic planning (average = 5.17; mode = 6 on a 7-point scale). Lastly, given the attention being directed at agility as a key organisational concern, we also note the results of annual SIM surveys where business agility has been repeatedly ranked as one of the top 10 IT executive concerns (often a top 3 or top 5 concern since 2009; Kappelman et al., 2017). Thus, agility is an important consideration for CIOs as they seek to help their firms respond to changing markets. Collectively, these measures indicate that our informants were suficiently competent to answer the questions in this study.

## 3.3. Survey measures

We measured the constructs in our model using multi-item scales. The survey instrument is provided in Appendix C. The survey design was refined using feedback from pilot tests with IT executive sponsors of member firms of the Center for Information Systems Research (CISR) at the MIT Sloan School of Management.

To measure BU agility, we used eight items taken from Tallon and Pinsonneault (2011). This measure assesses agility in terms of customer, partnering, and operational agility. Following the previous literature, we sought to validate this measure by examining its association with measures of performance (Liang et al., 2017, p. 874). The results of this analysis are shown in Appendix D. As expected, the measure of BU agility is positively associated with measures of performance (Liang et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). In line with prior research on the benefits of corporate IT platforms (Quaadgras et al., 2014; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015), we measure corporate IT platform competence using four items that tapped into the extent to which corporate IT platform capabilities are suficient to meet BU needs. Further, prior literature has examined process digitisation in terms of the use of IT to conduct business activities such as marketing and sales (e.g., Adomako et al., 2021) or the adoption and use of applications to enable customer and supplier relations (e.g., Xue et al., 2013). To measure BU process digitisation, we used five items adapted from Tallon (2008) that tapped into the extent to which the BU uses IT applications to support its core business processes in the areas of supplier relations, production and operations, product and service enhancement, sales and marketing, and customer relations. These processes span the breadth of the value chain (Tallon et al., 2016). To measure BU IT autonomy, we used five items that tapped into the extent to which the IT needs of the BU’s core business processes are met locally through BU applications rather than through applications housed in the corporate IT platform. Hence, BUs enjoy greater IT autonomy when they use local, BU-controlled applications. On the other hand, corporate eforts to increase the use of shared IT applications can limit BU IT autonomy. To confirm this assumption, we compared the measure of BU IT autonomy with measures of IT applications sharing in MBOs. We found significant negative associations as expected (see Appendix E).

Control variables were used to account for diferences in industry type, country of origin, ownership structure (public/private), BU contribution to firm revenue (percentage of total revenue), pace of technology change in industry, BU size, and BU strategic orientation. We used a measure taken from Tallon (2008) – which is based on the Treacy and Wiersema’s (1995) strategy typology – to distinguish between BUs pursuing operational excellence and those pursuing diferentiation through customer intimacy or product leadership. We used a dummy variable for this measure: BUs pursuing operational excellence are coded zero, while those pursuing diferentiation are coded one. To assess pace of technology change in industry, we used an item from our survey that tapped into the extent to which technology is changing in the BU’s industry. BU size was measured as the natural log of the number of employees.

## 3.4. Data analysis

We evaluated our measurement model by performing an exploratory analysis of the questionnaire items. To test the hypotheses, we estimated the moderated mediation model in Figure 1. In line with prior research that examined moderated mediation (e.g., Lee et al., 2018), we estimated our model using the PROCESS macro in IBM SPSS statistics (Hayes, 2017; Preacher & Hayes, 2008). PROCESS implements an ordinary least squares regression-based procedure based on bootstrapped estimates (Hayes, 2017). This method has been widely used in the literature to assess moderation, mediation, and moderated mediation efects (Lee et al., 2018; Kilduf et al., 2016; Schlager et al., 2018). We performed our analysis with 10,000 bootstrap samples (Lee et al., 2018). For this test we assessed the index of moderated mediation (Hayes, 2015). The index of moderated mediation is significant if the indirect efect varies significantly depending on the value of the moderator. When the index is positive, the moderator strengthens the indirect efect (e.g., Russo et al., 2018). When the index is negative, the moderator weakens the indirect efect (e.g., Yam et al., 2018). In line with prior literature, we averaged the items in each scale to form scale scores and mean centred measures used in interaction terms (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011; Yu et al., 2018).

## 5. Discussion

We began this paper by referencing a gap in our understanding of agility in MBOs. We suggested that eforts to understand agility in MBOs needed to first understand agility in BUs since MBOs are, in a basic sense, the sum of their BUs. In addressing this gap, we described an IT governance challenge facing MBOs as they try to decide how much or how little latitude to grant BUs: should they obligate BUs to use IT applications sourced through a corporate IT platform or should they allow BUs full reign to source and manage their own IT applications locally, or is there a middle ground? The desirability of a corporate IT platform has been outlined in prior research on IT in MBOs as a way to create significant IT synergies (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Tanriverdi, 2006), and yet BUs may feel exposed and threatened by the loss of IT autonomy and the prospects of moving to less-desirable applications. Requiring BUs to source their IT applications through a shared corporate IT platform is potentially damaging for BU agility if those applications are judged by the BU as being less than suitable for their unique IT needs as indicated by low IT platform competence. Allowing BUs greater IT autonomy may, on the other hand, be seen as an invitation to chaos if key BUs begin to dictate terms to the corporate parent, limiting what can be added to the corporate IT platform, or if other BUs use the example of an increasingly autonomous key BU as a reason for why they too should be allowed to select and control their own IT. Greater BU agility, in this instance, could paradoxically limit the agility of the MBO as a whole. Hence, MBO executives should carefully consider how much latitude to grant key BUs.

When viewed in their totality, our findings suggest three paths to BU agility. We summarise these paths and their underlying rationale in Table 5. Below, we outline a broader set of arguments for how these paths may unfold. These paths emerge from a review of Table 3 (path 1), Table 4 (path 2), and Figure 2 (path 3). The first path is likely to appeal to BUs and their belief that, if forced to use shared IT applications through a corporate IT platform, those applications should at least fit their IT needs. The challenge with this path is that not all BUs are likely to have their needs fully satisfied: the corporate parent may have to “play favorites” with some BUs while disappointing others. The second path to BU agility is likely to favour the corporate parent and their desire for centralised control as, somewhat counterintuitively, it allows the corporate parent to argue that reduced BU IT autonomy is good for BU agility. The challenge with this path is, not surprisingly, that it may motivate BU IT managers to question their place within the BU. A third path to BU agility – representing a blending of the first and second paths – suggests that if MBOs set out to limit BU IT autonomy, they should try to counter any negative fallout within the BU by simultaneously increasing corporate IT platform competence. We next consider each path in turn.

## 5.1. Path 1: strengthen corporate IT platform competence

The introduction of a corporate IT platform is unlikely to come as a surprise to BUs (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). Instead, there is likely to be a period of negotiation as BUs are presented with cost comparisons and arguments for why some of their local IT applications should be replaced with shared platform-hosted solutions. Knowing that these applications may not be a one-sizefits-all solution and that some aspects of their IT support may sufer, BUs may try to negotiate a solution that is at least in their favour. A flagship BU is more likely to win that argument than a less-important BU. This allows for an increase in IT platform competence as perceived by the flagship BU but such a move might not uniformly benefit all BUs. The corporate parent may resort to play ing favourites: accommodating requests from some BUs (those that are judged essential to the current and future success of the MBO) but not from others so that the corporate IT platform is closer in form and function to what the key BU wants. The last thing an MBO wants is to burden a key BU with IT applications that leave it vulnerable, exposed, and incapable of responding to a competitor. Key BUs are obvious targets for competitors: they are likely to be cash cows, to be profitable, visible in the marketplace, and to have high market share. Burdening a key BU with inefective IT support ultimately hurts the MBO and so the corporate parent may be more willing to bend to the needs of a key BU as it assesses shared IT applications for its corporate IT platform. Hence, the path to BU agility in this instance involves strengthening corporate IT platform competence for the most critical BUs as we note in our results in Table 3.

## 5.2. Path 2: reduce BU IT autonomy

This second path is unlikely to prove popular with BUs. As our results suggest (Table 4), one way for MBOs to afect an increase in BU agility is to limit BU IT autonomy, meaning that IT support for BU core processes comes through the corporate IT platform rather than through the BU’s local IT resources. Deciding to restrict BU IT autonomy might seem anathema under some IT governance forms (Weill & Ross, 2004; Williams & Karahanna, 2013); it harkens back to a time when IT was seen as centralised, monolithic, and bureaucratic. Yet, the prospects of the corporate parent knowing what is best for its BUs means that BUs may have limited options to assert their dominance and may instead have to go along. Knowing that the alternative is to allow BUs full IT autonomy (triggering a chaotic mix of diferent and potentially duplicative technologies) or having some BUs outvoted by a key BU, there may be begrudging acceptance that the only way forward is to rely on the corporate IT platform as specified by the corporate parent. Unlike the first path, MBOs may be less inclined to play favourites. BUs may be more likely, therefore, to exchange ideas and insights for how to work within the confines placed on them by a shared corporate IT platform. Leadership of key BUs may be unwilling to accept the loss of local IT autonomy and may work behind the scenes to restore some form of IT autonomy through shadow IT, sourcing IT applications through the cloud rather than through the corporate IT platform (Silic et al., 2017). As such, any moves by the corporate parent to limit BU IT autonomy may have to be assertive, forceful, and restrictive.

## 5.3. Path 3: strengthen corporate IT platform competence with low BU IT autonomy

The two paths identified thus far both lead to increased BU agility but leave open the possibility that some lesser-important BUs (path 1) or perhaps all BUs (path 2) will feel that increased BU agility has come at some cost to them. There is a third path suggested by Figure 2 that combines elements of the first and second paths. Our results suggest that increasing corporate IT platform competence could help to expand BU agility, but this would also help to placate BUs who argue that switching away from local IT applications to shared IT applications will undermine support for their core processes. BUs ultimately fear that the IT platform will prove inefective at supporting their activities and that eforts to restrict their IT autonomy will undermine their ability to develop alternatives. If corporate parents expand IT platform competence at the same time as curtailing BU IT autonomy – since our results show that both paths are associated with increased BU agility – there is a possibility that BUs may be less resentful of losing some of their IT autonomy. BUs may see some form of quid-proquo since the corporate parent is at least trying to limit any possibility that the BU’s core processes will be unsupported after the BU has surrendered its IT autonomy. In efect, increased corporate IT platform competence is compensation for a reduction in BU IT autonomy. BUs may feel more confident that the corporate parent has their best interests at heart and will not push IT capabilities through the corporate platform that they neither need nor want.

## 5.4. Contribution and implications for research and theory

This study contributes to the agility literature in three ways. First, by integrating insights from the streams of research on agility (Tallon et al., 2019) and IT governance challenges in MBOs (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Williams & Karahanna, 2013), we show that BU agility is determined by resource synergies based on the interplay between corporate IT platforms and BU-controlled IT applications. Our eforts to integrate theoretical insights from complementary research streams contribute to a more holistic picture of how IT resources in MBOs influence BU agility. The structure of MBOs and the complex ways that the corporate parent interacts with its BUs and how BUs interact with one another suggests that agility is more complex and nuanced than one might think. Introducing corporate IT platforms into this discussion adds a further layer of complexity to eforts to develop a theory of agility.

Second, we show that BU IT autonomy plays a contingent role in determining BU agility. By so doing, we extend research on the organisational boundary conditions that determine the role that IT plays in shaping agility (Park et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). As our plots show in Figures 2 and 3, when BU IT autonomy is low, BU agility depends mostly on corporate IT platform competence. The challenge for BUs in this case is their growing dependence on the corporate IT platform – over which they have minimal control – to drive agility. Any decrease in the level of support from the IT platform, perhaps due to corporate budgetary constraints or a lack of coordination between the BU and the corporate parent, could inhibit BU agility (Tallon et al., 2019). However, one must be careful to not read into this that eforts to create shared IT solutions and to embed those solutions within an IT platform are destined to fail. There is a time and place for corporate IT platforms but there are also limits as to what they can do in practice. Problems can emerge when BUs have little latitude to say what they want the corporate IT platform to contain or feel that the needs of a key BU are receiving preferential treatment.

Lastly, prior research on IT-business alignment has examined the benefits that organisations accrue from leveraging capabilities at both the corporate and BU levels (Queiroz et al., 2020; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). The question addressed in our study adds a new angle to this stream of literature. Specifically, it suggests that theory on IT-business alignment – and perhaps other theories also – needs to evolve to consider the nature of the relationships between corporate parents and BUs and how these relationships support or undermine BU agility. The desire for digital transformation may add a long-term consideration to the conversation around what needs to go onto the corporate IT platform and what needs to be retained locally. This is an important IT alignment challenge for executives in MBOs with related implications for IT governance. Just because a corporate IT platform is contributing to BU agility today does not mean that it will work tomorrow when a BU suddenly confronts an unanticipated market event.<sup>7</sup> By giving BUs some IT autonomy, MBOs can promote the development of local solutions that best suit unique BU needs. As highlighted in Figure 3, when BU IT autonomy is high – that is when BUs are allowed to use local applications to enable core processes – increased BU process digitisation enhances BU agility. On the other hand, when BU IT autonomy is low, local IT has little or no efect on BU agility. If we ponder the subject of how corporate parents can influence BU agility, one could argue that the answer to this is knowing when to step in and provide assistance to BUs and, equally, when to stay out of the way and allow BUs to use local IT to satisfy their idiosyncratic needs.

## 5.5. Implications for practice

Our findings also have implications for practice. The strategic challenge for an MBO is to develop structural arrangements that align the multifaceted roles of IT with both corporate-wide and BU-specific goals. This requires careful consideration of how IT applications sourced through the corporate IT platform should mesh with local IT applications over which the BU retains control.<sup>8</sup> In practice, the corporate parent’s eforts to leverage corporate IT platforms and the desire by BUs for local applications often lead to tension. Resolving this tension requires consensus between BUs and the corporate parent regarding how much latitude the corporate parent is willing to grant its BUs. In the pursuit of BU agility, our results question whether a corporate parent should remove all (or most) IT autonomy from its BUs. The question then becomes how to mesh diferent local and platform applications and how this meshing of IT capabilities should proliferate across BUs. Answering this question is not easy and requires corporate leaders to find a way to balance what they want from a corporate IT platform with what level of engagement in shared capabilities BUs are prepared to accept.

Our results reveal that the path to BU agility is conditional on resource synergies in MBOs. This implies that, because organisations will operate under diferent resource conditions, they should choose their own unique configurations. The most efective way to achieve BU agility appears to be one where MBOs retain some form of local IT autonomy within BUs rather than to force all BU IT managers to capitulate to corporate IT capabilities. This approach is likely to be more efective than following uniform “herd” industry practice of using corporate IT platforms to reduce or eliminate a proliferation of IT standards, bespoke applications or to better control IT budgets. While operational eficiency may be a desirable aim, the push to adopt large one-size-fitsall corporate IT platforms can curtail local responsiveness, and lead to a reduction in BU agility. Hence, managers should consider alternative IT configurations to promote agility. For example, could an individual BU, whether the key BU or another, create an internal market for IT applications so that a BU could consider whether to source its IT support from the corporate platform or from other BUs? Regardless of the sources of IT support, an MBO should choose a configuration that can be efective in achieving agility for their own contextual condition. In practice, managers are free to choose from various configurations but at the same time, our analysis is a source of guidance to managers. We show that the set of efective configurations available to managers is not unlimited. Rather, we reveal three paths to agility that can be used by managers to help reduce the cognitive load when deciding between IT configurations that influence BU agility.

## 5.6. Limitations and future research

The results of this study should be interpreted in light of its limitations and potential areas for future research. First, our study focuses on a key BU within each organisation in our sample. Because not all BUs are created equal, what works best for the flagship or most important BU might not work equally well for other BUs. Future research could benefit from looking in more detail at a small number of organisations with multiple BUs in order to discern the way that BUs work together or independently to influence one another’s agility. In addition, since key BUs may be better at using IT to support their business activities, further study investigating other, non-flagship BUs could enhance the generalisability of our results. We note, however, that our analysis controls for diferences in BU size, BU contribution to firm revenues, and BU strategic orientation. Second, the survey data collection was based on a single respondent per firm. This raises the potential for common method variance. As seen in Appendix B, we employed strategies to mitigate this concern and our tests found no evidence of common method variance.

Furthermore, given the possibility of corporate parent interventions in BU decision making, future studies could also trace firms’ IT investment decisions and new IT deployments over time to examine the evolution of corporate IT platforms and the implications to BU agility. Corporate IT platforms can evolve based on modular architectures that ofer generativity, meaning “the platform’s ability to foster unprompted innovation through continuous recombination of diferent modules” (Rai et al., 2019, p. iii). Generativity in a modular architecture is achieved through loose couplings across architecture layers whereby “innovations can spring up independently at any layer, leading to cascading efects on other layers” (Yoo et al., 2010, p. 728). Thus, a corporate IT platform can evolve to function as an innovation hub (Costantinides et al., 2018). In this case, individual BUs may leverage IT platform capabilities to build innovative applications, which later could be integrated to the platform for the benefit of other BUs.

Our results suggest that it is important to better understand how decisions are made by corporate and BU leadership as to what the IT platform will support and what residual activities must instead be supported locally by the BUs. Future research could ask how these decisions are reached if BUs are pursuing very diferent markets or if there is a power imbalance due to the disproportionate performance of one key BU. Theory may also need to consider what might happen if a BU needed to secede from a corporate IT platform and to re-establish its own applications. The unwritten assumption is that BUs switch away from their own IT to a corporate IT platform but what happens in the ebb and flow over time as new applications are added to the platform or older applications are retired from the platform? The advent of cloud technologies potentially presents a double-edged sword worth of further investigation. Cloud technologies can extend the scale and afordability of corporate IT platforms but, when faced with an overbearing corporate parent and the loss of IT autonomy, BUs may go rogue and turn to cloud technologies to restore some degree of agility that they feel has been lost by their forced reliance on shared IT applications. The question may be whether corporate leaders turn a blind eye to such subversive activities since it helps BU agility, at least in the short term, or whether they see this as ultimately hurting the MBO.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper develops and validates a new theoretical explanation as to when and how corporatesponsored IT platforms and BU-controlled IT applications enhance BU agility. The study’s findings have implications for our understanding of the boundary conditions under which distinct IT resources influence BU agility. Corporate IT platforms matter a great deal in the search for BU agility, but local IT resources matter too and should not be overlooked. Striking the right balance between corporateprovided IT and BU-controlled IT is not just relevant to researchers; it matters to practitioners too for they, at the end of the day, are the ones who must live with this decision.

## Appendix B. Common Method Variance and Non-response Bias

We sought to mitigate concerns with common method variance (CMV) in three ways. First, we followed the recommendations proposed by Podsakof et al. (2003) when developing the survey instrument, specifically, by ofering anonymity, protecting respondent identity, and separating scale items used to measure key constructs with questions relating to non-critical constructs. Second, we applied Harman’s single-factor test (Podsakof & Organ, 1986). The results of this test reveal no single dominant factor. The highest variance explained by any single factor is 31.7%. Third, prior research argues that archival data collected from publicly available data sources could be used to validate selfreported data and corroborate research findings, thus mitigating concerns with CMV (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006). We employed this strategy as discussed in Appendix D.

In order to investigate the potential for non-response bias, we analysed our sample using the extrapolation procedure presented in Armstrong and Overton (1977). No significant diferences were found between early and late respondents, suggesting that non-response bias is not a major concern. We also investigated group diferences based on respondent type and country of origin. Multivariate analysis of variance indicated no significant diferences in terms of respondent type (Wilks’ lambda = 0.952; F = 1.97; p > 0.1) and countries (Wilks’ lambda = 0.927; F = 1.47; p > 0.1).

## Appendix D. Assessing the Link Between BU Agility and Measures of Performance

Following the previous literature, we sought to validate our measure of agility by examining its association with measures of performance. For example, in their validation eforts, Liang et al. (2017, p. 874) found that agility is positively associated with both a self-reported measure of firm performance (β = 0.443, p < 0.01) and an objective measure of firm performance (β = 0.254, p < 0.01). Similarly, Ravichandran (2018) and Tallon and Pinsonneault (2011) found positive correlations between agility and diferent measures of performance. Accordingly, to validate our measure of BU agility, we used two separate performance measures. First, we used a selfreported measure of BU performance as a composite of five items adapted from Powell and Dent-Micallef (1997) and Kim et al. (2011). These items assess performance in terms of market share, sales growth, profitability, and revenue. Second, we collected archival data on firm revenues to create an objective measure of overall firm performance. We collected these data for a total of 69 organisations (the remaining organisations in our sample are either private or were anonymised by respondents under the rules set for our data collection). We collected data on firms’ revenues for 2012 (the year of the survey) and for the following year. Since rapid revenue growth is an indicator of agility (Ross & Beath, 2007a), we used these data to calculate the firm’s revenue growth rate (lagged one year). We correlate the measure of agility with the selfreported and objective measures of performance. The results showed that BU agility is correlated with the measure of BU performance $( r = 0 . 4 1 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and with the objective measure of firm performance $( r = 0 . 2 6 3 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$

## Appendix E. Assessing the Link Between BU IT Autonomy and IT Applications Sharing

As part of their eforts to create IT synergies, MBOs must decide which IT applications should be unique to BUs and which IT applications should be shared across the entire organisation. Hence, eforts to increase IT applications sharing across the organisation are likely to reduce BU IT autonomy. To confirm this belief, we correlated the measure of BU IT autonomy with items from our survey that assess IT applications sharing in MBOs. Specifically, the items assess the extent to which IT applications for supplier relations, product/service operations, product/service innovation, marketing and sales, and customer service are shared across BUs. As expected, we found significant negative correlations between BU IT autonomy and the sharing of IT applications for supplier relations $( r \ = \ - 0 . 6 0 7 , \ p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , product/service operations $( r = - 0 . 5 6 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , product/service innovation $( r = - 0 . 5 0 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , marketing and sales $( r ~ = ~ - 0 . 4 9 6 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and customer service $( r = - 0 . 4 7 1 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$
