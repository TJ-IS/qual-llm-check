# Negativity bias in the diagnosticity of online review content: the efects of consumers’ prior experience and need for cognition

Hamed Qahri-Saremi <sup>a</sup> and Ali Reza Montazemi <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>College of Business, Colorado State University, Fort Collins, Colorado, USA; <sup>b</sup>DeGroote School of Business, McMaster University, Ontario, Hamilton, Canada

## ABSTRACT

The importance of online review valence is a subject of debate among scholars. Prior studies mostly assumed valence as a “peripheral” cue derived from online review surface features (e.g., star ratings). This assumption has important implications as it restricts the negativity bias efects to a certain group of consumers who lack pertinent prior experience with the product/service domain and the motivation to assess the product/service. Focusing on online service context and drawing on an adaptational view to negative information, we investigate the negativity bias in the efects of the valence of the “content” of online reviews on consumers’ attitudes and show that it can be attributed to the higher perceived diagnosticity of negative reviews. This is determined by consumers’ in-depth elaborations of reviews’ contents, which are contingent on their prior experience with the domain of online service and need for cognition. Our findings provide a new perspective to negativity bias by showing that more experienced and thoughtful consumers are also influenced by negativity bias when the content of online reviews is considered. This is a novel account of negativity bias in the efects of online reviews that underscores the importance of response strategies for reducing their adverse efects.

## 1. Introduction

While seeking other consumers’ personal experiences with products/services for purchase decisions, traditionally known as word of mouth (WoM), is not a new phenomenon, online platforms, such as Yelp and TripAdvisor, have amplified the popularity and importance of WoM for consumers and providers to unprecedented levels by creating the online version of WoM: “online reviews” (also referred to as “electronic WoM” and “eWoM”). These platforms have facilitated ubiquitous and convenient access to a large number of online reviews about almost any product/service. As a result, compared to ofline WoMs, online reviews are profoundly more influential on consumers decisions. A recent survey found that in 2020, 87% of consumers consulted with online reviews before choosing a local business, and 79% of consumers trusted online reviews as much as personal recommendations from their friends and family (Murphy, 2020).

However, not all online reviews are equal in their impacts on consumers’ decisions. In this vein, an ongoing debate among scholars concerns the “negativity bias” in the efects of online reviews that happens when negative reviews (NR) have larger and/or more lasting impacts on consumers’ decisions as compared to positive reviews (PR) of comparative magnitude (Chen & Lurie, 2013). Indeed, if not addressed efectively, NRs can spark an “online firestorm” which can spread at an exponential rate reaching thousands or millions of consumers in a short period of time. An online firestorm entails a sudden discharge of large quantities of negative messages and complaints against a firm on social media platforms (Pfefer et al., 2014). Therefore, firms must detect and adequately respond to NRs in online platforms to avoid potential public debacles, online rating declines, customer defections, and profit reductions (Curchod et al., 2020; Pfefer et al., 2014). To that end, it is important for firms to understand how NRs influence their consumers and when their impacts on consumers are stronger than those of PRs (i.e., “negativity bias”).

Despite the relative importance of NR, prior research findings about its asymmetric efects on consumers’ opinions and decisions (i.e., negativity bias) are mixed (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019, 2020). While some prior works have found support for the negativity bias in the efects of online reviews (e.g., Chen & Lurie, 2013), others have not been able to find this efect (e.g., Wu, 2013). In most of these studies, valence (i.e., positivity and negativity) of an online review has been implicitly or explicitly assumed as a “peripheral cue” (a heuristic) simply derived from the surface features of an online review without giving much considerations to the contents of the message (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019). Indeed, in these studies, “star ratings” has been predominantly used as the basis for determining the positivity and negativity of online reviews. As an aggregated measure that serves as a heuristic, “star ratings can reduce cognitive processing efort” (Nazlan et al., 2018, p. 451); therefore, by relying on star ratings, prior studies on online review valence have predominantly assumed negativity bias as a peripheral efect. On this basis, a common explanation in studies that could not detect the negativity bias efects is that valence of an online review as a simple peripheral cue loses its significance for a certain group of consumers who pay more attention to the “central” cues related to the content of an online review, such as its quality (Wu, 2013).

Assuming the valence of an online review as a peripheral cue has important theoretical and practical implications. As explained by dual-process theories, such as elaboration likelihood model (ELM; Petty & Wegener, 1998), only a certain group of consumers who lack either pertinent experience with the product/service domain or the motivation to assess a product/service (that is they are either novice or uninvolved) rely on peripheral cues for decisionmaking. Thus, based on this assumption, it can essentially be inferred that negativity bias should only impact the decisions of novice and uninvolved consumers.

Nevertheless, a separate stream of research, outside of online review literature, has found that consumers who are more experienced and involved with a decision are indeed more afected by negativity bias in the contents of the messages related to their decision (e.g., Ahluwalia, 2002; Lane et al., 2021; Rozin & Royzman, 2001). One major diference in this stream of research as compared to the (majority of) extant online review research is their focus on the “content” of the message, rather than peripheral cues around it (e.g., star ratings in online review context), for investigating the negativity bias efects. Not only are their findings antithetical to the predominant assumption in online review literature about the peripheral nature of negativity bias, but also markedly change its importance for a diferent and arguably more important group of consumers, who are experienced in and involved with a product/service. While negative valence as a peripheral cue only afecting novice and uninvolved consumers may not invoke firms to devise strategies to respond to the NRs about their products/ services, negativity as a central cue impacting the decisions of experienced and involved consumers is suficiently crucial for firms to devise clear response strategies to contain the adverse consequences of NRs (Casado-Díaz et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2021). The reason is that experienced and involved consumers are not only more important targets for firms to increase their sales, but also more influential on other potential consumers’ decisions to adopt a firm’s product/service (Boyer & Hult, 2006).

Extant online review literature has paid little attention to the conditions and pertinent factors that can influence the negativity bias efects based on the actual contents of online reviews. In this paper, we address this important matter by extending the concept of negativity bias efects beyond simple star ratings and to the valence of the online review content. By masking the star rating efects in our study, we assess the asymmetric efects of NR (versus PR) reflected in the contents of the online reviews on consumers’ attitudes, in the pre-usage stage of online experience services, when no firsthand use experience with the online service is available. To that end, our research objective is to investigate whether the negativity bias based on the contents of online reviews is attributed to the diferences in the perceived diagnosticity of NRs for diferent consumers. For doing so, we take an “adaptational” view of information as explained in prior IS re search (Lee,2010; McKinney Jr. & Yoos II, 2010). We contend that negativity bias occurs when the negative information within an online review is a “perceived ‘diference that makes a diference’ (Bateson, 1973) to a subject” (McKinney Jr. & Yoos II,2010, p. 336). Perceived diagnosticity of NRs for diferent consumers can be a function of their in-depth elaborations of the online reviews’ contents. On this basis and drawing on the ELM and extant negativity bias literature, we hypothesize that the negativity bias in the efects of online reviews content is a function of consumers depth of elaboration of the online review messages, which is contingent on their prior experience with the domain of the product/service and their level of need for cognition. We validate our hypotheses via a randomized controlled experiment. Our findings provide a novel, counter-intuitive, and complementary account of the negativity bias in the efects of online reviews and help to explain some of the inconsistent findings in the extant literature in this regard.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

## 2.1. Negativity bias

Research in decision-making has shown that people generally give higher weight to negative information than positive information of comparative magnitude and that the former tends to invoke greater information processing and lasts longer in the memory (Rozin & Royzman, 2001). This efect, called negativity bias, is inherently a comparative notion as it is the function of the weight and importance of negative information in the formation of attitudes and judgements relative to those of positive information (Rozin & Royzman,

2001). Prior studies in psychology (e.g., Ito et al., 1998) found that the human brain responds more strongly to negative than positive information, and retains the memory of negative information longer, even when the behavioural response to the stimuli is over.

Negativity bias can arise due to negative information’s greater veracity, specificity, diagnosticity, and contagiousness than positive information (Rozin & Royzman, 2001). A common theoretical explanation for the negativity bias efect is based on the “category diagnosticity approach”, which explains that consumers’ judgements and attitudes towards objects are based on placing them into diferent categories using existing informational cues about the objects (Skowronski & Carlston, 1989). It further explains that some informational cues are perceived as more diagnostic than others in distinguishing between diferent categories and that more diagnostic informational cues will have a stronger influence on consumers’ judgements and leave more lasting traces in their memory than less diagnostic information (Skowronski & Carlston, 1989).

Drawing on the category diagnosticity approach, prior research shows that negative information can be perceived as more diagnostic than positive information for categorising objects into evaluative categories because social norms lead consumers to provide positive information about products/services more frequently than negative information, making the former more prevalent and the latter rarer (Chen & Lurie, 2013; Ketelaar et al., 2015). Therefore, “negative reviews may have greater ‘surprise’ value to readers” (Yin et al., 2012, p. 2). This is also consistent with the “Pollyanna Hypothesis” that “there is a universal human tendency to use evaluatively positive (E+) words more frequently, diversely and facilely than evaluatively negative (E-) words. Put even more simply, humans tend to ‘look on (and talk about) the bright side of life’” (Boucher & Osgood, 1969, p. 1). In other words, “as most products, whether low-, average-, or high-quality, have positive attributes, negative ones are much rarer. Hence, negative product attributes are considered to be more characteristic of poor-quality products than positive attributes are for high-quality products” (Ketelaar et al., 2015, p. 653). This position has been further corroborated by the fact that a positivity bias is observed in limited occasions where positive events are rarer than negative events (e.g., manifestations of exceptional human ability; Rozin & Royzman, 2001).

In addition, prior findings in psychology literature (Feldman, 1999; Skowronski & Carlston, 1987) explain that negative messages are conceived as more diagnostic because they are typically more specific and convey more information than positive messages. This happens because consumers often describe negative experiences in greater details than positive experiences. Moreover, the risk averse nature of most humans leads them to pay closer attention to negative messages to avoid and mitigate the risks associated with their decisions. As a result, they find negative information that inform them of the risks associated with their decisions as more diagnostic than positive information, which highlights the benefits.

Extrapolating the foregoing arguments to the context of online reviews, we expect that the lower frequency of NRs as well as their specificity and focus on risks (rather than benefits) of consumers’ decisions lead consumers to perceive them as more diagnostic information for assessing products/services than their more common counterparts, PRs. This higher diagnosticity can lead consumers to devote more attention and information-processing resources and assign more weight to NRs than to PRs in judgements and impression formation. Therefore, the asymmetrical efect of NRs (i.e., the negativity bias) is a function of their perceived diagnosticity.

The diagnosticity judgement is a subjective assessment contingent on contextual as well as individual factors (Ahluwalia, 2002; Ketelaar et al., 2015). A piece of information that is perceived as diagnostic for one consumer in a specific context may not be perceived as such by another consumer or in a diferent context. To this end, several studies (e.g., Lane et al. (2021) and Ahluwalia (2002)) have shown that when decision makers critically process messages – i.e., “high information elaboration”, negative messages are perceived as more diagnostic than the comparative positive information, leading to stronger negativity bias efects. For example, Lane et al. (2021) show that experts are more likely to engage in deep processing and elaboration of information when they are presented with negative information as compared to positive infor mation. Moreover, Ahluwalia (2002) shows that consumers who desire to hold attitudes and beliefs that are objectively valid and care about the accuracy of information find the negative messages as more diagnostic than the positive messages, leading to stronger negativity bias efects on their judgements as compared to other consumers. These findings are consistent with Taylor’s (1991) view that negative information stimulates a special set of cognitive processes designed to cope with the threat that involves elaborate information processing. Similarly, Peeters and Czapinski (1990) argue that, compared to positive information, negative information can have greater veracity which would require more cognitive elaboration to be elicited. Against this backdrop, in this paper, we investigate the role of information elaboration in negativity bias in the efects of online reviews. We draw on the ELM to theorise about the contextual and individual factors that can alter the negativity bias in the efects of online reviews.

## 2.2. Elaboration likelihood model and negativity bias

ELM explains that perceptions, impressions, and judgements can be formed via two conceptually distinct information processing routes representing the two ends of the elaboration likelihood continuum: central and peripheral information processing (Petty & Wegener, 1998). When the elaboration likelihood is high, people process and evaluate the object-relevant information based on their prior experience with the domain of the object (i.e., “attribute-oriented thoughts” (Sujan, 1985)) which would result in the formation of perceptions about the object that are well articulated and bolstered by their prior experience. This is referred to as the central route of information processing in ELM. However, when the elaboration likelihood is low, the extent of processing and scrutiny of information is significantly reduced and people’s perceptions about the focal object are formed as a result of less resource-demanding heuristics (i.e., “simple evaluative thoughts” (Sujan, 1985)) that do not require efortful processing of the objectrelevant information. This is referred to as the peripheral route of information processing in ELM (Petty & Wegener, 1998).

Prior studies on the efects of message valence explain that when the elaboration likelihood is high (i.e., central route of information processing in ELM), negative messages are perceived more diagnostic and bear more influence on consumers’ decisions than positive messages (Ahluwalia, 2002; Ketelaar et al., 2015; Lane et al., 2021; Peeters & Czapinski, 1990). This is because, in the central route of information processing, consumers base their judgements on efortful processing, detailed scrutiny, and the perceived diagnosticity of the message content (Petty & Wegener, 1998). Due to the non-normative nature of negative information in most instances and its higher perceived specificity and risk-focus, it tends to be viewed as more novel and diagnostic, than more frequent positive information (Ahluwalia, 2002; Skowronski & Carlston, 1989), causing central route processors to assign it greater weights during impression formation and decision-making, as compared to positive information. Consequently, when conditions favour the dominant use of the central route of information processing, negative messages are expected to be perceived as more diagnostic and produce greater persuasion, as compared to positive messages, leading to a negativity bias efect.

However, negativity bias is attenuated, eliminated, or even reversed when consumers only rely on the surface features of a message and its source in decision-making and the perceived diagnosticity of the message content is less important in formation of their judgements (Ahluwalia, 2002; Skowronski & Carlston, 1987). This occurs when conditions favour the dominant use of the peripheral route of information processing for decision making (Petty & Wegener, 1998). As a result, in the peripheral route of information processing, the asymmetrical efect of negative information – i.e., the negativity bias efect – is less evident.

Given the importance of central versus peripheral route of message processing for negativity bias, the factors that can determine consumers’ route of message processing can arguably be important determinants of negativity bias. What directs consumers towards the central versus the peripheral route of information processing is contingent on contextual as well as individual factors that shape consumers ability and motivation to elaborate on the content of a given message (Petty & Wegener, 1998). In particular, the ELM, like other dual-process theories, argues that because human cognitive resources are limited, people cannot process the details of every single message they receive in-depth.

Generally, the amount of information processing efort expended is a function of an individual’s ability and motivation to process a given message (Petty & Wegener, 1998). Consumers with higher ability and motivation to process a given message are more likely to follow the central route of processing (i.e., higher elaboration likelihood). Considering their need for more diagnostic information, these consumers are more likely to perceive negative information as diagnostic and persuasive, given the less common and more novel, specific, and risk-focus nature of negative information as compared to positive information, leading to more pronounced negativity bias efects (Ahluwalia, 2002; Lane et al., 2021). Therefore, higher consumers’ ability and motivation to process a given message can increase the likelihood of negativity bias efects in their message processing. Against this backdrop, we contend that consumers’ prior experience with the context of an online review and their need for cognition are two factors that can increase the likelihood of negativity bias in the efects of online reviews on consumers’ attitudes towards online services.

## 2.3. Consumers’ prior experience with the online service domain and negativity bias

Consumers’ prior experience with the context of a given message increases their ability to process the contents of the message (Qahri-Saremi &

Montazemi, 2016; Sujan, 1985). Prior experience with the context of a message refers to consumers experience and familiarity with other products/services that are within a similar context/domain as the ones presented in the message. This provides the consumers with the “cognitive schema” to scrutinise the message content (Peracchio & Tybout, 1996). This schema-based scrutiny and inference allows the consumers to engage in an in-depth elaboration and assessment of the diagnosticity of the information within a message, manifested in the central route of information processing. However, without the prior experience to shape their cognitive schema, consumers will simply lack the reference point for assessing a message content (Peracchio & Tybout, 1996). As such, they are more limited in the depth of their elaboration, manifested in the peripheral route of information processing. In other words, the cognitive schema determines the consumers’ ability for engaging in central route of information processing. Those who are less able to engage in central route of processing a message are likely to follow a peripheral route in assessing the message. As a result, they rely on the surface features of a message and its source in decision-making and the perceived diagnosticity of the message content is less influential in the formation of their judgements.

In the case of our study, consumers’ prior experience with the domain of the online service that is presented in the online review provide a cognitive schema for the consumers to scrutinise and evaluate the content of the review and its diagnosticity (i.e., central route). Therefore, we contend that the attitudes of consumers with higher prior experience with the online service domain are more likely to be influenced by the diagnosticity of review content. As explained before, negative information is conceived to be more diagnostic than positive information. Thus, the negativity bias efects are expected to be more pronounced in higher elaboration likelihood conditions (i.e., central route). On this basis, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: The negativity bias in the efects of online review content on consumers’ attitudes towards an online service is stronger when consumers have higher prior experience with the online service domain.

## 2.4. Consumers’ need for cognition and negativity bias

Motivation to process a given message is another important contingency factor for high elaboration likelihood even among consumers with high ability for elaboration (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). Prior research has shown that consumers’ level of need for cognition is an important determinant of consumers’ motivation to elaborate on a message content (Cacioppo et al., 1996; Srivastava & Sharma, 2012). Need for cognition is a well-established individual characteristic manifested in people’s disposition to engage in and enjoy thinking (Cacioppo et al., 1996; Gupta & Harris, 2010; Wood & Swait, 2002). In the persuasion literature, need for cognition is conceptualised as the proclivity to process information, which, distinct from intellectual ability, predicts consumers’ motivation to evaluate products/ services (Gupta & Harris, 2010; Srivastava & Sharma, 2012; Wood & Swait, 2002).

ELM explains that both ability and motivation to process a given message and elaborate on its content are necessary conditions for a consumer to engage in the central route of processing (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). Without high motivation, consumers are less likely to engage in efortful processing of the message content, even when they have high prior experience with its domain (Gupta & Harris, 2010). Against this backdrop and given the higher likelihood of negativity bias efects in the central route of processing, we contend that higher levels of need for cognition can strengthen the efects of prior experience with the online service domain on the likelihood of negativity bias in the efects of online reviews. To this end, we postulate a second-order (three-way) moderation hypothesis.

H2: Consumers’ level of need for cognition positively moderates the efect of their prior experience with the online service domain on the negativity bias in the efects of online review content.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Study context

In this study, we focus on the negativity bias in the efects of online reviews’ contents on consumers attitudes before using online experience services. Experience services are characterised by attributes that cannot be objectively evaluated, rather need to be experienced and subjectively evaluated by the consumers before usage (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). Examples of online experience services include online music services and online healthcare services. Given the subjective and personal nature of online reviews, they are an important source for reducing consumers’ uncertainty towards evaluating online experience services. Therefore, we focus on the online experience service context (called “online service” in this paper).

Consumers’ usage of an online service is a process that occurs over time consisting of two main stages – preusage and post-usage – with diferent decisions and actions occurring at each stage (Montazemi & Qahri-Saremi, 2015). Because, unlike the post-usage stage, consumers in the pre-usage stage have no experience with using the focal online service. Thus, external sources of information about the focal online service, such as online reviews, play an important role in determining their attitude towards using the online service in this stage. To this end, we focus on the efects of online reviews at the pre-usage stage.

## 3.2. Subjects

Subjects in our experiment were 450 (43% women, 57% men) undergraduate (freshman, sophomore, and junior) commerce students in a business school in North America, with an average age of 19.6 (SD = 1.11). Student samples were used in prior service adoption and online review studies (e.g., Yin et al., 2014). Students’ participation in our study was voluntary and was compensated by a 2% bonus course credit.

## 3.3. Experiment design

We conducted a controlled laboratory experiment to assess the two hypotheses. We randomly assigned subjects to six conditions based on a 3 × 2 betweensubject factorial design, as depicted in Table 1: three diferent conditions (negative, positive, and no reviews), in conjunction with two diferent conditions to represent higher/lower prior experience with the online service domain were manipulated between subjects.

## 3.4. Role and design of the control group

Consistent with prior studies (e.g., Ketelaar et al., 2015; Lane et al., 2021; Sundaram & Webster, 1999), we used a “no-review” condition as a neutral control group for validating the PR and NR manipulations. The reason is that due to possibly asymmetrical and subjective efects of negative versus positive information on a consumer’s attitude, it was impossible to ensure that any two-sided review would be perceived as equally neutral by all our subjects. Indeed, this, in itself, would have required a separate validation check.

## 3.5. Experimental manipulations

## 3.5.1. Higher/lower prior experience with the online service domain

To manipulate the higher/lower consumers’ prior experience of the online service domain, we selected two diferent domains of the online experience services with which we expected our subjects to have diferent levels of prior experience: online music service to represent higher prior experience domain (e.g., eMusic.com), and online healthcare service to represent lower prior experience domain (e.g., WebMD.com). Students are frequent users of online music services (Kennedy et al., 2008). Thus, we expected our subjects to have higher prior experience of browsing for and listening to their favourite music using online music services (websites). ## 3.5.2. Negative and positive reviews’ contents (online review valence manipulations)

To manipulate the valence of online review content in our experiment, we developed four fictitious NRs and four fictitious PRs for the healthcare/music online services to nudge our subjects’ perceptions against or for using the online service (see samples in Appendix B). Prior research has shown the importance of volume of online reviews on consumers’ decisions (Duan et al., 2008; Montazemi & Qahri-Saremi, 2014; Ren & Nickerson, 2019). Therefore, controlling the number of online reviews (to four online reviews) across our experimental treatments allowed us to control this efect. We randomly selected eight online music services and eight online healthcare services. Each fictitious online review discussed an online healthcare/music service (website) in terms of its usefulness, ease of use, and trustworthiness, where NRs criticised them and PRs endorsed them.

To control the potential confounding efects of other factors, such as source credibility and online review length and quality, all subjects were informed that the reviews were from a credible source with verified experience with the online service (for controlling source credibility). Furthermore, the fictitious positive/negative reviews had the same length (210 words). No significant diference between the four experimental treatments was found in terms of the review content quality (p= 0.71).

## 3.6. Measures and factors

The measurement instruments for our factors are provided in Appendix C. The dependent factor in our study is consumers’ attitudes towards using an online service, which represent users’ overall orientation towards an online service. To measure this factor, we adapted the measures from Pavlou and Fygenson (2006) (e.g., “using [this website] is a good idea”). Furthermore, for measuring PU, PEOU, and trust that we used in our manipulation check, we adapted scales from Pavlou and Fygenson (2006) that are based on Ajzen and Fishbein (1980)’s expectancy-value formulation for measuring attitudinal beliefs. According to expectancy-value formulation, these belief factors were measured as the product term<sup>2</sup> of belief strength (e.g., “[healthcare/music services] would enhance my efectiveness in getting useful information [healthcare/music]”), and the importance of its outcome for the respondent (e.g., “For me, getting useful information about [healthcare/ music] from [healthcare/music website] is important”; Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006). Furthermore, measures for the need for cognition were adapted from Wood and Swait (2002).

## 3.7 Experimental procedures

At the beginning of the experiment, all subjects were asked to complete an online questionnaire that collected their demographics and the extent of their prior experience with the online music/ healthcare domain. Next, to avoid possible confounding efects of subjects’ prior firsthand use experience with a particular online service while ensuring their suficient involvement with the online service, we adapted the two criteria suggested by Sundaram and Webster (1999) and Lim and Lee (2019) as follows. First, a randomly selected name of an online service (website) from either a music or a healthcare list was displayed on the computer screen for each subject. The subject was asked whether he/she had used it before. If he/she had used the online service, then another randomly selected name from the list was presented on the screen until it reached a name that the subject had not used before, which was then assigned to the subject. This allowed us to avoid the confounding efect of subjects’ prior firsthand use experience of the assigned online service. There was no incident in which the subject had used all of the eight online services in the list.

Second, all subjects (in control and treatment groups) received a general and neutral description of the assigned online service (see Appendix A). The description explained what the online service was about (music or healthcare services) and described the features that it ofered to its users, without any positive or negative information about the quality of those features. The descriptions were the same within each domain (music or healthcare) except for the name of the assigned online service. To ensure that students were suficiently involved in the online services, in the description for the online music service, they were told that they can search for their favourite music songs, albums, and singers in its directories and stream them, and in the description for the healthcare domain, subjects were told that it ofers healthcare contents including information about stress, its symptoms and efects, and stress management techniques. These topics are considered to be relevant to student subjects (Kennedy et al., 2008; Misra & McKean, 2000). For verification, regarding relevance of stress management, we asked subjects in the healthcare condition about the importance of stress management for them (see Appendix C), using a 5-point Likert scale. Their ratings (Mean = 4.02, SD = 0.23) were significantly larger than the midpoint of 3 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which verified the relevance of stress management to them.

Next, subjects in the treatment conditions were shown four distinct PRs or NRs regarding their assigned online services (see Appendix B). This step was skipped for the control group (i.e., no-reviews condition). Next, subjects were asked to complete an online survey regarding their evaluations of PU, PEOU, trust, and attitude towards using the assigned healthcare/music online service. We used these data for manipulation checks, and to measure respondents attitudes towards the online service.

This design of experiment is in accordance with Helson’s (1964) adaptation-level theory that consumers’ judgements are based on their prior experiences, context, and a stimulus (i.e., our experimental treatments: NRs and PRs). In particular, by ensuring subjects’ no prior firsthand use experience with the assigned online service and by ofering the same description to all subjects within each domain, we maintained a common frame of reference for all subjects (in control and treatment conditions), against which we then captured the efects of our experimental treatments: NRs and PRs.

## 3.8 Preliminary analysis

Before testing the hypotheses, we conducted a series of preliminary analyses to ensure that statistical and methodological artefacts did not afect our measurements and analyses. Results demonstrated high reliability and good construct validity scores for the factors, no serious skewness and kurtosis were detected, and square roots of average variance extracted were larger than 0.7 and the corresponding correlations for each factor which indicated acceptable validity of the factors (see Appendix D for details).

## 4. Analysis and results

## 4.1. Model specification

The full regression model for testing our two hypotheses is depicted in equation (1).

$$
\begin{array}{c} A T T = b _ {0} + b _ {1} A g e + b _ {2} F e m a l e + b _ {3} N R + b _ {4} E X P \\ \quad + b _ {5} N R \times E X P + b _ {6} N F G + b _ {7} N R \times N F G \\ \quad + b _ {8} E X P \times N F G + b _ {9} N R \times E X P \times N F G \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

ATT, the dependent variable, is the magnitude of efects on consumers’ attitudes towards the online service. The efects of PRs and NRs on consumers attitudes difer in terms of both the direction of the efect and the magnitude of the efect. The direction of the efects alters because an efective PR is expected to increase the attitude, while an efective NR is expected to decrease the attitude. The magnitude of the efect (ATT in our model) is the absolute extent of change in the attitude as a result of PRs and NRs, regardless of their direction (increase or decrease). The negativity bias occurs when NRs have a significantly larger magnitude of efect on attitude, as compared to the PRs, when the direction of efect is removed. Therefore, to accurately estimate the extent of negativity bias, we need to estimate the magnitude of the efect of NRs on attitude as compared to the magnitude of the efect of PRs on attitude, after removing the direction of the efects (decrease and increase). To do so, we reversed the attitude scores for NRs to compare their magnitudes with those of PRs.

NR in our model is a dummy variable (1: NR; 0: PR) to estimate the extent of the efects of negativity bias $( b _ { 3 }$ coeficient) on ATT (it estimates the unique magnitude of the efects of NR on ATT as compared to those of PR). As mentioned earlier, we excluded the no-reviews (control) group from our data for estimating our model to enable the direct comparison of the magnitude of the efects of NRs with those of PRs.

EXP is a dummy variable for prior experience with the service domain (1: high; 0: low). The coeficient for its first-order interaction term with NR (i.e., NR × EXP), $b _ { 5 } ,$ indicates the moderation efect of prior experience on the negativity bias in the efects of online review content. This result is used for testing H1.

NFG is a continuous variable with scores for the need for cognition. The coeficient for its second-order interaction term (i.e., NR × EXP × NFG), $b _ { 9 } ,$ indicates the efect of need for cognition on the moderating efect of prior experience on negativity bias in the efects of online review content. Therefore, it is used for testing H2. Age and gender (Female dummy variable in equation (1)) are used as control variables.

Given the higher-order interaction terms in equation (1) which can change the interpretation of lower order terms, we used hierarchical (fixed-order) regression approach (Cohen et al., 2003; De Jong, 1999) to add the independent variables to our regression analysis step-wise: Step 1 only includes the control variables (i.e., Model 1); Step 2 adds the main efect of negativity bias (i.e., NR) to Model 1 (i.e., Model 2); Step 3 adds EXP and its first-order interaction term to Model 2 (i.e., Model 3), which is used for testing H1. Finally, Step 4 adds NFG and its first-order and second-order interaction terms to Model 3 (i.e., Model 4). The coeficient of second-order interaction term in Model 4 is used for testing H2.

## 4.2 Results

As depicted in Table 2, higher prior experience with the online service domain significantly increases the negativity bias in the efects of online review content on consumers’ attitude towards an online service $( b _ { 5 }$ = 0.66, p = 0.000; see Model 3 in Table 2). Therefore, H1 is supported. Figure 1 provides a more nuanced view of this moderation efect. While the negativity bias exists under both low and high prior experience conditions, its magnitude of efects on attitude is significantly larger in high prior experience condition (i.e., online music service) as compared to low prior experience (i.e., online healthcare service).

## 4.3 Post-hoc analysis: individual efects of NR and PR

Moreover, as expected, our results show that higher levels of need for cognition significantly strengthen the moderation efects of prior experience with the online service domain on the negativity bias in the efects of online review content on consumers’ attitude $( b _ { 9 }$ = 0.82, p = 0.000; see Model 4 in Table 2). Therefore, H2 is also supported. In particular, as depicted in Figure 2, when need for cognition is high, the level of prior experience with online service domain exerts a larger impact on the negativity bias in online review efects compared to when need cognition is low. These results fully support our hypothesis that higher prior experience with the online service domain and higher levels of need for cognition disproportionately increase the magnitude of the efects of NRs (as compared to the efects of PRs) on consumers’ attitudes towards an online service.

So far, the results provide strong support for the strengthening efects of users’ prior experience with the online service domain and need for cognition on the negativity bias. Nevertheless, negativity bias is a relative concept reflecting the comparative efect of NR relative to those of PR on consumers’ attitudes. To understand the individual efects of NR and PR at diferent levels of prior experience and need for cognition, we conducted a post-hoc analysis. To that end, we split our sample into eight subsamples based on 2 (high/low prior experience) x 2 (high/low mean-split need for cognition) x 2 (NR + control groups/PR + control groups). Then, we conducted eight separate OLS regressions (one for each subsample) to test the individual efects of NR and PR on the users’ attitude as compared to the control group in the same condition. We controlled for the efects of users’ age and gender in all eight regressions. As presented in Table 3, the results show that when prior experience with the online service domain is low, both PR and NR significantly influence consumers’ attitudes towards the online service, almost regardless of their level of need for cognition. However, when prior experience with the online service domain is high, PR loses its potency in influencing consumers’ attitudes, while NR maintains its significant efects.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Contributions to research

Leidner (2020) describes a theoretical contribution as “ . . . insights that are unexpected, surprising, or counterintuitive (e.g., nonobvious) . . . ”. One counterintuitive contribution of this study is showing that the asymmetrical efects of NR contents are more pronounced among the experienced and thoughtful consumers. However, the consensus in the prior online review research has implicitly or explicitly been that online review valence is a peripheral cue (a heuristic) that predominantly influences novice consumers, as compared to experienced and engaged consumers (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019; Wu, 2013). NRs have often been attributed to the negative emotional state (e.g., anger) of the reviewers, rather than the product/service attributes (Kim & Gupta, 2012; Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019; Wu, 2013). On this basis, the prior research on online review valence has argued that “negative information might grab the attention more easily, but attention alone does not guarantee the value of the information” (Wu, 2013, p. 977), and has concluded that “bad is not necessarily stronger than good in relation to the perceived value of consumergenerated reviews” (Wu, 2013, p. 978). The consensus based on these findings is that negativity bias does not influence experienced and thoughtful consumers’ attitudes towards the products/services, because as per dual-process theories (e.g., ELM), these consumers are expected to rely on the central route of information processing where perceived diagnosticity of information is the main driver of influence.

In this study, we have directly tested this idea by focusing on the valence of the content of online reviews. Contrary to the implied consensus discussed above, our findings show that the asymmetrical efects of NR contents are more pronounced among the experienced and thoughtful consumers. Our post-hoc findings show that NRs are highly influential for both groups of consumers (novice and experienced) and comparing their efects with the efects of PRs reveals the disproportionate efects of NRs on the experienced and thoughtful consumers. While both PRs and NRs can move the attitudinal needle for novice consumers, only the NRs can do so for experienced and thoughtful consumers. As such, our results present the negative information as an element of message diagnosticity in online review content and a central cue that disproportionately influence the experienced and thoughtful consumers’ attitudes. Our findings provide a new perspective on the importance of NRs and the negativity bias in the efects of online reviews.

Another important issue that our findings illuminate is the importance of the operationalisation of message valence in the online review studies. Prior studies have largely operationalised the valence based on the star ratings attached to the online reviews, with a one-star rating representing a very negative and a five-star rating representing a very positive valence (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Wu, 2013). In this paper, we implemented and tested the efects of valence of the content of the online reviews, while masking the efects of diferent star ratings (i.e., star ratings were not presented to the subjects). As a result, our findings show that NRs can be perceived as more diagnostic by experienced and thoughtful consumers, which brings to question the predominant assumption in the extant literature that negativity bias is a function of peripheral processing. Nevertheless, scholars should be cautious in extending these findings to contexts where valence is operationalised via star ratings only (e.g., Chevalier et al., 2018). This is because experienced and thoughtful consumers predominantly rely on the elaboration and scrutiny of the online review contents, while the star rating is a simple heuristic that is disproportionally attractive to consumers who do not engage in the scrutiny of the online review contents (Nazlan et al., 2018). NRs tend to include more concrete and detailed information (Yin et al., 2012), but such informational value could only be realised by the consumers who read the NR content (text) rather than those who only rely on the star ratings.

This emphasises the importance of how valence is operationalised in the online review studies, which in turn determines the type of consumers (e.g., experienced vs. novice) who are more likely to be afected by the negativity bias.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Recently, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) fined a company, Fashion Nova, a popular online fashion retailer, \$4.2 million for concealing NRs from being posted to its website. “Deceptive review practices cheat consumers, undercut honest businesses, and pollute online commerce”, said Samuel Levine, director of the FTC’s Bureau of Consumer Protection in a statement. “Fashion Nova is being held accountable for these practices, and other firms should take note” (D’Innocenzio, 2022). Thus, concealing NRs is not a viable long-term strategy to avoid their adverse efects on firms. Instead, firms should opt to understand the adverse efects of NRs and devise appropriate response strategies to mitigate these negative efects. In this paper, we show that the efects of NRs are stronger and more consequential for online service providers as compared to PRs’ because more experienced and thoughtful consumers are more likely to engage with and be influenced by NR contents. Moreover, the opinions of this group of consumers are usually more influential on others, given their prior experience and depth of scrutiny. Therefore, attending to NR in a timely manner and ofering efective responses to consumers’ concerns in these reviews in a way that addresses the core arguments in them can be important to manage their impacts. If a NR is not responded to or resolved in a timely fashion, it can influence some of the most important consumers and sparks “online firestorms” (Herhausen et al., 2019). Indeed, the timeliness of a firm’s responses to NRs improves the firm’s performance, whereas responses to PRs do not have such efects (Chung et al., 2020).

In this vein, prior findings that the investors seek information about the firm’s performance from online review contents are noteworthy (Tirunillai & Tellis, 2012). Investors often have higher prior experience with the service domain (they have industry focus) and have high motivation to elaborate on online reviews as informational cues for their financial decisions (Aggarwal et al., 2012). As such, they fit the profile of experienced and thoughtful readers of online reviews that can be significantly afected by negativity bias (Aggarwal et al., 2012). This further emphasises the importance of efective management of NRs.

Furthermore, the fact that the scarcity and infrequency of NRs (as compared to PRs) render them more diagnostic for experienced and thoughtful consumers suggest that some organisational response strategies, such as “Channel Change” or “Censorship” of NRs (Howell et al., 2012), can have adverse consequences by making them even more scarce and thereby more diagnostic for this group of arguably influential consumers. This can give the NRs even more potential for sparking an online firestorm (Herhausen et al., 2019).

## 5.3. Limitations and avenues for future research

We acknowledge several limitations in our study that can open avenues for future research. First, we assessed negativity bias using fictitious onesided reviews. A one-sided review contains only positive or negative content, not both. Therefore, an extension of our study is assessing negativity bias within the two-sided reviews, which include both positive and negative content. Second, while we controlled the volume of online reviews (four reviews) in our experimental treatments and masked the potential efects of aggregated star ratings (by not presenting it to our subjects) in this study, extant research shows the importance of these factors afecting consumers’ judgements towards products/services. Therefore, one promising area for the future studies is looking into the importance of the variability of these two factors in addition to the valence of online review content for the efect of negativity bias. Third, while the two online service domains (music and health care) diferentiate in the level of our subjects prior experience with them, there might be other diferences between them (e.g., utilitarian versus hedonic use). Future studies can investigate these additional diferences and their role in negativity bias in the efects of online review content.

Fourth, similar to prior experimental studies in IS literature (e.g., Yin et al., 2014), the subjects in our study were students, not actual consumers. We ensured their involvement with the online services in our experiment via a neutral description that we provided to them, as outlined in the Methodology section. While we believe this minimised possible adverse efect of student sample on our results, future study can validate our findings using non-student consumers of online services.

Fifth, we focused on the “consumption” side of the efects of NRs that may present only a partial picture of the negativity bias phenomenon. A complementary perspective on the efect of negativity is its influence on the “production” side. Prior research in social psychology has shown that negativity can improve the quality and efectiveness of persuasive messages and social influence strategies (Forgas, 2007). In the online review context, this would mean that the consumers with negative experiences about products/ services are likely to post higher quality online reviews. While we controlled for the efects of message quality in our experiments, this means that NRs may not be only perceived as more diagnostic by receivers, but they may also be of higher quality than PRs. The production efect of negativity on online review quality is an important area for future research.

Finally, while we focused on the valence of online review content (NR and PR) to investigate the negativity bias efects in this paper, there are additional ways in which the valence framing can be discussed. For example, Levin et al. (1998) proposed a typology of three diferent kinds of valence framing efects: (1) “attribute framing”, where a single attribute of a product/service is the subject of the valence manipulation, and the dependent measure of interest is the extent of favourability or unfavourability of the product/service; (2) “risky choice frames”, where discrete choices between a risky option and a riskless option of equal expected value depend on whether the options are described in positive (i.e., gains) or in negative (i.e., loss) terms; and (3) “goal framing”, where the issue is framed to focus on the potential to obtain gain (positive frame) or the potential to avoid loss (negative frame), while both frames can enhance the favourability of the issue. Prior research has shown that the asymmetrical efects of negative versus positive can appear in all three kinds of framing efects (Levin et al., 1998). However, to the best of our knowledge, the negativity bias research in the online review context has been predominantly focused on the “valence”, rendering the need for further investigation into the efects of the other types of framing in online review literature.
