# Exploring the value of IoT data as an enabler of the transformation towards servitization: an action design research approach

Kuan-Lin Chen , Astrid Lassen, Chen Li and Charles Møller

Department of Materials & Production, Faculty of Engineering and Science, Aalborg University, Denmark

## ABSTRACT

Servitization is a well-recognised phenomenon in the manufacturing industry. Business potentials are demonstrated mainly from the marketing perspective on servitization. However, increased digitalisation makes servitization interesting to other research areas. This paper applies the resource-based view (RBV) to argue how to value the data and explore its potential and impact on servitization. We examine the evolving role of information technology (IT), particularly of the Internet of Things (IoT), and the role of data in the servitization journey. This research is based on a longitudinal study of a product-oriented manufacturing company’s transformation towards servitization. The engagement with the company also includes the development of an IT platform that consolidates IoT and enterprise data, which enables managers to explore new data-based insights. Our goal is to investigate the organisation and in particular cross-functional efects of data-based insights and their impact on supporting strategic decision-making. The action design research (ADR) approach is applied to unfold the servitizing activities inside the organisation and extract the lesson learned into the joint domains of servitization and information systems research. First, we argued that exploring the value of data is becoming a more complex process. Rather than having one design perspective, technical concerns, business considerations, and operational purpose must be aligned to frame the value of data to support decision-making. Second, we discuss the emerging managerial implications, especially the role of IT in the transformation. A crossfunctional team is a prerequisite to exploring complex transformation projects. Thus, managers must recognise the problematic situation and bootstrap the available organisational resources. Finally, we provide empirical evidence on how scholars and practitioners can address mutual interests by applying the ADR approach to a transformation project. In summary, servitizing a product-centric manufacturing firm to have a digital services business model is a transformation involving complex social and organisational relations. This study advocates resourcebased analysis as a lens and demonstrates how IT and data as heterogeneous and imperfectly mobile resources create value for a product-oriented manufacturing company. However, a critical question remains on how to manage the expanding operational condition of a servitizing organisation and its business system to sustain its competitive advantage and win the digital market.

## 1. Introduction

Servitization is a well-recognised phenomenon in the manufacturing industry. One of the critical motivations for manufacturing firms to continue working in this direction is to have a business model with two revenue streams from business customers and end-users. Scholars and practitioners endeavour to identify how to create this opportunity. Many scenarios and models (Baines & Howard, 2013; Tukker, 2004) have been proposed to support firms in this servitizing trend. In general, scholars argue in favour of the value-adding perspective (Ulaga & Reinartz, 2011) for the physical goods in order to create a new business opportunity from services. This movement is rapidly developing when digitalisation and smart products are involved (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2014; Porter & Heppelmann, 2014).

Manufacturing firms, which have the strength to service their smart products in the market, are in a better position to engage their end-users at the downstream value chain. Data from smart products (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2014; Liozu & Ulaga, 2018; Porter & Heppelmann, 2014, 2015) with the Internet of Things (IoT)/Internet of Services (IoS) setup are often involved in subsequent discussions. This approach opens up a new business horizon for manufacturing firms, especially in the business-to-business (B2B) scenario. It is often discussed from a marketing perspective and seen as a new competitive advantage to boost firms’ revenue. Many benefits are identified through cases (Boehmer et al., 2020; Iansiti & Lakhani, 2014); however, the servitization is still considered a black box (Kamal et al., 2020). Scholars are especially concerned about the challenge regarding reengineering organisational processes and structures (Porter & Heppelmann, 2014; Ziaee Bigdeli et al., 2021); it remains a subject of high-level discussions (Bigdeli et al., 2017).

In this research, we applied Barney’s resourcebased view (RBV; Barney, 1991; Mata et al., 1995) as a lens to investigate manufacturing, taking information technology (IT) and data as the means to servitize its firm. According to Barney (1991) and Mata et al. (1995), a firm with IT competence can better create its potential resources with characteristics of value, rareness, imitability, and substitutability, which can be the resources to develop the competitive advantage. If so, it can lead to generating sustained competitive advantage. In our study, the IoT and its data implementations are seen as enablers to explore the black box of servitization from an operational perspective. We are looking for how to materialise the value of IoT and its implications in the servitization context. Action design research (ADR; Sein et al., 2011) was applied to look at the combination of these two abovementioned practical factors, and it was argued that data could be an essential resource of sustained competitive advantage (Mata et al., 1995). How can a new layer of information transparency be created from this enabler to support the strategic business moving forward downstream markets? How can this set of IoT data together with the enterprise resource planning (ERP) system be consolidated to enhance the business system in supporting managers’ decision-making? How does the business system of a product-centric firm explore this new data asset to support the business goals? What changes should be initiated in the organisation? These questions need to be investigated in conjunction with the management information systems and servitization domains. Figure 1 illustrates the high-level research background of this study.

This paper follows ADR’s research process (Sein et al., 2011), as will be elaborated below. The first three sections establish the framework, in which the research background, the servitization challenges from an operational perspective, and the research objectives are provided. The case selection and case background is then introduced, followed by a discussion of the research design, methodology, and data collection. The subsequent sections address the experimental results and reflections on the selected case. Our learnings and abstraction knowledge are then presented. The discussion focuses on the aggregate eforts to utilise IoT data and its implications to develop processes and organisations in the servitization context. In conclusion, we revisit the framework and suggest that scholars should pay more attention to the significance of utilising data in connection to organisation development. Last but not least, we propose that industrial researchers apply ADR to engage more closely with practitioners to explore the use context of innovative solutions. This leads to relevant contributions to the industry as well as rigorous academic results.

## 2. Research background

## 2.1. The complexity of servitization

Servitization is a well-recognised phenomenon in the manufacturing industry. Scholars from diferent disciplines define servitization from various perspectives (Baines et al., 2009). There is, however, a common agreement that servitization is the combination of services and products (Tukker, 2004; Vandermerwe & Rada, 1988). From a marketing perspective, servitization is often a business model or strategy (Baines et al., 2009; Kastalli & Van Looy, 2013) to engage with customers and end-users, while some operations scholars emphasise that servitization involves the process of firms’ transformation. The concepts “added-value services” and “monetizing data” (Eggert & Ulaga, 2002; Liozu & Ulaga, 2018; Reinartz & Ulaga, 2008; Ulaga, 2003) are often associated with digital services or advanced services (Baines & Howard, 2013) to shape the strategic moves. Data evidence (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2014; Liozu & Ulaga, 2018; Porter & Heppelmann, 2014, 2015) is also involved in this scenario. In such a case, the IoT/IoS is an enabler to this movement. Manufacturing firms that can service their smart products in the market have a better opportunity to engage end-users. To this extent, the complexity of servitization can be illustrated in Figure 2, resulting from the combined arguments.

This complexity is packed with many uncertainties and challenges (Bigdeli et al., 2017; Chen & Møller, 2019). Managers who are developing transformational business strategies struggle with pressure from both outsides and within the organisation. One particular managerial concern often mentioned is the servitization paradox. Scholars describe the situation “substantial investment in extending the service business leads to increased service oferings and higher costs but does not generate the expected correspondingly higher returns” (Gebauer et al., 2005). It results from several factors, for example, the lack of market data and service thinking (Gebauer et al., 2005; Kohtamaki et al., 2015). The managers, however, take such a bold approach because of the rapid development in the external business environment. A developed organisation must absorb the rapid changes in the business environment (prerequisite 1) and develop new capabilities to win markets (prerequisite 2). We see clear evidence in the literature; however, few studies have addressed the situation within firms and the organisational changes the firms might make to achieve such a vision (prerequisite 3).

## 2.2. The challenges of servitization

Instead of investigating the organisational changes, researchers have identified several servitization challenges (Baines & Howard, 2013; Story et al., 2017; West et al., 2018). Scholars argue that working on solutions to overcome servitization challenges is a hierarchical process. First, the firm needs to be equipped with hardcore technical competencies to develop value-adding ofers and establish attractive business models associated with the product-service ofers to engage with end-users. Manufacturing firms might require diferent competencies to carry out the two diferent types of prerequisites; however, the fundamental consideration is the same principle, which is “increasing the value” for customers (Eggert & Ulaga, 2002; Reinartz & Ulaga, 2008; Ulaga, 2003). Second, each product-service’s mutation is also confronted with the soft value of the organisation, such as organisational inertia and the organisational culture (Baines & Howard, 2013; Story et al., 2017). They might be constrained the servitizing development. This variety might lead to a specific structure and processes to deliver the impact on servitizing a firm’s performance (Kohtamaki et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2018) and its’ servitization development. In addition to these aspects, the transparency of information was pointed out (West et al., 2018). They recognised that “sharing of know-how” with cross-functional teams is generally problematic because the organisational culture often binds an organisation in the transforming stage. This culture is often shaped by the long development of the product-centric vision (Chen & Møller, 2019). Most product-centric manufacturing firms have dificulty in breaking through their existing model towards providing advanced services. In this case, up-to-date information was limited to the service perspective of the products and the value proposition aspect of the product-service oferings, which is positioned around product capability (Baines & Howard, 2013).

## 2.3. The research gap in digital-enabled servitization

This research recognises how the IoT and its associated data implementations create the complexity of servitization for manufacturing (see, Figure 2). It extends the current focus of the servitization literature, which captures the potential value of the IoT data from a business perspective. Scholars argue for a need to monetise data (Liozu & Ulaga, 2018) as a new business proposition to ofer a digital service and/or a new form of business strategy. Only a few studies have begun to look into the organisational perspective, including the impact of external factors, e.g., IT (Bustinza et al., 2017), and to highlight the importance of using data to improve the operational process (Huang et al., 2014). In other words, servitization scholars and practitioners are mainly interested in servitizing activities that deliver commercial results and value propositions that might boost the firms performance (Wang et al., 2018). Servitizing activities and processes of gaining performance knowledge to promote servitization are often overlooked (Chen & Møller, 2019). So, we argue that the servitization community should also pay attention to the other half.

In this research, we propose to take into consideration the influence of IT (Mata et al., 1995), operational processes to generate insights from data (Sharma et al., 2014), and further recognising the power of using data to enable decision-making (Lycett, 2013) as the resourced to sustain the competitive advantage (Barney, 1991). Two highly relevant concerns are part of the current trend in discussions in the IS community: (1) the impact of digitalisation on the IT department (Urbach et al., 2019) and (2) the role of data in supporting a business perspective (Winter, 2019). The former topic is associated with our third prerequisite (how the back ofice and the setup of the business system operate to support the development of the digital-enabled servitization [see, Figure 2]). In general, scholars debate the great responsibilities of the IT department in the digital transformation journey. They argue that IT expertise is becoming prevalent in today’s business organisation; it is time to rethink IT’s position in the business and the organisation. To be more specific, they further point out three major areas in this digital transformation; a datadriven transformation is one of them. In addition to this broader picture, the second topic, focusing on the role of data, is to explore the data-driven approach from a business perspective (Blaschke et al., 2018; Winter, 2019; Winter & Yang, 2019). In this approach, they specifically focus on the “black-box model of data, which represents the interactions between the system and its environment” (Dietz, 2006, p. 67).

In summary, Figure 3 presents the structure of this paper. We acknowledge the role of IT and data as enablers to drive both marketing and operations perspectives of servitization; however, in the research, we focus on the operations perspective. Instead of using IT and data to design value-adding digital services (Lerch & Gotsch, 2015; Vendrell-Herrero et al., 2017), we argue that IT and data can also drive operational development of servitization. More specifically, we emphasise the link between servitizing activities and processes and gaining performance knowledge to promote servitization (Chen & Møller, 2019). The roles of IT and data (knowledge from the IS literature) are seen as enablers to facilitate this part of the transformation.

By employing ADR in this longitudinal study, we aim to answer the research question concerning how to transfer IoT data into relevant insights and actionable decisions to support servitization development. To do so, we will build up this research from the bottom up. First, we will leverage the power of IT to explore how to transfer IoT data into relevant insights and develop an artefact and an intervention to facilitate this process. Furthermore, we will investigate what to change in the business system and at the organisation level while we develop and use this artefact and intervention with the internal stakeholders. Last but not least, we would like to see how ADR plays a role in a transformation project like this one. Figure 3 summarises the abovementioned ideas into the four subpoints: (1) the value of IoT, (2) business system design, (3) managerial implication, and (4) ADR, to which we aim to contribute our findings in joint IS and servitization literature.

## 3. Relevance of the research objective

This research recognises the digital trend of servitization development, its challenges, and its complexity (see, Figure 2). We intend to leverage the IS domain’s expert knowledge to bridge the servitization research gap. We aim to investigate servitization from the operational perspective (see, Figure 3).

To align the research gap with the practical perspective, we advocate the principle of the “black-box model” (Dietz, 2006, p. 67) to interpret the IoT data from connecting a smart product and its usage environment. Here, the usage environment means to map the installed smart product’s functionality from the end-users’ side and the smart products’ default functionality setup from the bill of material (BOM) in the enterprise resources planning system (ERP). Figure 4 illustrates this conceptual hypothesis. It takes IoT data as the enabler to explore the end-users market in order to create transparent information to mitigate, e.g., the servitization paradox (Gebauer et al., 2005), resulting from the lack of market information.

So, the end-users market can be understood through exploring the business insights from the collective IoT data. This means that the end-users market can be the same region as the one for installed and in-use smart products. Those in-use smart products are also an index that indicates how active the market is using such types of smart products. This particular feature is seen as the departure point of this research. Furthermore, we consider how the existing business system consolidates this IoT data set together with, e.g., ERP to support managers in understanding the emerging end-users markets. In the first step, we probe into the business system and examine how the business system responds to exploring this new data asset to support the business goals. If it needs any changes to fulfill this task, what might be the initial changes in the organisation? What impact might be at the organisation and process levels?

## 4. Case selection and case background

This study was carried out in a Danish manufacturing firm with 350 employees and business partnerships in over 88 countries. Its core business is to design and deliver high-quality and leading farm equipment in the global markets. In 2006, the firm began developing a software product for the developed markets and transforming its product systems into smart products for its end-users. However, this software product was defined as an auxiliary tool rather than a digital service at that time. It sold with the manual in a box delivery. This software product aims to visualise the data from the installed product system, which configures to one smart controller and connects with its relevant functional products and sensors. The main product function is to detect and monitor the production conditions of farmhouses on the end-users sides. Together with the latest developed IoT/IoS platform, the company’s smart product was created and kicked of its servitization journey in 2017. They have bigger ambitions: creating a digital service and establishing a smart, connected product concept in their business model. The business goal is to reach end-users with the digital service to create a second revenue stream. Besides, they can further utilise and promote the physical product system. In short, the smart product is the keystone of this extended business model. Figure 5 shows a summary of the business model for this selected case firm and its business vision.

In this stage, the firm can ofer substantial smart products and manage well the concept of “customer who wants us to do it with them” (Baines & Howard, 2013). In other words, this firm is at the stage of a maturing intermediate service. Although the company provided an intermediate service to end-users, the firm’s deeper organisational thinking and organisation structure (see, Figure 6) remained in the product development form. This intermediate service was developed as a software product rather than as a digital service. In this sense, the Research and Development (R&D) team took charge of the developments rather than collaborating with other functional teams (e.g., the global support and the IT team, see, Figure 6).

In addition to software product development, the R&D team extended its competencies beyond physical product development and stepped into the information system. Several competencies, such as data and server management, programming in the application development, and technical-orientation skills, were used during the development process of this software product. However, the rest of the organisation’s teams remained the same as the product-centric company. The firm adopted lean manufacturing principles to carry out its logistics mission: to serve its business customers (e.g., dealers) with the right (soft) products in the right place at the right time. In the product business, the dealer has the greatest responsibility for taking care of the endusers. The primary mission of the IT team is to look after the organisation’s business systems. They must provide corresponding information to the functional teams, so they can carry out the logistics mission. However, constructive business insights are rarely used in understanding the end-user markets as the reference in this transformation stage. Managers are thus paralysed in this brand-new situation, in which they do not have the relevant information to make a strategic decision. ## 5. Research design and methodology

In the previous sections, we explained the rationale behind this practical research and emphasised its novelty in bridging the servitization gap and IS development. This section further sketches the link within the IT focus and its implications in Figure 7. In addition to the first challenge in consolidating the IoT data together with ERP data, we also need to consider its implications on the servitization from the operational perspective (refer to Figure 3). So, the research focus is not only on an IT artefact itself. We should also consider the impacts of developing processes in the servitization context.

To fulfill these considerations, we selected ADR to carry out this research. Unlike ordinary design research (DS) projects (Iivari & Venable, 2009; Järvinen, 2007), ADR prolongs the investigation beyond designing an artefact (Sein et al., 2011) and emphasises the role of organisational context in shaping and deploying innovative IT artefacts. So, the value of an experiment extends to the ensemble artefact’s value as an intervention concept. Therefore, the value of this DS project is in discovering the intended and unintended organisational consequences while designing, building, and evaluating innovative IT artefacts (Sein et al., 2011). In this situation, the interactions between design and use are often the aha! moments that researchers value investigating. Therefore, we adopted this research method in iterative experiments and evaluated the lesson learned from the organisational changes when materialising our proposed artefact (see, Figure 7).

## 5.1. Research structure and data collection

In principle, this research was driven by the industrial investigation and a literature review related to servitization. Together, we formed the conceptual model, which took IoT data as an enabler to explore the endusers market to mitigate the lack of market data in servitization development. Figure 8 explains the iterative process for developing the relevance and rigour of this research. It represents the first part of the research structure design, which extracts from the first two stages of the ADR approach.

In this first stage, we intended to formalise the big picture of the transformation context through a literature review of works on servitization and the role of IoT. In addition, an industrial investigation was undertaken in the case firm to probe practical challenges. Three prerequisites were identified through the literature and the empirical investigation (see, Figure 2). In this research, we focused on prerequisite 3, the internal processes and resources restructuring in the back ofice (see, Figure 3).

In the second stage, three iterations (alpha, beta, and gamma) were undertaken to work with practitioners. Each iteration had three main activities: investigation, building intervention, and evaluation to consolidate IoT and ERP data sets and evaluate their impact on the operationalised perspective of servitization development. The evaluation narrowed to understand the strength and limitations of the existing business system and to observe how internal stakeholders interact with each other and with the existing business system.

## (1) Step one: investigate the available data source.

The first step of the PSP design process was to identify the reliable data source as a basis. What kind of data was needed? Where were those data located? These two questions needed to be investigated before we started to develop the PSP. Initially, the two types of data in the existing setup were referred to as the fragmented understanding of the product market and the current product-in-use markets of the smart products (see, Figure 11). Examples of the second type of data were order info, product price info, and service license info, which can be used to relate to IoT/IoS and ERP systems. However, those two systems were located in the R&D and IT departments, respectively.

## (2) Step two: data analysis and processing.

After identifying the data sources in the first step, we further focused on how we used the data and what kind of processes we employed to process those data. Our principle for designing the PSP was to consider how to leverage the organizational IT resources and capability. The goal was to avoid any unnecessary interference in the current setup of the business to maintain the consistency of this research case. Therefore, we needed to investigate the current IT setup, especially the ERP and IoT systems, to understand what kind of technologies were used for data management within the organization and how we could obtain the required data from the current system setup. As soon as we imported the real-time data into the consolidated database, PSP needed to be able to explore the hidden relationship between the ERP and IoT systems. The feature extraction conditions needed to be pre-defined for processing the later data querying. However, due to the data sources being diferent, the data structure of the same data may vary between ERP and IoT/IoS systems. Therefore, the data pre-processing (e.g., data normalization) was a necessary step to continue the subsequent development.

## (3) Step three: data storage.

In order to have better PSP performance, a consolidated database was requested. Therefore, the third process needed to focus on integrating the extracted data from both ERP and IoT/IoS systems. The key point of this step was to build the data mapping rules (e.g., link the end-user to the software license info.) to fill the gap between relevant data sets. The abstraction of this key step was also transferred to the design principle in the next section. Furthermore, the chosen data storage method needed to be compatible with the current infrastructure.

(4) Step four: user interface design.

As one of the key features of the artifact, a userfriendly interface was required. Several options were available from a technique perspective, e.g., a local website, a desktop application. To get a better experience and less training cost, the user interface of PSP was designed to be self-explanatory and easily integrated with the consolidated database designed in step 3.

Furthermore, the Microsoft Power BI was used to visualise the consolidated data in a tree model format (see, Figure 11). This framing technique was adopted from Howard’s decision analysis process (Howard, 1988). After structuring the problem, the consolidated data was categorised according to rules set at the metalevel. The tree’s starting point was the sold product information, which was extracted from the ERP dataset. All products sold were categorised into the smart product group and the non-smart product group by leveraging the consolidated data set. This process helped estimate the market distribution of activated smart products. To understand the potential market X (e.g., which market (group of product-users) was the target market to promote digital service X). Metamapping was used to link the smart products’ information with the product sold customers’ records. The goal of utilising these data links was to infer the potential of promoting the relevant digital service to the end-users if their device was suitable to run the coming digital services. Therefore, each specific smart product market can be classified into a corresponding digital service type. Such a process provided business information on the potential digital service markets for managers to decide what and how they should promote digital services. In this project, another six categories were identified by applying this logic, which presented the potential business for other digital services or/and their auxiliary products.

The internal stakeholders could “see” the invisible changes in redirecting the available information to understand how their smart products were potentially being used in the markets. In the three iterations, evaluations were handled in both steps of designing and developing the artefact and semi-structured interviews. Thus, we received feedback and evaluations in a dynamic environment. First, the artefact itself was evaluated mainly during the building intervention phase. Internal stakeholders involved in those three iterations (see Appendix 1, Table 3) contributed their domain knowledge and evaluated the developed artefact’s feasibility and usability. Two senior ADR researchers, one business developer, one digital product manager, one data specialist, one marketing manager, and the senior management team (two executives and IT managers) were invited to evaluate this invention’s rigour and ensemble value. We focused on the potential impact of adopting a long-term perspective of servitization development and its associated tasks (e.g., recognising the business potential related to in-use users or markets).

## 6.4. Emerging managerial challenges

There were three managerial challenges identified from the evaluation interviews of the three iterations (see Appendix 1, Table A3). The first issue was the innovation dilemma. Within the highly defined responsibilities in the existing organisational setup, people have limited themselves to innovative thinking and activities within their realm of responsibility. The IT department (the data specialist and IT manager) stated:

This ADR way of developing an integrated system is an innovative approach for them. Under the existing organizational setup, developing an IT project is very isolated. The functional team has its responsibility to propose a mature idea (with the evaluation of the business value) of IT solutions. Then, the IT team will participate in the earlier project preparation meetings to develop further the proposed idea. With this functional approach, however, the integration solution like PSP will never be formed because no one will take the cross-functional ownership.

The second concern was the development of servitization thinking. The direct users of the PSP (the business developer, digital product manager, and marketing manager) acknowledged this explorative information in understanding products sold, and the reference in-use status was very beneficial for glancing at the potential digital service market. However, it required efort to adopt this way of thinking, learn the implications of the explorative data, and think of how to take action based on the indications from the explorative data.

The digital product (service) manager stated:

This information is good for me to develop a sales tactic plan for the regional sales team and is also good to share with our dealers. Now we know which smart product systems are more actively used in what regions; we are able to take a more proactive approach to promote the upcoming advanced services.

The third issue was to overlook the value behind the data’s initial intention. The CSO provided an excellent statement about this:

Developing the digital service and exploring the markets are two pillars in servitization transformation. By exploring our own data (ERP and IoT), we get one step closer to knowing how to identify the potential markets and customers and what digital services associated with our smart products (advanced services) might need to be promoted. IoT data creates this new interdependence relationship between the market and R&D teams. We should explore it more and see how to create value for us and also for customers.

In most situations, the value of data was only recognised for its original purpose, and its value was exploited accordingly. That was one of the phenomena we discovered in the servitization-related literature as well. Scholars and practitioners focused on exploiting IoT data to design value-adding digital services (Lerch & Gotsch, 2015; Vendrell-Herrero et al., 2017) rather than using it to facilitate organisational transformation. However, we turned this thinking around and explored the IoT data for operational considerations.

## 7. Prescriptive learning

## 7.1. The design principle of the intervention: product-service platform (PSP)

To explore the emerging needs of product in-use information, we had three iterative engagements to investigate the problematic situation, experiment with the feasible solutions PSP, and evaluate the usefulness of the solutions. One of the practical challenges of developing the PSP was revealing the internal stakeholders’ unspoken expectations. Each functional team had a specific responsibility with respect to the company’s newly defined vision for servitization. Through the three iterative engagements (see, Figure 12 and Figure 13), the ADR team was able to understand the synthesis of those responsibilities (the specific use context). They further identified what types of information the managers and servitization task owners were looking for (Sein et al., 2011; Sein & Rossi, 2019).

In this case, the firm is a B2B manufacturing firm, which intended to go towards downstream markets to provide digital services to its end-users. Except for the logistics information, which could be extracted from the ERP system, many questions were not recognised anywhere in the existing business system. For example, how were the sold smart products used in the markets? Which end-user markets were most prepared for the upcoming digital services? To put these considerations in a central position, the marketing and sales experts had to answer those questions. Based on their experience, they knew how to recognise smart products’ in-use status according to the end-users production setup. However, those valuable insights were missing from the existing business system. This change in business considerations and operational intention was challenging for the business system, which was designed to be a product-centric setup and a transaction-based business.

In addition to the changes in business considerations towards downstream markets, the emerging data asset also challenged the organisation’s competencies to handle the complexity of the digital servitization situation. It seems there was no connection between the two heterogeneous data sets because they were created in diferent places (ERP and IT vs. IoT/IoS and R&D), and their initial purposes (e.g., integrated enterprise resources vs. part of the new product development) were also varied. One purpose was to fulfill the logistics mission, whereas the other was to be part of the functionality of smart products. However, if we looked at the data collected from the business perspective, each data collection point in the IoT/IoS platform indicated a particular type of smart product and its product-in-use conditions. This means that the IoT data set was able to express the conditions of the smart product’s use. When the two heterogeneous data sets were consolidated into one unified database, we could better understand the business potential. The idea was to compare the amount of the smart products sold to the smart products’ in-use, which constituted the business potential for digital services.

## 7.2. Emerging constructs to develop a business system towards digital-enabled servitization

In addition, to examine the system changes at the data level, we formalise the learning from the impact on the organisation’s content and process level in this section. Furthermore, we suggest how to revise a manufacturing firm’s business system to create a data-driven approach to support managers in facilitating organisational transformation. In this case, we explored the IoT data’s potential value to understand the end-users market and questioned how to handle this part of the data asset in a product-centric manufacturing firm’s business system. The three iterative hands-on experiments and feedback provided us with some indications of what might need to change in the business system in the back ofice.

The current business system of the manufacturing firm took the ERP as the foundation of the IT platform to accommodate the enterprise data assets and establish the operational processes to achieve business purposes. It also mainly focused on the logistics mission: to serve its business customers (e.g., dealers) with the right (soft) products in the right place at the right time. This emerging data set (IoT data), designed by the R&D team, was collected from a smart product system installed on the end-users’ side. The ownership of this data set, therefore, belongs mainly to the R&D team. Therefore, a barrier to recognising the integrated data value stemmed from this separation and isolative development. So, even though the IoT data could represent the “product-in-use” market/end-users market, there was no consideration given to exploring this part of the data asset. The managers seeking additional business information to adjust their tactica plan had no reference information, and they therefore lost their sense-making in this new situation. This evolving demand for the product-in-use business insights came from the decision to expand the business direction from a product-orientation to the dualfocus of a product- and (digital) service- business. Therefore, managers were faced with a new operational scenario. Figure 15 illustrates this expanding view of the operational condition.

In this case, we also argued that the internal business system also needed to be expanded to explore the IoT data. Doing so can accommodate more relevant collaboration processes, which support the expansion towards the service-thinking business.

Our interviewee, the CTO, said that

R&D had minimal interactions with the business development and market department (sales and service teams) in the past; it (silo thinking) leads (R&D) us to know little about how competitive our product system is in the markets and how actively our smart products are being used on the end-users’ side. Through the development of the IoT/IoS platform and PSP, we began dialogues with business development and market teams. We both eagerly seek an answer to what digital services (advanced services) should be created for what customers (end-users) and also for which market (the aggregation of the endusers) should we deploy our digital business strategy.

To embrace this practical evaluation, we argued that the emerging business system concept needs to handle the upcoming (heterogeneous) data assets. Therefore, the explorative business insights can be used to support managers in decision-making to develop tactical plans towards servitization. In addition, the system itself plays a role in moderating the diferent opinions among the functional team about silo thinking. This means that explorative business insights should be commonly recognised across departments. Lastly, the information explored from the emerging business system needs to recognise the incentive points, which managers can use to communicate with cross-functional teams efectively.

## 8. Discussion

This section presents the research reflection of this longitudinal study. We took IT and data as enablers to commence this journey in engaging a global manufacturing firm that embarked on a journey of servitization. The three iterative engagements provided a concrete learning platform to observe this particular scenario, where we aimed to shed light on servitization and IS literature.

In the following section, we will respond to our research points (refer to Figure 3). First, we bring up reflections to discuss the value of IoT data as an enabler to drive the organisational transformation towards servitization and its causality as explored in the internal experiments and analysis. Second, we discuss the emerging managerial implications from this longitudinal study. Finally, we provide our empirical evidence on how scholars and practitioners can address mutual interests when applying the ADR method to a transformation project.

## 8.1. IT and data as enablers to servitization development

In this section, we present reflections to discuss how the IT and IoT data as enablers drive the organisational transformation towards servitization and explore their causality as seen in the internal experiments and analysis. The intention is to present the descriptive knowledge from designing, developing, and implementing interventions. According to Barney (1991), value, rareness, imitability, and substitutability are the four empirical indicators which categorise potential resources’ characteristics that can be used to create a competitive advantage. If a firm can maintain such conditions for its resources, it can generate a sustained competitive advantage. We demonstrate that the emerging IoT data resource and wellestablished IT can be enablers of the vital resource to drive servitization development. As a result, the two prescriptive resolutions we extracted from the three iterations also have threefold implications on IS and servitization literature.

First, the research design brings a new perspective to look into the design of a servitization project for scholars and practitioners. It extends the discussion in the servitization literature that the data are mainly seen as monetising data to ofer a digital service or/ and form a business strategy (Liozu & Ulaga, 2018). We propose a set of design principles, namely the “product-in-use” model to design and develop a datadriven approach to utilise IoT data value and support the development of servitization from an operational perspective (Bigdeli et al., 2017; Chen & Møller, 2019).

This innovative thinking opens up a new angle for exploring data value. So, the engineering deliverables should not be limited to squeezing data value from the original defined context (mainly for fulfiling the business purpose). It should also consider environmental factors. Empirical data show that the seed of servitizing thinking can also be planted through the hands-on cooperating process of developing a purposeful IT artefact across functional teams.

In addition, we also advocate the latest blackboxing research, which pinpoints that the need for managing data exploration is of the essence in the digitalisation era (Winter, 2019). Scholars in IS and servitization domains should consider that digital-enabled servitization is one particular case in the digitalisation trend. So, by demonstrating more data-driven cases, we can better develop the data-driven mechanism sophisticatedly to support business organisations to accommodate the growing heterogeneous data.

Third, the PSP experiment results provide empirical evidence that the power of using IT and IoT data in the right defined context can generate valuable marketing insights and stimulate service thinking in the organisation. We recognised this contribution from the managers, who participated in the evaluation meetings (refer to Table 1 and Appendix 3.2.2), and from the digital product manager’s feedback. These exploitative business insights and managerial explanations are becoming unique organisational resources for firms to develop their value propositions. The more practice with this data-driven process, the better the unique sustained resource (Barney, 1991; Mata et al., 1995) that the firm possesses. In addition, the exploited customer-based insights (based on the product-in-use concept) from the consolidated data set are considered the reference KPIs to boost the development of servitization as well.

## 8.2. Managerial implications

## 8.2.1. IT department’s responsibilities and dilemma in transformation

To take this research one step further, one of the interesting questions we are able to answer is how the datadriven approach impacts the IT department and the servitizing organisation. IS and servitization literature gave us a very vague answer on this matter (Bustinza et al., 2017; Coreynen et al., 2017; Dinges et al., 2015). Most servitization studies argue and provide empirical evidence for using IoT data to create concrete results in the business model (Ju et al., 2016) and/or value-adding product-service systems (Rymaszewska et al., 2017; De Senzi et al., 2016) rather than focusing on the process of how to deliver the organisational changes. So, servitization is still considered a black box (Kamal et al., 2020).

In this study, we applied the ADR approach to engage with the selected firm and explore how the organisational setup and its existing processes might create constraints on the development of a data-driven approach. In other words, we tried to reveal how the IT department and cross-functional teams align their responsibilities in this digital-enabled servitization journey. We extracted our learning regarding the interaction between IT and other departments from the three iterative engagements.

At the start of this study, we struggled to create the product-service platform based on the behaviour of the IT department for the following reasons. First, the organisation was in the initial stages of making changes. In Mintzberg and Westley’ world, all changes begin with learning (Mintzberg & Westley 1992). Before the internal stakeholders (either the individual worker or managers) inquire about insights into emerging situations, no changes or shifting of their mindset will have occurred. That was why, after the innovation workshop, there was no obvious enthusiasm for adopting the data-driven approach. The only immediate needs were those of the business developer, who was willing to under stand more about the data and explore the potential benefits of using data assets to support his arguments in the business plan. The information he needed was what markets or customers are available for the applicable product systems, where the markets or customers are ready for the digital service. Second, problems existed because of the organisational setup and its inflexible process. The IoT/IoS was developed in the R&D department; therefore, the authorisation for managing and using these data assets belongs to the R&D department. However, the enterprise data asset was the ERP system, which was managed by IT. Without a strong business motivation (in this case, a need to seek information to support decision-making regarding understanding the product-in-use mar kets), it is hard to establish joint cooperation. Also, it is impossible to recognise the connection between the two heterogeneous data sets without a clearly defined objective. However, the intuitive thinking related to calculating the potential return on investments (the comparison scenarios in the number of customers purchasing the product systems and the software product vs. the number of product-users connecting to the IoT/IoS platform) inspired us to look for a more comprehensive method for exploring the value of data. It also led us to dialogues further with the R&D, marketing, and sales units. Therefore we argued that practitioners and IS scholars need to consider forming multi-disciplinary teams from the beginning to explore a complex transformation pro ject like this one in order to leverage a firm’s poten tials (e.g., data, domain experts knowledge, organisational resources). To do so, we can also explore the success factors or/and limitations in working on a digital-enabled servitization project. We hope to understand the limitations of applying the data-driven principle and seek more empirical evidence on aligning the responsibilities between the IT department and cross-functional teams.

## 8.2.2. Communication is the key to drive organizational changes

Our learning is composed of three iterative engagements using an ADR approach. The evaluations by the direct system’s users gave them a second chance to learn and grow from how they handled the previous changes. This provided us with a concrete learning platform to observe what should be changed at the organisational content and process levels. Ackof et al.’s (2010) systems thinking was applied to examine the changing environment. The evaluation focused on “the relationships (rather than unrelated objects), connectedness, process (rather than structure), the whole (rather than just its parts), the patterns (rather than the contents) of a system, and context (Ackof et al., 2010)”. Instead of answering the question concerning – what changes should be made in servitization (as most of the case studies did), we were able to answer the question of how to address emerging change because we dealt with those changes in the development processes.

We observed three main areas and experienced changes when the IoT implementation was at the centre of the servitization development. First, task owners needed to change their mindset regarding servitization tasks. This “used-to” operational routing logic determined how they communicated/inquired about information from other teams. In the development of servitization, task stakeholders should be more concerned about for whom they are designing this new digital service and should seek new processes and collaboration within the internal organisation. Also, they need to create a two-way communication. This means that if there is a change in the operational logic, the counterpart needs to be open-minded to listen to the diference and to understand this new way of thinking and behaviour to support the emerging request. This dynamic, positive feedback loop explains why organisational changes always take a long time to unfold. They happen only in iterative situations because people need to learn the emerging patterns and then experiment in response. Last, hidden deep under the manufacturing firm’s cornerstone of the business system is the master data (e.g., in the ERP system). The ERP system mainly addresses the perspective of product thinking because it serves the mission, tracks, and traces how these goods are produced and delivered to the right customers in the right place at the right time. However, to design and promote relevant digital services, we must understand the capability of smart products. This capability is about the physical functionality of the system and how the end-users employ it in their production environment. Later emerging needs make today’s business system incapable of supplying the necessary information to internal stakeholders, but we gain supplemental information from the available IoT/IoS data sets.

## 8.3. Reflection on the research approach

In general, the ADR approach provides an excellent guideline for conducting such a highly complex project to investigate the development and use of an (IT) artefact in an organisation (Sein et al., 2011). This methodology has helped us to scope this research as an interdisciplinary study. By adopting the ADR method, we can study one defined objective from two examination layers. It is unlike an ordinated case and/or DS study, which investigates from a single perspective. It, therefore, limits the findings to one perspective. In this research, we were able to collect first-hand data in the broader spectrum and assess the cause and efect regarding this emerging IoT development in the servitization context. Besides, the ADR approach provides a rigorous method to look into the practical problem and secure this research’s relevance to the industry.

However, we also argue that the ADR method can do much more. The approach is about reshaping the specific use context of the artefact within the organisation. The investigated goals of each iterative engagement might begin with a conflict between existing processes, but they end up with the innovative intention of using the constructed artefact. For example, in this case, managers also saw the PSP as a reference KPI system, which can be used to communicate with both business customers and end-users. Therefore, we argue that the completion of Sein’s and Rossi’s models should also involve constructing the use context (see, Figure 16), rather than being limited to testing the utility only. Therefore, the functionality and limitation of the artefact will be set within this boundary by the users.

To conclude this longitudinal study, the lessons learned contribute to the research in three directions: First, we argue that IT and data are enablers to servitization development. The more practice with utilising explorative data assets, the better the unique sustained resource (Barney, 1991; Mata et al., 1995) the firm possesses. Second, developing servitization is more than handling the challenges of developing valueadded (digital) services and innovative business models. More fundamental soft organisational changes must be built to support servitization tasks. Third, we argue that adopting the ADR method is more than investigating the development and use of an IT artefact in the organisation. The method also enables us to examine the evolving use context of the artefact within the organisation. However, to practice this ADR approach, researchers and practitioners need to be open-minded to exploring possibilities from the existing environment. Also, to develop a rigorous IT artefact for a specific context (e.g., in this case, servitization), cross-domain experts are needed to codesign the artefact, the processes, and deliverables.

## 9. Conclusions

Servitization is a well-recognised phenomenon in the manufacturing industry. Evidence demonstrates that business potentials stem mainly from the marketing research perspective on servitization. However, due to digitalisation and the implications surrounding smart products, researchers from adjacent fields are also looking into this phenomenon. This paper applied the resource-based view (RBV) to argue how to value IoT data and explore their potential and impact on servitization. We examined the evolving role of IT, particularly IoT, and data in the servitization journey. This study set a broader spectrum of research background (see, Figure 1) to demonstrate the double efects of IoT and data in servitization. It can be from both marketing and operational perspectives. However, through the engagement with ADR, we zoomed in on the operational perspectives and investigated these four points: (1) the value of IoT, (2) the business system design, (3) managerial implications, and (4) ADR in joint IS and servitization literature. Three iterative experiments were carried out with a product-centric manufacturing firm that had embarked on its digital-enabled servitization journey. As a result, two prescriptive resolutions and three descriptive reflections were extracted from the three iterative engagements and contributed to the joint domains of servitization and information systems literature.

To the IS knowledge, the prescriptive focus was on the design principle of the data-driven approach (the aggregated efort of IT and data) that put the “in-use” concept at the centre when considering exploring the data value to support the servitization development. Second, we elaborated on the impact at the system level, discussed how the business system might be reshaped and used to facilitate the organisation’s service thinking and promote operational development in servitization. This study provides empirical evidence that exploring data value to support decisionmaking in the digital-enabled servitization context is more complex and similar to a black-boxing datadriven exploration (Winter, 2019). Purpose-driven (e.g., the “in-use” concept, in this case; see, Figure 14) is becoming the central assessment point when aligning with the cross-functional business stakeholders and exploring the heterogeneous data sets.

In addition, we have provided insights to shed light on the servitization literature from an operational perspective. First, digital service is a foreseen type of soft product for manufacturing. It leads to the generation of additional heterogeneous data assets and new business models. It is vital to value these (heterogeneous) data assets for managers to support strategic managerial decision-making. Therefore, we argue that the firm can create a competitive advantage if it knows how to manage and transfer its growing data assets to become a great sustained resource pool (Mata et al., 1995) because the value of data is self-explanatory, as a resource with heterogeneous, hard-to-imitate, and rare characteristics. A firm having such resources needs strong managerial skills to explore the value behind its presenting forms to meet the business goals. We further evaluated this argumentation through iterative engagements with the case firm and provided our first-hand insights on the impacts at the organisational and process levels. Therefore, we argue that the operational condition (see Figure 15) will be changed. The key to this transformation is management skills in recognising the problematic situation and bootstrapping the available organisational resources, including using the explorative data insights to support the strategic decisions. So, the explorative data can be used as KPIs to measure the performance improvement in executing the servitizing activities along the transformation journey.

We hope to use this case to highlight the importance of interdisciplinary research between IS and servitization. Digitalisation is an inevitable trend in today’s business world; increasingly, B2B manufacturing firms are adopting the IoT/IoS idea to go downstream to their end-users. The challenge is how they can reform their organisation to be quicker and smarter. Thus, we suggest that future servitization researchers investigate further how IT and data impact the organisation with respect to both technical and sociological aspects; in other words, they should investigate how to use the socio-technical systems lens to evaluate what socio-technical conditions of a servitizing organisation might be changed and how to conduct such changes efectively. It will also help practitioners if there is a new method to learn the emerging patterns (Snowden & Boone, 2007) to quickly restore a complex operations environment to a complicated one. Thus, there is a need to investigate further how firms handle this new asset if they choose to take a digitalenabled servitization path. Furthermore, there is no doubt that all kinds of online data will arise, similar to IoT data. IS researchers shall look into how to restructure these parts of emerging data and processes to support managers facilitating the dynamics changes in the external environment, business system, and immediate problem space.

Although this single study with a clear set of propositions is an excellent representative case, it has limitations. In the future, we will continue to engage closely with the case firm to investigate further the impact of this data-driven approach and the firm’s performance. ADR might be another scientific approach for engaging with practitioners for researchers interested in studying the operations perspective of servitization. It creates a learning-by-doing environment for internal stakeholders involved in the project to share their expertise and co-develop the outcome to achieve the same goal. However, the ADR approach as a research method also has its challenges, which also merit further research.

## Appendix 2: Semi-structured interview questions (used in the investigation of the Gamma iteration)

Q1: How do you see the impact of servitization in the firm?

● What impact may the firm experience because of the critical elements of servitization: “Digital technology” + “Process” + “Data”?

Q2: How do you see the current teams’ setup in operationalising the development of (digital) product-service inte grated solutions in the firm?

● Expectations for further strategy change?

● Challenges in executing strategies to operationalise?

Q3: Servitization brings up the business relationship to go beyond box-selling. How do you see the setup of the current strategies linked to servitization? Have you experienced any challenges in carrying out those defined strategies? If so, please name some.

● What points of the changes in strategies you have considered and what actions have you taken?

Q4: How do you see that the firm’s culture has changed because of the extension of the business orientation from the development of “(digital) product-service integrated solutions”?

● How do you address the changes in this core value?

● What are your opinions on the capability of the relevant functional teams in performing product-service excellence? What would you do to create a better-fit environment (to gather more relevant information, create an eficient process, and have better communication) to operationalise the currently defined strategies?

Q5: What challenges have you experienced in operationalising because of servitization? What changes have you made in leading your teams?

Q6: What is your opinion about the role of the current enterprise business system (in the organisational procedure, communication model, and IT system)? Is it able to carry out the relevant data/information generated from the new activities (e.g., design, support, and promote the advanced service)?

Q7: From a strategy perspective, servitization targets soft values such as service and data, to earn potential profits via helping customers improve their productivity. What are your opinions about this strategic movement? What are your opinions about the value of data?

Q8: Servitization means to investigate “product-service excellence”. How do the current performance system (e.g., KPIs) and managerial tools help?

## Appendix 3: The log of Three Iterations

## Appendix 3.1. The Organizational Impact of External Factors: The Alpha Iteration

The objective of the alpha iteration was to understand how external factors, such as the development of the IoT and IoS, impact the organisation in the context of servitization. The project began with a five-full-day innovation workshop with participants across-functional teams (see Appendix 1, Table A2). The goal was to get to know the competitive business environment the firm was facing and then to initiate a feasible and envisioned advanced service ofering that provides added value for their end-users. The service ofering can also secure the business as the market leader in the industry. Design thinking was used to facilitate this conceptualisation process.

The ADR researcher participated in the innovation workshop to get an overview of the problematic context and to initiate a potential direction for cultivating servitization development. Thus, the role of the ADR researcher was to observe the organisational development atmosphere, participate in the discussion, and look for hidden information links to propose a mechanism for probing and sensing possible directions for expediting development. In addition to the practical tasks, the ADR researcher was to investigate the potential misalignment of servitization development between the research community and practitioners.

The alpha version was developed with the business developer after the innovation workshop. The goals at this stage were to extract the available business insights from the installed smart product systems (the data from the first version of the IoT platform and experts’ knowledge). The first attempt at exploiting the information was to indicate the number of potential end-users, who were potentially “in-use” products. The outcome of the alpha version was to present a sort of conceptual method to bridge the critical value of data from ERP and IoT platforms. The explorative data were carried out in the Microsoft Excel spreadsheets. The explorative data value from this version was used to set up the initial business plan. So, the business developer can have the evidence-based data to pitch a high-level servitization plan to executive oficers. The initial design essence of the artefact, the product-service platform (PSP), was examined and evaluated with the business developer and senior managers. They were responsible for developing the servitization tactical plan (see Appendix 1, Table A3). It was also confirmed that “in-use” value-adding information was missing from the current enterprise system relative to making confident decisions. Managers were looking for this information when struggling to transform.

## Appendix 3.2. The Organizational Impact of the Data-Driven Approach in The Servitization Content and Context

Through carrying out the alpha iteration, ADR researchers also gradually observed conflicts within the organisation. During the innovation workshop, the European sales director stated, “We are a system provider; we should stay where we are. Such innovative product-service oferings would not bring us extra profits but consume our resources”. Some strong believers would not support broadening the core competencies that the firm had had for years and did not believe this type of innovation could bring financial profits. To understand these concerns arising from the middle and bottom levels of the organisation, we conducted two iterative engagements: beta and gamma, in which we intended to use the exploitative IoT data to support managers’ decisions in developing digital servitization. Through the development of IT artefacts, we learned what to change and how to deal with the organisational changes at the process level and how to evaluate its contributions together with the management team.

## Appendix 3.2.1. Beta Iteration

The ADR researcher participated in orientation meetings to develop a comprehensive understanding of the case company’s existing working process and organisational culture. The purpose of attending these orientation meetings was to get comprehensive knowledge of the history of the firm, the available smart product systems and configurations, the present organisational tactics, the technical information infrastructure, and business processes (see Appendix 1, Table A2). In this iterative engagement, two other groups from the case company participated and a group of graduate students, who had been invited to work on the beta version. The goal of this iteration was to investigate potentia changes in the organisational environment when trying to consolidate two heterogeneous data sets. In addition, we aimed to gain a comprehensive view of how managers adopt value-added information from the exploration of IoT data (which we later called the product-service platform) to make tactical decisions about promoting the development of advanced services and associated business ideas.

During the orientation meetings, the ADR researcher observed that news about the firm servitizing was spreading within the organisation. However, senior management recruited only a small project team to work on this topic. Thus, there were two attitudes towards servitization. One attitude was held by those who would not be immediately impacted by the servitization project and doubted the rationality of expanding the business direction towards digital services. They also mistakenly thought that the firm would lose its core strengths and move its business focus to digita services. A prominent voice of resistance rose up among the middle management teams. A one-day servitization workshop took place to mitigate such noise, and an expert was hired as a consultant to preach the rationality of this movement, its benefits, recommended areas of change, etc. The other attitude was held by those in the operations cycle of the servitization project, who understood the need for the transformation but struggled to find reasonable grounds for their actions. For example, the digital product manager found it hard to seek further operational supports when he did not know which markets or customers had the greatest potential for the ongoing development of digital services. He was aware of his job responsibility; however, he had no supporting evidence for further operational support. It was hard to maintain the cost-benefit under many uncertainties due to the limitation of the existing enterprise information assets.

To overcome these barriers, the ADR teams tried to consolidate two heterogeneous data sets (ERP and IoT/IoS) based on geographic information, where the geographic location was identified by the IP address and customers’ delivery addresses. In this approach, product-centric thinking was still adopted. Structured query language (SQL) was applied to connect the two data sets; then, Microsoft Power BI was used to present the exploitative business insights. Four evaluation meetings were arranged with managers. The purpose was to investigate the data value of those exploitative business insights, extracting from the consolidated data set (see Appendix 1, Table A3). We got a better understanding of the information demands of the managerial and operationa aspects. In addition, the necessary (invisible/not yet digitised) knowledge, the limitations of the existing organisational setup, and the enterprise system (e.g., ERP) were revealed. In summary, the ADR team members were able to state the benefits, challenges, and limitations of adopting a data-driven approach under product thinking.

## Appendix 3.2.2. Gamma iteration

The third iteration was set up after a series of semistructured interviews with functional managers and executive oficers (see Appendix 1, Table A2). The study was intended to explore the operational paths of developing digital servitization. The McKinsey 7S Framework (Waterman et al., 1980) was adopted in the interviews to understand the synergy created from each union within the organisation at this development stage of servitization. Nine managers were asked eight pre-defined interview questions (see Appendix 2) between June and August of 2018. Each semi-structured interview lasted from 1 to 4 hours and had 30 hours of interview data in total. The interviews were recorded for later data analysis purposes. Interview insights into the interactions between the 7S McKinsey aspects, not an individual aspect itself, were the goal. Thus, the questions had at least two facets. Each perspective was generally applied to more than one interview question to verify the consistency of the managers’ operational insights. The minutes of each interview were summarised, and the document was emailed to each interviewee. Interviewees were asked to confirm the accuracy of the data collected (Yin, 2014) to ensure the efectiveness of the communication.

The chief sales oficer (CSO) provided a good summary of the investigation: “There is no doubt about taking this servitizing direction; however, transparency about information, process, and organization platform needs to be devel oped”. Several managers also addressed that they used trialand-error approaches to learn about this emerging situation. A European sales director stated that he did not have enough valuable information to be the reference for adjusting strategic actions. The managers (CTO, CFO, global support manager) also experimented with which skills were necessary and which could be outsourced in terms of performing product-service-related tasks. Overall, the interviewee recognised why they were transforming the firm but had little knowledge of how and what to do. With an understanding of the environment and available resources, the ADR researchers designed the third iteration of the study. This time, a researcher from the computer science domain was recruited to the team. We aimed to materialise the identified invisible knowledge (from the beta experiment) to bridge the missing data links between two heterogeneous data sets, from the ERP system and the IoT/IoS platform. A platform called the product-service platform (PSP) was introduced. It contained three key results: (1) a web-based platform, providing an interface to materialise that invisible knowledge; (2) a database, which consolidated the ERP and IoT/IoS data sets; and (3) a PSP business intelligence platform, where the penetration of “product-in use” was presented as the potential market of the digital service.

The data sources for building such a PSP platform came from the ERP system (e.g., sales order table, customers information table, etc.) and the IoT platform (e.g., a model of device). To deliver a solution that could be smoothly applied to the organisation’s current IT infrastructure, we investigated two systems’ setups, ERP and IoT. Microsoft SQL Server and .NET web application were identified to support the back-end data management and user interface, respectively. Thus, we selected the Microsoft SOL Server as our PSP database for managing consolidated data sets. Asp. NET is used to design and develop the PSP platform as a web application with a user-friendly front-end interface. Therefore, the internal stakeholders (functional users and managers) can easily access the PSP. The preliminary technical development was described in an earlier publication (Chen et al., 2019). In principle, this platform can check a customer/market’s readiness for installing the digital service based on how well it equips the product system, components, and software products. Figure A1 illustrates an overview of these relations.

In addition, to sort out the diferent types of product systems fitted for the specific digital services, we presented the four questions below, including the idea of the “in-use” concept, in the operational pages of the PSP platform. Therefore, internal stakeholders easily recognised the critical conjunctions of physical and software products and how one became a smart product and then a smart, connected product in which smart product family.

Q1) If the product was sold with the essential local area network (LAN) devices, would the product itself become a smart product and have the capability to be the hub to attach the upcoming digital services (advanced services)?

Q2) What is the assumption of the (end-users’) production scenario of using one product system?

Q3) What type of software licence is compatible with using one smart product?

Q4) What type of software licence is needed for the compatible upcoming digital service?

The aim was to examine the product attributes from the perspective of how the smart product could be used, rather than what it was designed for and to whom it could be sold. With this mapping idea (see Figure A2), we were able to close the relationship between product and service and establish an eligibility loop between the product and (digital/advanced) service.

To visualise those thinking processes, the PSP users were given step-by-step guidance in the platform to link the “inuse” products from the IoT dataset to the sold smart, connected products from the ERP dataset. A web application using ASP.net(C#) was designed as a framework. The PSP web application had three core layers (see Figure A3): the data process layer, the data storage layer, and the data visualisation layer.

In the third iteration, we completed the design essence of the PSP and evaluated this ensemble artefact in two layers: a) technical concerns and b) organisational changes, and c) impacts on the process and content. Seven meetings were arranged with the relevant internal stakeholders (see Appendix 1, Table A3) to receive comprehensive feedback on this data-driven creation and its impact on the organisational changes.
