# BIBGT: combining bibliometrics and grounded theory to conduct a literature review

Isabelle Walsh <sup>a</sup> and Frantz Rowe<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Campus de Sophia Antipolis, Digitalization, SKEMA Business School, Sophia Antipolis, Valbonne 06902/Université Côte d'Azur, France; <sup>b</sup>Universite de Nantes, IAE Nantes- Economie et Management, Nantes 44035/SKEMA Business School, France

## ABSTRACT

In the current context of scientific information overload, we propose a method combining bibliometrics and grounded theory to conduct literature reviews that have a descriptive, understanding or explanatory purpose. This overall inductive combined method, which we name BIBGT (BIB = Bibliometrics; GT = Grounded Theory) provides a powerful instrument for researchers. This instrument has been made more readily accessible by recent technological developments, and scientific advances in the field of bibliometrics. Notably, BIBGT helps grounded theorists identify colleges of thought of the field being reviewed, and representative texts to be reviewed in depth, in each college; it improves the trustworthiness and eficiency of reviews. BIBGT helps bibliometricians achieve in-depth analysis and interpretation of their findings. For all researchers, BIBGT can nurture the emergence of novel insights towards indepth description, understanding or explanation of a core concept, theme, or research domain.

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 10 September 2019 Accepted 31 January 2022

KEYWORDS Bibliometrics; grounded theory; literature review; scientific information overload; systematicity; transparency

We detail the four steps of this flexible method, providing essential elements and some guidelines as well as an illustration, to help other researchers who wish to apply it.

## 1. Introduction

The number of scientific publications has been increasing exponentially (Bornmann & Mutz, 2015) and the number of relevant texts required to conduct a literature review is often too great for the human mind to cover comprehensively, in depth and in a short period of time (Larsen et al., 2019). Thus, traditional narrative reviews may not be considered trustworthy and are increasingly difficult to publish in top journals because their lack of transparency and systematicity is becoming an increasing issue in the current context of “big bibliographic data” (Jung, 2015), and scientific information overload.

To respond to this situation, new types of literature reviews – some emphasising comprehensiveness, others focusing on depth – have been proposed (e.g., Leidner, 2018; Paré et al., 2015; Rowe, 2014; Sylvester et al., 2013; Templier & Paré, 2018; Wall et al., 2015). In addition, diverse methods for conducting diferent types of reviews have flourished in response to the need to increase systematicity and transparency and, thus, reinforce trustworthiness (Templier & Paré, 2018). All literature reviews require a high level of systematicity, with the exception of those aimed at understanding, which may be less comprehensive (Paré et al., 2016; Rowe, 2014). However, when thousands of texts match the keywords or queries entered in databases, it is challenging to produce a review that can be termed systematic. Some authors (e.g., Templier & Paré, 2018) also strongly advocate transparency, emphasising the need for high quality explicit reporting of the review process in order to facilitate the reproducibility of the method applied.

Therefore, it is important to develop approaches, methods and/or guidelines that can increase trustworthiness in what we already know about a topic, guide the interpretation of results, and help focus more rapidly on the in-depth understanding of the topic. There is a need for reviews that achieve both depth of insights and a good level of systematicity in describing a phenomenon, or its theoretical understanding or explanation. While recent eforts have looked at these issues separately, we aim to consider both simultaneously in this present work.

Some researchers, while conducting literature reviews, have turned to bibliometrics, which uses citation patterns, and enables a great number of texts/authors/journals/etc. to be clustered into groups, and graphically mapped. Other researchers have turned to grounded theory (GT; Glaser, 1978; Glaser & Strauss, 1967), which involves the systematic generation of theory from data that have been systematically obtained (Glaser, 1978, p. 2). In this paper, we show that these approaches can be complementary. We propose a novel method, which we name BIBGT, with a four-step iterative workflow: (1) Defining boundaries; (2) Treating bibliographic data; (3) Clustering, mapping and interpreting; and (4) Synthesising. This method uses both bibliometric and corresponding visualisation techniques (primarily for eficiency, objectivity and comprehensiveness, transparency, and interpretation guidance purposes) and GT (primarily for sense-making, to fulfill the need to interpret and provide sensitivity to the level of depth or insights needed to enable interpretation). While GT is based on a systematic search for patterns in data and conceptualisation from these data, bibliometrics can help highlight patterns in bibliographic data. We show that combining and interlacing the two methods at each step of the review process compensates for the limitations of each when applied separately – see, Wolfswinkel et al. (2013) in relation to GT; and Zupic and Cater (2015) in relation to bibliometrics.

A few published reviews (Kim & So, 2022; Renaud et al., 2016; Walsh & Kalika, 2018; Walsh & Renaud, 2017) have used approaches that appear to combine bibliometrics and some GT precepts, mostly in non-transparent, nonexplanatory, tacit ways. However, although these studies are very precise regarding the bibliometric side of their research, they mostly only mention GT, if at all, “en passant” in the discussion section (e.g., Walsh & Renaud, 2017), or use some GT precepts solely as a means to code textual contents (e.g., Kim & So, 2022). Thus, to our knowledge, previous research has neither fully described this type of combined approach in such a way that it can be mastered and applied, nor highlighted its advantages and discussed its relevance for various types of literature reviews. Our paper intends to fill these gaps.

The article is organised as follows: Section 2 briefly describes diferent approaches to conducting literature reviews; Sections 3 describes the BIBGT method, proposes guidelines for applying it, and presents an illustration of its use; Section 4 discusses its added value, along with its limitations and avenues for future research. The article finishes with a brief conclusion.

## 2. Diferent approaches to conducting literature reviews

In this section, we briefly present recent typologies/ taxonomies of literature reviews, and some methods proposed for conducting reviews. We introduce bibliometric techniques as helpful tools to support the conduct of literature reviews and GT as a method for rigorous literature reviews. We then highlight how combining bibliometrics and GT to conduct literature reviews, can help address the limitations of each of these methods when applied separately and as proposed in the literature.

## 2.1. Literature reviews

There are diferent types of literature reviews. Among various recent typologies for reviews (e.g., Leidner, 2018; Schryen et al., 2020), Rowe (2014) proposes a typology based on four dimensions: (1) the goal of the review with respect to theory, which distinguishes those reviews that merely describe, using categories without theoretical novelty (reviews for describing), those that seek to construct a theoretical explanation of what we think we know (reviews for explaining), those that help us understand what we do not know well enough (reviews for understanding), and those that aim at testing a theory or model (reviews for testing)<sup>1</sup>; (2) the breadth of the review; (3) the systematicity of the review; and (4) the argumentative strategy of the review. Thus, while some reviews focus on theoretical explanation or understanding, others are more descriptive or oriented towards theory testing (Rowe, 2014; Templier & Paré, 2018). Descriptive reviews are often criticised for not providing an in-depth synthesis of the relevant literature. Reviews for theory testing require the utmost level of systematicity, but are significantly less challenging than other types of reviews in terms of interpretation (Rowe, 2014).

While recognising that there are no universal quality criteria for all types of literature reviews, it may be argued that systematicity and transparency should always apply, but must be contextualised depending on the type of review being conducted (Paré et al., 2016).

Systematicity is not understood as designating specifically what has been termed a “systematic literature review” (Paré et al., 2015). A systematic literature review (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007) requires the application of a very specific method, which may appear overly restrictive (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015; Chiasson, 2015); notably, this method involves a systematic quality appraisal of all material in a dataset, something which may not be required in all types of reviews (Templier & Paré, 2018). We propose to understand “systematic” as a qualitative rather than classifying adjective (Okoli & Schabram, 2010; Paré et al., 2015). Based on the definitions proposed by Borko et al. (2007) and Valanides and Charoula (2008), systematicity is defined “as a disposition towards organized, methodic, and orderly inquiry that uses various methods and processes to search, screen, assess, analyze and interpret relevant information” (Paré et al., 2016 p. 496). The contextualised and flexible application of this definition means that “while not all reviews need to be equally systematic, they all require a minimum level of systematicity” (p. 496). The systematicity of a review evolves on a continuum that induces richness, reproducibility, trustworthiness and utility (Simsek et al., 2021).

Beyond systematicity, Paré et al. (2016) also advocate transparency, which they present as the counterpart of systematicity. Transparency relies on the quality and explicitness of the reporting of the review process, and facilitates the repeatability of the methods applied, i.e., based on the information presented, identical methodological steps and protocols can be used by others (Cram et al., 2020). Although we agree with Leidner (2018) that over-emphasising transparency may be detrimental to creativity when performing reviews, we argue that some rigour in reporting as well as execution is always beneficial.

Collecting documents and synthesising the information they contain are steps common to all literature reviews and epitomise the genre. The resulting data then need to be interpreted and synthesised before one can identify gaps or biases, and propose avenues for future research (Rowe, 2014; Schryen et al., 2020). Whether one stops at the synthesising stage, goes further and identifies gaps, or further still develops research avenues and/or some broad or specific theorising, depends on factors such as motivation, relevance and the contribution sought (Leidner, 2018). However, in all cases, experts recognise literature reviews as involving an iterative process, between data collection and interpretation. Reducing the process to a simple cycle that includes one stage for data collection, one for screening, one for reading, memoing and extracting data, and one for interpretation and writing may be an illusion. This is because interpretation plays a crucial role, often leading the researcher to revise the boundaries and/or the purposes of their review, obliging the whole cycle to begin again (Leidner, 2018). Thus, our underlying assumption in this paper is that a literature review is inevitably iterative in nature, with multiple feedback loops between the diferent stages.

The growth in production of scientific knowledge (Jung, 2015) means that pursuing both comprehensiveness – a component of systematicity – and variety of sources (e.g., journal articles, books, conference papers, reports) – a consequence of breadth – can be dificult in conducting reviews. Whether to integrate grey literature (less mature from a scientific viewpoint, but not necessarily of lower quality) is always an important decision to make. This can be relevant for all types of literature reviews, but particularly so for two types of review for understanding (Templier & Paré, 2018): (1) critical reviews, which benefit from moving away from ideologies that may prevail in top journals and include original ideas that appear in outlets such as conferences (Wall et al., 2015); and (2) scoping reviews, which emphasise new research avenues and may be relevant in venues for less mature work. Reviews for describing, explaining and testing all require very systematic coverage – even exhaustive in the latter two cases – which may require consideration and a thorough quality assessment of the grey literature (Templier & Paré, 2018).

Combining systematicity (notably, the quest for comprehensiveness and, possibly, quality assessment of sources depending on review type) and iterative cycles in the review process can be a daunting task (Okoli & Schabram, 2010), particularly when the review is transdisciplinary (Rowe, 2014). Numerous techniques have been used to improve the eficiency of the iterative cycles of literature reviews – these include linguistic and machine-learning techniques (Larsen & Bong, 2016; Larsen et al., 2019; Prester et al., 2021) and bibliometric techniques (Zupic & Cater, 2015) which we focus on here.

## 2.2. Using bibliometrics to conduct literature reviews

Given the current proliferation of scientific information, some researchers have turned to bibliometrics to improve the systematicity and eficiency of literature reviews (Zupic & Cater, 2015). These authors propose a synthesis of bibliometric methods for management scholars.

Bibliometric techniques and analyses were first developed by De Solla Price (1963), Garfield (1963), and Pritchard (1969) for investigating how scientific works relate to one another. These techniques help to highlight patterns in bibliographic data and, when conducting a literature review, support the speedy identification and prioritisation of influential papers (Wagner et al., 2020).

Bibliometric techniques are based on statistics, and allow for the reproducibility of results i.e., an independent party can reproduce results, when using the same data (Cram et al., 2020; Peng, 2011). Like other statisticsbased techniques, bibliometric techniques can be applied within an overall confirmatory, or exploratory, approach. They provide objective means for describing and classifying existing scientific production; one of their greatest strengths is the identification of subfields within a broader field through the analysis of bibliographic notices of published material (Zupic & Cater, 2015).

Most bibliographic techniques rely on cooccurrences of words, authors, cited references, etc., and cluster analysis or related approaches (e.g., exploratory factor analysis). These exploratory data analytic methods use common characteristics to cluster the units analysed (e.g., words, texts, journals, authors) into groups, to help identify publication patterns in the investigated field. In bibliometrics, commonalities used to efect this clustering are defined by bibliometric indices, for instance, co-citation indices (i.e., the number of times two references/journals/ authors are cited together), or document bibliographic coupling indices (i.e., the number of references common to the bibliographies of two documents). For a detailed description of main bibliometric techniques, see, Zupic and Cater (2015).

Hence, one of the main purposes of bibliometrics is to explore a field, and identify its “invisible colleges” (Crane, 1972; De Solla Price, 1963) – for example, authors or articles that share some hidden commonalities. Based on citation and co-occurrence patterns, these invisible colleges emerge through the grouping of authors, articles etc. into clusters. This clustering can be graphically mapped to give an overall picture of the field that helps interpret the bibliometric results (see Appendix A for examples of such maps). The “nodes” (usually represented by circles) in these maps can be cited references, citing documents, authors, etc., depending on the bibliometric analysis carried out; their relative size indicates their significance. Notably for literature reviews, reference cocitation analysis (Small, 1973) has been used to identify main schools of thought and seminal papers in each school (e.g., Córdoba et al., 2012), and document bibliographic coupling analysis (Kessler, 1963) has been used to identify main current research themes in an investigated field (e.g., Walsh & Renaud, 2017). In these instances, the node size is proportionate to the number of citations (raw or normalised), thus highlighting visually the most significant texts in terms of citations. Other bibliometric techniques such as coauthorship and keyword co-occurrence analysis may be used to assess collaboration and evolution of topics (e.g., Khan & Trier, 2019).

Zupic and Cater’s (2015) ground-breaking synthesising work is, indeed, “a meaningful single-source reference for management and organization scholars interested in using bibliometric methods” (p. 1). These authors provide an overview of bibliometric techniques and a linear five-step workflow: (1) Research design – Definition of the research question and possible techniques to be applied to address the question, (2) Compilation of data – Selection of bibliographic database and collection of document bibliographic data, (3) Analysis – Choice of software, data cleaning, production of similarity matrices, and identification of subfields (4) Visualisation – Choice of method and software, and (5) Description and interpretation of results. The authors caution against applying bibliometric techniques in a mechanistic way, arguing instead for adaptation and customisation, depending on the purpose of the review. When interpreting their results, researchers are warned to avoid preconceptions and remain open to patterns that emerge from bibliometric analyses, which may not fit with their existing knowledge of the field.

To apply most bibliometric techniques, and to reduce the number of texts/authors/journals/etc. to be investigated in depth, researchers have to choose relevant citation thresholds (or cut-of points). As highlighted by Zupic and Cater (2015), the choice of thresholds cannot be settled ex ante but necessitates a trial-and-error approach, which “is definitely more art than science” (p. 13). They give scarce indications on how to efect this choice and state only that a nonlinear, iterative process is needed to reach “the best representation of the data” (p. 16). Probably due to its not being fully explained and described, we find that this repetitive and lengthy process is often only covered fleetingly, if at all, in published bibliometric studies, inducing a lack of transparency.

Furthermore, even though they show that bibliometrics can help highlight interesting patterns to help understanding and guide interpretation of literature, Zupic and Cater do not actually provide the means to do so beyond description. Indeed, bibliometric studies are often limited to the identification of invisible colleges. They often fall short of in-depth investigation of the literature, probably due to a lack of methodological guidance beyond the bibliometric aspects of the work.

To counteract these limitations, as well as to facilitate transparency, depth of analysis and a comprehensive review and representation of a field/discipline/domain/topic, Felps et al. (2014) argue that bibliometric analyses could be combined with other analytical techniques.

## 2.3. Using grounded theory to conduct literature reviews

Some researchers have turned to GT as a method for nurturing in-depth analysis in systematic literature reviews and providing “new insights and conceptualizations” (Wolfswinkel et al., 2013, p. 2).

GT is philosophically neutral and open to any type of data; it is a systematic, yet flexible, approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Its main tenets are (1) emergence – “the researcher remains open to what is discovered empirically in the area under study, free of preconceived ideas” (Walsh et al., 2015, p. 8); (2) theoretical sampling – data are sampled iteratively, based on theoretical ideas that emerge as analysis progresses; and (3) constant comparative analysis – “data slices”, i.e., sets of data, are collected, and “continuously compared with previously collected and analyzed data, as researchers look for similarities and diferences” (Walsh et al., 2015, p. 9). All data are systematically coded, categories/concepts emerge from the data, and a “core category” is identified. This core category, which recurs in all collected data, represents the “central phenomenon” (Corbin & Strauss, 1990, p. 14) to which most other identified categories are related (Glaser, 1978). Relationships between the core category and other emerging categories are investigated. Coding proceeds until saturation of concepts and relationships is reached.

Beyond being simply a method, GT has been viewed as a “research paradigm for discovery” (Glaser, 2005, p. 145). As researchers embraced this paradigm, GT has been applied in diferent ways and “remodeled” (Glaser, as cited in Simmons, 2011), with this remodelling often influenced by the researcher’s philosophical positioning (Walsh et al., 2015). The diferent philosophical backgrounds of Glaser and Strauss, the founding authors of GT, has led to two main strands of GT: “classic” or “Glaserian” GT, and “Straussian” GT (Holton & Walsh, 2016).

Some issues have not been reconciled between the diferent strands of GT and include coding details (open, selective and theoretical coding for Glaserian GT; open, axial and selective coding for Straussian GT), and the role of the literature review.<sup>2</sup> However, these issues are of limited relevance to our proposal. All grounded theorists agree that they must engage with existing literature and conduct a literature review, to engage in existing research “conversations” (Huf, 2009) and obtain recognition of their theories grounded in empirical data through publication in research outlets. This point is further elaborated in Section 4 (Discussion).

Moving beyond these issues, and considering existing literature as data, Wolfswinkel et al. (2013) describe a ground-breaking use of GT as a method for conducting a rigorous literature review. To our knowledge, this is the only published article to date that describes, in detail, GT as a method for conducting a literature review. Starting from the work of Webster and Watson (2002), Wolfswinkel et al. (2013) propose five iterative stages. These are: (1) Defining – the researcher defines the criteria for inclusion/exclusion of texts in the dataset, identifies the fields of research to be investigated, determines the appropriate sources and decides on search terms; (2) Searching – the identified sources of data are investigated; (3) Selecting – from all the sources identified, the sample of texts for in-depth investigation is refined; (4) Analysing – the selected texts are coded and analysed using constant comparative analysis to identify relevant concepts; and (5) Presenting – the core findings and insights are structured, using tables, diagrams or flowcharts, as necessary.

Wolfswinkel et al.’s (2013) proposal involves the careful but somewhat arbitrary selection of a sample of academic literature, whose systematicity is dificult to optimise as it is solely based on the judgment of the authors of the review, who evolve in a context of scientific information overload. Important works could be inadvertently bypassed. Furthermore, as the authors clearly state, they apply GT precepts only during the fourth stage (Analysing) of the approach, which risks precluding important avenues of investigation. However, if GT is a paradigm in itself (Berente et al., 2019; Walsh et al., 2015), a researcher should be able to apply its precepts and the full GT toolbox profitably at all stages of a literature review, and to all the available data (not just at one stage of the process or to the content of texts arbitrarily deemed relevant, as proposed by Wolfswinkel et al.).

## 2.4. Addressing the limitations of bibliometrics and GT as they are proposed in the literature to conduct literature reviews

As noted above, some researchers have turned to bibliometrics to enhance their conduct of literature reviews, while others have turned to GT. However, limitations have been observed with both approaches as they have been proposed by Zupic and Cater (2015), with regard to the use of bibliometrics, and Wolfswinkel et al. (2013), with regard to GT. In response, we propose that combining and interlacing both methods can help address these limitations and provide better eficiency and quality in the review process than when either method is applied separately. (1) GT, as proposed by Wolfswinkel et al. (2013) to conduct a literature review, could be complemented with bibliometrics to improve systematicity and coherence.

Using bibliometrics, all relevant texts could be considered at the outset of the research process, even if the sample of texts is very broad, and without the temptation to reduce it through arbitrary choices. The final sample of texts selected for in-depth investigation would not rely on the opinion of one (or a few) researcher(s) but emerge from the field itself: it would be based on raw or normalised citation counts to “enable the researcher to (. . .) base their opinions on the aggregated opinions of the scholars working in the field” (Zupic & Cater, 2015, p. 8). Thus, combining bibliometrics with GT could achieve a level of systematicity in a review, relevant to the type of review being conducted, and some objectivity in the means to achieve systematicity. Furthermore, as “all is data” (Glaser, 2007), data slices could include not only the content of texts, but also bibliographic data, and the results of diferent statistical inquiries linked to bibliometric techniques applied to the bibliographic data of the texts sampled for review.

Using bibliometrics, the grouping of articles into clusters, based on their referencing and citations, could help highlight patterns through the identification of the “invisible colleges” (Crane, 1972; De Solla Price, 1965) of a research field, and their relationships. In turn, this would help in selecting objectively relevant and focused data for open coding and in-depth analysis using further systematic coding of textual contents. Thus, the description of clusters highlighted by the bibliometric analyses could be considered as researchers’ memos (and, hence, also as data) to be coded to help with the interpretation and nurturing of emerging interesting patterns.

(1) Conversely, bibliometrics, as proposed by Zupic and Cater (2015) to conduct a literature review, could be complemented with GT to improve transparency and depth of analysis

Combining GT with bibliometrics could enable greater transparency in the choice of citation thresholds that allow bibliometric reviews to be done at diferent levels of granularity. GT provides systematic procedures, and a corresponding readymade “lexicon” (Berente et al., 2019), which could make the reporting of the process of threshold choice easier, while still leaving room for creativity. For instance, using the GT lexicon, the numerous iterations necessary to validate bibliometric threshold choices could be legitimated and explained through the “constant comparative analysis” of “data slices”: successive data slices (including bibliographic, statistical and textual data), resulting from “theoretical sampling”, are added, analysed and constantly compared to reach “saturation”. Using GT coding protocols and lexicon, statistical techniques could be integrated into the interpretation process to help provide depth of analysis: exploratory factor analysis, principal component analysis, or network analysis, which are used in some bibliometric techniques to cluster texts/authors/journals etc., could be understood as quantitative axial coding (i.e., coding for relationships between categories) in Straussian GT; after the emergence of the core category of the literature review the investigation of statistical linkages between clusters of texts/authors/journals etc. could be understood as quantitative selective coding (i.e., coding around the core category) in Glaserian GT.<sup>3</sup>

Using a GT approach to systematically code the content of clusters means that invisible colleges could be more easily identified and named to reveal significant concepts/categories and help the researcher move beyond description towards a deep and synthetic understanding of the field that leads to an easier interpretation of bibliometric results, and to theoretical contribution. Thus, GT coding schemes could make the indepth analysis of a careful bibliometric-based selection of papers easier; they can also help with investigating the relationships between clusters.

## 3. BIBGT: combining bibliometrics and GT to conduct literature reviews

This section describes our proposed BIBGT method, which combines and interlaces bibliometrics and GT. We detail the four main steps of its workflow, and the necessary iterations between these steps, as summarised in Figure 1; this figure illustrates the embeddedness and interlacing of bibliometrics and GT in the proposed methodological approach. We also present a practical illustration of the method. Important elements and guidelines are highlighted in italics in the text.

## 3.1. BIBGT workflow and guidelines

As a foreword, researchers should be clear and explicit about the BIBGT combined methodology they are applying (Transparency).

Like many systematic literature review methods, BIBGT can be applied for a variety of purposes that will impact the definition of its boundaries. Thus, before starting the actual review, it is necessary to lay down its main preliminary purpose and broad orientation: this could, for example, be to map the literature of a broad field, investigate a given theory about a phenomenon covered in the literature, search for a novel explanatory theory, or, simply, to better understand what is known about a phenomenon, theory or method (Rowe, 2014). The preliminary purpose could also be to investigate how an emerging theory, grounded in empirical data, fits with (or contradicts) existing literature. As documents are collected, read and analysed, and the researcher proceeds through the steps described below, another more focused purpose (the research question that is being addressed) may emerge – sometimes not until close to the end of the review process. This purpose could, for instance, be the identification of research gaps in a given domain or topic, solving an issue of relevance to the field, or explaining a paradox.

## 3.1.1. Step 1: defining boundaries

The boundaries (i.e., the breadth of the literature review), should be clearly defined to support systematic investigation (Systematicity), and may evolve and be readjusted during the review process.

Sometimes, several disciplines and/or domains need to be covered in a systematic way. The discipline-(s) to be investigated and the domain(s), topic(s) and time span to be covered should be defined. Bibliographic data sources should be identified. There are currently two main bibliographic databases<sup>4</sup> holding bibliographic information<sup>5</sup> about research works: Scopus<sup>6</sup> and Web of Science.<sup>7</sup> Both databases compile bibliographic information about publications in all main research domains and have benefits and limitations (Adriaanse & Rensleigh, 2013; Chadegani et al., 2013; Harzing & Alakangas, 2016). However, the data available are limited to the outlets indexed in these databases, and additional data may have to be compiled separately – and possibly manually – if outlets that appear significant to the field are not indexed in the selected database (systematicity).

Depending on the purpose of the review, and as in other approaches to systematic literature reviews, relevant articles supposed to be representative of the field under investigation are theoretically sampled (Data slice 1) using, for example, keyword protocols, or the selection of journals and/or articles that cite another single work. Furthermore, to obtain a high-coverage or nearexhaustive sample, and to achieve systematicity, diferent methods might have to be combined (e.g., keywords may be readjusted through systematic reading, the selection of journals may be broadened). Bibliographic data related to all texts in data slice 1, are then collected.

## 3.1.2. Step 2: treating bibliographic data

Bibliographic data treatment – bibliographic data used, data cleaning, bibliometric technique(s) applied, data normalisation – should be described, and information provided about the software used. Clear explanations and reasoning should be given for the specific bibliometric techniques chosen and the way they are applied, and for the choice of citation thresholds and normalisation of indices (Transparency).

Data slice 2, added during this step, includes the bibliographic data related to the set of relevant articles theoretically sampled during Step 1, on which the bibliometric techniques applied will be focused: these data could be for instance, the references cited in the bibliography of selected articles, the journals in which they were published, the keywords used, etc. Bibliographic data extracted from databases must be cleaned either manually or using available software. 8 Data cleaning should not be bypassed as this risks invalidating the results.

The number of relevant texts (Data slice 1), and their related bibliographic data (Data slice 2), is usually too great to allow in-depth investigation of them all. This number is, therefore, reduced using citation thresholds. This leads to the theoretical sampling of a reduced set of clean bibliographic data (Data slice 3), which will provide the basis for bibliometric analyses and in-depth investigation. If the field is found to be limited, citation thresholds can be brought down to 1 in some bibliometric analyses, or 0 in others, thus equating Data slices 2 and 3, and all units highlighted in Data slices 1 and/or 2 could then be investigated in depth.

The bibliometric data treatment, i.e., the techniques that are applied, should be described. Depending on the purpose of the review, diferent bibliometric techniques may be applied (Zupic & Cater, 2015), in many diferent ways. Indeed, the subsequent interpretation of resulting clusters and maps depends on the how and why bibliometric techniques are applied. For instance, with the same set of articles (Data slice 1), diferent subsets of bibliographic data (Data slice 2) could be used, and the same analysis e.g., co-citation analysis, could be conducted on authors, journals and/or references for diferent purposes, leading to very diferent results and subsequent interpretations.

Quantitative coding of bibliographic data is done with the help of bibliometric indices (e.g., co-citation, co-occurrence, or bibliographic coupling indices). Depending on the analysis, diferent bibliometric indices assessing the linkages (i.e., similarities and diferences) between the units analysed (cited references, authors, citing documents, journals, etc.) are used to feed the clustering process and subsequent mappings/graphical representations of these units. These indices could, for instance, be co-citation indices of authors or of cited references, i.e., the number of times authors/ references are co-cited in the bibliographies of the documents collected. They could also be document bibliographic coupling indices, i.e., the number of references documents have in common in their bibliographies, or simple citation indices, i.e., the number of times a word/text/ author/journal is cited. The bibliometric indices are usually normalised to take account of, for instance, the diferent number of references cited in each bibliography or the number of co-authors per document.

The iterative quantitative coding of bibliographic data through bibliometric analyses, together with GT constant comparative analysis of all successive data slices, eventually allows the researcher to finalise the reduced theoretical sample (Data slice 3) for further in-depth study. Depending on the bibliometric technique applied, Data slice 3 could include, for instance, reduced samples of cleaned cited references (for cocitation analysis of references), or citing texts (for bibliographic coupling of documents).

3.1.3. Step 3: clustering, mapping and interpreting The units analysed are clustered (grouped) and mapped, and the results interpreted. The clustering and mapping help with the coding of textual contents and the emergence of categories; it helps researchers explore their whole dataset. Some description of coding categories should be provided, to allow the path taken by the researcher towards conceptualisation to be retraced (Transparency).

In this sub-section, we do not elaborate on the qualitative coding of textual contents as Wolfswinkel et al. (2013) did a fine job describing this, using the Straussian GT coding scheme (i.e., open, axial and selective coding). A very similar approach can be adopted using the Glaserian GT coding scheme (i.e., open, selective and theoretical coding), as used in the illustration provided below. Rather, we focus on what the combination of bibliometrics and GT, and the quantitative coding of bibliographic data, can bring to the literature review process and the emergence of patterns during Step 3.

Data slice 4, added at the beginning of Step 3, includes the results of the bibliometric analyses conducted during Step 2, which are traditionally presented as matrices. This data slice can include, for instance, matrices of co-authorship, co-occurrence of keywords, co-citation of references/authors/journals, bibliographic coupling of citing documents/authors/ journals, etc. Quantitative coding is applied to these matrices using various clustering approaches (for a review of these approaches, see, Zupic & Cater, 2015). Through cluster analysis, exploratory factor analysis or network analysis, the units of analysis (references, authors, journals, etc.) are grouped, and then mapped, based on their linkage indices i.e., quantitative coding of commonalities and diferences. In the literature, cluster analysis, which groups the units of analysis based on their characteristics, while looking for similarities and diferences, has been likened to the GT comparative method (Larsen, 2003); the same applies to exploratory factor analysis or network analysis, which are both based on the same principles as cluster analysis.

Data slice 5, also added during this step, includes the textual contents of the units of analysis highlighted in the reduced sample obtained during Step 2 (Data slice 3), grouped/clustered and mapped using bibliometric analysis.

Graphical representations in the form of mappings (see Appendix A), based on bibliometric results and produced during the first part of this step, can help visually with the identification of patterns when coding textual contents. These mappings show the clustering of the units of analysis based on bibliometric indices. They can also visually highlight significant nodes, for instance, “landmark nodes” (Chen, 2004; Li et al., 2017) usually indicated by size: in reference co-citation or document bibliographic maps, these can help identify in each cluster the most significant texts to be read, investigated in depth and coded as a priority.<sup>10</sup>

Bibliometric techniques help and support GT coding. The groupings highlighted by the various quantitative analyses can guide the coding of categories/concepts, which could be important for interpretation. For instance, clusters of cited references can help identify the main schools of thought in a co-citation analysis map, and clusters of recent citing documents in a bibliographic coupling map can help identify the main current research themes in a field (Walsh & Renaud, 2017). The thorough investigation of nodes, cluster by cluster, enables the identification of common denominators, and hence enables indepth interpretation of the clusters and their eventual naming within the whole mapping of the field. The qualitative description of the clusters highlighted in the bibliographic analyses can be used as an important memo.

The investigation and qualitative coding of textual contents cluster by cluster can help to illuminate the main – and emerging – concepts found in the literature, and help the researcher develop a concept-centric review; they can also help to identify the core category which will frame the review. When coding textual contents, clustering can help with open and axial coding in the Straussian approach and with open and selective coding in the Glaserian approach.

Unless the purpose of a review is mainly descriptive, it may be suficient to provide the bibliometric maps showing the named clusters along with a brief one-paragraph description of the field in the results section of a study, and to provide more detailed descriptions of the clusters in appendices. Reviewers need reassurance that the work conducted has carefully followed a method, and it is suficient to provide proof of this in appendices; they also like to be able to read the main arguments and contributions of the work easily and smoothly, and these elements should, thus, be provided in the main body of the review.

## 3.1.4. Step 4: synthesising

Results should be synthesised. The theoretical contribution of the review (in GT terms, the “theory” that emerges as grounded in bibliographic data, bibliometric and statistical results, and textual data) should be highlighted and developed.

GT coding for relationships between the various emerging categories/concepts will help the review move from description to theoretical development during the synthesising step. The synthesis will provide an overall picture of the literature review conducted, drawing on all five data slices as one set, to move towards theoretical contribution. In Straussian GT, this synthesis could be a description of the investigated field structure; in this case, cluster description would be an important element of the review. In a classic/Glaserian approach, which usually does not recognise description as a theory, the review should probably move beyond description; cluster description would then only be one tool to help, for instance, the detection of research gaps, the development of a conceptual model, or propositions for further research, which could be considered more particularly essential in a stand-alone literature review.

Following a GT approach, where the research question often emerges at the end of the research process, it might only be during this last stage that the contribution of the review, the gap it fills and the research question it addresses fully emerge and are finalised. However, these elements should ultimately be presented upfront in the introduction of the review to ease understanding.

## 3.1.4.1 Iterations between steps

The four BIBGT steps are described above in a simplified manner. In reality, the BIBGT workflow does not constitute a linear process: it is iterative, rather “messy”, and dificult to describe. However, the iterations between steps should be broadly outlined in published work (Transparency).

The iterative nature of BIBGT is illustrated in Figure 1 by the arrows (a, b and c) between each step of the workflow; these iterations are described and discussed below. From a GT perspective, these iterations arise from coding revisions and successive theoretical samplings and help the review reach theoretical saturation.

The main path (Figure 1, paths a) leads from one step to the next, but the final step is rarely, if ever, reached without iterations between the diferent steps. The passage between Step 2 and Step 3 is particularly delicate: the iterations between these steps cease when the authors feel they have developed, through successive theorical samplings, the most interesting and synthetic representation of the literature possible about a given topic.

Through the BIBGT process, diferent data slices are added/tried, constantly compared and analysed, and eliminated if not relevant. At steps 3 and 4, if the researcher has been unable to identify a relevant core category as the focus of the review or feels that theoretical saturation cannot be reached, it may be necessary to retrace previous steps, possibly several times.

During Step 2, the theoretical sampling to define the reduced sample of data for in-depth investigation (Data slice 3) is carried out through an iterative process of constant comparative analysis of all data slices. A number of iterations between steps 2 and 3 are usually needed to identify the optimal reduced sample, i.e., to arrive at the best representation of the field, that makes the most sense when all data slices are taken into account. Using diferent citation thresholds, trial data slices are added to previous slices, iteratively compared, and analysed. When investigating diferent citation thresholds, the researcher might find that the set of documents in Data slice 1 is too limited or too heterogeneous to achieve meaningful results. For instance, in the case of co-citation analysis of references, this could result in low numbers of citations, leading to few co-citations, of limited relevance and meaning; in the case of bibliographic coupling of documents, it might lead to an insuficient number of selected documents or a set of documents with limited commonalities between their bibliographies, and insignificant bibliographic coupling linkages. This can lead to the reconsideration of the original sample of texts. The researcher would then have to go back to Step 1 to revise her search protocol, and perhaps her choice of keywords, with the aim of broadening the set of relevant texts deemed representative of the investigated field. She may even have to complement manually the data already collected (Path b1).

During Step 3, when the researcher maps the field and starts to analyse the clusters through open coding to find possible common denominators between the nodes in each cluster, she might find that the chosen citation threshold is too low, thus inducing too much “statistical noise” to allow a clear mapping of the field, and clusters that are not clearly delineated. Conversely, if she chose a high threshold, this might result in an insuficient number of nodes to allow interesting patterns to emerge. She must then go back to Step 2 and efect theoretical sampling based on diferent thresholds (Path b2). Open coding of the contents of each cluster is undertaken for each tentative choice of threshold during Step 2. For instance, if the units of analysis are citing documents, coding is usually first done through a preliminary “coarse” review of titles and abstracts; then, when the citation thresholds are close to being finalised during Step 2, the content of those articles included in the reduced sample are fully investigated and then recoded in depth during Step 3.

The researcher might also find that her data cleaning was insuficient, as the same node (unit of analysis) appears twice, with slight diferences, in her mapping/ clusters. She would then have to return to Step 2, to further clean her bibliographic data (Path b2).

When mapping the field and starting to qualitatively analyse the clusters and textual contents, the researcher might realise that some important keywords were omitted from the search. She would then have to go back to Step 1 to revise her protocol and redefine the relevant texts to be investigated, resulting in a new Data slice 1 (Path c1).

During synthesis (Step 4), and while, for instance, developing a conceptual model, the researcher might need to go back and revise her open coding of textual data for a deeper analysis of the clusters highlighted during Step 3 (Path b3).

While reading in depth and selectively/theoretically coding the contents of highlighted texts, the researcher might realise that she has missed some important authors/references/documents because her citation threshold was too high, thus sending her back to Step 2 (Path c2). Alternatively, she may realise that her initial sample of texts (Data slice 1) was not broad or complete enough, necessitating a return to Step 1 to complement this initial theoretical sample of texts (Data slice 1), either through a broader bibliographic database search, or through manual add-ons (Path c3). In either case, she would have to be clear and transparent about what guided her choices and how the final sample retained for bibliometric analysis was achieved.

When the synthesis is near completion, the researcher might also have to go back to Step 1 to collect new data related to a diferent point in time (Path c3) – the researcher may, for instance, wish to conduct her review using a diferent time span to check for some possible evolution in her results and/ or to challenge them. The researcher might also need to readjust the boundaries of the review, for instance, adding another discipline/domain, or readjusting the protocol for defining relevant texts (Data slice 1). A complete iteration involving the full cycle and all steps would then be necessary.

## 3.2. Illustration: Renaud et al. (2016)

To illustrate the steps and necessary iterations of the proposed BIBGT method, we use the work of Renaud et al. (2016). This critical literature review combines bibliometrics (using tri-citation analysis, a specific form of co-citation analysis of references<sup>11</sup>) with GT (classic/Glaserian approach), to identify the main schools of thoughts within the strategic alignment field and synthesise the corresponding literature to explain a paradox. The review illustrates one possible application of BIBGT, with several elements of the BIBGT workflow as described above identifiable. This is discussed below and summarised in Figure 2. We focus on the elements that can be inferred from the reported study: probably due to space limitations, the review process is not fully transparent, particularly concerning some of the iterations and the interlacing of the two methods.

At the outset of the review, Renaud et al.’s (2016) main preliminary purpose was to investigate the structure of the strategic alignment field, and how its seminal model, the Strategic Alignment Model (SAM; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993) was difused and legitimated over time. SAM aimed to optimise the alignment of information technologies (IT) with business needs, and help managers to eficiently manage information systems (IS). Since its inception, SAM had remained one of the most utilised models in this field, both in the literature and in the corporate world. According to the Glaserian school of thought, the researcher must first identify the participants’ (in our illustration, strategic alignment researchers’) main concern. The authors identify this main concern as the disparity between the intended contribution of the strategic alignment literature, which includes SAM as a prominent model, and the apparent negative consequences of its application in corporations. They aim to understand and explain this paradox. In turn, this allowed the authors to adopt this more focused purpose, which emerged as the review proceeded.

## 3.2.1. Step 1: defining boundaries

From the text of the study, one can infer that the field is social sciences, the discipline is management, and the domain is information systems. The topic is strategic alignment. The authors set no time limit for their review.

The data source is Web of Science. The same data (Data slices 1) are collected at two points in time: 2011 and 2014. As the authors adopt a GT exploratory stance, the second dataset is used not to confirm the results obtained with the first dataset, but “rather to densify, saturate categories (that previously emerged using the first dataset) and extend the scope of the resulting theory: it allowed (the authors) to formalize (. . .) the substantive results previously obtained” (p. 8). As the review focuses on SAM, the set of documents that the authors theoretically sample (Data slices 1) includes all articles that cite Henderson and Venkatraman (1993), the seminal work describing this model. For data collected in 2011, this comprises 159 articles. For data collected in 2014, this comprises 365 articles.

## 3.2.2 Step 2: treating bibliographic data

As the authors wish to apply co-citation of references, Data slices 2 include all references (9,726 and 21,303) cited in Data slices 1. These references are cleaned manually by the authors, reducing the number of cited references to 3,725 and 13,553 single references for the 2011 and 2014 datasets respectively. After data cleaning, the most cited references are investigated to achieve a reduced sample of cited references. Through successive iterations, using diferent citation thresholds, diferent slices of data are sampled out of the original sets of single cited references. After analysis and comparison of the results obtained with various thresholds, 39 references cited 14 times or more (2011 dataset) and 40 references cited 21 times or more (2014 dataset) are finally theoretically sampled (Data slices 3) to process through cocitation analysis, before in-depth investigation. Some information about the process of threshold choices is provided in the study appendices and these are summarised in the iterations section below.

The authors apply co-citation analysis to this reduced number of references, i.e., they investigate how often pairs of references are cited together in the bibliographies of the articles in each dataset, and hence, how strongly they relate to each other. These numbers of co-citations (co-citation indices) are normalised using the inclusion index (Callon et al., 1986) to address the “scale efect”, which occurs when two co-cited references have very different numbers of citations. Bibliometric treatments are undertaken using BIBEXCEL software<sup>12</sup> and normalised co-citation matrices are produced as bibliometric results, and provided in appendices to the study.

## 3.2.3. Step 3: clustering, mapping and interpretation

To efect quantitative coding, using bibliometric results obtained during the previous step and normalised co-citation matrices (Data slices 4), cited references are clustered and mapped through principal component analysis (PCA) and multi-dimensional scaling (MDS), using SPSS software.

The textual contents of the references highlighted, clustered and mapped are then open-coded. The description of the clusters of cited references (memos), which helps with the open and selective coding, is provided in appendices to the article, and not in its main body, as this study aims at moving beyond a purely descriptive analysis of the field, towards critical investigation. The invisible colleges that correspond to each cluster are identified and named by the authors. The authors finally identify 8 and 7 clusters in the mapping of the 39 (2011 data collection) and 40 (2014 data collection) references most cited by articles that build on Henderson and Venkatraman’s legacy (see, Figure 3). Examples of open coding of textual contents (e.g., “perspective on strategy”, “user-oriented perspective vs. managerial perspective”) are provided in the methodological section of the paper. Open coding is efected until the core category (i.e., dealing with the dissociation phenomenon between SAM and its conditions of validity) is identified.

The authors then efect the selective coding of the textual contents of the references, i.e., they code around the core category. They identify three main premises and assumptions of the field (P1, P2 and P3), related to this core category. These premises, which appear to be implicitly accepted, despite being no longer valid are: (P1) the true nature of strategy that is planned, explicit, known, accepted, and followed; (P2) the performativity of the model; and (P3) managerial rationality and the redundancy of organisational members. Illustrations of this selective coding related to the identified premises and assumptions are provided in tabular format. For instance, the cluster identified as the strategic management perspective grouping (“g1” in 2011) “considers IS/IT as a source of competitive advantage”. More specifically, the references in this cluster consider that IT is part of strategic planning and carry a Porterian legacy (P1); they anticipate, based on SAM, the conditions under which IT will be a source of temporary or sustained advantage (P2); and, finally, managers make strategic choices, based on the management of resources at the origin of the competitive advantage (P3).

## 3.2.4. Step 4: synthesising

The authors then synthesise their findings with the help of theoretical coding summarised in diagrams: they show that, even though proven as no longer valid, the three identified premises are not discussed or challenged in practice, and that SAM keeps being applied, which explains the dissociation phenomenon.

The authors highlight systematically how references in each cluster implicitly or explicitly espouse these premises, and how SAM is applied in a more and more prescriptive manner; more generally, they highlight the performativity of SAM, and its false promises.

Thus, theoretical coding leads to a possible explanation of the paradox that is a major concern of the field. The synthesis of all results is provided in the discussion section of Renaud et al. (2016). Through tri-citation analysis combined and interlaced with a classic GT approach, the authors propose a theory, which highlights that, even though the premises and assumptions of SAM have been largely challenged in the literature, they remain unchallenged in practice. SAM, which is dissociated from its conditions of applicability, continues to be applied in firms, even though its conditions of validity are no longer respected (dissociation phenomenon). This is a possible explanation for the paradox being investigated and why firms continue investing in IT even though investment no longer appears to be correlated consistently with productivity gains. Thus, the authors go beyond description, and propose a theory grounded in textual contents but also in bibliographic data and the statistical results of bibliometric techniques, as graphically represented in maps.

## 3.2.4.1 Iterations between steps

Some iterations between the various steps are described, even though they are not specifically designated as such by the authors.

Iterations between steps 2 and 3 are termed “trial and error” by the authors rather than as successive theoretical samplings, which would be more appropriate based on the GT lexicon. These iterations correspond to diferent citation thresholds (Figure 2, path b) and are described to justify the authors’ choice of thresholds. These “trials” lead to various reduced samples of cited references. For data collected in 2011, the authors conduct analyses with references cited at least 13 times (49 references), at least 14 times (39 references) and at least 15 times (25 references). For data collected in 2014, analyses are conducted with references cited at least 19 times (51 references), 20 times (49 references) and 21 times (40 references). The authors argue that their final choice of thresholds is based on two combined criteria – statistical relevance and meaning: they retain thresholds leading to results that are statistically valid and make the most sense for the whole dataset. Appendices provide the results of the analyses corresponding to some of the diferent thresholds.

Data are first collected in 2011. Given the significant time elapsed between data collection and publication, data are collected again in 2014. Then, the full analysis is conducted and reported twice to investigate if the initial results could be challenged by supplementary data, if any evolution can be identified, or if new elements emerge. Having reached Step 4 with their 2011 data, the authors go back to Step 1 in 2014 and collect further data (Figure 2, path c). This iteration demonstrates that the field has not really evolved between these two points in time, ensuring theoretical saturation of their results.

## 4. Discussion

The present article justifies and describes the general process/workflow of BIBGT, a new literature review method for use in diferent contexts, for diferent types of reviews, and diferent paper genres, while dealing with varying volumes of publications, and accommodating diferent authors’ experiences. In this section, we discuss these elements, the limitations of the proposed method and possible avenues for further research.

## 4.1. A method adapted to the current context of bibliometric development

Our proposed method is named BIBGT as it combines and interlaces techniques and precepts from both bibliometrics and GT. This method is of particular interest because of the recent exponential growth in both bibliometrics as a research field and the availability of related automated software.

Until very recently, it was not possible for a researcher without deep bibliometric and statistical knowledge to conduct bibliometric analysis. One interesting feature of the illustrative study we used is that it was published in 2016. In the years since then, the bibliometric field has developed and expanded, and reached many other sciences. Thus, while Renaud et al. (2016) used basic statistical software and did several of the bibliometric data treatments manually and “painfully”, much of the work undertaken can now be done automatically, using recently developed software. All treatments on the data collected in 2011 were done manually, and took significant time to process, resulting in the authors having to collect data again in 2014. Also, the basic statistical software they used resulted in rather unsophisticated graphical representations of their results. They did not have access to the many functionalities (e.g., identification of landmark nodes by size, or linkages between nodes) that facilitate further interpretation of bibliometric results that are now available in bibliometric software such as ARTIREV<sup>13</sup> or VOSviewer.<sup>14</sup> Some examples of results obtained in just minutes and the type of maps produced using up-to-date bibliometric software are provided in Appendix A.

However, a word of caution is needed: researchers should ensure that reliable and automated cleaning of bibliographic data, and of cited references in particular, are indeed available in the operational functionalities of the software they use; alternatively, researchers should be prepared to do this task manually or by other means (Walsh et al., 2021).

## 4.2. A method that is “open” not mechanistic

Our argument is not that a mechanistic and automated process is suitable for conducting reviews. Quite the opposite in fact. No review process can be fully automated: however, in the current context of scientific information overload, we must look for tools to help the human mind conduct meaningful reviews. When coupled and interlaced with a GT approach, the recent automation of bibliometric treatments of bibliographic data allows researchers greater creativity in the way these treatments are applied. Automation also frees up time, allowing researchers to focus more attention on the content of the most significant texts. However, while bibliometrics can definitely help guide the open coding stage of reviews by highlighting patterns in bibliographic data/invisible colleges, and prioritising necessary readings, it mostly cannot help the latter stages of GT coding of textual contents that lead to insightfulness and move beyond simple description, and which require researchers’ reflexivity.

Our presentation of BIBGT does not preclude any type of bibliometric techniques from being used, combined and interlaced, with GT. For instance, journal cross-citation analysis could be used at the beginning of the proposed workflow to help identify relevant research fields (Mingers & Leydesdorf, 2015). It is also interesting that, in a follow up review (Walsh & Renaud, 2017) to the one used to illustrate our method, two of its authors explored the main current research themes on the topic (application and difusion of SAM in the literature) using document bibliographic coupling analysis, which they showed, provided complementary information to the one provided by co-citation analysis, on both the recent advances of knowledge and the remaining gaps, and allowed them to be more precise on research avenues, and to infer additional ones. Typically, impactful reviews include three elements: synthesis (what we know), gaps (what we should know), and research avenues (how to get there) (Wagner et al., 2021). Whereas reference co-citation analysis can inform us on what we already know, bibliographic coupling of documents can inform us on currently emerging research themes (i.e., what we should know) and may give some indications about how to get there. Therefore, combining both co-citation and bibliographic coupling analyses with GT can be fruitful and is recommended with the workflow we have presented.

## 4.3. Usefulness and appropriateness of the method

In terms of evaluating BIBGT as a method to conduct literature reviews, Walsh and Renaud (2017) clearly demonstrate the benefits of combining bibliometric techniques with an interpretive review. They compare a traditional interpretive review of their research topic (Chan & Reich, 2007) with their own review combining bibliometrics and GT. They highlight the more exhaustive results obtained as well as the “time gained and the detection of patterns in the literature” (p. 103).

We argue that by articulating the interlacing of bibliometrics with GT in the various steps of our method, we contribute to the development of a trustworthy and eficient method for conducting a literature review that also helps increase the depth of analysis. The method enables systematicity and transparency, key features that lead to trustworthiness (Paré et al., 2016; Templier & Paré, 2018), while also helping the researcher strive towards depth of analysis. It can help identify the concerns of the investigated research field/domain/topic, and provides the means to address these concerns, thus moving a review beyond description. Highlighting a limited number of the most significant and representative texts can help the researcher prioritise her readings and obtain a clear understanding of the investigated field, while also speeding up the review process. By helping to define a reduced sample of texts, and to focus on colleges of thought and representative papers from each college, the method allows considerable time gained.

Grounded theorists – both Glaserian and Straussian – conducting stand-alone literature reviews or reviews related to findings of empirical studies, could find our approach useful as it would allow them to fully remain within the GT paradigm.

However, with respect to reviews linked to empirically grounded findings, our approach would probably be most useful to grounded theorists when their work leads them to develop new perspectives in wellestablished and mature, hence dense, theoretical research areas. In such contexts, the field might include heterogeneous results and multiple perspectives which may lead to scientific information overload. However, even in a less-developed field, scientific information overload can strike – see, for instance, the hundreds of thousands of texts that have been published within a decade on blockchain technology, or the millions of texts published within two years on the COVID 19 pandemic.<sup>15</sup> Furthermore, even if the topic is not extensively covered in the literature as such, there will always be a need to investigate adjacent topics, domains, or other disciplines to achieve a required level of systematicity, which might again induce scientific information overload.

When conducting empirical studies, grounded theorists usually engage in the literature review process later than other researchers. They traditionally spend less time on this task, having dedicated much of their allocated research time to fieldwork, to collect, analyse and constantly compare empirical data. Some grounded theorists argue that if a researcher enters a field with an open mind and without preconceptions, they cannot conduct a relevant literature review before knowing what they are going to find in their data. Others argue that a researcher cannot approach a project as a tabula rasa, without taking account of existing literature. However, whichever strand of GT grounded theorists align with, they all agree that they cannot ignore the literature. Depending on their stance, grounded theorists can use BIBGT to conduct a review before a GT project, grounded in empirical field data, starts and/or as it proceeds: they might engage in a rather descriptive “non-committal” review and mapping of one (or several) broad research fields, to develop their “theoretical sensitivity” (Glaser, 1978; Hekkala & Urquhart, 2013), aiming not to apply, but to eventually extend and transcend extant theories (Glaser, 1978). Once their theory grounded in empirical data has emerged, grounded theorists can also use BIBGT to conduct a more focused and “integrative” review, with the aim of positioning their empirical findings with respect to existing literature (Martin, 2006; Urquhart & Fernandez, 2006) of the relevant – or adjacent – field(s).

Indeed, beyond grounded theorists, researchers who wish to take a more conservative hypotheticodeductive approach to their work might also find BIBGT useful. For instance, a researcher looking for hypotheses to verify in her research could use results obtained with BIBGT to infer and lay down these hypotheses.

The present article should encourage researchers to be more explicit, by including essential information in the methodological section of their articles and providing information that is suficient to allow the verification of their work (e.g., technical parameters selected for mappings, qualitative description of clusters, etc.) in appendices. However, no method or technique used to conduct a literature review, including BIBGT, can be a substitute for understanding and knowing a field. Whatever the techniques used to conduct a review, they are only tools to help researchers gain insights and deepen their knowledge and understanding of a given field, in a limited time.

Diferent types of literature reviews blossomed in the early 1990s because of the value attached to rigorous evidence-based practice in health sciences and, subsequently, in management (Tranfield et al., 2003), software engineering (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007; Petersen et al., 2008), library and information science sector (Grant & Booth, 2009), and, more recently, in IS (Leidner, 2018; Paré et al., 2015; Rowe, 2014; Schryen et al., 2020; Templier & Paré, 2018). Given the diversity of types of literature reviews, readers may wonder which types BIBGT will be most appropriate and useful for. Our proposed method can assist along the four dimensions (the goal of the review, its breadth, systematicity and argumentative strategy) highlighted by Rowe (2014) in his typology of literature reviews, as briefly described in Section 1, and we elaborate on this below.

Goal: While bibliometric techniques are mostly useful for describing, and GT for explaining, BIBGT is most useful for the goals of describing, explaining and understanding,<sup>16</sup> but serves less purpose for that of testing. In fact, if the purpose is to test a theory, comprehensiveness of sample and quality appraisal of each element of the full set of articles selected for investigation (Aguinis et al., 2011) are required along with statistical techniques for testing publication bias and aggregating weights (Kepes et al., 2012; Kepes & Thomas, 2018).

Breadth and systematicity: The use of thresholds in BIBGT allows account to be taken of both the “grey” and “white” literature (Adams et al., 2017; Larsen et al., 2019; Lawrence et al., 2014). For instance, when using co-citation analysis of references, grey literature, which may not be published in top research outlets and may not have undergone long peer-reviewing, will still be taken into account as long as it has been cited often, irrespective of the outlet or the way/format in which it was published. At the same time, covering the “white literature” can be ensured. For instance, one could select documents cited more often than average or published in the most renowned outlets as a dataset from which to extract bibliographic data, and then conduct document bibliographic coupling analysis.

BIBGT can also prevent a review from being limited to a given domain or discipline, regardless of the number of relevant texts to be investigated (breadth), while guaranteeing greater objectivity and replicability of procedures (systematicity and transparency). Even when a researcher has significant ex ante knowledge of a field, BIBGT may be profitably applied when that field is mature, rich and dense, to help structure the review conceptually. It can also be applied in more limited or less dense research fields, in order to provide a synthetic mapping of existing knowledge. Emphasising systematicity is not antagonistic to flexibility, which is also a characteristic of BIBGT. The choice of bibliometric techniques, the way they are applied, the number of iterations and the thresholds chosen must be tailored by the researchers, through their own “theoretical sensitivity” (Glaser, 1978), and further prescription could be dangerous.

Argumentative strategy: Regarding argumentative strategy (De Vaujany et al., 2011), we do not consider BIBGT as a fixed method. Rowe (2014) has already shown that argumentative strategies for literature reviews can vary, even within a given type, and flexibility in presentation can vary depending on factors such as the maturity of the topic. However, the ability to visualise bibliographic data using BIBGT should give additional value to researchers by helping to convey what is encompassed and designated by a cluster (of words, journals, cited references, citing documents, etc.), and providing some objective support for a given interpretation or argument.

It might be relevant to indicate here that the methodological theory presented in the current work is grounded in the methodological data of some of our previous works; hence, it is a grounded theory and, as such, it will probably evolve as it is applied. We have implicitly used BIBGT in the past to conduct literature reviews for empirical articles, as well as stand-alone reviews, which have been published during the last decade. Unfortunately, in these past published studies, the GT side of our work had to be mostly “obliterated” (resulting in some lack of transparency), mainly because of space restrictions: as no written account of the BIBGT method already existed, a great deal of details would have been required to justify it fully. The delineation and description of the main steps of BIBGT in the present article should help researchers apply the method in the future and be transparent about it, with less detailed justifications required.

## 4.4. Limitations and considerations for use and future research

One significant limitation of BIBGT is that those using it need to be trained in bibliometrics and GT methodology, both of which necessitate some time investment. However, GT is now taught in many doctoral schools, albeit often confined to its application with qualitative data, and integrated and automated pieces of bibliometric software (e.g., ARTIREV, VOSViewer) that researchers can use with little training investment are now emerging.<sup>17</sup>

BIBGT has three further clear limitations. The first limitation of BIBGT is related to its scope. The creative intertwining of GT precepts with bibliometrics can help reduce a common basic tension in literature reviews between the number and range of articles covered (comprehensiveness and breadth) and the in-depth analysis of a sample of the most significant articles (depth). Beyond its obvious applicability to large bodies of literature, the essentially inductive character of BIBGT makes it appealing for new entrants in a given research field, for those who want to gain time, or for those who are not interested in testing a particular theory a priori, but rather whose review is mostly phenomenon driven. However, it is clear that reviews for testing, such as meta-analyses, have very specific requirements, not addressed by BIBGT. Second, while a stand-alone literature review should optimally inform us on what we already know, what we still need to know and how we can get there (Wagner et al., 2021), BIBGT is of more limited help with the third task. Third, as is the case in all bibliometric analyses, the thresholds that emerge as most relevant may still lead to the bypassing of some works that might eventually reveal themselves to be significant, even if the choice of thresholds is properly justified, explained and legitimated.

While using BIBGT, the researcher must interpret her results. One might argue that interpretation always introduces subjectivity and bias, but we would argue that there are elements of interpretation (and, hence, subjectivity and bias) in any literature review. Furthermore, if the systematic procedures of GT that guide a researcher’s interpretation are described and applied clearly, this should facilitate replicability of the approach (Cram et al., 2020; Olbrich et al., 2017).

Finally, our proposed workflow should be viewed as an inspiring starting point, not as a straitjacket, especially as both of its interlaced components (bibliometrics and GT) have essential exploratory, flexible elements, which can lead to interesting, unexpected results. It is important that flexibility and creativity are built into a methodological approach such as the one proposed in this article to accommodate the essence of the iterative process of a literature review and to leave room for future methodological innovation/evolution. Some machine-learning and natural-language processing have been proposed as ways to improve data treatment in literature reviews (e.g., Abdel-Karim et al., 2020; Larsen et al., 2019; Prester et al., 2021). Combining these techniques with BIBGT could be interesting to further support the review process.

## 5. Conclusion

In this article, we proposed a new method – BIBGT – that combines and interlaces bibliometrics and GT precepts to help conduct literature reviews that aim to describe, understand or explain. We outlined the workflow and main steps of BIBGT and provided some practical guidelines and an illustration to help other researchers apply the method. BIBGT helps to enhance trustworthiness (Paré et al., 2016) and increase eficiency; it helps guide the researcher in her readings and interpretations, and in mapping and understanding the investigated field. This inductive combined method is particularly useful when the field investigated is rich and dense and ofers a great number of documents to be reviewed. It can also help to rapidly map a less rich and dense field of research that is new to a researcher or to rapidly synthesise existing knowledge in such a field.
