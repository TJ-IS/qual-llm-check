# Towards a model of social commerce: improving the efectiveness of e-commerce through leveraging social media tools based on consumers’ dual roles

Xiaolin Lin<sup>a</sup> and Xuequn Wang <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, College of Business, California State University, Sacramento, CA, USA; <sup>b</sup>Schoo of Business and Law, Edith Cowan University, Joondalup, Western Australia Australia

## ABSTRACT

Social media has been integrated into traditional e-commerce, creating an innovative technology-based approach to changing business practice and service, yet few researchers have attempted to provide an understanding of how this approach is changing consumer decisionmaking and purchase behaviours. Combining the expectation-confirmation theory and the expectation-confirmation model and information systems continuance, we propose a model for social commerce, illustrating how social media is utilised in online shopping as a three-fold process (pre-purchase, purchase, and post-purchase stages) from a consumer perspective. The model consists of three phases: social commerce motivation, social commerce adoption, and e-commerce efectiveness. Using two rounds of surveys, we find that (1) autonomous motivation and controlled motivation have positive efects on social commerce information seeking and sharing, and (2) social commerce information seeking formulates consumers’ pre-purchase decisions, thus afecting their actual purchase outcomes, repurchase intentions, and social commerce information-sharing intentions in the post-purchase stage. In addition, our study indicates that consumers may play dual roles in social commerce: as information seekers in the pre-purchase stage and as information providers in the post-purchase stage. Our findings have important implications for literature and practice.

## 1. Introduction

Social media has been actively integrated into traditional e-commerce sites (Doha et al., 2019), providing an innovative channel for sharing shopping information with “friends”. This is known as the social commerce phenomenon. The literature has proposed two types of social commerce (K. Z. Zhang & Benyoucef, 2016). In the first type, traditional e-commerce sites integrate social media to facilitate social interaction; in the second type, social media integrates commercial features to support sales. In this study, we focus on the first type of social commerce. For example, Amazon adds social media features such as customer reviews and links to social media, supporting diferent consumer behaviour before and after purchase. Consumers may gather product information and peer consumer usage experiences from customer reviews before they make purchase decisions. Consumers may then share their product evaluations and shopping experiences after they purchase or use the products. These social media-enabled consumer behaviours have a substantial impact, leading to more informed purchase decisions and improved purchase outcomes (Lin et al., 2019; T.-P. Liang et al., 2011; H. Zhang et al.,

2014). This also represents a great opportunity for e-vendors because the continuous growth of e-commerce and the popularity of social media make social commerce essential for e-vendors to increase their sales.

D. J. Kim et al. (2009) noted that consumer behaviour can be afected as a three-fold process, consisting of the pre-purchase, purchase, and post-purchase stages. In the social commerce context, the impact of social media on consumer behaviour has been seen in each of these three purchase stages (Liang & Turban, 2011; Stephen & Toubia, 2010; T.-P. Liang et al., 2011). For example, consumers can gain information by reading product-related information shared on social media by “friends” or posted in online branding communities (Goh et al., 2013; T.-P. Liang et al., 2011). This consumer-generated content is viewed as a credible information source for consumers to observe product evaluations by other consumers, shaping their perceptions of products before they make purchase decisions (Lin et al., 2019). Consumers may also write product reviews and share information about what they buy and their experiences of using the products (Bugshan & Attar, 2020), all of which facilitate consumer behaviour after they purchase or use products. Both pre-purchase and post-purchase consumer behaviours have evolved as a result of advanced social media technology. Consumer behaviour as a three-fold process is further supported by the argument that social commerce is a dynamic environment, combining consumers, technology, and information (Lin et al., 2019), which can engage consumers and afect their behaviours at different purchase stages (K. Z. Zhang & Benyoucef, 2016). In other words, as a relatively new phenomenon, social commerce involves both information systems (IS) adoption and consumer behaviour in diferent purchasing stages. Therefore, it is important to examine the complex process and uniqueness of consumers’ behaviours in social commerce (K. Z. Zhang & Benyoucef, 2016), and additional theoretical insights are essential to understanding the emerging social commerce phenomenon and providing theoretical and practical implications. Drawing from the three-fold process perspective, the first objective of our study is to conceptualise consumer social commerce adoption and its influence on consumer behaviour in this new research context – social commerce.

Furthermore, social commerce facilitates an innovative form of consumer–consumer collaboration in the online shopping process (Lim, 2020). Social commerce behaviours are voluntary, and consumers can decide whether to interact with other consumers, as well as the ways in which they interact (Prentice et al., 2020). Because the value of social commerce is highly dependent on consumer behaviour, e-vendors are interested to know how to support consumers’ social commerce behaviours. Therefore, our second objective is to examine how to motivate consumers to engage in social commerce behaviour. Drawing upon selfdetermination theory (SDT; Ryan & Deci, 2000), in our study we examine how consumers’ social commerce motivations influence their social commerce behaviour. SDT is used to examine humans’ motivations, as well as their basic psychological needs, which, when satisfied, support individuals’ autonomous motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000). We apply SDT in the context of social commerce and provide valuable insights regarding the efects of diferent types of motivation.

We test our model using two rounds of surveys, collecting data from Amazon.com customers. In the first-round survey, we focus on how consumers’ motivations influence their social commerce information seeking and purchase intention (the pre-purchase stage), whereas in the second-round survey, we deal with consumers’ social commerce information sharing and repurchase intention (the post-purchase stage).

To summarise, we propose a modelling framework of social commerce for conceptualising consumers social commerce adoption as well as examining their motivations and consequences. More specifically, we delineate specific consumer behaviour before, during, and after purchase as a three-fold process. By focusing on the first type of social commerce, our study can provide useful suggestions regarding how to manage the integrated social media features for promoting products and increasing sales in the traditional e-commerce setting. Specifically, e-vendors can facilitate social interactions when they are able to manage these social media features efectively. However, when e-vendors try to control these social media features and manipulate social interactions, these controlled social media features may result in negative outcomes. For example, e-vendors may control the display of reviews and intentionally remove negative reviews. While such controls may temporarily increase sales, customer satisfaction might decrease in the long term, because consumers’ expectations towards products based on reviews will not be met. Our study thus provides further insight regarding how e-vendors can improve the eficiency and efectiveness of e-commerce through the application of social commerce, thus paving the way for understanding how advanced technology could be used to generate new IT efects.

The proposed framework is novel in the following respects: First, we examine social commerce adoption by delineating specific consumer behaviour before and after purchase as a three-fold process. Social commerce is a system consisting of multiple platforms that facilitate information exchange among peer consumers and influence consumer behaviour at diferent purchase stages. At the pre-purchase stage, consumers are information seekers who gather product information in diferent formats, such as customer reviews, ratings, recommendations, and usage experiences shared by peer consumers. Here ratings refer to customers’ ratings towards products, such as star scores. Customer reviews refer to text comments covering a variety of information such as product opinions and usage experiences. In addition, customers may check out recommendations (e.g., product links) from their friends and read their shopping/usage experiences on social media. At the post-purchase stage, some consumers switch their role to information contributors who post their ratings and comments on products, shopping experiences, usage experiences, recommendations, and so on. Although prior e-commerce studies have examined the prepurchase and post-purchase stages (e.g., D. J. Kim et al., 2009), they focus on investigating how confirmation results in various outcomes such as satisfaction. The integrated social media and information sharing in social commerce have not been examined in the e-commerce setting. Further, most social commerce studies have focused on either social commerce information seeking (Voramontri & Klieb, 2019) or information sharing (Bugshan & Attar, 2020), rather than examining them through an integrated approach.

One exception is from Goraya et al. (2021), who examined both information seeking and sharing. However, they did not clarify how these two types of social commerce adoption occur at diferent purchase stages. By diferentiating consumers’ social commerce adoption at various purchase stages, in our study, we contribute to the literature by clarifying the dual roles that consumers can play in social commerce. These dual roles can improve consumers’ shopping experiences and shape positive perceptions of online shopping, which have a substantial efect on their purchase decisions.

Second, we posit that consumers’ social commerce adoption is voluntary and largely determined by their motivation. By applying SDT to explore consumers’ motivation in social commerce, we extend SDT by leveraging a new research context (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Johns, 2006) and identify two types of social commerce motivation (autonomous motivation and controlled motivation) based on previous literature. Although prior authors have also examined social commerce motivation (Yang et al., 2016; J. Zhang & Mao, 2016), few have assessed their roles at diferent purchase stages. By examining the efect of social commerce motivation on social commerce adoption, we contribute to the literature by clarifying the diferential efects of motivation at diferent purchase stages.

Third, we contribute to the literature on the relationship between purchase intention and purchase. The literature has mainly been focused on consumers purchase intention (Doha et al., 2019), while relatively few scholars have examined consumers’ actual purchase behaviours. Using two rounds of surveys, we test the efect of purchase intention on purchase behaviour to show the benefits of social commerce on consumer purchase behaviour. From the practical perspective, a rich understanding of social commerce behaviour can help e-vendors create a better shopping environment, improve its efectiveness, and increase sales.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Social media and online consumer behaviour: identifying social commerce constructs

In our social commerce research context, the integrated social media features on traditional e-commerce sites enable consumers to exchange consumergenerated content via online reviews and social media, thus changing consumer behaviour (Amblee & Bui, 2011; Hajli & Shirazi, 2021; Huang & Benyoucef, 2013; Osatuyi et al., 2020). Therefore, in our social commerce work, we draw on the literature dealing with (1) online customer reviews and (2) social media features (i.e., sharing information about shopping via social networking sites), as summarised in the following.

Customer reviews significantly influence purchase decisions (e.g., Forman et al., 2008) because they can reduce uncertainty about product characteristics (X. Li et al., 2011). Customer reviews plays an essential role in driving e-commerce business success because customers actively seek information about products and services during their transaction activities (e.g., Madlberger & Matook, 2017; Safi & Yu, 2017). Motived by this, e-commerce has integrated advanced technologies (e.g., social media) to encourage customers to generate reviews online, which is considered an electronic form of word-of-mouth (WOM). With the added social media attributes (e.g., ratings and reviews) in e-commerce, more and more consumers are starting to exchange product-related information with other consumers. In addition, e-commerce has applied various elements (e.g., awards such as points and badges) to motivate customers to contribute reviews (WOM) and improve the usefulness of reviews for purchase decisions (L. Wang et al., 2020). Using an experiment, L. Wang et al. (2020) demonstrated that badges can enhance the perceived competence of WOM contributors, which in turn increases the perceived review usefulness. In recent studies, researchers have found that customer reviews can also be enhanced via various integrated social media attributes in e-commerce, in particular when customers have higher trust in the platforms (Goraya et al., 2021; Meilatinova, 2021). Peer consumers often read these customergenerated reviews and used them to evaluate products and services, which can form their attitudes towards purchases, therefore leading to changes in behaviours (Lin et al., 2019; L. Wang et al., 2020). More specifi cally, Lin et al. (2019) used survey data to show that customer review quality can increase consumers’ trust in social commerce, which is positively related to their satisfaction and purchasing behaviour. L. Wang et al. (2020) reported that perceived usefulness of customer review could lead to consumers’ positive evaluations. All these studies support the importance of customer reviews in driving online consumer behaviour because consumers perceive customer reviews to have higher trustworthiness and afective quality than provider recommendations (Benlian et al., 2012). Customer reviews can increase the usefulness and social presence of e-commerce sites (Kumar & Benbasat, 2006). Previous researchers have examined the efects of var ious attributes of customer reviews, such as afect (Ludwig et al., 2013), identity information (Forman et al., 2008), and volume and ratings (Dellarocas et al., 2007). Depending on the contexts and product types, customer reviews can have diferential efects on product sales. For example, Moore (2015) classified customer reviews into those explaining actions versus those explaining reactions, and conducted experiments to show that reviews explaining actions were more helpful for utilitarian products, whereas reviews explaining reactions were more helpful for hedonic products. These studies again demonstrate that customer reviews indeed play an important role in the process of consumer purchase decision-making.

Social media is changing consumers’ decisionmaking and purchasing in terms of the way they share personal shopping-related information with their “friends”. Consumers share their shoppingrelated information and observe others’ on social media, thus afecting their purchasing decisionmaking (e.g., Anderson et al., 2014; H.-W. Kim et al., 2011; Hajli et al., 2017; Harris & Dennis, 2011; Ng, 2013; Wang & Chang, 2013). Based on a survey, Doha et al. (2019) found that social capital and customer engagement facilitated via social media are main drivers for purchase intention because they can enhance shopping-related information sharing among consumers. Voramontri and Klieb (2019) also used survey data to show that social media usage facilitates information search and product evaluation, which can support consumers’ online shopping. Consumers can also interact with their peer consumers, exchange social support, and develop social ties in social mediaenabled branding communities (such as a Facebook fan page; Doha et al., 2019), which can build close relationships between companies and customers, enhance brand loyalty (Hudson et al., 2015; K. Z. Zhang et al., 2016), and afect consumer behaviour (Anderson et al., 2014; Bugshan & Attar, 2020; Hajli & Frooman, 2018; Ng, 2013). In addition to studying the impacts of social media information sharing on consumer behaviour, the existing literature highlights its importance by examining its antecedents from diferent perspectives. For example, X. Wang et al. (2021) collected survey data from users of Facebook brand page and ofered insightful understanding about how trust in social media and trust in brands can motivate consumers to share their shop ping information on their personal social media accounts. All these studies suggest the importance of consumers’ shopping-related information sharing on social media in exerting influences on peer consumers shopping behaviour. As such, consumer engagement on social media creates an innovative approach for consumers to exchange various resources to assist with decision-making in online shopping.

D. J. Kim et al. (2009) demonstrated that the consumer purchase process can be considered a three-fold process consisting of the pre-purchase, purchase, and post-purchase stages, which is thus used as the theoretical lens for our study. The pre-purchase stage suggests that consumers’ information-searching behaviour can shape their perceptions about products and determine how they make pre-purchase decisions. The postpurchase stage suggests that consumers confirm their shopping expectations based on their experiences shopping for and using products. Specific to social commerce, pre-purchase and post-purchase are often the focus, reflecting the view that consumer engagement in the content exchange can foster consumers perceptions of online shopping, influencing their purchase behaviours (e.g., Hajli, 2015; Ng, 2013; Prentice et al., 2020). In the pre-purchase stage, consumers play the focal role of information seeker and engage in product-related information searching and use before the actual purchase decision, with the goal of making informed product choices (Bronner & de Hoog, 2014; Pöyry et al., 2013); we call this social commerce information seeking. In the post-purchase stage, consumers play the focal role of information provider and engage in activities such as expressing their opinions about products, recommending products, and sharing shopping experiences, among others (Bugshan & Attar, 2020; Harris & Dennis, 2011; Kang & Johnson, 2013; T.-P. Liang et al., 2011; H. Zhang et al., 2014), and we call this social commerce information sharing. These two activities work mutually to ofer a better explanation of the social commerce phenomenon as a three fold process, as shown in Table 1.

## 3. Theory development and research model

In this section, we propose our conceptual model and describe social commerce adoption by defining social commerce information seeking and social commerce information sharing as a three-fold process in the social commerce environment. These then serve as the foundation of our research model.

## 4. Conceptual model

We note how social commerce deals with both IS adoption (namely social commerce adoption) and consumer purchase in the emerging online shopping environment and identify two well-established theories that are relevant to social commerce context efects: expectation-confirmation theory (ECT; Oliver, 1993) and the expectation-confirmation model and IS continuance (ECM-ISC; Bhattacherjee, 2001). Social commerce involves both IS adoption and purchasing behaviour; thus, combining the ECT and ECM-ISC frameworks could explain this emerging phenomenon. We incorporate two specific social commerce constructs into the underlying theoretical frameworks.

The ECT holds that consumers’ repurchase intentions are determined primarily by their satisfaction with prior use of products or services (Oliver, 1993), and the ECM-ISC holds that users’ IS continuance intentions are determined primarily by their satisfaction with prior IS usage (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Consistently, in our conceptual model, we posit that consumers’ satisfaction is positively related to their IS continuance intentions and repurchase intentions in the context of social commerce. Thus, in the following, we develop our social commerce model by integrating and extending these two frameworks from a three-fold process perspective (see, Figure 1). Furthermore, we extend perceived performance to the context of social commerce and operationalise them as consumers anticipated performance of social commerce prior to consuming through capturing consumers’ beliefs about the performance of social commerce in terms of improving online shopping outcomes (e.g., McKinney et al., 2002; Oliver & Burke, 1999). The underlying logic of our proposed framework of social commerce can be described as follows: First, consumers form their expectations (namely the perceived usefulness of social commerce) based on prior social commerce adoption (namely social commerce information seeking) before they purchase from traditional e-commerce sites (the pre-purchase stage). Second, consumers assess social commerce performance following actual purchase and determine the extent to which their original expectations are confirmed (the post-purchase stage). Third, their perceived level of confirmation determines their satisfaction level (Osatuyi et al., 2020), which thereafter forms repurchase intentions and IS continuance intentions (namely social commerce information-sharing intentions). Therefore, in our proposed model, we aim to investigate how social commerce can afect consumers’ behaviours and IS continuance at diferent purchase stages by combining the ECT and ECM-ISC frameworks. We describe our newly added constructs and the empirical research model in more detail in the following subsection.

## 5. Social commerce adoption

Consumer content sharing is an important pattern of social media that enables digital marketing to influence online consumer behaviour and serves as a marketing strategy to increase sales and brand and product awareness (Lin et al., 2019; Oh et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2014). Social media has changed online consumer behaviour in various phases of the purchase process, including pre-purchase behaviour (i.e., searching for product information and reviewing customer comments) and post-purchase behaviour (i.e., sharing product information via personal social media accounts). As such, consumer adoption of social media is the primary change in consumer behaviour in online shopping in those two purchase stages. Therefore, social commerce adoption illustrates the impacts of social media use on online consumer behaviour at the pre-purchase and post-purchase stages.

## 6. The pre-purchase stage

## 6.1. Social commerce information seeking as social commerce adoption

In the traditional e-commerce environment, productrelated information is usually generated by e-vendors and “consumed” by consumers. In this scenario, consumers usually use the same information – marketergenerated product information – to make prepurchase decisions. Social media opens new channels for consumers to acquire product-related information and assist with their product evaluations in the prepurchase stage. A variety of product-related information is generated and shared among many consumers on e-commerce websites, such as the quality of products and consumers’ usage experiences. Consumers consider this information more valuable in their purchase decisions than marketer-generated content (Trusov et al., 2009). Customer reviews have become an important resource for consumers to evaluate products in their purchase decision-making (Lin et al., 2019),<sup>1</sup> thus changing their behaviour in seeking product-related information when purchasing through e-commerce.

In addition, consumers can also receive productrelated information on social media. Consumers can be directed to e-commerce websites by clicking product-related posts on social media (J. Zhang & Mao, 2016). Consumers can also observe others’ purchase experiences and follow product recommendations on social media (Matook et al., 2015; T.-P. Liang et al., 2011), which also plays a role in consumer prepurchase behaviour. Social media features have afected consumers’ behaviour in terms of productrelation information seeking before they buy a product. Consumer-generated product-related information thus changes online consumer prepurchase behaviour in terms of acquiring productrelated information to assist with purchase decisions via social media content-sharing features. Overall, social media has become an attractive resource for obtaining information, and, in the pre-purchase stage, consumers play a primary role as information seekers in the social commerce environment (Prentice et al., 2020). We use social commerce information seeking to illustrate how social commerce adoption can change online consumer behaviour, owing to the integrated social media features in e-commerce.

## 6.1.1. Perceived usefulness of social commerce as expectation

In the ECT framework, expectation refers to consumers’ beliefs about the performance of products or services at the pre-purchase stage (Oliver, 1980, 1993), and it can be operationalised as the anticipated performance of products and services (e.g., Oliver & Burke, 1999). In other words, the ECT examines only the influences of consumers’ pre-purchase (ex-ante) expectations, not post-purchase (ex-post) expectations. In contrast, the ECM-ISC examines only users ex-post-expectations following a period of IS usage, and it is represented by perceived usefulness. In the social commerce context, consumers’ expectations include both pre-purchase (ex-ante) expectations and ex-post expectations following consumers’ prior social media usage.

With social commerce features, consumers have richer product information in their purchase decisionmaking than they do in purely e-commerce settings (e.g., Goraya et al., 2021; T.-P. Liang et al., 2011; H. Zhang et al., 2014). Social commerce can also serve as a channel for user interaction and to exchange user experiences, which can provide supplemental information for consumer decisions (Prentice et al., 2020; Shin, 2013). In addition, social commerce can help consumers browse between shops, thus providing them with many alternatives for discovering more shops in the large online marketplace (Stephen & Toubia, 2010). Therefore, social commerce benefits consumers in assisting with their purchase decisions and improving their shopping experience, fostering their belief that social commerce is useful for online shopping (Ryu & Park, 2020). Perceived usefulness is defined as “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance” (Davis, 1989, p. 320). Accordingly, we use the perceived usefulness of social commerce to illustrate consumers’ beliefs that using social commerce can enhance their online shopping outcomes, representing their anticipated performance of social commerce before purchasing products or services.

## 6.2. The post-purchase phase

## 6.2.1. Social commerce information sharing as social commerce adoption

Social media features provide multiple digital channels for consumers to review products, share product links, and communicate shopping experiences after completing transactions. Thus, consumers have been increasingly engaging in post-purchase activities and sharing product-related information over the long term, including expressing their opinions on the quality of products in the form of customer reviews, sharing their shopping information on social media, and recommending products to peer consumers and their “friends” on social media (e.g., Bugshan & Attar, 2020; Hudson et al., 2015; Laroche et al., 2013; Stephen & Toubia, 2010; T.-P. Liang et al., 2011; Zheng et al., 2015). In this scenario, consumers play the primary role of information providers who produce socia media content at the post-purchase stage. Social commerce thus creates an innovative environment supporting user interactions to improve shopping experiences and driving user engagement at the post-purchase stage.

Information sharing can also change the power relationship between consumers and e-vendors and put consumers in a better position in online shopping. As described by Lim (2020), information sharing can function as social activity when consumers share product information. During such a process, consumers exchange important resources by communicating product information with each other. Davlembayeva et al. (2020) also argued that consumers can engage in collaborations and receive reciprocation from each other. These studies indicate that consumers can assist with each other’s purchase decisions through informationsharing activities. We use social commerce information sharing to capture how social commerce adoption can afect consumers’ post-purchase behaviour, such as sharing product-related information and shopping experiences over the long term after a purchase.

In summary, we conclude that social commerce information seeking and social commerce information sharing are the revolution by which social media afects online consumer behaviour in the e-commerce setting, and, together, they comprise social commerce adoption.

## 7. Social commerce motivation

To understand how consumers are motivated to engage in social commerce, in our study we draw upon SDT (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Although many theories have been developed to explain individual motivation (Kanfer, 1990; Pinder, 1998), most of them treat motivation as a unitary concept difering in amount rather than orientation (Meyer et al., 2004). In contrast, SDT argues that motivation difers by the degree of autonomy based on the degree of regulation internalised (i.e., the degree to which the regulation is perceived to be autonomous). Therefore, we here apply SDT to examine how social commerce information seeking and sharing can be supported by diferent types of motivation.

## 7.1. Autonomous motivation of social commerce

According to SDT, motivation can be divided into two broad types based on the degree of autonomy: autonomous and controlled motivation (Gagné & Deci, 2005; Reinholt et al., 2011; Ryan & Deci, 2000). When individuals are autonomously motivated, they are more likely to understand the rationale for certain activities. In such a scenario, they often enjoy engaging in those activities and integrate them into their values. Thus, in our study, we define the autonomous motivation of social commerce as the degree to which consumers perceive social commerce behaviours as autonomous and self-determined. In social commerce, when consumers are autonomously motivated, they understand that interactions between consumers, such as sharing product information and purchase experiences, are helpful for others’ purchase decisions. In this scenario, consumers accept this rationale for interactions and integrate it into their own values. As a result, consumers usually expend considerable efort and show consistent commitment towards their interactions with other consumers (Deci & Ryan, 1991; Sheldon et al., 1997).

## 7.2. Controlled motivation of social commerce

In contrast, when control motivated, individuals feel pressured to obtain either external (e.g., money or high ranking) or internal (e.g., a good image) rewards (Ryan & Deci, 2000). In this context, individuals do not accept the rationale behind these activities and do not integrate those activities into their values. Individuals may thus engage in these activities simply to obtain the desired rewards and focus on short-term returns by minimising the efort spent. Thus, we define controlled motivation of social commerce as the degree to which consumers perceive social commerce behaviour as controlled. In social commerce, when control motivated, consumers may still interact with others and share their purchase experiences; however, their focus is to obtain more rewards instead of to provide more useful information to assist in others’ purchase decisions.

To summarise, consumers may interact with others in social commerce because of autonomous or controlled motivation. Although these two types of motivation may result in similar activities, individuals may engage in those activities with diferent orientations.

## 9. Hypotheses development

## 9.1. Pre-purchase stage

## 9.1.1. The efect of autonomous motivation and controlled motivation

Motivation reflects the extent to which individuals intend to act (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Regardless of being autonomous or control motivated, individuals may have similar strengths of intention to engage in certain activities (though with diferent orientations). In the previous literature, Wang (2016) demonstrated that autonomous and controlled motivation are positively related to browsing content on social media after crises. Y. Li et al. (2016) noted that perceived benefits are positively related to health information seeking. In the context of social commerce, consumers can be motivated by certain benefits (e.g., enjoyment for autonomous motivation or recognition for controlled motivation; Doha et al., 2019). When highly motivated, consumers are more likely to expend more efort and engage in information seeking.

H1a: Autonomous motivation of social commerce is positively related to social commerce information seeking.

H1b: Controlled motivation of social commerce is positively related to social commerce information seeking.

## 9.2. The efects of social commerce information seeking

Social commerce information seeking provides consumers with opportunities to experience social media features and interact with other consumers in diferent ways during their purchasing process. Several researchers have suggested that social commerce information seeking is helpful for consumers’ prepurchase decision-making, such as evaluating products/services, observing peer consumers’ purchase experiences, and following others’ product recommendations (Benlian et al., 2012; Goraya et al., 2021; Hajli, 2015; Trusov et al., 2009). For example, social media-enabled customer reviews can provide a trusty consumer-generated product/service assessment, which ultimately supports evaluations of products and services. Consumers can read useful comments, helpful stories, and other pieces of information that facilitate online purchase decision-making. All these factors can enhance consumers’ beliefs about the anticipated performance of social commerce to improve their shopping outcomes. Therefore, we hypothesise the following:

H2: Consumers’ social commerce information seeking is positively related to the perceived usefulness of social commerce.

In our study, we use perceived usefulness of social commerce to refer to the degree to which consumers perceive that using social commerce features will help with their purchases. For example, useful social commerce features can provide utilitarian value, which is positively related to consumers’ repeat purchase intentions (Chiu et al., 2014). Therefore, we argue that the perceived usefulness of social commerce has positive efects in driving consumers’ purchase intentions. According to the ECT, when perceived usefulness is high, consumers feel that social media features can meet their expectations and support their purchase process (Osatuyi et al., 2020), leading to purchase intention. In ECM-ISC, Bhattacherjee (2001) also suggested that perceived usefulness is positively related to IS continuance intention. Thus, our next hypothesis is as follows:

H3: Consumers’ perceived usefulness of social commerce is positively related to their purchase intention.

## 10. Purchase stage

It is well understood that individuals’ actual behaviour is positively afected by their behavioural intentions (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1977). In the IS discipline, prior studies have confirmed the positive relationship between users’ behavioural intentions and their actual technology use (Venkatesh et al., 2012). Similarly, consumer purchase intentions are positively associated with their purchase behaviours in the future. Therefore, we propose the following:

H4: Consumers’ purchase intention is positively related to their self-reported purchasing.

## 11. Post-purchase stage

The ECM-ISC suggests that the extent of confirmation is positively associated with the perceived usefulness of IS use (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Here, the argument is that individuals may initially perceive a low level of usefulness because they are not sure what to expect from the new IS. Later, the perception of low usefulness may be adjusted to be higher because of confirmation experiences. These arguments also suggest that after using social commerce features and perceiving these features as highly useful, individuals’ expectations towards social commerce features are more likely to be confirmed. Additionally, according to the ECT, using social media features will result in (ex-post) perceived usefulness of these social media features. Perceived usefulness can therefore refute or confirm consumers’ expectations regarding social media features. High perceived usefulness thus indicates that consumers’ expectations have been confirmed. Therefore, we hypothesise the following:

H5: Consumers’ perceived usefulness of social commerce is positively related to their extent of confirmation.

Both the ECT and ECM-ISC suggest that the extent of confirmation is positively related to satisfaction. Furthermore, the ECT asserts that consumers’ satisfaction can positively afect their repurchase intention, and the ECM-ISC claims that consumers’ satisfaction can positively afect their social commerce continuance decisions. The hypothesised relationships are also supported in other studies (Lin et al., 2017; Osatuyi et al., 2020; C. S. Lin et al., 2005) that empirically validate the relationships between confirmation and satisfaction, as well as satisfaction and IS continuance intention. Therefore, drawing from these two models, we propose the following:

H6: Consumers’ extent of confirmation is positively related to their satisfaction with an e-commerce website.

H7a: Consumers’ satisfaction is positively related to their repurchase intention.

H7b: Consumers’ satisfaction is positively related to their social commerce information sharing continued intention.

## 11.1. The efect of autonomous motivation and controlled motivation

Autonomous and controlled motivation can also lead to social commerce information seeking. Wang (2016) illustrated that autonomous and controlled motivation are positively related to sharing content on social media after crises. Here, content sharing in Wang (2016) is similar to social commerce information sharing. In the context of social commerce, consumers may be motivated by autonomous motivation or controlled motivation. When highly motivated, consumers are more likely to exert more efort and engage in information sharing.

H8a: Autonomous motivation of social commerce is positively related to one’s continued intention to share social commerce information seeking at the postpurchase stage.

H8b: Controlled motivation of social commerce is positively related to one’s continued intention to share social commerce information seeking at the postpurchase stage.

## 12. Research method

## 12.1. Data collection procedures and participants

To understand the impact of social media on purchase behaviour, we conducted two rounds of data collection with 1 month in between in 2017. This approach is consistent with the literature (Etter et al., 2014). A survey company maintaining national panels helped recruit participants. The survey company provided a certain amount of bonus to encourage participation, which could be converted to money. We used several approaches, such as quality assurance questions, to detect fraudulent or duplicate records. The survey company kept track of who filled in the survey through unique IDs from each participant. These IDs were also used to match those participating in both rounds of the survey.

We selected Amazon as the context of our study for several reasons. First, Amazon integrates social media features into its e-commerce site, which is consistent with the focus of our study. Second, Amazon is the most popular site, and it has been actively examined in the literature (Osatuyi et al., 2020). Because the settings across diferent social commerce sites are not always the same, we focused on Amazon rather than several social commerce sites (e.g., eBay) when adapting the measures and modified them to fit the research context of our study. For example, Amazon develops reviewer rankings to rank consumers who provide reviews, and such rankings can motivate consumers. We considered this ranking system when developing the measures for the variable of controlled motivation in our study. In addition, Amazon integrates Facebook, Twitter, and Pinterest, and customers can share product information to these sites from Amazon. We also used these three sites as a criterion when recruiting our participants.

For the first-round survey, we sent an invitation to collect constructs dealing with social commerce motivation and repurchase on Amazon. Participants would only qualify for the survey if they: 1) shopped at least once per month on average on Amazon.com; 2) often used Facebook, Twitter, or Pinterest; and 3) used certain social commerce features on Amazon.com. By using social commerce features, we mean that participants should have either 1) read other customers’ ratings and reviews, 2) rated and commented on products purchased on Amazon, or 3) shared product information on Facebook, Twitter, or Pinterest.

In total, we received 903 responses. Approximately 1 month later, we conducted the second-round survey to collect participants’ purchase behaviour in the past month and constructs dealing with post-purchase. We invited those who took the first-round survey and asked them to complete the survey based on their experiences in the previous month. On average, participants spent 150.54 dollars (SD 281.28) and purchased 6.08 items (SD 8.23) on Amazon during a 1-month period. In total, 408 participants responded to the second-round survey, resulting in a response rate of 45.18%. There were no significant diferences regarding demographic variables (e.g., age, gender, and education) between those who responded and those who did not. Only those who completed both rounds of the survey were included for the following analysis. Most participants were White Americans. More than half of participants had associate degrees or above. About half of participants were older than

50. Participants had shopped at least four times during the previous 3 months on Amazon. The average number of years of shopping on Amazon is 6.99 (SD 4.10), and the average number of years of using social media is 6.45 (SD 3.22). The demographic data for the participants are shown in Appendix A.

## 13. Measurement

We adapted our measures from the previous literature (refer to Appendix B for complete measurements). Specifically, we adapted items of autonomous and controlled motivation from Wang (2014) and then modified them to fit the research context of this study. We adapted items of perceived usefulness of social commerce from Bhattacherjee (2001) and (Davis, 1989). We adapted items of purchase intention and repurchase intention from Coyle and Thorson (2001). We adapted items of confirmation from McKinney et al. (2002) and D. J. Kim et al. (2009). We adapted items of social commerce satisfaction from Bhattacherjee (2001). We developed the items of social commerce information seeking/sharing from T.-P. Liang et al. (2011) to fit the research context of our study.

We measured all items on a 7-point Likert scale. Self-reported purchase was measured with the number of items purchased on Amazon during the previous month. We used formative constructs to measure social commerce information seeking/sharing, such that these two constructs could capture consumers behaviour on the sites of e-vendors as well as those on social media. Here, we measured self-reported purchase and constructs to do with post-purchase (i.e., confirmation, social commerce satisfaction, information sharing, and repurchase intention) in the secondround survey, whereas the other variables were collected in the first-round survey. On Amazon, customer reviews are generated by customers with purchasing experience (i.e., spend at least \$50 with a valid credit or debit card during the past 12 months)<sup>2</sup> Not all participants may be eligible for submitting reviews, so we focused on consumers’ willingness to share information rather than their actual sharing behaviours at the post-purchase stage.

## 14. Data analysis and results

Because we measured constructs dealing with social commerce motivation and pre-purchase in the same round (i.e., the first round) of the survey, we assessed the potential for common method bias (CMB) with a pooled sample (Podsakof et al., 2003). First, we conducted a Harmon one-factor analysis. The results showed that five factors were present, with no single factor accounting for a majority of variance and significant loading (at the p <10 level) for all items.

Second, we included a common method factor including all the principal factors’ indicators, and we calculated the variances of indicators explained by the principal factor and by the method following the procures described by H. Liang et al. (2007). Our results showed that the average substantively explained variance of the indicator wa 85, whereas the average method-based variance was 009. The ratio of substantive variance to method variance was around 99:1. Also, most loadings from the method factor were not significant. We followed similar procedures to assess CMB for constructs dealing with post-purchase measured in the second-round survey, achieving similar results. Therefore, we concluded that CMB was unlikely to be a concern in our study.

We tested our model using partial least squares (PLS). We used SmartPLS (Ringle et al., 2005) with the bootstrap re-sampling method (using 1000 samples) to determine the significance of the paths. We chose PLS because it is compatible with formative measurements (Chin et al., 2003). In our study, we measured social commerce information seeking and information sharing as formative constructs, easily accomplishing this with PLS.

We first evaluated the measurement model. As shown in Appendix C, each item loaded significantly on its respective construct, and none of the loadings were below 0.50 (Hulland, 1999). The composite reliabilities (CRs) and Cronbach’s alpha values were over 0.70, and the average variance extracted (AVE) was over 0.50 (Appendix C). Therefore, convergent validity is supported (Gefen & Straub, 2005). We also confirmed discriminant validity by ensuring that for each construct, the square root of its AVE exceeded all correlations between that factor and any other construct (Appendix D). Therefore, our measures demonstrated good psychometric properties.

## 15. Discussion

Overall, our research clarifies how consumer motivation influences social commerce behaviour, and we examine the efects of social commerce behaviour on consumers’ purchase behaviour. ## 16. Theoretical implications

## 16.1. Social commerce – a new research stream

In our study, we developed a new research framework of social commerce focused on investigating the joint impact of social media and e-commerce on consumer behaviour. Our framework serves as the foundation for future work in the application of social commerce and suggests that consumers engage in social commerce activities in three purchase stages, as well as that social commerce information seeking (prepurchase behaviour) and social commerce information sharing (post-purchase behaviour) are both drivers of consumer (repurchase. At the pre-purchase stage, we conceptualised social commerce information seeking as behaviours using social commerce to observe product-related information in various formats. In such a context, consumers play the role of information seeker. At the post-purchase stage, we conceptualised social commerce information sharing as behaviours using social commerce to provide product-related information based on consumers’ assessments of products and usage experiences. In such a context, consumers play the role of information provider.

Previous researchers have commonly addressed social commerce behaviour on a single platform such as social media and have not specified consumer activities in diferent purchase stages (e.g., Goraya et al., 2021; T.-P. Liang et al., 2011; H. Zhang et al., 2014). Although these scholars captured important social commerce activities in the social media-based social commerce environment, they overlooked the fact that the impacts of social commerce on consumer behaviour may vary at diferent purchase stages. Our study is among the first to distinguish consumer social commerce behaviour at diferent purchase stages and extend our understanding of social commerce behaviour as a three-fold (pre-purchase, purchase, and post-purchase) process involving multiple platforms. From a consumer perspective, our study captures the nature of social media use for online shopping purposes based on how consumers interact with social commerce systems and their peer consumers at the three purchase stages. It shows that advanced IT can be used by consumers to support their purchase decisions and trigger purchase behaviour, facilitating an innovative approach for e-commerce to better engage customers and increase sales.

In addition, our study indicates that consumers may play dual roles in social commerce: as information seekers at the pre-purchase stage and as information providers at the post-purchase stage. Motivated by the dual roles that consumers can play in social commerce, we develop and validate measures of social commerce information seeking (prepurchase behaviour) and social commerce information sharing (post-purchase behaviour), which again reflect the nature of social media use for online shopping purchases. The dual roles enable consumers to enjoy the purchase process, enhancing the hedonic component of the social commerce system. This finding extends our understanding of hedonic shopping from traditional e-commerce to social commerce and enriches our understanding of consumer behaviour as playing a dual role (Doha et al., 2019; Overby & Lee, 2006; Van der Heijden, 2004).

Finally, our study demonstrates the efect of social commerce adoption on purchases. By conducting two rounds of surveys, we showed that using social commerce and engaging in information seeking and sharing can increase consumers’ (repurchase intentions and their subsequent purchase behaviour. These results show that social commerce is indeed important and relevant when examining consumers’ behaviour in social commerce. This finding enriches our understanding of the relationship between purchase intention and purchase behaviour, which is important in the IS discipline.

## 16.2. Extending SDT theory – social commerce motivation

In our study, we applied SDT to the context of social commerce and clarified consumers’ social commerce motivation. We advanced SDT theory by conceptualising two types of social commerce motivation and their diferent influences on consumers’ pre-purchase and post-purchase behaviours. Our study thus enriches the understanding of social commerce motivation and social commerce use.

Based on SDT, we argue that consumers’ social commerce motivation can be divided into autonomous and controlled motivation. Although autonomous and controlled motivation both have positive efects on information seeking and sharing, the efects of autonomous and controlled motivation are not consistent, and they have diferent levels of importance. We thus examined the diferential efects of autonomous motivation and controlled motivation on social commerce information seeking and information sharing following Keil et al. (2000) in Appendix E. The results show that controlled motivation has greater importance when consumers are seeking information (in the role of information seekers) than autonomous motivation. In contrast, autonomous motivation has greater importance when consumers share information (in the role of information providers) compared to autonomous motivation. These results indicate that autonomous and controlled motivation have diferent orientations, even with similar levels of strength. Indeed, information sharing can be challenging because consumers need to organise product-related information in a meaningful way (Wang, 2014). When people are autonomously motivated, they are more likely to expend considerable efort and engage in information sharing (Wang, 2014). These results are also consistent with the previous literature showing that autonomous and controlled motivation have diferential efects in promoting subsequent behaviour. For example, Wang (2016) illustrated that although autonomous and controlled motivation are both positively related to sharing content via social media after crises, sharing content can only help individuals better understand crises under autonomous motivation. Overall, these results support SDT in the context of social commerce and indicate that consumers’ motivation should not be treated as a unitary concept. Instead, consumers have various types of motivation that are supported by diferent factors and lead to inconsistent consequences.

## 17. Implications for practice

Given the importance of social media in business transformation (Aral et al., 2013), our study has important implications for practice. First, our proposed model of social commerce demonstrates how social media can afect consumer behaviour and purchase decisions in the e-commerce setting. It provides practitioners with an enhanced understanding of the application of social commerce to increase customer engagement and purchases. Thus, the model can inform practitioners seeking to increase sales by integrating social media features and building an efective social commerce environment. For example, because social commerce information seeking formulates consumers’ perceived usefulness of social commerce, practitioners could better engage their customers in online shopping activities to create a hedonic shopping environment. Because social commerce behaviour can indeed lead to consumers’ subsequent purchase behaviour, e-vendors could increase sales by creating a social commerce environment where customers can exchange information. For example, instead of just allowing consumers who make purchases to contribute reviews, social commerce sites can also develop a separate area allowing consumers to ask questions. Then answers from other consumers can be provided to resolve these queries. Consumers can also be allowed to share useful information on social media to further facilitate information exchange.

Second, our study indicates that e-vendors can support diferent types of social commerce motivation. For controlled motivation, e-vendors such as

Amazon could provide external rewards to motivate consumers. Besides reviewer rankings, e-vendors could also distribute gift cards or discount ofers periodically to recognise reviewers (e.g., top reviewers per month). To avoid possible bias in the reviews, the external rewards should be provided based upon the quality of reviews (e.g., usefulness and honest reviews) rather than whether the reviews are positive, so that other consumers can have higher trust beliefs towards product reviews and e-vendors.<sup>3</sup> This will also help other consumers make more informal and accurate purchase decisions through improving the credibility of customer reviews (e.g., Jabr, 2021). Furthermore, e-vendors could assist consumers to gain better recognition among their peers. For example, e-vendors could integrate with popular social media sites such as Facebook and allow consumers to log in with their social media accounts so that their postings could be more easily recognised by their friends.

For autonomous motivation, e-vendors could establish guidelines regarding how consumers should interact with one another. Moderators could also be used to facilitate consumer interactions. After consumers post reviews, e-vendors can also let consumers know periodically how other consumers think about their reviews and how they find these reviews helpful to their purchases. Given that the efect of autonomous motivation on information sharing is stronger and can be sustained without external rewards, consumers’ autonomous motivation should be encouraged over the long run.

## 18. Limitations and opportunities for future research

Our study has several limitations. First, we collected data only from consumers of a single e-vendor (i.e., Amazon). It is possible that consumers using other e-vendors behave diferently, and the efects of social commerce behaviour on purchase behaviour are not fully consistent with those identified in our study. Second, not all participants who took the first-round survey completed the second-round survey. Although we did not consider that there was any diference regarding demographic background between those who completed the second-round survey and those who did not, it is still possible that some participants chose not to participate in the second-round survey for potential reasons that may have afected our results. Third, our study focuses on consumers’ willingness to share information. We admit that those who actually share information are probably fewer than those who are willing to share information. Future studies can focus on consumers’ actual information sharing behaviour. Furthermore, we only focused on consumers from Amazon.com, which are mainly from the United States. Consumer motivation may be supported diferently for consumers from other countries with various cultures.

Future studies could extend our research in several ways. First, future scholars might examine consumer motivation in greater detail. Specifically, our study divides consumers’ extrinsic motivation into autonomous versus controlled motivation. Future researchers could examine all four types of extrinsic motivation, including external, introjected, identified, and integrated motivation. Studies could also be conducted to investigate consumer behaviour across diferent social commerce sites (e.g., eBay). Because we conducted our study pre COVID-19, future researchers may explore how consumers’ social commerce behaviours change post COVID-19 and the impacts on e-commerce purchase. Given that consumers rely more on online shopping since COVID-19, the role of consumers’ social commerce behaviours may become even more important in driving their purchase decisions. Third, future investigators could also extend our study by examining how various factors moderate the relationship between motivation and information seeking and sharing, as well as the relationship between information seeking and sharing and purchase decisions.

Future researchers can also examine the purchase process of consumers across diferent age groups. About half of our participants were 50 or older. It shows that older consumers can also be active consumers of social commerce. One the other hand, older consumers may engage in social commerce behaviours diferently than younger consumers. For example, product reviews can not only be texts and pictures but also include YouTube videos and Instagram TV. It is possible that the impacts of diferent formats of reviews are not consistent across consumers from various generations.<sup>4</sup> Consumers of various generations can also use social commerce sites such as Amazon and Facebook in diferent ways. Future studies are thus needed to examine patterns of customer engagement across diferent generations of consumers.

## 19. Conclusions

As e-vendors increasingly integrate social media into their sites, consumers engage in information seeking and sharing to support their purchase decisions (Goraya et al., 2021). However, few scholars have examined social commerce adoption at diferent purchase stages. In our work, we proposed a theoretical model of social commerce illustrating how consumers can use social commerce features for online shopping. The model elaborates social commerce motivation, social commerce behaviour, and social commerce consumer behaviour from a consumer perspective. Based on survey data collected from Amazon consumers, our study demonstrates that consumers’ motivation has a significant efect on their information seeking and sharing behaviours, which in turn determine their purchasing behaviour. Furthermore, whereas controlled motivation is more important in influencing information seeking at the pre-purchase stage, autonomous motivation plays a more vital role in shaping information sharing at the post-purchase stage. Future authors could extend our study by investigating how consumers behave across diferent social commerce sites.
