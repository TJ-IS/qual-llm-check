# How mindfulness decreases cyberloafing at work: a dual-system theory perspective

Qian Chen<sup>a</sup>, Yeming Gong <sup>b</sup>, Yaobin Lu<sup>c</sup> and Patrick Y.K. Chau<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Huazhong Agricultural University, Wuhan, Hubei, China; <sup>b</sup>Emlyon Business School, Ecully, France; <sup>c</sup>Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, Hubei, China; <sup>d</sup>University of Nottingham Ningbo China, Ningbo, Zhejiang, China

## ABSTRACT

Cyberloafing is a prevalent phenomenon in organisations that may cause serious problems. In previous studies, cyberloafing has been studied as planned and intentional behaviour. Interestingly, we have observed that cyberloafing frequently happens when employees use smartphones to assist with work tasks, but they may not necessarily intend to engage in this activity. We distinguish between active and passive cyberloafing and focus on studying the latter. Based on dual-systems theory, we introduce the construct of mindfulness in the exploration of the antecedences and internal mechanism of passive cyberloafing. We conducted a research survey with employees who use smartphones to assist with work tasks and collected 474 valid questionnaires to examine our research model. The results showed that dual systems are associated with cyberloafing by influencing mind-wandering, and mindfulness significantly decreased mind-wandering when using a smartphone. This study analyses cyberloafing from an innovative neuroscience perspective and contributes to the literature by proposing a new classification of cyberloafing behaviour, reveals the internal mechanism of how dual systems impact an individual’s behaviour, and introduces mindfulness as an antecedent factor of dual systems.

## 1. Introduction

In the context of IT consumerisation (Steelman et al., 2016), more employees are using smartphones to assist in completing their work tasks in organisations (Crossler et al., 2014). Recent surveys also show that 84% of workers use smartphones for personal matters during working hours (Deloitte, 2020) and waste an average of 56 minutes per day during working time, nearly five hours a week, using their smartphones for non-work activities. Smartphone use has become the largest distractor of employees, and 55% of employees have reported they are routinely distracted by their phones (Business News Daily, 2020). Cyberloafing on smartphones (or mobile-loafing) is “the voluntary act of employees using smartphones for non-work-related purposes during working hours” (Xu & Ding, 2014, p. 2). Previous studies agree that cyberloafing is counterproductive and further describe it as malingering at work (Khansa et al., 2017), an abuse of an organisation’s internet resources and a workplace production deviance (Lim & Chen, 2012), a deviant behaviour, antisocial online behaviour (Moor & Anderson, 2019) and misuse of internet resources (Piccoli & Ives, 2003). Specifically, cyberloafing with various content and activities will lead to negative consequences of difering severity (Burleson & Greenbaum, 2019), such as work productivity loss, threats to network security and strains on organisational bandwidth (Vitak et al., 2011). Therefore, organisations are concerned with their workers’ loafing problems and are trying to find efective ways to solve them (Hensel & Kacprzak, 2020).

Previous studies have explored the antecedents of cyberloafing from the perspective of an individual’s personality traits, such as the Big Five, self-control and self-eficacy (Gökçearslan et al., 2016; Mercado et al., 2017; O’Neill et al., 2014); attitudes (Askew et al., 2014; Mercado et al., 2017; Sheikh et al., 2015); and context factors (Khansa et al., 2017; Lim, 2002; Mercado et al., 2017; Ugrin & Michael Pearson, 2013). Using various theories, such as person-job fit theory, social learning theory, social exchange theory and the theory of planned behaviour, previous studies have deemed cyberloafing an intentional behaviour (Venkatraman et al., 2018) that is afected by the environment, attitude, and individual personalities. The theories and antecedents described by previous studies are listed in Table A.1 of Appendix A.

Previous studies have regarded cyberloafing as an intentional, deliberate and active behaviour of employees. However, some employees engage in cyberloafing unconsciously and uncontrollably. In many cases, cyberloafing may not be the result of social learning and imitating the behaviour of others, a planned behaviour or a retaliation caused by a perception of a lack of organisational justice but rather could be an impulsive, automated behaviour performed without awareness. Employees can passively engage in cyberloafing. For example, after using a smartphone to solve a work problem, such as sending or recording workrelated information, an employee may not immediately continue their tasks but rather open Facebook to check messages or automatically open a news report app. We describe this kind of activity as passive cyberloafing, and it is as if the phone has an invisible power over an individual. From a research perspective, we wondered exactly what was causing this apparent unconscious behaviour, and why it appeared to be out of control in workplaces. There are few studies in the literature that describe this type of passive cyberloafing behaviour, and existing theories, such as the theory of planned behaviour (TPB), cannot adequately explain it. Therefore, we looked to the field of cognitive neuroscience in order to analyse employees cyberloafing behaviour based on the characteristics and functions of the brain. Cognitive neuroscience provides a new perspective from which to understand people’s behaviour, which may be afected by physiological factors of brain structure and functions instead of being afected by attitude or intention. In this study, we use dual-system theory (Lieberman, 2007) to analyse cyberloafing behaviour. This theory is often used to explain an individual’s unplanned, impulsive and automatic behaviour (Polites et al., 2018; Turel et al., 2015).

Furthermore, in order to consider how to reduce cyberloafing from a cognitive neuroscience perspective, we introduce the concept of mindfulness, which is an important emerging research topic in the information systems (IS) field (Dernbecher & Beck, 2017; Thatcher et al., 2018). In Dernbecher and Beck’s (2017) review work of mindfulness in IS research, they highlight the importance of the mindfulness concept in investigating IS phenomena and call for more future IS research that uses the concept. Researchers have found that mindfulness is positively related to a range of psychological health outcomes (Hulsheger et al., 2014; Van de Veer et al., 2016), the innovative ability of organisations (Teo et al., 2011) and job satisfaction (Hülsheger et al., 2013). The Economist (2013) suggests that mindfulness will help decrease the problematic information technology (IT) use of individuals. As such, mindfulness may also be an efective way to reduce employees’ cyberloafing in organisations.

Because we have observed the widespread phenomenon of passive cyberloafing, this study aims to determine the influencing mechanism for it in order to find ways to decrease it. This research complements the existing cyberloafing literature, which has primarily studied active cyberloafing. This research also empirically examines the impact of mindfulness on personal IT use and enriches the research on mindfulness within the IS literature. Specifically, we propose two research questions: (1) What are the antecedents of employees’ passive cyberloafing at work? (2) From the perspective of the dual-system theory, will mindfulness decrease cyberloafing? If yes, what is the internal mechanism?

This study makes several theoretical and practical contributions. Regarding the theoretical contributions, first, this study proposes a new classification of cyberloafing. Employees’ cyberloafing during work time can be an intentional, deliberate, and planned behaviour, and it can also be impulsive, automatic, and unconscious behaviour. The former is active cyberloafing, which has been primarily researched in previous studies. The latter is passive cyberloafing, which has been under-studied. In the research context of using a smartphone to assist in work tasks, passive cyberloafing happens frequently. This new classification contributes to the concept of cyberloafing. Second, the current study contributes to dual-system theory by explaining how an imbalance in the internal mechanism can be associated with ones’ behaviour. Previous studies have indicated a causal relationship between the dual systems and behaviour, but there was a lack of study of the internal mechanism of how this happens. Our results show that the dual systems can describe reactive behaviour through mind-wandering. Third, by introducing mindfulness as an influencing factor, this research shows how mindfulness can help decrease impulsive and automatic behaviours, which are due to an imbalance in dual system processing, revealing the internal mechanism of how mindfulness works. In addition, by examining the impact of mindfulness in the dual systems, mind-wandering and cyberloafing, we respond to researchers’ call for more application of this concept in IS research (Dernbecher & Beck, 2017) and enrich its research on personal IT device use in the IS field. Regarding the practical implications, this study helps managers understand the phenomenon of passive cyberloafing and suggests that organisations provide a mindfulness training programme to employees, which can improve their mindfulness ability and thereby reduce the cyberloafing problem.

## 2. Conceptual and theoretical background

According to dual-system theory, there are two systems in the human brain: the impulsive system and the reflective system. The two systems work together in influencing human behaviours (Noël et al., 2006). In terms of information processing, the impulsive system is responsible for the emergence of an individual’s spontaneous behaviour, and the reflective system helps an individual in regulating progress towards goals and self-control (Hofmann et al., 2009). Based on cognitive neuroscience studies, the two systems roughly correspond to diferent regions of an individual’s brain and afect an individual’s behaviour (Bechara et al., 2005). However, the two systems also act together, associate with each other, and impact human decision-making simultaneously (Ouimet et al., 2012). Therefore, dual-system theory can be used to explain human behaviour. Studies regarding how people make decisions have promoted the development of dual-system theory (Leaptrott, 2008), and the perspective of dual-system theory has supplemented the findings of research on behaviour (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016).

As stated previously, the impulsive system is afective and automatic, while the reflective system is purposeful and rational. The impulsive and reflective systems are distinct in four aspects – consciousness, evolution, functional characteristics and individual diferences (Evans, 2008). The two systems can be characterised by a group of opposing concepts, such as automatic afective reactions versus self-control, habit versus self-regulation and heuristic processing versus systematic processing (Chu et al., 2015; Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016). The concepts represented in dual-system theory are listed in Table 1. Although the concepts may be expressed in a variety of ways, the core meaning focuses on the processing mode of the impulsive system as fast and efortless, while that of the reflective system is thoughtful and systematic (Brown & Ryan, 2004).

From the perspective of cognitive science, Whether a person can control themselves depends on which system can “win the race” when the responses from the two systems would conflict, because the impulsive system is influenced by arousal, while the reflective system is influenced by factors, such as motivation and cognitive load (Ouimet et al., 2012). The occurrence of impulsive, unplanned and unconscious behaviour is the result of an imbalance between one’s impulsive and reflective systems; that is, the persistent urges generated by the impulsive system are stronger than the motivations or control ability generated by the reflective system (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016). To sum up, when one’s impulsive system overrides the reflective system, inappropriate behaviour may arise. Therefore, dual-system theory can be applied to explain various inappropriate behaviours, such as addiction (Dernbecher & Beck, 2017) and the problematic use of social network websites (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016).

The current study uses cognitive emotion occupation (CEP) with smartphone use and cognitive behaviour control (CBC) over smartphone use to reflect the functions of the impulsive system and the reflective system, respectively. CEP is generated from the impulsive system, and it refers to “obsessions and persistent thoughts about the behaviour” (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016, p. 1901), such as obsessions and persistent thoughts about using a smartphone. CBC is generated from the reflective system, which means “one’s ability to inhibit, interrupt or change desired and impulsive cognitive and behavioural response like thoughts and behaviours” (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016, p. 1902).

In this study, we explore the user’s passive cyberloafing. Employees may not actively choose to engage in cyberloafing. They are supposed to use smartphones to assist in work tasks. However, they may turn to cyberloafing unconsciously and automatically while using their smartphones for work. The phenomenon is related to mind-wandering, which means “a fluctuation of attention away from a task to unrelated concerns” (Mrazek et al., 2013, p. 1). In mind-wandering, an individual’s thoughts drift when they are using a smartphone. They initially use a smartphone to assist in work tasks. However, in the process of using the devices, their thoughts automatically drift to a non-work-related use. An individual’s attention shifts away from the primary task at hand, and it occurs without intention or awareness, which are the key features of mind-wandering (Smallwood & Schooler, 2006). Mind-wandering is different from distraction. The former is a mind state, while the latter is an avoidance-oriented response (Davis et al., 2002; Endler & Parker, 1994). Distraction can be caused by external stimuli unrelated to the task as well as generated by a stimulus associated with mind-wandering (Forster, 2013).

Extensive previous research has demonstrated the negative impact of mind-wandering on performance, such as in reading comprehension, task-relevant processing and the use of working memory (Mooneyham & Schooler, 2013). In mind-wandering, when using a smartphone, an individual forgets the original purpose of using the device, and their attention automatically drifts to habitual phone behaviours, such as checking social media, online shopping and reading online news with a mobile application; in a work context, this behaviour can be characterised as cyberloafing.

The reasons for mind-wandering include taskrelated factors as well as individual-related factors. From the perspective of task-related factors, diferent tasks require diferent cognitive resources and attention levels. When individuals perceive tasks as easy or well-learned, completing the tasks would occupy limited executive resources in their brains and thus can easily be dominated by unrelated thoughts (Beier & Oswald, 2012). From the perspective of individual characteristics, executive control theory explains that an individual’s success in accomplishing tasks lies in their executive control, which is the ability to control attentional resources when pursuing goals and completing tasks, especially in the face of disturbances and distracting stimuli. Mind-wandering is caused by a failure to maintain attention towards the current task (McVay & Kane, 2010).

In this study, mind-wandering, which occurs during the process of using a smartphone to assist one’s work, is related to an individual’s executive ability. A smartphone is a source of stimuli, and the mere presence of a smartphone can lead to distraction (Ward et al., 2017). Using a smartphone to help accomplish work tempts an individual to use their smartphone for a non-work-related purpose. According to executive control theory, in this situation, individuals with higher executive control will manage their attention resources better and draw their attention away from their smartphones and back towards work. In contrast, individuals whose executive control abilities are weak will experience more mind-wandering.

In this research context, the employees intend to use their smartphones to execute work tasks. However, mind-wandering and passive cyberloafing occur unconsciously, automatically, and impulsively. Dual-system theory can used to explain this behaviour. The imbalance of CEP and CBC functions helps us understand the reasons and internal mechanisms that drive automatic and impulsive behaviours. Therefore, dual-system theory is suitable for understanding an employee’s mind-wandering and cyberloafing behaviour in the process of using a smartphone. This study also used mind-wandering as a mediator to explain the internal psychology mechanism associated with cyberloafing. Through its impact on mind-wandering, the imbalance of CEP and CBC was related to the behaviour of passive cyberloafing.

Because passive cyberloafing is not triggered by an individual’s subjective intention but is related to the imbalance of the dual systems in their brain, we try to find a solution to reduce passive cyberloafing from the perspective of neuroscience. After reviewing the literature on how brain functions change people’s behaviours, we have found numerous clinical reports and empirical studies that demonstrated mindfulness can have a positive efect on chronic physiological and mental diseases; can significantly change mental constructs; improve people’s attention, memory and emotional control; can increase their self-acceptance and self-compassion and can enhance their psychological well-being (Alberts & Hülsheger, 2015). Also, the topic of mindfulness has attracted increasing attention in the fields of management, IS, and marketing due to its significant impact on user behaviour.

Specifically, mindfulness is“receptive attention to and awareness of present events and experience”(Brown et al., 2007, p. 212). It is fundamentally a way of being in the present moment with no judgment (Alberts & Hülsheger, 2015). The concept of mindfulness has been a central element of the Buddhist tradition. In the Buddhist view, mindfulness is a key to achieving spiritual liberation (Chaskalson & Hadley, 2015). Through practicing mindfulness, people can observe, understand, accept and let go of pain, thereby gaining eternal joy and peace of mind.

The business world also recognises the importance of mindfulness. Many organisations have introduced mindfulness training programmes to improve employees’ physical and mental health and increase their creativity, work and decision-making eficiency. For example, Monsanto has conducted mindfulness training courses, and Google has launched the SIY Project (the full title is “Search Inside Yourself: Mindfulness-Based Emotional Intelligence”; Bush, 2015). Since Weick and Roberts (1993) first introduced mindfulness in the management research field, a growing body of management literature has studied the impact of mindfulness on various aspects within organisations (Weick & Roberts, 1993), including employees’ work-life balance, job burnout, work engagement, working memory and work performance (Choi & Leroy, 2015).

In addition, mindfulness has also attracted researchers’ attention in the IS field. Studies on mindfulness in the IS field focus on the individual, collective and organisational level (Dernbecher & Beck, 2017). Research themes on the individual level include IT interruptions (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018), individual training to resist phishing attacks (Jensen et al., 2017), information system use (Butler & Gray, 2006; Maier et al., 2019) and technology acceptance (Sun & Fang, 2010). Collective mindfulness research topics in the IS field include risk control and mitigation in complex socio-technical systems (Carlo et al., 2004) and organisations acting in digital domains to achieve high reliability (Salovaara et al., 2019). Organisational mindfulness research themes in the IS field involve the efect of mindfulness on organisational agility (Lee et al., 2020), business process management in the context of digital transformation (Baiyere et al., 2020) and organisations’ mindful adoption of an agile development approach (Cram & Newell, 2016). In these various research contexts, mindfulness is related to specific positive outcomes, such as better individual performance (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018), high team decision quality (Curtis et al., 2017) and the reliable performance of an information system (Butler & Gray, 2006). We reviewed the above studies on mindfulness in the IS field and state the level of analysis, research topic, the definition of mindfulness, role of mindfulness, related concepts of variables and scale used in the studies in Table B.2 of Appendix B.

Although cyberloafing is a widespread phenomenon, the efect of mindfulness on cyberloafing is under-studied. This study argues that cyberloafing may be regarded not as a deliberate and intended behaviour but rather as a passive, unconscious, unplanned and impulsive behaviour. The concept of mindfulness highlights one’s clear-minded attention to and awareness of the present moment, including one’s inner mind, thoughts and the external environment (Quaglia et al., 2015). Therefore, introducing mindfulness to this research context can help us explore the causes of passive cyberloafing and methods to mitigate it. Regarding the attributes of mindfulness, previous studies mainly define its attributes from three perspectives: mindfulness is an ability (Dernbecher & Beck, 2017), a trait or a state. As an ability, mindfulness is the ability to be present or focus on current objects through attention and awareness at the moment (Brown et al., 2007; Weick & Putnam, 2006) or the “ability to dispassionately observe the experience of the present moment with non-judgmental openness”(Baer et al., 2009, p. 3). As a trait, mindfulness is a long-lasting dispositional attribute that varies in individuals and i similar to other personality traits, such as extraversion or neuroticism (Good et al., 2016). As a state, mindfulness is a condition “of alertness and lively awareness” (Langer, 1989, p. 138). The ability of mindfulness can be gained through training or practice. People who achieve a relatively high ability of mindfulness or have mindfulness as a trait can more easily attain a mindful state and obtain it faster than others (Dernbecher & Beck, 2017). In the 20 studies on mindfulness in the IS field that we examined (as shown in Table B.1 in Appendix B), 17 studies regarded mindfulness as an ability that can be strengthened and changed, and eight of the studies noted the multiple characteristics of mindfulness, including being considered as an ability, state or trait.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Figure 1 shows our research model, which explains how employees cyberloaf on a smartphone when using it as a tool to accomplish work or learning tasks and how mindfulness can reduce such behaviour. We hypothesise that mind-wandering when using a smartphone will be positively associated with cyberloafing, and occurrences of mind-wandering will be the result of the combination of the strength of the CEP and the weakness of CBC. We also hypothesised that mindfulness will be inversely related to mindwandering by decreasing the efect of CEP and increasing the efect of CBC.

## 3.1. Mind-wandering when using a smartphone and cyberloafing

The smartphone is a highly personalised IT device used in various contexts. As organisations generally allow employees to bring their personal smartphones to work, the phenomenon of cyberloafing on smartphones during work time has increased (Xu & Ding, 2014). This study shows that a smartphone can be a stimulus that triggers distraction; a smartphone can occupy people’s limited cognitive resources, leaving fewer for their inthe-moment decision-making (Ward et al., 2017). Mind-wandering is the state in which an individual’s thoughts drift away from the task at hand to unrelated things (Smallwood et al., 2012; Smallwood & Schooler, 2006). When using a smartphone to handle work tasks, an employee who is in a mind-wandering state temporarily forgets their initial work purpose and engage in non-work-related smartphone use. A smartphone redirects one’s attention and triggers taskunrelated behaviour (Ward et al., 2017). According to an associative network memory model (Chu et al., 2015), the current behaviour of using a smartphone is associated with past experiences of non-work-related use. An internal stimulus would make an individual unconsciously turn to social media or other work task-unrelated applications. If mind-wandering does not happen, that is, an individual’s mind is not diverted by a non-work-related smartphone-using memory, the cyberloafing behaviour would not happen. ## 3.2. Dual system model and mind-wandering

The dual-system theory indicates that two brain systems work simultaneously when people execute tasks or make a decision. The two systems represent two processing modes, and the operation of the reflective system relies on one’s cognitive ability (Dernbecher & Beck, 2017). As a result, the two systems have diferent impacts on mindwandering when using a smartphone. We next describe how the dual system literature describes mindwandering. Based on Turel and Qahri-Saremi (2016), we adapted constructs to measure impulsive and reflective systems with regard to our context, which were cognitive-emotional preoccupation (CEP) with smartphone use and cognitive-behavioural control (CBC) over smartphone use. The two constructs were validated in the context of the problematic use of social networking software (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016), which is related to the context of inappropriate use of a smartphone.

## 3.2.1. CEP with smartphone use and mind-wandering

The impulsive system can be regarded as a simple associative network. Once an element is activated, other related elements in the network will be rapidly activated (McVay & Kane, 2010). CEP of the impulsive system represents obsessive and persistent thoughts about a specific behaviour that people have. In this research context, when an individual uses a smartphone, their brain systems will automatically associate the device with past non-work-related smartphone use, such as checking Facebook (Bechara, 2005). The more an individual regularly uses an app like Facebook, the harder it is to control themselves from impulsive use (Vishwanath, 2015). A computer or laptop may be used more often for work purposes. However, smartphones are incredibly mobile and can be indispensable for necessary daily activities, so they are also used often to connect with family or friends, watch entertaining videos, check social media updates, etc. Individuals have great psychological comfort when having access to and using smartphones (Melumad & Meyer, 2020). When an individual picks up a smartphone to accomplish a work or learning task, the action of holding and opening the smartphone becomes a point of origin of external stimulus. In addition, the impulsive system will amplify the perceived enjoyment of the information system use, thereby enhancing one’s intention to continue using it (Turel et al., 2011). As a result, in this research context, the impulsive system will increase both non-workrelated thoughts and the intention to continue to use a smartphone, and this in turn further enhances the CEP function by strengthening the associative network in the brain. Moreover, concerning the brain structures, the impulsive system is related to areas that trigger afective states, such as the amygdala and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (Collins & Loftus, 1975). As a result, the external stimulus of using a smartphone will activate afective memory elements in an associated cluster, such as past pleasing memories of using applications on the smartphone. The activation process is very rapid and automatic, which induces one’s attention to shift away unconsciously from the task at hand. Hence, we hypothesised that

H2.CEP with smartphone use is positively associated with mind-wandering.

## 3.2.2. CBC over smartphone use and mind-wandering

The reflective system is responsible for higher-order mental operations, such as deliberate judgment, evaluation and reasoning (Hofmann et al., 2009). The operations of the reflective system depend on managing resources in enacting the CBC function. CBC mechanisms increase vigilance (Piccoli & Ives, 2003), bring about reasoned and restrained behaviour and increase self-monitoring and consequent corrections (Hofmann et al., 2009). For example, Turel (2015) suggests that guilt was a significant factor in his study of discontinuing Facebook use. The reason is that guilt is a state of pain, soberness and selfconsciousness, which stems from self-regulation. Guilt makes people make rational decisions and behave in a socially acceptable way. According to dualsystem theory, guilt is generated when the reflective system works (Turel, 2015). In contrast, a deficit of CBC will decrease vigilance and self-monitoring. In addition, executive control theory indicates that mindwandering is the result of a failure of executive control, which is the function of CBC, especially when confronting interruptions and a distraction stimulus (McVay & Kane, 2010). When using a smartphone to cope with a task, apart from the device being a distraction stimulus, notifications from mobile applications are also interruptions. Both of them may lead to mind-wandering when there is a deficit of CBC. Thus, we hypothesised that

H3.CBC over smartphone use is inversely related to mind-wandering.

## 3.3. Mindfulness and the dual systems

The impulsive and reflective systems correspond roughly to diferent regions of the brain (Bechara, 2005). Extensive studies on mindfulness indicate that activity in certain areas of the brain will change in a mindfulness state (Gregor et al., 2014). Short- or long-term mindfulness training can cause structural changes in various brain areas (Hölzel et al., 2011, 2008). Individuals who have a higher level of mindfulness may difer in brain area features from those whose mindfulness level is low. Individuals with high levels of mindfulness show less emotional sensitivity and more self-control (Hölzel et al., 2011, 2008). In the next section, we explain how mindfulness may impact the dual system.

## 3.3.1. Mindfulness and CEP with smartphone use

Studies related to emotion find that when people are in a state of mindfulness, their brain activity changes; the activity in the prefrontal area is increased, while the activity in the amygdala is reduced (Gregor et al., 2014). The amygdala is responsible for triggering afective and emotional responses, and it is a critical neural structure that corresponds to the impulsive system (Bechara, 2005). Mindfulness decreases the functioning of the impulsive system. In addition, in the context of using a smartphone as a tool to solve a work problem, CEP reflects a state of obsession with a smartphone. Although an individual may initially aim to use the smartphone as a tool, their thoughts and emotions may be attracted by the smartphone automatically so that they do not focus on their task. Mindfulness can help decrease these automatic and habitual behaviours for the following reason: people in a mindfulness state or with higher mindfulness ability can be more clearly aware of the external environment and their internal thoughts and are sensitive and alert to the distinction (Vella, 1997). Mindfulness indicates that people accept their thoughts without judgment. They just watch and observe the thoughts, thereby decreasing automatic reactions to them, and can cope more efectively with their current situation (Shapiro et al., 2015). Therefore, with mindfulness, an individual who uses a smartphone to assist with work can be more aware of the role of the smartphone at the moment and can observe changes of thought, whether non-work-related thoughts occur and further anchor their minds in a working state. Hence, we hypothesised that

H4.Mindfulness is inversely related to CEP with smartphone use.

3.3.2. Mindfulness and CBC over smartphone use Mindfulness reflects unconditional acceptance and non-judgment about the present, indicating that an individual in a mindful state can examine their feelings and thoughts as they come and go. They can observe when their thoughts are being drawn away by distractions and can focus back on the tasks in the present moment; a more mindful person can stay focused on the task at hand better. When an individual is in a mindful state, their reflective system overrides their impulsive system (Liang et al., 2016). As a result, an individual who has a higher mindfulness ability is more likely to be in a mindful state, which activates the reflective system, thereby increasing the function of CBC. In addition, studies of brain function show that specific regions are associated with emotion control, learning, and memory processes (e.g., the inferior frontal gyrus and anterior cingulate; Bechara, 2005). These brain regions correspond to the reflective system and are in charge of higher-order mental operations (Dernbecher & Beck, 2017). Using voxel-based morphometry, brain images of people who had received mindfulness training revealed that they possessed higher mindfulness ability, as shown by greater grey matter concentration in those regions. Therefore, they were more likely to be emotionally stable and selfcontrolled (Hölzel et al., 2008). In addition, studies on the efect of mindfulness on an individual’s performance have found that mindfulness plays a significant role in self-control (Friese et al., 2012; Van de Veer et al., 2016). Compared with automatic or habitual smartphone use, the instrumental use of a smartphone to aid in the completion of a work or study task usually requires more cognitive resources, more working memory capacity and more logical thinking, which are the functions of CBC from the reflective system. According to the analysis above, mindfulness enhances the function of regions that are in charge of learning, memory and self-control in the brain. Thereby, we hypothesised that

H5.Mindfulness is positively associated with CBC over smartphone use.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Data collection

The research context of this study is cyberloafing on smartphones by employees at work. Therefore, we selected adult employees who were allowed to use smartphones during work as the participants of this study. We first conducted a pilot survey in which the questionnaire draft was distributed to 20 PhD students and faculty members and asked them to provide suggestions for revision. Based on their feedback, we refined and improved the questionnaire.

We then performed the main data collection. We printed out questionnaires for distribution. The respondents we selected were from two sources: MBAs from three universities in China and employees from five enterprises. The industries of the five enterprises were retail, media, education and training, intellectual property consulting and construction. We reimbursed each respondent with 20 RMB. In the questionnaire, we used questions to ensure that the cyberloafing behaviour the respondents described was in a work environment. We had a total of six requirements that were presented to the respondents as part of the questionnaire: (1) you are currently working full-time; (2) your main workplace is in your organisation; (3) you use a smartphone daily and can skilfully operate it; (4) your organisation allows you to use your personal smartphone during working hours; (5) you can handle or complete a part or all of your work tasks with your smartphone; (6) you use your personal smartphone to assist in handling or solving workrelated problems. After initially distributing 600 questionnaires, we received a total of 542 from participants. When we entered the data, we excluded incomplete questionnaires as well as questionnaires with the same answer to all the questions. Finally, 474 valid completed questionnaires remained.

## 4.2. Measures

The research model contained two first-order and three second-order variables. We used a 7-point Likert scale (1= “very strongly disagree” and 7 = “very strongly agree”). We measured all the items using a well-established and reliable scale from previous studies. The mindfulness construct measure was adapted from Kohls et al. (2009). Previous studies have developed a variety of scales to measure mindfulness, such as the Kentucky Inventory of Mindfulness Scale (KIMS; Baer et al., 2004), the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS; Brown & Ryan, 2003), the Freiburg Mindfulness Inventory (FMI; Kohls et al., 2009), the Toronto Mindfulness Scale (TMS; Lau et al., 2006) and several others. Previous reviews have compared the diferences between the scales (Sauer et al., 2013). After reviewing this information, we selected an 8-item short version of the FMI to measure mindfulness for the following three reasons. First, Kohls et al. (2009) define mindfulness as “the mental ability to focus on the direct and immediate perception of the present moment with a state of nonjudgmental awareness, voluntarily suspending evaluative cognitive feedback” (p. 224), which is consistent with the definition of mindfulness as an attribute that mindfulness is an ability people have or can be developed by training (Brown et al., 2007). The items in the MFI reflect mindfulness as an ability, such as “I am able to appreciate myself” and “In dificult situations, I can pause without immediately reacting” (Kohls et al., 2009, p. 227). Other widely used scales like MAAS and KIMS measure mindfulness as a trait or state, which did not fit this research context. Second, compared with other scales, an outstanding advantage of FMI is that it captures a clear basis of Buddhist psychology (Sauer et al., 2013); therefore, it captures the essence of mindfulness and presents the meaning of mindfulness comprehensively (Evans, 2008). Third, compared with one-dimensional scales, FMI measures two important aspects of mindfulness, which are presence and non-judgemental acceptance. Some other scales, such as MAAS and TMS, do not measure the aspect of acceptance. In this research context, nonjudgemental acceptance indicates that people can let their feelings or thoughts come and go; thereby, they can draw their attention back to the task at hand. Nonjudgemental acceptance is necessary when analysing the efect of mindfulness on cyberloafing. Fourth, the 8-item short version, which has been verified as reliable and valid, is conducive to filling in the questionnaire attentively with accuracy.

The constructs CEP with smartphone use and CBC over smartphone use were adapted from Turel and Qahri-Saremi (2016), who used them to examine the antecedents of problematic use of social networking software, which is similar to the problematic use of smartphones in cyberloafing. The items on mindwandering were adapted from Mrazek et al. (2013) and measure whether a person is easily distracted in a variety of contexts. The items on cyberloafing were adapted from Khansa et al. (2017). The scale is shown in Table C.1 of Appendix C. In addition, some studies have found that gender, age and education may relate to cyberloafing behaviour (Hartijasti & Fathonah, 2014; Lim & Chen, 2012; Sheikh et al., 2015). Therefore, we set the demographic features of gender, age and education as control variables.

## 5. Discussion

The current study explored the antecedents of passive cyberloafing with a smartphone and examined how mindfulness decreases cyberloafing. Thus, we elaborated on the concept of mindfulness and examined (1) the direct factors that influence passive cyberloafing, (2) how the dual system functions are associated with passive cyberloafing and (3) the impact of mindfulness on the two systems. In the following, we discuss the findings from the study and elaborate on how our findings support, complement and extend the previous research.

First, to explore the essence and antecedents of passive cyberloafing, we used the dual-system theory to test how each system influenced passive cyberloafing. We found that CEP of the impulsive system was positively associated with passive cyberloafing by increasing mind-wandering. The results demonstrated that passive cyberloafing was related to an imbalance of the two systems, which complements the previous research on cyberloafing behaviour (Askew et al., 2014; Lim, 2002). The results explain why even if an employee picks up a smartphone for work purposes, he or she may uncontrollably turn to cyberloafing; this indicates that in addition to being a planned behaviour, employees’ cyberloafing behaviour at work can be unconscious and impulsive.

Second, we found that a directly related factor of passive cyberloafing was mind-wandering. Our research was from the perspective of neuroscience and focused on what happens in the brain. In the context of using a personal smartphone to assist in work tasks, the smartphone is an intelligent personal assistant as well as a distracting stimulus, which induces one’s thoughts to automatically drift away from work to unrelated activities. Once the thoughts transfer from work to unrelated smartphone use, cyberloafing may occur. The results also show that mind-wandering was positively associated with cyberloafing.

Third, we found that the imbalance of CEP and CBC was significantly associated with mindwandering; that is, when CEP overrides CBC, mindwandering occurs. This result is in line with Turel (2015), who suggests that self-generated thought is an individual’s default state and has a tendency to occupy attention resources at any moment. Maintaining mind-wandering does not consume resources, and no extra efort is needed to shift one’s attention to task-unrelated thoughts (Turel, 2015). Nevertheless, the activation of the reflective system requires extra efort and more cognitive resources (Evans, 2008). In addition, the results show that CBC had no significant impact on mindwandering. Through careful review of the indicators of CBC, we think the possible reason was that the items reflected people’s desire for concern and restriction of their own behaviour. However, in reality, self-control is relatively dificult and requires considerable efort. People’s subjective wishes may deviate from actual behaviour, so that when cyberloafing occurs, they actually cannot control or restrict their behaviour well.

Fourth, in order to find an efective way to reduce the imbalance of the impulsive system and reflective system and thereby also reduce cyberloafing, we introduced the construct of mindfulness in this research. The results show the internal mechanism of how mindfulness was negatively related to passive cyberloafing. Passive cyberloafing is an impulsive, unplanned, and unconscious behaviour that occurs without awareness. Mindfulness highlights a clear awareness of the thoughts that emerge in one’s brain and accept them without judgment. Once an individual can clearly observe the thoughts in their brains, they give themselves the opportunity to proactively make choices and can reduce automated and impulsive behavioural reactions. Being able to accept ideas without judgment means that an individual does not add considerations of good or bad, right or wrong to their existing thoughts. They just allow the thoughts to come and go, so that they can live in the present moment. In this research context, an employee who is in a mindful state can better focus on a work task that is being done. By decreasing an employee’s mind-wandering towards nonwork-related smartphone use, it can help decrease passive cyberloafing.

## 6. Implications

## 6.1. Implications for theory

First, this study classified active cyberloafing and passive cyberloafing based on whether employees intentionally engaged in the behaviour in a state of selfawareness. Previous IS studies deemed cyberloafing as an intentional, deliberate and planned behaviour and used the TPB (Askew et al., 2014; Sheikh et al., 2015; Venkatraman et al., 2018), social exchange theory (Lim, 2002) and social learning theory (Khansa et al., 2017) to explain it. The antecedents of cyberloafing include peer cyberloafing, neutralisation, perceived risk, organisational justice, behavioural attitude and norms, which are shown in Table A.1 of Appendix A. In this research context, employees’ cyberloafing was not for a particular purpose or reason but was an impulsive, automated and unconscious behaviour that was done without awareness. When an employee uses a smartphone to handle a work task, their purpose is to complete the work. However, their mind-wandering can cause them to involuntarily turn to cyberloafing. This impulsive and automatic cyberloafing without clear awareness and a particular purpose is passive cyberloafing. Therefore, this study adopted dualsystem theory to explore how passive cyberloafing happens from the perspective of neuroscience. This new classification of cyberloafing fills the gap related to passive cyberloafing in the IS literature, which is a theoretical contribution to the concept of cyberloafing.

Second, by adopting a neuroscience perspective, we reveal the internal mechanism of passive cyberloafing. This study found that before an employee engages in cyberloafing, their thoughts first drift away unconsciously from the work at hand to non-work-related smartphone use. The dual systems were associated with cyberloafing through mind-wandering. The core of dual-system theory is that there are two systems with diferent functions and abilities in people’s brains (Evans, 2008), which can explain human behaviour and especially explain why unconscious and impulsive behaviour happens (Ouimet et al., 2012). Previous studies have empirically examined the direct relationship between the dual systems and behaviour (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016). In this study, we found the impact of mind-wandering, which was a direct factor of impulsive and unconscious behaviours. Specifically, CEP’s overriding of CBC was related to mindwandering, and mind-wandering was positively associated with the behaviour of cyberloafing in this study. Thus, this study revealed how the dual systems were associated with an individual’s passive cyberloafing by demonstrating the efect of mind-wandering.

Third, by introducing mindfulness, we demonstrated theoretically how to change dual system processing by improving individual abilities regarding mindfulness, thereby influencing their behaviours. Previous studies have discussed and testify to the efects of the dual system on human behaviour and ability, such as with problematic behaviour (Turel & Bechara, 2016; Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016) and selfcontrol (Evans, 2008). However, limited literature has explored how human behaviours and abilities can afect dual systems. This empirical study showed that mindfulness significantly decreased CEP of the impulsive system, whereas it increased CBC of the reflective system, which verified that higher mindfulness was associated with stronger CBC and weaker CEP in dual systems. Further, mindfulness is related to mindwandering and passive cyberloafing through its significant relation to CEP and CBC.

Fourth, this study extends the research on how mindfulness impacts the use of personal IT devices. Previous IS studies of mindfulness have focused either on a collective level (Curtis et al., 2017) or on managers (Fiol & O’Connor, 2003). Limited literature has explored the efect of mindfulness on an employee’s IT use at an individual level. This study showed the impact of mindfulness and the internal mechanism of how mindfulness works. Passive cyberloafing was triggered by employees’ mind-wandering, in which their thoughts drifted to non-work-related smartphone use. Mindfulness reflects one’s sober awareness of what the thoughts inside one’s brain are and how to accept them without judgment (Jha et al., 2007). People with high ability of mindfulness can clearly observe their thoughts drifting before they automatically react to them. Previous studies have shown that mindfulness can reduce mind-wandering (Friese et al., 2012), but there has been a lack of exploration into its internal mechanism. Based on dual-system theory, we found mindfulness increased the function of the reflective system and reduced the function of the impulsive system simultaneously, thereby decreasing the impulsive system’s overriding of the reflective system. Consequently, cyberloafing behaviour was reduced. The process shows how an employee’s ability to stay in a mindfulness state transforms into selfcontrol when one executes a work task. Introducing mindfulness into this research model is an example of how to enrich mindfulness studies in the IS field.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

First, this study provides evidence for managers to guide smartphone use in the workplace. Personal smartphone use at work can lead to cyberloafing. However, as an employee’s taking a smartphone to work and using it as a work assistant has become a pervasive phenomenon, it is hard for organisations to supervise employees’ use of smartphones during work. As a result, it is critical for managers to guide employees in using their smartphones efectively. Organisations can take some measures or propose some suggestions for work-related smartphone use. For example, managers can advocate that employees instal and use mobile applications for time management and eficiency improvement.

Second, this study provides an efective way to reduce cyberloafing in the workplace. Our findings demonstrated that mindfulness was significantly related to cyberloafing when using a smartphone to handle work tasks. As mindfulness is an ability that can be cultivated (Kohls et al., 2009), organisations can take measures to foster an employee’s mindfulness ability. For example, managers can implement various mindfulness-based training programmes.

## 7. Limitations and future research

This study sufers from several limitations, and we propose some future research. First, we did not consider the type of task in this research. This study considered cyberloafing on a smartphone during work. Cyberloafing behaviour can result from nonwork-related thoughts during the process of using a smartphone to cope with work tasks. However, different task dificulties require diferent cognitive resources. As a result, task dificulty may moderate the relationship between mind-wandering and cyberloafing. Therefore, future research can explore the antecedents of cyberloafing under diferent work task types and task dificulties.

Second, regarding cyberloafing as a counterproductive behaviour (Askew et al., 2014), this study did not consider the impact of mindfulness on other counterproductive behaviours, such as the misuse of information and communications technologies in general (D’Arcy et al., 2014; Piccoli & Ives, 2003; Vishwanath, 2015), underutilisation of new information systems (Venkatesh et al., 2008) and computer abuse (Chen et al., 2012). Future research can examine the influence of mindfulness on these other counterproductive behaviours.

Third, as this study focused on cyberloafing with a smartphone, we did not consider the efect of diferent types of IT devices and other characteristics on users’ cyberloafing behaviour. These various characteristics may relate to (1) the ownership of the device, (2) diferent sizes of the screens and (3) an organisation’s policy or rules regarding IT device use. For example, in our research context of cyberloafing on a smartphone, if an organisation owns the smartphone, it has the right to set controls for its use or instal specific applications on the device. Whether these or other organisational-level factors afect employees’ cyberloafing need further study. In addition, the diferent sizes of screens of IT devices may also afect cyberloafing behaviour. In addition, regardless of the ownership of the smartphone, organisations can set policies and rules of IT device use by employees at work. For example, if a business network is used for connectivity, they can set controls and barriers to websites that employees can use at work, they can control access to the network or they can restrict the websites that employees can access in the workplace. In addition, the premise of our research context is that organisations allow employees to use smartphones. However, we can further consider the following conditions: (1) organisations expect employees to use smartphones to assist with work tasks, or (2) organisations allow employees to use smartphones to assist with work tasks. These conditions reflect organisations’ diferent attitudes towards smartphone use in the workplace. As stated above, organisations may set distinct policies and rules based on their attitudes. Future research can examine the types of organisational policies and rules regarding personal IT device use and analyse their efects on cyberloafing at the workplace. In addition, in this research, we studied passive cyberloafing, which is unintentional behaviour that happens mindlessly. Future research can explore the consequences of mindless cyberloafing.

Fourth, in this research, we did not consider the efects of the Big Five personality variables, such as conscientiousness. Conscientiousness may be an unmeasured variable that is directly related to dualsystem processing, mind wandering, deliberate or passive cyberloafing and levels of mindfulness. Future research can investigate the efects of conscientiousness. In addition, as we studied mindfulness in an ITuse context, we mainly theorised the concept within IS mindfulness research. There is an abundance of research on mindfulness in the psychology and medicine fields. Future research can further integrate theories of mindfulness into the IS field.

Fifth, in previous studies, there were significant efects of demographic features on cyberloafing. However, demographic features were not related to passive cyberloafing in this study. The reason for this inconsistency may lie in the diferent research contexts. In some specific contexts, these demographic variables may matter. For example, men or younger employees may cyberloaf more than women or older employees in some environments. Future research can explore in which specific contexts demographic features afect cyberloafing. In addition, some measurements of cyberloafing in the existing literature were vague, for example, the indicator that “number of times per day or week that employees loaf”. It would be helpful to develop more precise measures of cyberloafing, and indicators may include frequency, duration and whether employees are able to do multitask by performing work at the same time as they cyberloaf.

Sixth, we also found an unexpected result when we tested the relationship between the first- and secondorders of CEP. The construct of govern was negatively related to CEP. This may be because emotions and cognitive preoccupation reflect CEP from the angle of feelings and thoughts, and the construct of govern involves actual behaviour. Although this did not afect the final results, we still think future research may be needed to study the concept of CEP.

## 8. Conclusion

This study explored (1) the causes of passive cyberloafing, (2) how to decrease passive cyberloafing, and (3) how mindfulness can decrease passive cyberloafing. We found passive cyberloafing was directly and positively associated with mind-wandering, which was significantly associated with the dual systems in the human brain. We also found that mindfulness could improve the CBC function in the reflective system and reduce the CEP function in the impulsive system, which helped to reduce non-work-related thoughts when using a smartphone to handle work tasks, thereby decreasing passive cyberloafing behaviour. This research explored the essence of passive cyberloafing – that it was an unplanned, impulsive, automatic IT-use behaviour – and it can be reduced by mindfulness. This study distinguished passive cyberloafing from the active cyberloafing examined in previous studies and explored antecedences and internal mechanisms from a neuroscience perspective. Furthermore, we have provided practical suggestions for managers to control cyberloafing from the perspective of enhancing employees’ mindfulness abilities.
