# What most influences consumers’ intention to use? diferent motivation and trust stories for uber, airbnb, and taskrabbit

John Tripp <sup>a</sup>, D. Harrison McKnight <sup>b</sup> and Nancy Lankton<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management, Wilbur O. and Ann Powers College of Business, Clemson University, Unitied States; <sup>b</sup>Professor Emeritus at Michigan State University - Eli Broad College of Business, United States; <sup>c</sup>Brad D. Smith Schools of Business, Marshall University, United States

## ABSTRACT

While many factors drive the sharing economy’s growth, trust and motivations play critical roles. However, which factors are more important to young consumers? This research compares a trust model with a motivation model for predicting intention to use Airbnb, Uber, and TaskRabbit, comparing within each platform, not between them. The study uses a survey sample of Midwest United States business undergraduates (nearly all Generation Z). Each sample varies in experience levels: relatively high experience Uber respondents, very low experience TaskRabbit respondents, and medium experience for Airbnb. Results show that within Uber, the trust model predicts significantly better than the motivation model. Within Airbnb and TaskRabbit, the motivation model predicts only slightly better. By combining the trust and motivation variables into one model by platform, we find difering degrees of complementarity between the trust and motivation variables, with those for Airbnb being the most complementary in terms of predictive power and those for Uber being the least complementary. Within Airbnb and TaskRabbit, combining the trust and motivation models shows the two most significant variables were enjoyment and trusting intention. Within Uber, trusting intention and trusting beliefs in the provider were the most significant. We provide implications and directions for future research.

## KEYWORDS

Sharing economy; gig economy; Uber; Airbnb; TaskRabbit; trust; risk; motivation; enjoyment; convenience; intention to use

## 1. Introduction

The sharing economy (SE) market is expected to grow from \$373.7 billion in 2019 to \$1.5 trillion by 2024 (BCC Research, 2020).<sup>1</sup> SE platforms, which enable peer-to-peer transactions, have penetrated market segments such as ridesharing (e.g., Uber, BlaBlaCar), hospitality (Airbnb, VRBO), and general tasks (e.g., TaskRabbit, Fiverr). Given the SE’s unique, straightforward business model and its growth trajectory, many researchers have examined why consumers use an SE platform (Klarin & Suseno, 2021).

Researchers have debated whether the sharing economy is a single phenomenon or multiple, based upon the kinds of transactions enabled by an IT platform (e.g., Ertz et al., 2016; Frenken & Schor, 2017). Although diferences exist regarding the characteristics of the various platforms, consistent with Schlagwein et al. (2020), we use the term “sharing economy” as an umbrella term encompassing other terms (e.g., gig economy). The term “sharing economy” communicates a broad depiction of these firms across both scholars and practitioners (Frenken & Schor, 2017). See Appendix A for an extended discussion of the SE term.

Scholars consider trust to be one major SE use determinant (Botsman & Rodgers, 2010; Cheng, 2016; Möhlmann, 2015; Sundararajan, 2016). Trust is vital because SE users typically deal with strangers (e.g., an Uber driver, an Airbnb host), in many cases in direct physical proximity. This close contact and dependence upon the goodwill of others increases felt safety and privacy concerns, increases complexity, and challenges use (Mittendorf et al., 2019). The pandemic has exacerbated such feelings despite eforts by each service to create guidelines and specific methods to help providers and users conduct business safely (OECD, 2020). Safety was also important before the pandemic. One survey found that 40% of SE users felt unsafe regarding bodily harm, and 30% experienced less-than-ideal service quality (Maru/matchbox, 2017). Trust helps one cope with unsafe and risky SE situations (Cheng, 2016). Because trust is key, this paper provides a strong emphasis on trust factors.

Research Question #1: How do trust constructs influence consumer intention to use each SE service?

Consumer motivations are another major determinant of SE use (Hawlitschek et al., 2018). Motivation embodies “forces acting either on or within a person to initiate behaviour” (Petri & Cofer, 2021:1). Forces acting “on” are extrinsic motivators; forces acting “within” are intrinsic. Scholars investigate the motivators or reasons consumers use SE services such as Sharetribe (Hamari et al., 2016), Car2go and Airbnb (Möhlmann, 2015), and accommodation-sharing platforms (Tussyadiah & Pesonen, 2018; Wu et al., 2017). Findings show that intrinsic and extrinsic motivations are key predictors of SE use intention (Böcker & Meelen, 2017; Hawlitschek et al., 2018; Mahadevan, 2018). Hence, we include them in this study.

Research Question #2: How do motivation constructs influence consumer intention to use each SE service?

We examine both a trust model and a motivation model for predicting intention to use SE platforms (Figure 1). The SE literature has considered both trust and motivations in the use of the SE, and some SE literature characterises trust as a motivation. Although they are clearly related to each other, trust and motivations are conceptualised diferently within their theoretical traditions. Motivations typically involve assessing a service’s benefits. By contrast, one who trusts evaluates “how others behave” in an uncertain/vulnerable situation (Gefen et al., 2003: 55) and then moves ahead in the relationship (or not) despite potential risks (Möllering, 2006). This should make trust and motivation somewhat complementary to each other. Because consumers may consider both SE benefits (motivations) and the provider’s desirable attributes (trust) when contemplating SE use, this paper also tests a combined motivation/trust model that empirically examines the complementary nature of trust and motivation constructs within each platform.

Research Question #3: What is the diferential impact of trust and motivation models/constructs on intention to use each SE service?

We use trust and motivation theory and scholarly SE studies to examine these research questions. We base our trust model on prior research (Benamati, J. “Skip”, Serva, M. A. & Fuller, M. A., 2010; Mittendorf, 2017; Tussyadiah & Park, 2018) that examines specific trust-related constructs: two trusting beliefs, trusting intention, and perceived risk. We use self-determination theory (SDT, Ryan & Deci, 2000) and the SE literature to categorise SE motivations from intrinsic (entirely self-determined, such as enjoyment) to extrinsic (partially or fully other-determined such as convenience). We also use economic benefits, community belonging, and sustainability as motivation constructs due to their significant efects in prior SE studies (Hawlitschek et al., 2018; Klarin & Suseno, 2021). We evaluate the trust and motivation models using model eficacy/selection measures: R<sup>2</sup>-adjusted, Q<sup>2</sup>, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC).

This study tests the models using questionnaire data about Uber, Airbnb, and TaskRabbit. We chose these SE services because they are international, wellestablished, and mostly well-known. Sutherland and Jarrahi (2018) find Airbnb ranks first, Uber second, and TaskRabbit fourth in the number of academic publication references found. These services also fit under the SE umbrella term, meaning they have certain common criteria (Schlagwein et al., 2020, Appendix A) that relate to underlying trust and motivation theories. For example, all three provide temporary access to a good/service for payment, which may involve trusting strangers. All three services are IT facilitated, possibly making trust in the app important. That the transactions relate to underutilised assets/services and are peer-to-peer might motivate consumers interested in sustainability and community belonging. Indeed, prior researchers have used both trust and motivation theories to study Uber and Airbnb (Guttentag et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2018; Mittendorf et al., 2019; Möhlmann, 2015).

Within this common SE definition, some platform/ service diferences may matter to trust and motivation theories, which apply to our three studied services. Interestingly, these services represent diferent SE types. TaskRabbit involves providers sharing their skills, tools, and labour, making it a gig economy service.<sup>2</sup> Airbnb involves hosts sharing their underutilised property assets, making it a conventional SE service. Uber, of the gig economy, difers from TaskRabbit by going across assets (the vehicle) and skills (driving). These diferences may make trust or motivation more or less predictive within each platform service.

Assessing our research questions within three SE platforms helps reveal why young consumers are willing to use these three SE services. Our sample is United States business students (96% Gen Z). Based on each service’s popularity and size, we felt confident a priori that a high percent of our respondents had already used Uber, that a somewhat smaller percent had used Airbnb, and that few had used TaskRabbit. This is the situation SE vendors must deal with. They must try to persuade people to start using, or continue using, their service based on the levels of experience that naturally occur in such target samples as ours. While examining a sample with difering levels of experience makes it more dificult to sort out the reasons behind the results, it has the advantage of increasing generalisability to both current and potential users. Researchers have to make such realismversus-control choices, and in either case, they operate on what McGrath (1981) called the horns of a dilemma. We chose realism over control in our sampling.

For each such sample, this research contributes by reporting whether a trust model is a strong predictor of intention to use a platform and how well its four trustrelated constructs predict within each SE service (RQ#1). This study next shows how well the motivation model and its constructs predict (RQ#2). But trade-ofs among competing models usually exist. We also contribute by examining (within each service) whether the trust model or the motivation model performs more strongly and how complementary the combined trust and motivation constructs are (RQ#3). Overall, our results tell a diferent story for Uber, Airbnb, and TaskRabbit.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Trust theory

Scholars have used trust theory to understand e-commerce, online marketplaces, and the sharing economy. Trust is relevant in these situations because it helps consumers overcome the associated environmental risks and engage in trust-related behaviours such as using websites and buying products/services. Trust does not remove the complexity of these behaviours, but instead reduces its efects. Trust is crucial in the sharing economy because of its complexities and risks, such as non-recurring interactions, encounters between strangers, and hybrid in-person and digital interactions (Botsman & Rodgers, 2010; Mittendorf et al., 2019). Both consumers and providers may feel vulnerable (ter Huurne et al., 2017). Overcharging, personal safety, and poor service quality present salient consumer risks.

Trust, referred to here as trusting intention, is defined as “the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor . . . ” (Mayer et al., 1995: 712). For the SE consumer, the other party is the service provider (e.g., Airbnb host). Trust theory also posits that people, before choosing to become vulnerable, usually consider three trustworthy attributes (i.e., trusting beliefs) of the other party: ability (competence), benevolence (good intentions towards the trustee), and integrity (honesty, principledness; Mayer et al., 1995; McKnight et al., 2002a, b).

This study examines trusting intention and trusting beliefs in both the SE provider (their ability, benevolence, and integrity) and in the app (beliefs about the app’s functionality, helpfulness, and reliability). We chose to study trust in the app both because it should influence SE use and because it involves the IT artefact (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003). Despite machines not having the volition or will to act in a particular manner, trust theorists explain how people can trust technologies (McKnight et al., 2011). Trust in a technology represents a person’s beliefs about the technology’s functionality (has the needed functions), helpfulness (provides aid via a help function), and reliability (dependable, does not fail; Lankton et al., 2016). While some have studied trusting beliefs in SE platforms (Mittendorf et al., 2019; Möhlmann, 2016), the app does not represent the complete platform of an SE firm, reflecting all the firm’s governance and reputation. However, researchers suggest digital trust (such as in the app) has been critical in advancing the SE (BlaBlaCar, n.d.; Gerwe & Silva, 2020). Apps are now ubiquitous. Since 2017, most internet trafic has been generated from mobile devices (BroadbandSearch, n. d.). This shift is especially pronounced among U. S. Gen Zers, 98% of whom own smartphones. In 2019, Gen Zers spent 71% of their total digital minutes using smartphone apps (MindSea, n.d.). Because of this massive shift to mobile app consumption, and because all Uber users, most Airbnb users (at least during actual travel time), and TaskRabbit users utilise the app at some point during the transaction, the app becomes the primary face of the company and the mechanism for both starting the relationship and later finding recourse if needed.

Trust and risk are closely related and often studied together (Hawlitschek, Teubner, Gimpel et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2018; Zamani et al., 2019). Trust and risk play related key roles in predicting intention to use technology because risk indicates one’s felt uncertainty and complexity while trust helps one overcome them. We define perceived risk as consumer perceptions about the probability of sufering a loss while using the SE platform (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). Researchers study trust and perceived risk together to help explain the climate of the relationship between two parties and the psychological processes that lead to behavioural intention (Jarvenpaa et al., 2000).

Much research has examined trust’s efects on SE behaviours (Klarin & Suseno, 2021). Ert et al. (2016), who examine host picture-based trustworthiness, find perceived host trustworthiness results in higher listing choices and prices. Tussyadiah and Park (2018) find that trust afects intention to rent, but they do not study other significant determinants of intention. Lutz and Newlands (2018) find that host trust in Airbnb influences host home-sharing frequency and that while trust out-predicts privacy concerns, it is not as influential as monetary benefits. Hawlitschek et al. (2018) find that trust in other users, modelled with financial benefits and sustainability, has a small efect on attitude and perceived behavioural control. Möhlmann (2015) finds that trust and other factors predict satisfaction with the platform for Car2go and Airbnb.

Some studies examine multiple types of trust. Notably, Hawlitschek, Teubner, Weinhardt et al. (2016) measure three types of trusting beliefs and find that trust in the platform, the supplier, and the product each significantly influence Airbnb rent intention. Mittendorf et al. (2019) find that the dimensionality of the service afects the impact of users’ trust beliefs. They argue that sharing economy services are characterised by five key dimensions: duration, financial risk, personalisation, control, and social interactions. All five dimensions are higher for Airbnb than for Uber. For example, users spend a relatively short amount of time using an Uber driver’s service (e.g., the duration of a car ride), while Airbnb stays may involve a week or more. Further, the financial risk in an Uber transaction is much less than for a lodging stay through Airbnb. For higher-dimensionality services like Airbnb, both trust in the platform and trust in the provider afect intention to use, but for lower dimensionality services like Uber, only trust in the platform predicts intention. Overall, research finds trust is among key variables that encourage SE use by helping people deal with SE uncertainty and complexity. Studies also find that trust in other users is not as key as user trust in the provider and that trust is more vital to the consumer than to the provider (e.g., Mittendorf et al., 2019). These findings influenced the trust constructs we chose. Our study contributes to this research by adding trusting beliefs in the app and perceived risk to a trust model and by comparing it with a motivation model, which we describe next.

## 2.2. Motivations and self-determination theory

Motivations describe forces that initiate and regulate goal-directed behaviours (Gagné & Deci, 2005). Our second theoretical lens is self-determination theory (SDT). Self-determination is the process of being able to make choices that allow control over one’s life. SDT distinguishes between types of behaviours based on how much they are self-determined (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Largely self-determined behaviours are intrinsically motivated (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Intrinsic motivation denotes performing an activity for its own sake, to experience the enjoyment and satisfaction the activity provides (Vallerand, 1997). It is the inherent desire to learn, achieve, and experience stimulation (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Vallerand, 1997). Less self-determined behaviours are extrinsically motivated. They are motivated by a desire to achieve positive outcomes or avoid negative ones (Vallerand, 1997).

Previous research has studied the efects of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation on SE use intention. Hamari et al. (2016) theorise and test an SE model based on SDT and find enjoyment, an intrinsic motivator, and economic benefits, an extrinsic motivator, significantly influence intention to use collaborative consumption services. Wu et al. (2017) also find that intrinsic and extrinsic motivators influence intention to use room-sharing services in China. Based on these and other SE motivation studies (see, Hawlitschek et al., 2018 for examples), we investigate five commonly used motivations.

We examine enjoyment because it is an important intrinsic motivation for using the SE (Hamari, 2013; Hamari & Koivisto, 2015). Enjoyment is performing an activity because it is pleasurable in its own right, not because of external consequences (Davis et al., 1992). In some studies, it is the strongest predictor of intention and satisfaction (Tussyadiah, 2016). It relates to the positive social experiences factor Hawlitschek et al. (2018) describe and the novelty factor Guttentag et al. (2018) describes. Enjoyment is key because using SE services may have enjoyable social aspects, and using a novel service is exciting.

We examine four extrinsic motivations. As Hawlitschek et al. (2018) and Gerwe and Silva (2020) point out, economic benefits is one of the most studied and strongest predictors of sharing economy intention to use. We include it in our study because of its continuing positive efects on use intention over time (Amaro et al., 2019; Hamari et al., 2016; Hawlitschek et al., 2018; Lamberton & Rose, 2012; Tussyadiah, 2016). Early literature pointed to community belonging (being part of a group of like-minded people – Möhlmann, 2015), as an important SE motivation. We include this variable because it reflects the social and connectedness aspects of the SE (Hawlitschek et al., 2018). Eckhardt et al. (2019) and ter Huurne et al. (2020) recommend that scholars examine socia motivations further. One study found that 80% of adults believed it was an important SE benefit (PWC, 2015). Other studies confirm its significance (Barnes & Mattsson, 2017; Hawlitschek et al., 2018; Möhlmann, 2015), making it an important predictor. Convenience is the perceived time and energy savings involved in using SE platforms (Zhang et al., 2016) and is an oftused SE use motivator. It relates to ubiquitous availability and efort expectancy mentioned in literature reviews (Gerwe & Silva, 2020; Hawlitschek et al., 2018). Studies show how the SE provides convenient and better-located lodging (Guttentag et al., 2018; Sundararajan, 2016) and saves time (Hamari et al., 2016). Sustainability is also a common SE use predictor (Hawlitschek et al., 2018). It means one perceives that the SE helps maintain the environment through resource reuse (Mahadevan, 2018). While some empirical studies find it has no efects (e.g., Möhlmann, 2015) or even negative efects (e.g., Tussyadiah, 2016), we include it because many other studies find it significantly afects important SE outcomes (e.g., Barnes & Mattsson, 2017; Hamari et al., 2016; Oyedele & Simpson, 2018; Wang et al., 2019) and because it was an initial SE driver (Sundararajan, 2016).

In summary, we include these five motivations in our model because they are oft studied and influential in prior literature. They provide a good mix of predictors, with enjoyment fully on the intrinsic side, economic benefits and convenience on the highly extrinsic side, and sustainability and community inbetween. They also spread across the economic, environmental, hedonic, and social/community type motivations the literature describes (Böcker & Meelen, 2017; Hamari et al., 2016; Tussyadiah, 2015; Wu et al., 2017). These factors include a mix of motivations as depicted in SDT that can help us examine which are the better predictors.

## 2.3. Comparison of trust and motivation models

Previous research has reviewed the SE literature and tested comprehensive models of consumer motivations (Hawlitschek et al., 2018). Other studies have investigated how trust influences SE use behaviours (Mittendorf et al., 2019). To our knowledge, no study has contrasted these two theoretical perspectives for explaining SE use intention. While some research includes both trust and motivations in the same model (Hawlitschek et al., 2018), the relative predictive strength and complementarity of the two models has not yet been studied. Understanding these is important because consumers form intentions based on trade-ofs (e.g., trust versus benefits) that may favour one model over the other. Model and construct predictive power comparisons can lead to more focused academic research and more usable practitioner implications.

## 3. Research models and hypotheses

This section develops the hypotheses for the research models based on trust theory (the trust model) and self-determination theory (SDT) (the motivation model). We present these research models in Figure 1 and describe them as follows.

## 3.1. Trust model hypotheses

We base our trust model on studies that position both trusting beliefs and risk as predictors of intention to use (Mittendorf, 2017; Nicolaou & McKnight, 2006). SE firms want both to retain new customers and recruit new ones. We study both those with little-orno-experience with the service (especially for TaskRabbit, for whom we measure initial trust, McKnight et al., 1998) and those with some or much experience (especially for Uber, measuring ongoing trust). Consistent with these models, we predict that consumer trusting beliefs in the provider will influence intention to use the SE platform. Consumers will consider the provider’s beneficial attributes in deciding whether to use an SE platform. Individuals who believe the SE provider has integrity, benevolence, and competence will believe the provider will not act opportunistically, and there will be less relationship conflict between user/provider strangers (Howcroft & Bergvall-Kåreborn, 2019). Trust in the SE may erode if providers do not exhibit trustworthy actions. Providers that demonstrate misconduct, inconsistently apply rules, and provide low-quality experiences would erode consumer trust and lower intention to use SE platforms (Gonzalez-Padron, 2017). On the other hand, consumers are more likely to have confidence in providers that deliver high-quality and consistent services and thus develop higher intention to use (Gonzalez-Padron, 2017).

H1a: Trusting beliefs in the provider will positively influence intention to use an SE platform.

We note two things about H1a and our other hypotheses. First, we hypothesise each antecedent of intention to use within an SE platform, not between them, because each respondent was assigned to report on only one of the three platforms. Second, for each intention to use antecedent we hypothesise, we control for two personal characteristics – gender and personal innovativeness – and for two situational characteristics – the respondent’s familiarity with the assigned platform and their length of experience using the assigned platform. We felt all four controls could be important to respondents’ trust and motivation perceptions. Because we control for these, one can think of each hypothesis being preceded by the controls, e.g., “Controlling for gender, personal innovativeness, familiarity with the platform, and length of experience using the platform, [H1a]: Trusting beliefs in the provider will positively influence intention to use an SE platform]” We briefly justify the controls in the Measures section.

One main reason trust is necessary in the SE context is that serious situational or interpersonal risks exist. While H1a applies to all three platforms, in H1b we predict that trusting beliefs in the provider will be a significant positive predictor of intention to use for Uber but not for Airbnb and TaskRabbit. We primarily base this on negative news about the three platforms, which can change public attitudes about SE services (Huertas et al., 2021). Based on a highly structured search method using Nexis-Uni (LexisNexis Academic – a comprehensive news source), we find that Uber has been in the news for personal safety and injury issues much more often than Airbnb and TaskRabbit have. We used the advanced search of Nexus-Uni, limited to news only (no legal/company financial items), in English only, and United States only (to match our sample). All news sources were searched: i.e., newspapers, newswires/press releases, Web-based publications, blogs, industry trade press, news transcripts, weblinks, magazines and journals, newsletters, others. We searched for the number of negative personal (as opposed to business) news articles during the twenty-four months before our survey was conducted. The search terms were the SE platform name and a personal harm word (e.g., “Uber AND injury”). To ensure our results were not simply the function of a word, we searched for five terms: danger, harm, injury, safe, and safety.

These searches resulted in 8149 news articles. 7169 (88.0%) were for Uber, 948 (11.6%) for Airbnb, and 32 (0.4%) for TaskRabbit. This means consumers were 7169/948 = 7.6 times more likely to encounter news about personal safety and injury issues regarding Uber than regarding Airbnb, and 7169/32 = 224 times more likely to encounter it regarding Uber than TaskRabbit. This should translate to Uber being perceived as having significant bodily-harm risks, while Airbnb and TaskRabbit do not, making trust in the Uber provider needed to reduce the risks and increase intention to use. On the other hand, this will not be the case for Airbnb and TaskRabbit since bodily harm is not mentioned nearly as much by news articles, making it a nonsalient concern.

However, the risks associated with the SE include other issues beyond bodily harm. Privacy concerns have been posited to be especially salient in the SE (Lutz & Newlands, 2018). For this reason, we also investigated news articles that mentioned privacy. The proportions of results were like our search regarding bodily injury – 11,066 total articles (Uber 8614/77.8%; Airbnb 2403/21.7%; TaskRabbit 49/0.4%). These results suggest the awareness of negative impacts of Uber use exceeded that of the other platforms.

In addition, to see if students like our respondents felt these risks, we asked students from another semester of this same course: “What are the major risks that might arise by using the [Uber/ Airbnb/TaskRabbit] service? (Name at least two.)” We found that 47% of responses were about bodily harm for Uber, but only 28% for Airbnb and 10% for TaskRabbit. Regarding privacy, 14% of the responses were for Uber, 46% for Airbnb, and 41% for TaskRabbit. However, most of these privacy concern responses (88%) were associated with platform issues, specifically data breaches. These results show clear personal risks for Uber and indicate that Uber will be perceived as having significant provider-based risk, making trust in the provider necessary. By contrast, this will not be the case for Airbnb and TaskRabbit, making trust in the provider not a significant predictor of intention to use.

Further, user/provider interaction can provide rich trust-building cues. More trust is needed if interpersonal interaction is reduced. With Uber, transactions are centralised/automated through the platform’s algorithm. Uber’s algorithm-driven application does most of the transaction organising centrally, which often removes the initial part of the user-provider interaction (Sutherland & Jarrahi, 2018). By contrast, Airbnb and TaskRabbit use “bottom-up organizing” (Sutherland & Jarrahi, 2018., p. 21) of the transaction, meaning hosts and taskers often talk to the consumer before the consumer consents to the transaction. These interactions potentially build trust. Trust is most needed, like water, when it is scarce (and uncertainty and felt stranger danger is high). Hence, the Uber transaction, whose initial interaction is computerised instead of personalised, will produce a substantial felt need for trust in the provider. In our model, a greater need for trust would translate into a significant (p < 0.05) beta coeficient for trusting beliefs in the provider for Uber. This will not be the case for Airbnb or TaskRabbit.

H1b: Trusting beliefs in the provider will be a significant predictor of intention to use (i) for Uber. Trusting beliefs in the provider will not be a significant predictor of intention to use for either (ii) Airbnb or (iii) TaskRabbit.

Note that H1b is somewhat counterintuitive. Mittendorf et al. (2019) argue in the opposite direction – that Airbnb’s added duration and more personal interaction with the user will make trust more significantly predictive, but that this will not be the case for Uber. However, we feel that the duration of use decreases the felt need for trust (and thus its predictive salience) because interaction duration decreases the level of felt uncertainty.

Like H1a, hypotheses H2, H3a, H3b, and H4 apply equally to each of the three platforms. H2 relates to trusting beliefs in the app. The app itself presents the consumer with specific risks, including the app not working right, not being able to fix transaction-related problems (e.g., being overcharged for a ride), and not providing help with its functions. Anything that presents felt risk necessitates trust, leading us to posit that trusting beliefs in the app will positively predict intention to use SE platforms. Further, trust in the app will be very diferent from trust in the provider, such that their predictive impact will have little overlap since they address diferent risks – tech risks versus people risks. Positive beliefs in a technology’s functionality, helpfulness, and reliability (the three app-oriented trusting beliefs) have been found to significantly influence intention to use a specific technology (Lankton et al., 2016). By being functional, helpful, and reliable, the app provides signals about how the company works, how transactions work via the app, the quality of the service, and other trust-related information (e.g., provider ratings). These signals will increase the influence of trusting beliefs on intention to use an SE platform.

H2: Trusting beliefs in the app will positively influence intention to use the SE platform.

We predict that trusting intention will influence SE use intention. Trusting intention in the SE context means an individual is willing to depend on the provider (e.g., an Uber driver) in uncertain conditions. When consumers have trusting intention, they make a conscious choice to put aside uncertainty and doubt and to move forward with the transaction (Holmes, 1991). Being willing to depend means one is prepared to make oneself vulnerable and engage in trusting behaviours or develop the intention to perform trusting behaviours (Mayer et al., 1995). Tussyadiah and Park (2018) use the term “trusting intention” to mean booking intention, which is a specific risky behavioural intention (McKnight et al., 2002b) that difers from our definition of trusting intention – a general willingness to depend. Their booking intention is closer to our construct “intention to use” the SE service.

Studies find that trusting intention influences intention to use an e-commerce website (e.g., Benamati, J. “Skip”, Serva, M. A. & Fuller, M. A., 2010; Dimitriadis & Kyrezis, 2010). Based on trust theory and research, we predict that trusting intention will lead to a willingness to utilise a particular SE service (e.g., an Uber ride).

H3a: Trusting intention will positively influence intention to use the SE platform.

We believe trusting intention will be a stronger predictor than trusting beliefs. Whereas trusting beliefs are perceptions about the SE service provider’s attributes, trusting intention goes beyond mere belief in the other’s trustworthiness to a general intention to depend on, or count on, that service provider. Trusting intention involves “suspension of doubt” or treating “uncertainty and vulnerability as unproblematic, even if it could turn out that they are problematic” (Möllering, 2006: 116).. Möllering’s “suspension” or Giddens’ “leap into commitment” (Möllering, 2006: 109) is what makes trusting intention more salient than trusting beliefs in enabling risky actions. We believe Giddens’ committed trusting intention would lead to transacting with a party better than would mere beliefs about that party. Research finds that trusting intention in the web vendor had a larger efect on using the website than did trusting beliefs (McKnight et al., 2002b). To our knowledge, no one has tested the predictive strength of trusting beliefs versus trusting intention in the SE.

H3b: Trusting intention will be a stronger predictor of intention to use than will either trusting beliefs construct.

We predict that perceived risk will predict users intention to use the platform. Risks are detrimental to SE participation and can include the risk of incomplete transactions, poor service quality, theft, and bodily harm. Again, our qualitative findings showed respondents like ours perceived one or more of these risks, probably due to news media reports. Consumers who perceive these risks to be high will be less likely to use the platform (Hawlitschek et al., 2018).

Researchers find that perceived e-commerce risk negatively influences intention to transact with electronic marketplace sellers (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). Likewise, researchers find that risk negatively influences intention to use SE services (Mittendorf, 2017).

H4: Perceived risk will negatively influence intention to use the platform.

## 3.2. Motivation model hypotheses

In the motivation model, we first examine four extrinsic motivators: economic benefits, convenience, community belonging, and sustainability. Economic benefits is an extrinsic motivation because it is a reward for engaging in a behaviour. Consumers take advantage of SE platforms because sharing is cheaper than buying and can ofer better value (Mahadevan, 2018; Möhlmann, 2015). For example, it is generally much less expensive to use ride-sharing services than to own, insure, and maintain a car. Similarly, it might be cheaper to use TaskRabbit than to buy tools that might only be used once. Economic benefits significantly influences the intention to use various SE services (Bardhi & Eckhardt, 2012; Hamari et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2017).

H5: Economic benefits will positively influence intention to use the SE platform.

Community belonging is an extrinsic motivation that can influence intention to use. It is an extrinsic motivation because it is motivated by significant others with whom one feels connected (Ryan & Deci, 2000). In the SE, these people might be the providers or other consumers using the platform. Airbnb consumers, for example, may want to use the platform to feel connected to hosts (Tussyadiah, 2015). Consumers may want to interact with others who share similar values and norms. While some SE participants might be commercially oriented, “even for commercially-oriented participants, sociability and volunteerism are important factors” (Sutherland & Jarrahi, 2018: 16). Researchers find platforms enabling community belonging positively influence SE intention to use (Möhlmann, 2015; Sutherland & Jarrahi, 2018).

H6: Perceived community belonging will positively influence intention to use the SE platform.

Convenience is an extrinsic motivation because it is an external means to an intrinsic end (Deci & Ryan, 2000). A convenient service may give one more time to do other intrinsically motivated activities (Zhang et al., 2016). For example, the ability to access and use a ride-sharing platform to get across town can save the time and efort of keeping and maintaining a car and provide extra time to enjoy one’s hobbies. Similarly, employing the services of a tasker can save the time and efort involved in performing household tasks like painting, repairs, and running errands, leaving time for intrinsically rewarding activities. In this way, the motivation of convenience can increase intention to use.

H7: Perceived convenience will positively influence intention to use the SE platform.

Individuals may also participate in the SE because they believe it is ecologically sustainable (Guttentag, 2015). Sustainability is an extrinsic motivator because of the desired outcome of preserving the environment. Many people are motivated to adopt green technologies due to social influence, a felt duty to preserve the environment, or to be perceived as living a green lifestyle. The SE is often perceived as more sustainable and environmentally friendly because it can reduce the development, production, and consumption of assets (Botsman & Rodgers, 2010; Sundararajan, 2016). In this way, consumers who prefer greener consumption in travel, hospitality, and home projects will be more likely to intend to use the SE.

H8: Perceived platform sustainability will positively influence intention to use the SE platform.

Enjoyment is an intrinsic motivation because it is derived from experiencing sensory or aesthetic pleasure, fun, and excitement by performing the activity (Ryan & Deci, 2000; Vallerand, 1997). Just as someone might be intrinsically motivated to go to college because of the pleasure they experience reading about various interesting topics (Pelletier et al., 1995; Vallerand, 1997), consumers might be intrinsically motivated to use SE services because they anticipate the service will be fun, interesting, or enjoyable. For example, consumers might want to use Uber because it is a new way of travelling that can be relaxing and pleasurable. Enjoyment has been found to influence intention to use in many information systems studies (Davis et al., 1992; Van der Heijden, 2004) and in studies predicting its influence on SE intention to use (Hamari et al., 2016; Tussyadiah, 2016; Wu et al., 2017). We predict:

H9a: Enjoyment will positively influence intention to use the SE platform.

Among the H5-H9 motivations, we believe enjoyment will have the strongest influence on use intention, as Tussyadiah (2016) found. According to selfdetermination theory (SDT), the need for growth drives behaviour. As an intrinsic motivator, enjoyment focuses more on personal growth in terms of trying something new and exciting or gaining knowledge or independence than are extrinsic motivators, which are more focused on gaining outcomes and awards (Ryan & Deci, 2017, 2020). Previous research indicates that enjoyment has a higher efect on intention to use when individuals have less experience with a technology or online service (Venkatesh et al., 2012). During early usage, individuals pay more attention to the services’ novelty, anticipating fun/enjoyment. As SE experience increases, the novelty will wear of, and users will concentrate on extrinsic motivators. This research is relevant here because of the newness of the SE to most people at the time of our study (2017).

H9b: Intrinsic motivation (enjoyment) will be a stronger predictor of intention to use than will be any of the extrinsic motivations.

We posit that trust is particularly important for Uber. Thus, the trust model will better predict intention to use Uber than will the motivation model. This may sound counter-intuitive since motivations are typically very powerful persuaders. However, for Uber, news reports will fully inform a user about Uber’s risks, such that safety becomes a top-of-mind fear. Because the transaction is centralised (Sutherland & Jarrahi, 2018) and not under user control, this fear may increase. While the Uber app displays the driver’s picture and the type of vehicle coming, one still does not know the driver’s background, driving ability, physical health, mood, alertness, or character. Knowing the Uber driver is not an accountable-tothe-firm employee (Rosenblat & Stark, 2016) also heightens perceived risk.

Still, trade-ofs exist. Knowing one can rate the driver partially allays felt risk. However, getting in a car controlled by an unfamiliar driver may still be unnerving, especially since riders must focus their hopes (and fears) on the driver’s trustworthiness. Given the salience of these emotions and the preponderance of perceived bodily harm risks for Uber respondents, the trust model will be highly poignant and overshadow motivating factors favouring Uber. Safety issues would outweigh whether the service is green, convenient, enjoyable, or builds community.

H10: For Uber, the trust model will be a stronger predictor of intention to use than will the motivation model.

In contrast, for Airbnb and TaskRabbit, trust should not be as salient to the user. While much news about Airbnb exists (Huertas et al., 2021), more of it has been about its impacts on neighbourhoods and the accommodation’s quality, not about guest safety (Sthapit et al., 2020). Researchers find that feeling safe about the neighbourhood influences intention to use (Cheng & Jin, 2019). However, hosts falsifying property features may eclipse neighbourhood problems. Therefore, the consumer needs fewer safety assurances and can focus on motivation issues. For Airbnb, enjoyment may be more salient to the user due to the trip’s anticipated pleasure. For both Airbnb and TaskRabbit, not having to worry so much about provider safety lets them focus on economic benefits of the service. For TaskRabbit, the user could also focus more on the convenience of more time for other endeavours (producing enjoyment) instead of chores. The TaskRabbit user may also be able to focus on the community belonging aspect of extrinsic motivation, knowing that the tasker belongs to one’s community and is community-loyal, not an employeeloyal to some distant corporation, as with Uber.

H11: For Airbnb and TaskRabbit, the motivation model will be a stronger predictor of intention to use than will the trust model.

## 4. Methodology

We tested the models using questionnaire data about Uber, Airbnb, and TaskRabbit. Uber and Airbnb are well-known. TaskRabbit is an on-demand hiring platform that matches taskers with consumers who need task/errand help. TaskRabbit operated in 21 cities in 2017, 42 cities in 2018 and has expanded to approximately 90 cities across North America and Europe in 2021 (data based on Taskrabbit.com/locations and taskrabbit.co.uk/locations obtained on April 82,021). We chose these platforms to test the models robustly within diferent services.

Our sample was business undergraduate students enrolled in 18 lab sections of a required introductory information systems course in a large public Midwest United States university in April, 2017. Mean age was 21.5. 41% of the sample was female, and 59% male. Ninety-six percent of the sample was born after 1994 and before 2000, making them Generation Zers. We used 1994 as the Gen Z cut-of, because many sources use the term “middle of the 1990s” as the beginning of Gen Z, and the years proposed in most sources for the beginning of Gen Z range from 1990–1997 (see, U.S. Census Bureau, 2020). Students are appropriate because 32% of 18- to 35-year-olds use car services like Uber, 30% use Airbnb-like services, and 22% use cleaning/repair services, far more than do 35+-yearolds at 12%, 11%, and 10%, respectively (Maru/matchbox, 2017). Student opinions are also important as potential early adopters of digital services, to help an SE firm reach critical mass. Martínez-González et al.

(2021) argue Generation Z students are especially relevant because they have capabilities and attitudes/ worries that fit SE use. Of course, studies like ours do not generalise to larger populations. Appendix B lists other sample considerations.

Respondents took the survey during class. For incentives to do so, they received 1.5% of total course points and had a chance to win one of two doublescoop ice cream certificates per lab. This yielded a 96% response rate. We performed mean diference tests among the 18 lab sections and found only 9 of 2394 significant diferences, suggesting no systematic variations. Survey respondents took 14 minutes on average. We eliminated five responses that had missing data or low completion times (cases > 2 standard deviations below the modal time) for a final sample size of 571 (188 Uber, 193 Airbnb, and 190 TaskRabbit).

## 4.1. Procedure

In April 2017, we gave participants an online Qualtrics questionnaire. Respondents were randomly assigned across the three SE platforms. They gave informed consent and provided gender, age, and year in school. The software presented them both a list of SE firm examples (“Uber, Airbnb, Lyft, TaskRabbit, Ola, WeWork . . . ” etc.) and a Wikipedia-based SE description (Appendix C). Next, they reported their impressions of the sharing economy.

Then we provided a description of their randomly assigned SE platform: Uber, Airbnb, or TaskRabbit. This description included a prompt (based on its Wikipedia page – end of Appendix C) and a threeframe picture of their platform app (Figure 2). We expect both the initial and experienced users to learn by seeing the three-frame pictures. Even one exposure to the app picture provides trustworthiness signals to potential users about the provider, firm, and app. Respondents may be able to compare it to an SE, or similar, app they have previously used. For experienced users, seeing the three-frame view of the app could bring up memories of other features, and of its perceived functionality, helpfulness, and reliability. For potential users, the app’s appearance, attractiveness, and anticipated interaction help them form trust in the app. Such initial impressions form beliefs very quickly, even without prior experience (Keith et al., 2015; Tractinsky et al., 2000). We asked them to examine these “closely” and then told them to “answer the best you can based on what you know about [e.g., Uber]”. Next, they answered the questionnaire (see items, Appendix C).

Although the nature of Wikipedia leads to diferences in the manner of presentation and language style across articles, we adapted the Wikipedia articles to ensure that each prompt provided common cues regarding each platform. Each prompt included information regarding 1) the founder, context of founding, and early history, 2) attributes of the platform transaction, 3) current market position and, 4) general characteristics of the platform providers (see Appendix C for the full text of all prompts). We also asked students to rate the prompt from strongly negative (1) to strongly positive (7) towards the service. An ANOVA test found no significant diferences in resulting mean scores between Uber (5.5/7.0), Airbnb (5.4/7.0), and TaskRabbit (5.4/7.0).

## 4.2. Measures

We adapted all but one scale (convenience) from prior research (Appendix C). We developed the convenience scale by examining papers for the theoretical meaning of convenience and finding it means saving time and efort (Zhang et al., 2016), which we incorporated into three items: saving time, being simple to use, and overall convenience. To eliminate alternative explanations, we controlled for gender, the trait of personal innovativeness (Tussyadiah, 2015), platform familiarity (which reduces anxiety – Gefen & Straub, 2004; Hawlitschek et al., 2018), and the reassuring length of platform experience. The latter means the length of time one has used the service and thus difers from Mittendorf et al.’s (2019) “duration”, meaning the length of time one transaction takes.

## 5. Data analysis

We tested the measurement and structural models using the Partial Least Squares methods and SmartPLS 3 software (Ringle et al., 2015). PLS-SEM can handle small sample sizes, slightly nonnormal data, and complex models (Ringle et al., 2012). An a priori power analysis using the combined model recommends a sample size of 190 to detect a .10 efect size, a .05 probability level, and 13 predictors for .80 desired power level. To perform these calculations, we used the danielsoper. com website. Our sample sizes are at or near the recommended number of 190 (Uber: 188, Airbnb: 193, TaskRabbit: 190). Using an anticipated efect size of .15 requires a sample size of 131. This power analysis indicates our sample sizes are small but adequate. All item skewness/kurtosis lie between +1 and −1, except familiarity (between +2 and −2), indicating minor non-normality. Our model has 14 latent constructs (ten research variables and four control variables), with 34 indicators and 13 structural relationships, which are above the average PLS model of 8, 27, and 11, respectively (Ringle et al., 2012), suggesting our combined model is complex, which recommends PLS use.

Following Hair et al. (2019), we analysed measurement model convergent validity, discriminant validity, multicollinearity, common methods bias, and secondorder factor validity (see Appendix D). Results provide adequate evidence for measurement model validity.

Though not hypothesised, this model has informative results (Table 1). ## 6.2. The uber story discussion

Ninety-four percent of our student sample had experi ence with Uber. On average, respondents had used Uber for 1.75 years. They understood Uber by their own experience, as well as by what their peers, the news, and the Wikipedia prompt said. People prefer experiential knowledge over initial impressions or assumptions because personal experience is more reliable, reducing uncertainty (McKnight et al., 1998). What we find interesting is that for the trust model, three variables were significant in predicting intention to use very well $( \mathrm { R } ^ { \bar { 2 } } \ = \ . 6 7 )$ – trusting intention $( \mathtt { p } < . 0 0 1 )$ , trusting beliefs – provider $( \mathtt { p } < . 0 0 1 )$ , and perceived risk $\left( \mathtt { p } < . 0 1 \right)$ . Trusting beliefs – app was not significant in producing the high $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ for the trust model, although it was close $( \beta = . 1 5 ; \ p < . 1 0 )$ , contributing very little to the $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } .$ . The trust variables together formed a very predictive model, explaining two-thirds of the variance in intention to use. What is even more impressive is that trusting intention, trusting beliefs-provider, and perceived risk all stayed significant when motivation variables were added. Further, adding the trust variables to the motivation model decreased the efects of enjoyment, a strong predictor $( \beta = . 4 2 ; \ p < . 0 0 1 )$ in the motivation model, to not significant $( \beta = . 1 2 ; \ p < . 1 0 )$ . ## 6.2.1. It’s counter-intuitive

To us, this is a rather counter-intuitive finding. First, Uber trust tends to be rather high (\~5.0/7.0 for each trust construct). So why would anyone’s trust be so influential? Second, why is trust so necessary when Uber respondents had experience? After all, experience decreases uncertainty and provides hard facts. Wouldn’t solid knowledge about Uber replace the need for trusting it? What is still uncertain about Uber?

Based on our results, the uncertainty isn’t the app. It’s a known quantity to our users, and the app functionality numbers show it is very highly regarded (5.8/ 7.0) with modest variance (std. dev. = 1.00). The uncertainty lies with the provider. First, algorithmic assignment of providers keeps one mostly in the dark about the driver until they arrive; and often, arrival occurs quickly. There’s no bottom-up transaction organising as one finds with Airbnb and TaskRabbit (Sutherland & Jarrahi, 2018). The control one has over the driver choice is pre-empted by the algorithm. Lack of control can lead to a felt need for trust (Grabner-Krӓuter & Kaluscha, 2003).

The Uber data shows the provider is the key area in which trust is needed, since it leads to use intention. Both trusting beliefs – provider and trusting intention focus on the provider. Trusting beliefs-provider taps the provider’s benevolence, honesty, and job competence. These are key attributes so the user can avoid getting in an accident or being late (competence), being robbed (honesty), or being physically harmed (benevolence). Our 94%-experienced subjects have well-formed trusting beliefs in Uber providers. Trusting intention examines the subject’s confidence – for whatever reason – in depending on the provider in a situation of risk.

## 6.3. The airbnb story discussion

Respondents’ scores on their familiarity with Airbnb averaged 4.7/7.0, which translates to just under “5-Moderately Familiar”, on average. Fifty-four percent of our student sample had experience with Airbnb. On average, respondents had used Airbnb for 0.45 years. This means that just over half understood Airbnb by their own experience; others knew only what their peers, the news, and the survey’s Wikipedia prompt said about it.

In contrast to Uber’s unitary trust story, the Airbnb story is that a mixture of trust model and motivation model constructs best predicts use intention. However, the results give a slight edge to motivation. For Airbnb, the motivation model predicts somewhat better (see $\mathrm { Q } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { s } )$ than the trust model for several reasons. These factors likely decrease the importance of trust and risk for Airbnb users. Perceived risk and conve nience that were significant in the separate models became not significant in the combined model. That left two significant trust concepts and two motivation concepts. Still, the combined model predicted at a somewhat higher rate (.70) than either the trust (.60) or motivation (.61) model. ## 6.4. The TaskRabbit story discussion

The TaskRabbit sample had low familiarity (1.3/7.0) and very low length of experience (1.1/7.0). Only nine out of 190 (4.7%) had experience, and four of the nine had less than one year’s experience. $\mathrm { S o } ,$ , this is an initial trust (McKnight et al., 1998), initial motivations sample. People are forming beliefs quickly and using them to form usage intentions. Because of this, some beliefs will probably be less confident and predictive.

The finding (per the BIC/AIC analysis) that the combined model improves prediction over the motivation model shows that using both motivation and trust constructs is best for predicting TaskRabbit intention to use. In the initial phase, one uses whatever first impressions or dispositions they feel they can (McKnight et al., 1998, 2020). The key combined model constructs are enjoyment, trusting intention, and perceived risk. Respondents can feel confident about using each of these variables because they often rely on general initial impressions. For example, driving on an unfamiliar freeway, we see a bridge ahead over the freeway. One glance tells us it is built with the same structurally sound concrete engineering as the bridge we just passed and we quickly trust it not to collapse on us.

Appendix C’s measures show trusting intention is about the general willingness to depend on the provider, whereas trusting beliefs taps beliefs about the provider’s specific attributes. The risk and enjoyment items show these variables to be general. These variables are like reputation, which forms quickly from second-hand information about the trustee (McKnight et al., 1998). Because they are general beliefs, trusting intention, perceived risk, and enjoyment predict intention to use. By contrast, due to low experience using TaskRabbit, it appears the subjects were not able to predict their intention to use by employing the extrinsic motivators or the two specific trusting beliefs. This may be because lack of experiential knowledge kept them from feeling confident in their more detailed trusting beliefs and motivation variables. Therefore, the more general first impressions – trusting intention, perceived risk, and enjoyment – were efective intention-to-use predictors, while the more specific trust and motivation

## 6.5. Additional general research implications

We have been careful not to compare across platforms due to the diferent experience levels, platform attributes, and other factors. However, we have found some common results across platforms:

1. Trusting intention is consistently a key intention to use predictor; and, except for the combined model with Uber, so is enjoyment. So, these two variables should be considered in future research. Table 4 shows the top three predictor variables for each platform.

2. Neither economic benefits, community belonging, nor sustainability usually predicted. These may be due to our narrow sample. However, based on our results and previous work in this area, researchers should pre-test samples to see what might best motivate them.

3. Both the motivation and trust models predicted well. But combining them also seemed to improve prediction, except for Uber, where trust dominated the story. This suggests that both trust and motivation are each important for Airbnb and TaskRabbit.

4. Moreover, even though it is important to consider each model individually, our combined model examines the complementary nature of trust and motivation constructs. The Airbnb and TaskRabbit results imply that considering either set of constructs alone may miss important factors that drive SE use. Future research should do more to examine how motivation and trust constructs complement each other. In addition, due to our eforts to compare “apples to apples” models, we did not utilise the wider nomological networks of either trust or motivations. Adding a broader set of constructs and interrelating them between the two models would be an interesting and fruitful investigation.

An additional point regarding future contextual research involves the long-term impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic. Some SE services require close contact (e.g., Uber, Lyft, etc.), while others, such as Airbnb, require guests to trust that their hosts properly clean and sanitise their facilities between uses. The importance of these aspects of trust should be studied. Although it is likely that these issues may be more salient to SE users post-pandemic, these risks may be similar in non-SE contexts such as taxis and traditional hotel accommodations. Researchers should identify any potential diferentiation in impact of the pandemic on trust perceptions between SE and non-SE providers.

Finally, the results point to surprising strength in the trust model. Not only was the trust model significantly stronger for Uber, as predicted, but the motivation model was not significantly stronger for Airbnb and TaskRabbit. Trust model constructs emerge 67% of the time as the top three predictors in our findings (Table 4). This suggests researchers should consider using a broader set of trust/risk constructs in future SE research.

## 6.6. Practical implications

First, the trusting beliefs in the app results show (for Airbnb and TaskRabbit, not Uber) that how consumers perceive the app’s trust-related attributes (functionality/helpfulness/reliability) can influence intent to use the service. Airbnb’s initial app won an award, suggesting app quality is important. However, our average app trust numbers for Airbnb (4.9/7.0) and TaskRabbit (4.2/7.0) show room for improvement. Therefore, more should be done to perfect the app by enhancing its functionality, its helpfulness to users, and its reliability. The app can also provide trustworthiness signals about the provider and the service. Platforms may have to do more to engender trust than list provider background checks and customer ratings. Firms should be more like BlaBlaCar, whose app displays multiple provider and platform safety assurances via its D.R.E.A.M.S framework (BlaBlaCar, n.d.), adding items like applicable licenses/credentials and provider skill ratings by the platform.

Second, the Uber results suggest that companies may need to strongly address news media reports regarding safety issues of one’s service. A firm can restore trust by devoting significant corporate resources to permanently fix safety issues. Excellent examples of transforming company processes and policies to address damaging news are found in Gillespie et al. (2014 – for a United Kingdom utility) and McKnight et al. (2020, see its Appendix E – for Samsung). For example, Samsung devoted 800 engineers to fixing its Note 7 battery problems and completely revamped its product testing procedures. Then it publicised its eforts about improved processes via a massive press conference and full-page ads in major news outlets (e.g., The Wall Street Journal).

A related way in which trust may be strengthened is through actions that increase the perceived legitimacy of the firm, since trust and legitimacy are related. Newlands and Lutz (2020), in an experimental study of a hypothetical SE hospitality service, found treating people with better procedural, interpersonal, and informational fairness led to higher perceived platform legitimacy. Fairness and legitimacy each relate to trust in the platform and would encourage user adoption. To maximise perceived fairness and legitimacy, the platform can make communication more transparent and give friendlier customer service (Newlands & Lutz, 2020).<sup>3</sup>

Third, this research can help SE platforms improve participation through motivational enhancements. To increase the efects of the enjoyment motivation, firms can do more to instil intrinsically rewarding consumer experiences. For example, although there is some level of gamification for providers in Uber (“Star Rating”) and Airbnb (“Superhosts”), little gamification exists for consumers. Although these platforms heavily encourage user feedback, users do not receive higher status based on their feedback. Recognising users volume and quality of reviews should increase their enjoyment (Hamari, 2017).

Further, firms can improve perceived convenience, such as by streamlining project initiation procedures while ensuring users are protected. Economic benefits can be better illustrated by adding cost comparisons with traditional local lodging options. Sustainability motivations could be addressed by allowing users to request low emission vehicles or lodgings that possess environmentally conscious characteristics, such as alternative energy sources.

## 7. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although utilised previously (e.g., Martínez-González et al., 2021; Möhlmann, 2015), the use of student data strongly limits generalisability. Diferent population sectors will have diferent motivations, as Böcker and Meelen (2017) found. However, university student opinions are important to digital economy firms seeking critical mass. And though our results do not generalise to all university students or even to all Midwest U. S. students, they do at least provide one data point in the literature – one of many studies from which to draw inferences. Second, like Hawlitschek et al.’s (2018) sample, we study subjects with a mix of experience. Our study examines initial trust in TaskRabbit and initial-toearly trust for Airbnb. Therefore, TaskRabbit and Airbnb results may not hold for more experienced phases of trust. However, 94% of the Uber respondents did have experience. In each equation run, we addressed varied experience levels by controlling for both experience and familiarity (see, Table 1). However, due to the respondents’ generally high levels of experience with Uber and lower levels of experience with the other platforms, researchers should build on our findings by using a sample with a broader range of experience on each platform. Third, this is a cross-sectional study. Thus, it provides no hard causal evidence. Future longitudinal studies can remedy this. A fourth limitation is the grouping of items on our questionnaire. Although the debate on the grouping of items in psychometric studies is not resolved, the grouping of items has the potential to cause respondents to “guess” hypotheses, leading to potential method bias (Straub et al., 2004). Further, the grouping of items has been proposed to artificially inflate some reliability measures such as Cronbach’s alpha, while at the same time reducing construct validity (Goodhue and Loiacono, 2002). Such cautions suggest the results of this study should consider any biases that may be present due to our grouping of survey items.

## 8. Conclusion

We investigate how a trust model and a motivation model work within three SE platforms. For Generation Z university students, we find the motivation model works marginally better than the trust model for Airbnb and TaskRabbit but works somewhat better when trust constructs are added. In this way, trust constructs complement the motivation constructs. That is the main story for Airbnb and TaskRabbit. The story for Uber is quite diferent. For Uber, the trust model works significantly better, and the motivation constructs add hardly any predictive power beyond that of the trust constructs, meaning the constructs are not complementary. Another story emerged for TaskRabbit, a platform with which nearly all our respondents lacked experience. In the combined model, the variables that worked best for it were general beliefs (enjoyment, trusting intention, and perceived risk) rather than specific beliefs (the extrinsic motivators, the two trusting beliefs). This is likely because lack of direct personal experience with the service makes these specific beliefs less mature or well-informed (Lankton et al., 2016). This means the respondent is less confident in them, which makes them less powerful predictors of use intention.

In terms of individual constructs, we find that enjoyment was by far the most powerful of the motivation predictors, followed by convenience. In the combined model, enjoyment also nominally out-predicted any of the trust model variables for Airbnb and TaskRabbit, but not for Uber, where three trust constructs all out-predicted enjoyment. The four constructs of the trust model tend to predict better than the extrinsic motivators, with trusting intention consistently being nominally the most predictive trust model construct. Trusting beliefs in the provider only predicted well for Uber, while trusting beliefs in the app worked especially well for Airbnb. We also show that using both motivation and trust constructs for Airbnb and TaskRabbit improved prediction. We recommend scholars continue to test and refine which combinations of trust and motivation constructs work best in particular situations, experience levels, ages, and platforms.
