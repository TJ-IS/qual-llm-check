# Generational diferences in handling technology interruptions: a qualitative study

Corey Baham<sup>a</sup>, Pankush Kalgotra <sup>b</sup>, Forough Nasirpouri Shadbad<sup>c</sup> and Ramesh Sharda<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Management Science and Information Systems, Oklahoma State University Stillwater, Stillwater, Oklahoma, United States; <sup>b</sup>Department of Systems and Technology, Harbert College of Business, Auburn University, Auburn, Alabama, United States; <sup>c</sup>Business Information Systems, Oregon State University, Corvallis, Oregon, United States

## ABSTRACT

Digital native and digital immigrant user types characterise the diferences between those who grew up in a world of ubiquitous information systems and those who pre-date it. The rise in computer-mediated communication (CMC) technologies such as emails, text messages, and social media creates more opportunities for interruptions. Researchers have explored the impact of growing up in a world of technology. However, little research has explored the potential diferences between digital natives and digital immigrants handling technology interruptions. This paper uses a qualitative approach to examine the extent to which individuals handle CMC interruptions diferently based on the role of technology and its level of pervasiveness in the environment in which they grew up. Thirty-three interviews were conducted with individuals from diferent population groups based on age and gender to understand how technology interruptions are handled in work and social life. The findings support existing pre- and post-technology interruption frameworks and explain how and why generational diferences exist in the process of CMC interruption handling. The theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

KEYWORDS Computer-mediated communication; interruptions; multi-tasking; technostress; digital natives; digital immigrants; qualitative analysis

## 1. Introduction

Computer-mediated communication (CMC) refers to the communication between two or more individuals through computer networks. CMC technologies such as instant messaging, emails, Internet-based social networking, and microblogging have made communications more readily available. However, these technologies are also likely to interrupt a user, delaying a task and decreasing task performance (Adler & Benbunan-Fich, 2012; Aral et al., 2012; Speier et al., 1999). O’Conaill and Frohlich (1995, p. 262) defined an interruption as a “synchronous interaction that is not initiated by the subject, is unscheduled, and suspends the current activity of the recipient”. Although some CMCs are essential for completing work-related tasks, many interruptions caused by CMCs are considered as the dark side of information technology due to their negative impacts on task performance (e.g., task delays and management costs; Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Tarafdar, DArcy et al., 2015; Tarafdar et al., 2013).

Several factors have been proposed to improve understanding of the sources of interruption in our daily lives. Kalgotra et al. (2017) linked the number of interruption sources, the absence of barriers to interruptions, and the proximity to sources of interruptions to increased interruption frequency. Such conditions challenge our ability to sustain attention on primary tasks (Ward et al., 2017). For example, research shows that knowledge workers spend an average of more than two hours a day attending to emails alone (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018; Marulanda-Carter & Jackson, 2012). Other CMCs are often combined with email, leading to further intrusions and taking attention away from primary tasks (Sonnentag et al., 2018). The time to resume a single task after an interruption can be up to 25 minutes, and in some cases, workers may not return to their initial tasks at all (González & Mark, 2005; Iqbal & Horvitz, 2007; Jackson et al., 2003; Roth, 2018). Due to their perceived consequences, CMC interruptions may induce stress and negative perceptions of CMC technologies in some individuals (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar et al., 2011).

Furthermore, the phenomenal growth in working from home, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, has increased our reliance on IT and susceptibility to CMC interruptions. Overall, the growing potential for interruptions in work and social life due to CMCs, our understanding of their impacts, and the challenges in managing them motivate more research. One potentially impactful yet understudied context for studying why and how individuals handle CMC interruptions diferently is the role of technology and its level of pervasiveness in the environment in which they grew up.

Because the rapid innovation and adoption of information systems have led to technological ubiquity in a relatively short amount of time, generational diferences have been suggested to explain why individuals interact with technology diferently. Prensky (2001) introduced the concepts of digital natives and digital immigrants and argued that the younger generation (i.e., born after 1980) has higher information and communication tech nology (ICT) competency than the older generation. Digital natives (DN) are “those who have grown up in a world where the use of information and communications technology is pervasive and ubiquitous” (Vodanovich et al., 2010, p. 711). In contrast, digital immigrants (DI) are “those who were not born into the digital world – they learnt to use information systems at some stage in their adult lives” (Vodanovich et al., 2010, p. 711). Researchers have explored the impact of growing up in a world of pervasive information systems (Niehaves & Plattfaut, 2014; Srivastava, 2004). Not only does Prensky (2001) contend that DNs process information diferently because they are raised in a technologically ubiquitous environment, but he even suggests that the brains of DNs have changed physically. Additional research suggests that DNs exhibit higher comfort levels for disclosing personal information, more collaboration over peer-to-peer networks, and increased needs for immediate gratification (Leung, 2003; McMahon & Pospisil, 2005). However, some researchers have criticised the arguments by Prensky (2001), including the existence of a digital divide between the generations. For instance, Guo et al.’s (2008) experiment found no significant diference between generations in ICT competency in teacher education. Similarly, La and Hong (2015) found no statistically significan diference among generations in technology usage.

Despite mixed results about diferences between generations in IT usage or competency, age is an important factor in understanding diferences in the impact of CMC interruptions. Researchers report that older adults are less adept at handling interruptions than younger adults (Arnau et al., 2019; Tams & Hill, 2016). Studies of millennials have found that most engage in multiple tasks simultaneously, switching rapidly back and forth from one task to another. However, younger and older adults experience similar mental limitations in the types of tasks that can be multi-tasked (Carrier et al., 2009; Mokhtari et al., 2015). Using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), Clapp et al. (2011) found that interruptions have a more disruptive efect on the performance of older adults who were unable to reactivate the mental processes to the same level as before the interruption occurred. In comparison, younger adults could re-engage the task to the same level after an interruption.

In addition to the CMC interruptions literature, we draw upon literature that has examined how individuals in diferent age groups perceive CMC interruption-based stress. Technostress, or stress induced by technologies such as CMCs, may occur when there is a misfit between a person and the technological environment and a person cannot cope or deal with technology healthily (Ayyagari et al., 2011). This research has shown a positive relationship between interruptions and technostress (Agogo & Hess, 2018; Camacho et al., 2013; Tams et al., 2018). However, results were not conclusive about whether there is a diference in technostress perceptions between younger (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar et al., 2011) and older generations (Çoklar & Sahin, 2011; Czaja et al., 2006; Darowski et al., 2008; Tams et al., 2018).

The varying results in past research concerning the potential diferences in the perception and use of IT across generations suggest that more context is needed to understand the degree to which this phenomenon exists or can be perceived, especially when handling CMC interruptions. Prior work examines the physiological efects of cognitive ageing. We contend that a generational analysis is appropriate for moving the CMC interruption handling literature forward. The generational analysis encompasses both age and circumstances (Walker, 2016) to study the sociological impacts based on the rapid growth of technology. Thus, in this study, our research question is: To what extent do generational diferences (i.e., DN/DI) impact how individuals handle CMC interruptions?

Prior studies have investigated the deleterious efects of CMC interruptions, such as decreased productivity, increased task errors, and increased work stress (Lin et al., 2013). Several frameworks highlight the causes and efects of CMC interruptions. However, there is limited understanding concerning how diferent user types (i.e., DNs and DIs) perceive and manage technology interruptions. Therefore, it is vital to understand how DN and DI user types perceive technology interruptions to develop efective management strategies for helping people deal with CMC interruptions. In addition, prior studies have primarily focused on the consequences of the interruption. We use Kalgotra et al.’s (2017) adaptation of routine activity theory to identify antecedents of the interruptions across diferent generations and the consequences to understand the causes and efects of interruptions in one model.

While experimental design methods ofer tight control over the research setting, such methods are limited in their ability to simulate real-world situations of CMC interruptions where several contextual factors are at play (Galluch et al., 2015). Prior quantitative studies suggested that individual diferences such as age, gender, and computer confidence significantly impact CMC interruption-based technostress perception and work performance (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tarafdar, Pullins et al., 2015; Tarafdar et al., 2011). However, why some groups feel and perceive diferently is not completely understood. In addition, the factors related to technostress perceptions are understudied, and researchers have called for further investigations on group diferences (Tams et al., 2018; Tarafdar et al., 2007). Therefore, in this study, we use a qualitative approach (i.e., interviews) to understand the dynamics of real-life CMC interruptions for both DN and DI users. We leverage the strengths of qualitative research (e.g., answering how and why questions) to better understand how diferent user types handle and are impacted by CMC interruptions. The remainder of the paper is as follows. Section 2 provides a review of the literature on CMC interruptions and technostress to frame our understanding of the impact of CMC interruptions on DNs and DIs. Section 3 describes the methodology and details the participants, data collection, and data analysis. Section 4 discusses the results of interviews, including the emerging themes. Finally, we conclude the paper by discussing the implications of the findings.

## 2. Literature review

Several studies have sought to understand the impact of CMC interruptions on individual performance (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Clapp et al., 2011). In addition, scholars have recently investigated mediating factors in this relationship (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018; Chen & Karahanna, 2018; Sonnentag et al., 2018). However, although CMCs have permeated the lives of all generations, relatively few studies consider the influence of age, particularly generational diferences

To understand the literature on the impact of CMC interruptions, we sought to understand the perceptions of CMC interruptions across DNs and DIs. The discussion of DNs and DIs began in the late 1990s when the Internet was transforming all facets of society (Tapscott, 1999). By the mid-2000s, researchers began to argue that technology had spawned an “age of interruptions” as we were continuously interrupted by emails, instant messages, mobile security notifications, and social media alerts (Friedman, 2006). The combination of mobile devices and push notification technology, which alerts users when information arrives, has increased the potential for and concerns about CMC interruptions. In addition, the rapid adoption of CMCs has led to two distinct generational cohorts (DN/DI). Their difering levels of technological ubiquity highlight the importance of understanding the potential implications of age diferences on CMC interruption handling. Thus, we review the studies that consider the impact of age or generational diferences on CMC interruptions and highlight the knowledge gaps in this literature.

Recent literature finds that the motivation to use CMCs is diferent across generations (Kesharwani, 2020). Reportedly, DNs prefer to use CMCs for entertainment and hedonic purposes, whereas DIs prefer CMCs for information search and performing utilitarian activities (Calvo-Porral & Pesqueira-Sanchez, 2019). Additionally, because of diferences in the level of embeddedness of technology in the lives of the user types, DNs are more prone to technology addiction (Wang et al., 2019) and more likely to experience anxiety when separated from their cell phones (Van Volkom et al., 2014). While the rapid growth in CMC usage has potentially negative consequences for DNs and DIs, prior studies found that the older generation is more susceptible to the negative impacts of CMC interruptions (Arnau et al., 2019; Clapp et al., 2011; Tams et al., 2018). Beyond lower task performance, scholars have observed that the older generation is more susceptible to more significant mental workload, frustration, annoyance, and stress due to CMC interruptions (Tams & Hill, 2016; Tams et al., 2018).

In addition to the CMC interruptions literature, the recent technostress literature has yielded novel insights concerning the role of stress caused by CMC interruptions. For example, Agogo and Hess (2018) found that CMC use can lead to unintended consequences that make users feel stressed. Furthermore, some CMC interruptions, such as those in mobile technologies, are sources of stress that a person cannot process and handle (Tams et al., 2020). Consequently, CMC interruptions reduce an individual’s cognitive focus on primary work-related tasks and take longer to resume their primary tasks (Camacho et al., 2013). Furthermore, scholars propose that specific technology characteristics (e.g., usability, the pace of change) are related to stressors such as techno-overload, techno-uncertainty, techno-invasion, and technocomplexity<sup>1</sup> (Tarafdar et al., 2007). These technostressors enhance the misfit between an individual and a technological environment (Ayyagari et al., 2011) and exacerbate the adverse efects of CMC interruptions. In other words, CMCs impose performing more tasks, dealing with complex systems with continuing changes, and being continuously connected, all of which ultimately could create stress. Moreover, the stress resulting from not coping with technology leads to further task interruptions and a loss in individual work performance, productivity, job satisfaction, and organisational commitment (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Tams et al., 2014; Tarafdar et al., 2010).

Individuals’ evaluations and perceptions of technostressors can be diferent depending on individuals characteristics. In particular, studies on the role of age on stress caused by CMC interruptions suggests evi dence of age diferences in low levels of cognitive abilities, computer self-eficacy, computer anxiety, and distraction in the older generation implying that DIs perceive high levels of technostress (Czaja et al., 2006; Darowski et al., 2008; Tams et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2016). However, these studies mainly focus on ageing as impacting one’s physiological response to CMC interruptions. More insights are needed to explain age diferences in CMC interruption handling, such as potential sociological and generational diferences in a person’s level of submergence in a technologically ubiquitous world. Prior research suggests that being raised in a digital environment fosters increased levels of computer experience, reliance on CMCs for communication, and comfort with multitasking (Teo 2013). A sociological perspective can ofer deeper insights into how social processes shape human lives. In the context of our study, the socialisation of consistent emersion, experience, and practice with CMCs has made DNs intimately familiar with CMCs. However, the implications of this digital nativity have not been explored for CMC interruption handling. The findings regarding the impact of age on the stress caused by CMC interruptions and the potential value of a more sociological understanding of the efects of technological ubiquity on CMC interruption handling motivate this research. We use a qualitative approach to address these knowledge gaps and answer our research question. Against the backdrop of related research, we describe the current research gaps and why they merit examination in Table 1.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Participants and sampling strategy

Participants included faculty, staf, and students from a large Midwestern university in the United States, as listed in Table 2. These participants were appropriate for this study because they used similar technologies to complete their daily work and dealt with similar CMCs interruptions. The common institutional and technological backdrop was a strength of our sample because it provided a relatively consistent set of technologies for understanding CMC interruption handling in a real-life rather than laboratory context.

Potential faculty and staf professionals were identified via the university website. Variations in age and gender were needed, so we used background information (e.g., biography, years of service, year of graduation, self-identifiers) to identify potential participants. Faculty and staf participants were recruited via email, asking for their agreement to a one-hour face-to-face interview about their experiences with technology. Student participants were recruited by emailing the professors teaching diferent courses in the university’s business college.

Thirty-three adults from ages 19 to 62 participated in the study. Those born in or after 1980 were DNs, and those born before 1980 were DIs. One participant was born after 1980 but considered himself a DI (DIM7), so we put him in the DI group. Participants self-identified as male or female. In total, 16 males (7 DI and 9 DNs) and 17 females (9 DI and 8 DNs) participated in the study. These participants comprised of four groups based on generation and gender: digital natives females (DNF) with an average age of 21 years (SD = 1.85), digital natives males (DNM) with an average age of 22.56 years (SD = 4.30), digital immigrant females (DIF) with an average age of 46 years (SD = 8.75) and digital immigrant males (DIM) with an average age of 45.86 years (SD = 10.57). We maximised variability across DNs and DNs by enlisting participants with a range of ages and individual characteristics.

Additionally, we accounted for occupational diferences through within and cross-group analysis as discussed in Appendix A. Rather than only comparing older professionals to younger students, we compared older and younger professionals (e.g., comparing opposite poles of DIs such as those around 60 years of age to those around 35 years of age). Adding students (ages 19–25) provided additional points of comparison. Therefore, we compared younger professionals to both students and older professionals to examine their similarities and diferences in handling interruptions.

## 3.2. Data collection

We conducted face-to-face, semi-structured interviews with each participant using open-ended questions. The interviews lasted between 45 and 60 minutes, focused on interruptions caused by IT during the participants’ daily work, and were audiotaped and transcribed verbatim. All participants were asked similar questions. The interview protocol followed Spradley (2016) approach to interviewing, which consisted of three sections: descriptive, structural, and contrasts. The data were collected and analysed iteratively. Following Myers and Newman (2007, p. 17), rather than aiming for a large enough sample to generalise results from, we focused on making sure that the people interviewed represent varied voices on the issue of CMC interruptions. We gathered interviews until we reached the point of saturation (i.e., no new insights were discovered in the interviews). Table 3 contains a sample of the questions from our interview protocol.

In our thematic analysis, the broader code categories were mapped in the dimensions of technology interruptions (reviewed from the past literature) to position our results and then identify the emergent themes. Early on, McFarlane (1997) identified seven dimensions underlying the consequences of human interruptions: source, individual characteristics of receiver, coordination method, the meaning of interruption, the method of expression, the channel of conveyance, and the expected change by the interruption. Later, Speier et al. (2003) developed a framework with four dimensions impacting user performance from interruptions. The first dimension is the characteristics of interruption tasks such as frequency, timing, duration, content, complexity, form of the interruption, level of the interrupter, and social expectations. The second dimension is primary task characteristics such as the complexity or newness of the task. The third dimension considered was individual characteristics, such as age, gender, and expertise. Finally, the fourth dimension defined the processing mechanisms or coordination method, such as sequential, pre-emptive, or simultaneous. Extending the Speier et al. (2003) framework, Basoglu et al. (2009) explained that antecedents of interruptions have indirect efects on performance through a cognitive load (i.e., cognitive load mediates the relationship between interruption frequency, task complexity, and individual characteristics with the user performance).

For precursors of human interruption, we adapt the factors afecting interruption frequency by employing routine activity theory, as discussed by Kalgotra et al. (2017) (i.e., number of interruption sources, targetness, and absence of guardians). First, we define the source of an interruption as a device or an application that seeks attention and triggers an interruption. Second, the factor related to the management of interruptions is called an absence of guardians. We define a guardian as a tool, application, or any medium that reduces an opportunity for the source to interrupt the individual. We verified that using tools to manage interruptions can afect interruption frequency. Finally, the third factor was targetness, which we define as the degree to which the individual positions themself in an environment suitable for the proximal sources to interrupt him. Drawing on routine activity theory in the criminology literature, our study tested how exposure to interruption sources makes an individual a suitable target for the interruption. In other words, an individual provides more opportunities to the interruption sources by making themselves a potential target of interruptions. Ward et al. (2017) also suggested that the mere presence of a smartphone next to an individual yields negative consequences on sustaining attention. The authors argued that a smartphone occupies cognitive resources even in the absence of physical interaction with the device, thus afecting the performance of the primary task.

Following Speier et al. (2003), Basoglu et al. (2009), McFarlane (2002), and Kalgotra et al. (2017), we adopt eight dimensions of interruptions: number of sources, targetness, absence of guardians, primary task characteristics, interruption characteristics, individual characteristics, cognitive mediators and impact of interruptions. In addition, we employ a deductive approach to map codes to the dimension of interruptions, as described by Elo and Kyngäs (2008; see, Table 5).

We briefly describe the codes in each dimension. The sources of interruptions considered by the participants were software applications such as emails, social media, and text messaging. Software applications were the most frequently cited interruptions because most participants had smartphones, which allow notifications from several applications. Interestingly, participants sometimes used language referring to their devices (e.g., “My phone interrupts me”) while referring to the software applications that were the source of the interruptions. The targetness cell contains the codes related to the opportunities due to the proximity of individuals to the interruption sources. Participants often highlighted how their smartphones, a primary source of distraction, are often kept nearby. For example, DNM3 mentioned that “I keep it next to me”. Thus, the targetness cell includes codes such as “keep next to me”, “always with me”, “check it no matter what happens”, “part of life”, and “immediate response”. The absence of guardian cell contains the codes related to the opportunities created due to a lack of managing interruptions. These include “no fixed time”, “no notification management”, and “no fixed plans to manage communication”.

From our participants’ perception, characteristics of technology interruptions included the frequency, duration, content, timing, form, medium, importance, priority, urgency, afect, source, and complexity of interruptions. The category of codes related to the characteristics of primary tasks found across the participants included: importance, priority, complexity, newness, size of the task, and stage of the primary task. Regardless of age and gender, participants noted that all interruptions were not equally weighted. Also, an interruption for one person may not be an interruption for another. For instance, an email from a subordinate might not compel someone to stop working on their primary task. On the other hand, an email from a superior might result in someone interrupting their primary task. Perhaps, the interruption could become a primary task based on its importance and priority.

Characteristics of the decision-maker included gender, age, generation, experience, practice, evolution, and life experiences. These characteristics enabled us to understand better the participants demographics and how the diferent demographic clusters process interruptions. Through coding, we also learned that the efect of interruptions on performance is mediated through cognitive factors. Cognitive mediators include methods of coordination (i.e., whether the response was immediate or negotiated), if the task processing mechanisms were simultaneous or sequential, mood, cognitive load, time of the day, interestingness, and engagement in the task.

Finally, we also coded the perceived impacts of interruptions on performance. We found codes related to both negative impacts and positive impacts of interruptions. The codes related to negative impact include “focus shift”, “lose concentration”, “break train of thoughts”, “decrease in productivity”, “distract from work”, “impact on after-work life”, “time-waste”, “annoy”, “frustrate/irritate”, “more errors”, and “habit”. On the other hand, the codes related to positive impact are “feel better”, “relax”, “keep in touch”, “improve mood”, and “more connected”. The negative codes appear related to the utilitarian nature of the communication, whereas the positive codes are more related to the hedonic nature of CMCs.

The process of mapping codes into the interruption framework dimensions required multiple iterations. As a result, some emergent codes led to new themes, which we discuss in the next section. Furthermore, we conducted constant comparisons between our codes and CMC interruptions literature. We also began to identify technology interruption-related studies in the technostress literature. In addition, we observed several patterns in the data that fit within the existing dimensions that had not been elaborated on in the existing literature. We discuss these findings in the next section.

## 4. Results

Our findings support the general distinction of DI and DN user types and suggest that DNs tend to be more immersed in CMC technology use from younger ages than DIs. DIF1 said, “[DNs] have used technology from since they were a baby, unlike me”. The majority of participants in both groups pointed out how DNs have a higher comfort level with technology overall. Since DIs have seen more radical changes in personal communication technology than the DNs, most DIs can recall both a world without readily available CMCs and the transition from that world to today.

Second, the role that CMCs played in a person’s life impacted how they perceived CMC interruptions. Verifying the existing literature, DIs commonly viewed proper technology use as staying within the rules or guidelines (Oblinger, 2003). Many DIs referred to the proper use or actual purpose of specific technologies and insinuated that DNs do not always understand or use technology within its intended purpose (DIM3). Such guidelines discouraged the continuous use of CMCs beyond task fulfilment or beyond prescribed time limits (e.g., 10 minutes, DIF3; weekends, DIF8; evenings, DIF9, DIM5). Crossing preset boundaries was seen as wasting time or non-productive. These guidelines were used as a baseline for self-regulation because CMC interruptions were identified as threats to productivity and needed to be managed. Unlike most DIs in the study, many DNs did not see incoming CMC content during a primary task as an interruption but considered it a part of life. DNM2 commented,

I think now [interruptions are] part of life. They are just so twined with us so that everyone always has their smartphones on them so not just a phone but smartphone and [interruptions] just come to be expected and become a part of life now.

Third, the role that CMCs played in a person’s life impacted how they perceived how others interacted with CMCs. For instance, because DIs tended to place stricter boundaries on their technology usage, they often noted the lack of conformance to such standards in DNs. When asked about the diference between their and DN’s use of CMCs, several DIs even expressed their concerns about DNs’ CMC addiction. To this point, DIF2 stated,

I think (younger) people need to watch [out] that they’re not like addicted to [CMC technology]. (Speaking as a DN) ‘I need to know something instantly.’ Then you’re constantly checking, and you never have any downtime.

You can definitely tell that people are addicted to wanting to know what’s going on the minute it’s going on. They’ll want to pull out their phones, and when you have little breaks to work on stuf and the minute they’re finished with their task, they want to pull out their phone and [see]. They just feel like they’re missing out if they don’t know the instant that something happens.

These comments and others point to a more cautious and rule-based approach to using CMCs. In comparison, DNs generally viewed themselves as more fastabsorbing and efective in using CMCs than DIs. DNF3 explained,

They [DIs] do not use technology fast or efectively. So they get more frustrated, but I do not think they use it often. Like if I text my mom, she would take hours to respond as opposed to my friend, who would text me back in minutes.

Comparing her parent’s adoption of technology to that of the younger generation, DNF1 stated, “I think the younger generation adapts quickly. My mom never had a smartphone. When she got an iPhone, she got used to it. But it took longer for her to get used to it, compared to teenagers”. Also, DNs suppose that DIs have higher levels of techno-complexity since the adoption and learning of new CMCs was seen as too complex and required expending time and efort to learn, which was frustrating for some. For example, DNF3 stated, “My parents are usually (get) more frustrated because it is something they have to learn and adopt to because they are not used to it”. These types of comments further illustrate many DNs’ perceived adeptness with modern technology in comparison with DIs. Beyond these general observations, we elaborate on specific instances in which the DNs and DIs difered in our analysis compared to existing interruptions frameworks. We present these findings in the following sections.

## 4.1. Digital natives vs digital immigrants: precursors to CMC interruptions

Beginning with factors that afect interruption frequency (i.e., sources, targetness, and guardians; Kalgotra et al., 2017), sources of CMC interruption were similar across user types in terms of emails, social media, and text messaging. Still, user types difered in the frequency of using these sources. For instance, DIs, who were older and tended to be employed full-time, reported higher uses of email for work. In particular, DIMs mentioned email as their most frequent interruption, which, although related to their work, also interfered with the task at hand. Both user types used social media primarily for leisure, but it was more consistently cited as an interruption for DNs. They tended to connect more throughout the day via smartphone. Several DNs discussed their connection to social media as follows: “I check Facebook constantly to see if there are any new likes on my page”. DNM9. “I grew up on my phone, so I feel weird when I don’t have it. I am lost”. DNM8.

In terms of targetness, most respondents carried their phones or additional mobile computing devices with them. Employees primarily used computing devices in their ofices. Beyond the proximity of CMC interruption sources, the negative impact of CMC interruptions was related to the degree to which guardians and methods of managing interruptions were used. On average, DN and DIs difered in the use of guardians. While DNs used a broad spectrum of guardian approaches to deal with interruptions (e.g., no guardians at all, silencing phones, setting aside phones, shutting down phones, actively managing phone interruptions), DIs implemented stricter guardian approaches on average. The DIMs, 85% or 6 of 7, take the strictest measures to prevent interruptions (e.g., no absence of guardians, disabling notification systems). We referred to these precursors as interruptions pathways (i.e., precursor – -> interruptions) because we observed that they lead to interruptions (e.g., no guardians lead to more frequent message notifications) that were then perceived and handled in a variety of ways.

## 4.2. CMC interruption dynamics

To understand the complex interruption handling dynamics, we examined the interaction between user type (DN/DI) and interruption characteristics, primary task characteristics, cognitive factors, and technostress perceptions. Prior studies examined the relationship between interruption frequency and cognitive mediators such as cognitive load (Tams et al., 2018); we considered how additional interruption characteristics such as interruption importance and interruption medium might interact with frequency to impact several cognitive mediators. Interruption importance refers to the perceived level of importance (e.g., high or low) of an interruption relative to the primary task. Interruption medium refers to the type of CMC through which the interruption is experienced (e.g., email message or text/instant message). We also noted the subsequent impacts of the relationship between interruption characteristics and cognitive mediators on outcomes (e.g., technostress and task performance) and whether there was a significant diference in CMC interruption handling between DNs and DIs based on these relationships.

Both DNs and DIs related experiencing stress due to cognitive overload in situations where CMC interruptions were persistent and highly important relative to the primary task. For instance, irritation and annoyance were often heightened when interruptions were frequent, or the primary task required undivided attention. When the frequency was high and the importance was low, DIs reported stress (i.e., technooverload, techno-invasion) due to cognitive overload. In contrast, DNs reported little to no stress related to cognitive overload due to lower perceptions of technoinvasion. When the frequency was low and the importance was high, neither user type reported undue stress due to cognitive overload. Except for a few DNs (DNM7, DNM8, DNF8), most respondents used a mental or digital priority system to narrow their attention to the primary task while prioritising subsequent tasks in order of importance.

The impact of the interruption medium on the relationship between frequency, importance, and cognitive mediators was complex. Participants reported frequent interruptions and cognitive overload from all CMCs. When task importance and interruption frequency were high, some participants turned of notifications. email notification was the most dificult to halt due to its everyday use in communicating workrelated tasks. Other interruption mediums often accompanied frequent email notifications (e.g., a text from a supervisor about an email), contributing to greater techno-overload. DIs tended to halt social media notifications first and regulate text and emai notifications, whereas DNs employed less strict regulations to CMCs in general. Additionally, both user types were consistent in saying that CMC interruptions were easier to deal with while performing simple tasks but more disruptive while performing more complex tasks. We referred to the interaction between DN/DI user types, interruption characteristics, and primary task characteristics as interruptions context.

They provide the context concerning how an interruption was perceived and impacted how it was cognitively processed (i.e., interruption context – -> cognitive mediators).

Cognitive mediators (i.e., process mechanism, cognitive load, and coordination method) were pivotal in understanding the impact of technological embeddedness on CMC interruption processing and its overall impact on outcomes. First, there is little doubt that practice makes people more eficient. Long-term memory theory suggests that practice can help develop the ability to manage interruptions eficiently (Oulasvirta & Saariluoma, 2006). This notion of practice and experience manifested several times during the study for the increasing CMC interruptions. Often the way a person perceived CMC use and dealt with CMC interruptions was derived from their personal experiences that fit the context of CMC use. Our findings suggest that being raised in a technologically ubiquitous environment has led to diferent perceptions of CMC interruptions between DIs and DNs. Respondents consistently pointed to diferences in the increased amount of practice and experience with CMCs that DNs have had compared to DIs. This diference was most pronounced on the extreme ends of each user type (i.e., youngest DNs and oldest DIs) unless there was an intervening factor such as a DI working in IT or a DN growing up in a rural area void of modern technologies. For instance, DIF1, an IT professor, noted how much more comfortable she is with CMCs than most of her peers. Among DIs, younger professionals had more practice and experience than older professionals.

Furthermore, individuals born near 1980 are considered part of the millennial generation and tend to have both user types characteristics in their CMC interruption handling practices. These participants cited how they were old enough to see a significant shift in personal communication devices, yet young enough to have seen these devices dominate how they communicate for most of their lives. For example, DIF3, born in 1983, described being “on the cusp of millennials and Generation X”. She stated that she might go five months in a row of daily social media use and then three months with little to no use. She also stated that constant email and text notifications were both welcomed and seen as disruptive. The differences between the extreme ends of both user types and the tension felt by those in the centremost point further illustrate the general impact of growing up in a technologically ubiquitous environment. These differences impacted a person’s cognitive processing of CMC interruptions.

In terms of cognitive processing mechanisms, we found that technological embeddedness seemed to improve perceived simultaneous task completion (i.e., multitasking) ability using CMCs as observed in DNs. This improvement was most apparent in the perceived multi-tasking ability of DNMs (44%, 4 of 9) versus DIMs (0%, 0 of 7). Because DNs were generally more comfortable with multi-tasking using CMCs than DIs, DNs were better able to avoid technooverload and deal with interruptions as they occurred. In line with previous findings (Stoet et al., 2013), we also found diferences across gender as females engaged in more simultaneous task completion than males.

Apart from DNs growing up in a technologically ubiquitous environment, multitasking practice and experience were honed in previous jobs and DIFs balancing multiple roles such as wife, mother, and employee. While some of these findings may follow societal gender norms or stereotypes, there were indeed exceptions. Participants detailed the sources where they honed their skills (e.g., “I think that probably has to do with raising kids”: DIF7; “I learned [to multitask] through my job”.: DIF9). According to DIFs in this study, multiple roles in their life have enabled them to perform multiple tasks quickly. We note that multitasking practice and experience, whether developed from digital nativity or other contexts, impact how a person thought about interruptions (i.e., interruption context – -> multitasking – -> cognitive mediators).

Second, a person’s cognitive load and processing mechanism for task completion afected their interruption coordination methods. For instance, a limited ability to process multiple tasks necessitated a sequential task completion approach and coordination strategies to reduce technostress and the negative impacts of CMC interruptions. Once interrupted, respondents vacillated between negotiated or immediate coordination methods of responding to CMC interruptions. On the one hand, immediately responding to an interruption can complete the interruption task quickly but may result in a more significant overall disruptive efect on productivity. On the other hand, a negotiated solution can delay the interruption task for a long time and may not afect the primary task as much. Prior literature suggests that negotiated solutions can be the best solution for managing interruptions (McFarlane, 2002) because negotiated solutions allow the user to handle interruptions at their time of choice. We found that DIs used more negotiated solutions, while DNs used more immediate solutions because they were more easily distracted by interruptions. Because of an inability to multi-task, DIMs tended to take more drastic actions to quell CMC interruptions (e.g., locking doors, shutting of phones, ignoring emails) and conditions of techno-invasion. In comparison, DIFs tended to be less restrictive for extended durations (e.g., put the phone aside, except for scheduled social media breaks). One DIF noted a stark diference in how she and her male colleagues dealt with relatively high volumes of unread emails. She said,

I also think there’s a gender diference there. My male [colleagues] will just delete [subordinate] e-mails that they don’t think is worthy of their response, like, “Eh, just delete them.” I have not gotten to the place of being able to do that, even when it’s completely an absurd request.

Despite experiencing annoyance and frustration under the most stressful conditions, DNs tended to view the frequent inflow of important interruptions as a part of life and either implemented temporal strategies (e.g., placing their phone in silent mode) or none at all. In the latter case, DNs dealt with CMC interruptions as they came as best as they could, without any specific coordination strategy. Consequently, we observed the phenomenon of habitual phone checking more in DNs who used immediate solutions and lacked regulations in their CMC use (i.e., real-time coordination – -> emergent impacts). This habit was dificult to resist even while completing important tasks. One DNF described, “Even while I am in the midst of an important task, if my phone beeps, I am tempted to look at my phone or check my e-mail or social media, which is obviously a distraction”. -DNF8

Moreover, DIs also tended to adjust their coordination strategies by addressing the antecedents to interruptions and instituting “pre-coordination” mechanisms (e.g., meetings with known interrupters) to reduce real-time interruption coordination and their overall ability to be interrupted (i.e., cognitive mediator – -> pre-coordination mechanism). This diference between DNs and DIs is striking because it links coordination methods, which are part of postefect interruptions models, to pre-efects (i.e., interruption context (DN/DI) – -> cognitive mediators – ->impacts), which helps explain how and why diferences in CMC interruption handling exist.

## 4.3. Impact of CMC interruptions

Consistent with prior research, we found unanimously that CMC interruptions introduce interruption and resumption lag, decreasing task performance (Gupta & Sharda, 2008; Trafton et al., 2003). Furthermore, these interruptions increase task completion times because it takes additional time to refocus on the task paused by the interruption (Kalgotra et al., 2019). In other words, using CMCs can be perceived as techno-overload. This is because it requires performing additional tasks (e.g., responding to emails, text messages) and handling or processing more information simultaneously (Tarafdar et al., 2007). For instance, DIF8 stated how she perceived technooverload due to the diferent types of technologies, “The constant bombardment all the time (from) the computer, TV, and the phone (and its notifications) . . . becomes an overload. You get too much information coming in at the same time, and it’s overwhelming”.

Several comments reflected DNM3ʹs experience with interruptions during his regular reading tasks when he said, “It takes more time to complete a reading because I do not start where I stop [due to the interruption]. I go back to a couple of sentences prior. No matter what, it is going to take longer”. Still, results were mixed concerning the efect of technostress perceptions on CMC interruption outcomes. While some found CMC interruptions to be stressors during critical tasks, when feeling overloaded (DIF8), or while already stressed (DNF6), others found that CMC interruptions provided relief from the primary task (DIF2), which was a welcomed change of pace (DIF9). We noted the impact on interruptions of both cognitive mediators and technostressors to be both positive and negative.

Both DNs and DIs felt stress when CMC interruptions were persistent and highly important relative to the primary task.

## 4.4. Summary of the findings

Overall, the findings from our qualitative study suggest that digital nativity and the practice of simultaneously doing multiple things can increase one’s comfort level and perceived ability to process interruptions with a lower adverse efect on the primary task performance. DIs show more distinction between males and females in perceived multi-tasking ability due to specific instances of practice and experience cited by DIFs (DIF5, DIF7, DIF9). Our findings suggest that the diferences in perceived multi-tasking ability between DNMs and DNFs are less pronounced than between DIMs and DIFs. For DNs, one impact of growing up in a world of ubiquitous information systems is increased practice and experience in dealing with CMC interruptions.

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

We conducted a qualitative study to answer our research question, “To what extent do generational diferences impact how individuals handle CMC interruptions?”. Through interviews, we were able to gain a deep understanding of the diferences between DNs and DIs in how they are impacted by and manage CMC interruptions. The results of this study provide several contributions to knowledge. First, we justify the application of the DN/DI user types as a theoretical lens by which we can study diferences in CMC interruption handling. Despite mixed results about diferences between generations in IT usage or competency, our findings suggest diferences in CMC interruption handling based on an individual’s socialisation as a DN or DI, particularly based on the proximity of their birthdate to 1980. Recent literature has shown age as an influential variable impacting one’s physiological response to CMC interruptions (Çoklar & Sahin, 2011; Czaja et al., 2006; Darowski et al., 2008; Tams et al., 2018). However, the sociological diferences in CMC interruption handling due to a person’s level of submergence in a technologically ubiquitous world (i.e., DN/DI) have been understudied. Our results support using the DN/DI user types as a theoretical lens to identify diferences in CMC interruption handling in future research. The DN and DI lens explain why sociological diferences in interruption handling exist across generations. Being raised in a digital environment shapes the way people communicate, socialise, create, and learn to interact with CMCs. As a result, DNs show higher comfort levels with CMCs and more resilience against CMC interruptions due to increased multitasking experience, better real-time task coordination, and reduced technostress during CMC interruptions.

Second, several studies have investigated the relationship between age and interruptions. Our study ofers several realism benefits over previous studies, which mainly simulated interruptions using experiments (Çoklar & Sahin, 2011; Czaja et al., 2006; Darowski et al., 2008). Our study findings rely on individuals’ perception of actual rather than simulated CMC interruptions (e.g., email, text). This qualitative approach enabled us to increase the number of mechanisms that could be explored and develop a richer model. A specific benefit of this study approach is our contribution concerning the interactions between age and CMC interruption characteristics. While Tams et al. (2018) provide meaningful insights into the positive relationship of age and CMC-enabled technostress, they could not statistically demonstrate that age can directly interact with CMC interruptions significantly to increase mental workload resulting in increased stress. Our study extends such work by demonstrating how and why age, being understood through DN/DI user types, can directly interact with CMC interruptions (i.e., frequency, importance, and medium) to increase cognitive workload resulting in increased stress. By establishing this relationship, other factors can describe how and why they can influence this interaction. We found the salience of interruptions to be significant but challenging to capture through the simulated interruptions found in previous studies. We were able to gain a deeper understanding of how age directly interacts with interruption characteristics by understanding how DNs and DIs difer across varying levels of interruption importance and interruption mediums as the participants described their experiences with real-life interruptions.

Similarly, we were able to gain a deeper understanding of the cognitive factors used to process CMC interruptions. Although several studies correlate ageing with higher levels of distraction and reduced cognition (Darowski et al., 2008), how diferent groups cognitively regulate CMC interruptions was less understood. Our findings revealed that DNs and DIs employ diferent cognitive factors to respond to CMC interruptions, such as diferent methods of coordinating tasks, immediate or negotiated, and processing mechanisms (i.e., multi-tasking ability).

Third, drawing on routine activity theory and the DN/DI theoretical lens, we were able to combine preand post-efect models of CMC interruption frequency into a more comprehensive process model. We established that the number of sources, level of targetness, and absence of guardians are the antecedents responsible for interruption frequency. We then added post-efect models that examine the efects of interruption characteristics on task performance. By viewing the pre- and post-efects through the DN/DI lens, we explained how and why the number of sources, level of targetness, and absence of guardians impact interruption characteristics. For instance, we observed diferences in the degree of guardianship and targetness across the two user types. Additionally, we explained the cognitive processing and technostress that user types experience once interrupted, the impact of these interruptions on task performance, and the adjustments some users make to the antecedents to regulate the efects of future interruptions. The novel model presented in this study contributes to research by providing insights on the diferent paths, both linear and recursive, that link antecedents and consequences of CMC interruptions. Overall, our model demonstrates that through DN/DI user types, age can help explain how diferent generations view CMC interruptions, the cognitive mediators they employ to deal with stress due to these interruptions, and the subsequent impact of the interruptions.

Fourth, prior studies note that CMC interruptions may lead to positive and negative impacts, with the latter mainly being associated with reduced task performance. In addition to explaining how diferent groups deal with CMC interruptions, we also identified a unique efect of consistent interruptions, which we refer to as habitual phone checking. This behaviour has become prominent due to the always-on CMC use and interruptions. Although this phenomenon has been noted in recent literature (Gerlach & Cenfetelli, 2020) and was observed consistently in our study, to our knowledge, the relationship between CMC interruptions and habitual phone checking has not been proposed in the literature. This finding is novel and significant because while most prior studies focus on the impact of interruptions on task performance, habitual phone checking, in this context, is indicative of future interruption handling. Furthermore, this phenomenon was observed more with DNs, who were more susceptible due to real-time coordination methods, fewer guardians, and closer proximity to CMC interruption sources.

Fifth, our study contributes to the technostress literature, which contains contrary findings concerning whether perceptions of technostress due to CMC use difer between the older and younger generations. We extend the technostress literature to the context of CMC interruptions by explaining how and why generations difer in the types of technostressors induced by CMC interruptions. Our findings suggest that both DNs and DIs perceive CMCs as a source of stress since CMCs feature overload, complexity, uncertainty, and invasion. Notably, during CMC interruptions, DIs perceive high techno-overload, techno-complexity, and techno-uncertainty. Our findings revealed that DIs perceive CMC interruptions as work-overload and contradictory with their primary tasks, creating complexity. Also, because of the uncertainty and unfamiliarity with CMC, it takes longer for DIs to learn and adapt. As such, they experience low work eficiency and reduced work performance. In contrast, DNs perceive lower techno-invasion. Our findings revealed that DNs do not consider interruptions as barriers to task productivity but as blurred with their life. Overall, the results provide insights into why diferent generations of users perceive CMC interruptions related to technostress and how interruptions could lead to diferent perceptions of stressors (e.g., complexity, overload, invasion). The findings highlight the diference between technostress perceptions based on generational diferences and provide a promising research avenue for further studies investigating the relationship between CMC interruptions and technostress among diferent types of users.

## 5.2. Practical implications

The results of this study have interesting implications for practitioners. It suggests the necessity to discover diferent solutions that accommodate the limitations of diferent genders and user types in managing interruptions to avoid adverse efects. Research on IS leadership and related streams can develop strategies to deliver on the promise of this research in several ways.

Drawing upon our findings, we recommend the following management strategies. First, the diferences in the degree of targetness and guardians between DNs and DIs suggest that diferent interventions are required to control the frequency of interruptions. Applications that manage the time spent on mobile devices, such as “Screen Time” on iOS devices, can assist users in mitigating the interruption frequency through awareness. However, such applications are only efective if implemented by the user. To be efective in the workplace, enterprise-level versions of these applications need to be instituted by management. Such applications could be especially helpful to DNs who preferred immediate, real-time coordination of CMC interruptions. Setting temporal barriers such as blocking notifications outside the business network for short durations could help minimise distractions during critical events. Finally, novel machine learning and artificial intelligence approaches may be required to control interruptions while working on a primary task. Machine learning models to automatically detect and control work interruptions can be built using the actual activity data of users from computers or smartphones (Stachl et al., 2020). The actual activity data may reveal additional diferences across the generations concerning the type of interruptions, the frequency of interruptions, the time of interruptions, and other unknown patterns.

Second, our findings indicated that CMC interruptions are a source of stress due to high overload, uncertainty, complexity, and invasion. Since these technostressors might reduce work performance, organisations should consider strategies to reduce the level of perceived stress due to CMCs. For example, mobile devices come equipped with health monitoring features. Thus, using CMCs to collect health-related data can help monitor employee stress levels. In addition, stress levels could be compared to message loads from CMCs to understand their potential impact on stress. Other strategies for reducing technostress could include using a filtering mechanism to reduce technooverload resulting from high email loads, improving employee technical skills, and providing additional training on CMCs. Moreover, they can limit the use of CMCs and ofer options rather than obligations to control techno-invasion.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research directions

Like all studies, our study contains limitations that pave the way for future research. We created four groups in our sample to analyse diferent group perceptions. Future research could employ a larger sample to test the generalisability of the findings. This sample could include individuals from various geographic locations, cultural backgrounds, and occupations. A larger sample also allows for more age groups to be created. Participant observation or a diary study could help expose possible diferences in perceptions across the same interruptions in the workplace. Additionally, while we found the sampling of students and faculty helpful for understanding the impact of CMC interruptions across generations, future studies could focus on working professionals. In doing so, such studies can overcome the limitations of using student/faculty sampling (Peterson & Merunka, 2014). Of course, we should note that college students form one important group of digital natives.

Due to the ubiquitous nature, continuous innovation, and pervasiveness of IT in both work and social life, many more opportunities exist for future research on CMC interruptions. Several codes in our analysis, such as the impact of interruptions on after-work life and the phenomenon of continuous phone checking, have not been heavily explored. Such findings are particularly interesting as conditions such as the COVID-19 pandemic have increased remote work, and subsequently, our reliance on IT and susceptibility to CMC interruptions. Although our data were collected prior to the COVID-19, we suspect that the core findings would hold in most cases. This would mean that an increase in CMC interruption frequency would be particularly challenging for DIs. However, the intensity of the disruptions to workflows could challenge both DN and DI’s ability to handle interruptions synchronously. Future work should investigate the changes in interruption handling under such conditions. Regarding our emergent themes, we suspect that societal pressures on some groups, such as DI women, have increased their ability to process interruptions without afecting primary task performance. However, linkages between this abil ity and specific social fabrics have not been deeply explored.

It is important to note that the DI and DN user types examined in this study will only be available for a limited time. Soon all adults will have been exposed to CMCs from their earliest days. There will no longer be a generation of people not exposed to the technologies highlighted in this study. As the world undergoes technology-induced changes, new diferences between groups will likely emerge and impact daily life. The study provides insight into how the impact of such changes can be understood. However, future generations will continue to age and experience cognitive changes. At the same time, technology will continue to evolve and grow in complexity. As a result, older users’ specific challenges show no sign of abating, even if the categorisation as “DI” based on the birth year of 1980 no longer applies.

## 6. Conclusion

In summary, we have investigated diferences between DIs and DNs in CMC interruption handling and proposed practical and future research implications. We believe that our findings will aid future research eforts in providing deeper insights into managing the evergrowing interruptions due to technologies. This is becoming an important issue as we employ voice and video chatbots to monitor our surroundings and alert us, either at our direction or when a specific action/ event occurs.

## Appendix A. Within-group and cross-group analysis

We accounted for occupational diferences using within and cross-group analysis (see Figure A1). Rather than only comparing older professionals to younger students (cross-group: A vs B), we accounted for diferences in occupation by comparing older and younger professionals (e.g., comparing opposite poles of DIs such as those around 60 years of age to those around 35 years of age) (within-group: C to D). Adding students (ages 19–25) provided additional points of comparison. Therefore, we compared younger professionals to both students and older professionals to examine their similarities and diferences in handling interruptions.
