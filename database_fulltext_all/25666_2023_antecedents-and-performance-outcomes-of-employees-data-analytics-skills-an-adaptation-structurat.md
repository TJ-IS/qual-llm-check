# Antecedents and performance outcomes of employees’ data analytics skills: an adaptation structuration theory-based empirical investigation

Zhen Shao<sup>a</sup>, Jose Benitez<sup>b</sup>, Jing Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Hanqing Zheng<sup>c</sup> and Aseel Ajamieh<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Economics and Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, Heilongjiang, China; <sup>b</sup> EDHEC Business School, France; <sup>c</sup>Shanghai Aerospace Control Technology Research Institute, Shanghai, Hubei, China; <sup>d</sup>King Talal School of Business Technology, Princess Sumaya University for Technology, Amman, Jordan

## ABSTRACT

How do organizations develop and manage employees’ data analytics skills to create business value and enhance organizational competitive advantage? In order to address this prominent and critical research question for IS research, we conceptualize and operationalize data analytics skills at the individual level and develop a nomological network model to examine its critical antecedents and outcomes from the lens of adaptation structuration theory. We test our core proposition and research model using survey data collected from 258 frontline employees of three data-intensive research institutes in China. We discover that data-driven culture, data analytics afordance, and individual absorptive capacity are positively associated with employees’ data analytics skills, which in turn, have positive influences on their task and innovative performance. We classify the employees into digital immigrants and digital natives based on age and examine the diferent influences of three salient antecedents on data analytics skills between the two groups. The research findings suggest that data-driven culture plays a more significant role in driving data analytics skills for digital immigrants, while data analytics afordance exhibits a stronger influence on data analytics skills for digital natives.

## KEYWORDS

Data analytics skills; task performance; innovative performance; adaptation structuration theory; digita natives; digital immigrants

## 1. Introduction

With the proliferation of mobile devices, sensors, and the Internet of Things (IoT), big data has emerged as a vital resource for organizations to achieve a competitive advantage in the dynamic environment (Demirkan & Delen, 2013; Dong & Yang, 2020; G. Gupta & Bose, 2022; S. Zhou et al., 2018). Despite organizations devoting a large amount of investment to big data infrastructures and applications, only a few have succeeded in achieving business success from the massive data (Marchand & Peppard, 2013). According to the prediction of Gartner, a large number of firms failed to obtain insights from big data, and approximately 60% of big data projects went into trouble before achieving tangible benefits (Grover et al., 2018; Heudecker & Hare, 2016). Compared with deploying technical infrastructures and applications, understanding how big data is used to achieve business value is more important (Dong & Yang, 2020; Marchand & Peppard, 2013). Thus, there is a call for a shift from the technology orientation to the managerial and personnel focus of big data analytics to achieve business value and benefits for organizations across various industries (M. Gupta & George, 2016; Marchand & Peppard, 2013; D. Q. Chen et al., 2015; Sharma et al., 2014; Y. Wang et al., 2018).

The term big data is first proposed in McKinsey’s report, and it is added in Gartner Hyper Cycle for an emerging new technology in 2012 (Stephenson, 2018). Characterized by volume, variety, velocity, and veracity, the value of big data largely depends on its capability of applying advanced techniques to analyze real-time data generated from multiple sources and in various formats, which gives rise to the concept of big data analytics capability (H. Chen et al., 2012). Big data analytics capability is conceptualized as organizational ability to capture and analyze data to generate business insights by efectively deploying its data, technological infrastructure, and talent (Akter et al., 2016; Castillo et al., 2021; Mikalef et al., 2020). Particularly, organizational-level data analytics capability comprises of three core dimensions, namely tangible resources (e.g., physical and financial), intangible resources (e.g., organizational culture and learning), and human skills (e.g., employee’s skills and knowledge) (Akter et al., 2016; M. Gupta & George, 2016). Prior studies argue that one of the biggest barriers for organizations to deploy data analytics is a lack of skills (Giermindl et al., forthcoming). Given the emergence of the decentralized organization structure, frontline employees are provided with more autonomous rights to extract data insights for task implementation and innovation (Ahuja et al., 2003). As such, firms would have greater demands for employees’ data analytics skills to deploy big data-enabled practices and obtain greater productivity benefits (Wu et al., 2019). Improving employees’ data analytics skills may serve as a solution to obtain an efective data analytics implementation and persistently achieve business values from big data. There is a call for investigating employees’ analytic skills at the individual level in academics (Phillips et al., 2021).

Despite the significance of individual-level data analytics skills, to our knowledge, a majority of extan literature focuses on organizational-level data analytics capability (Akter et al., 2016; M. Gupta & George, 2016; Mikalef et al., 2020; Upadhyay & Kumar, 2020). Only a handful of studies pay attention to talent skills in the age of big data (Di Gangi et al., 2022; Ke et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2020). Specifically, Barton and Cour (2012) report that frontline employees’ expertise and skills in using data analytics tools play a significan role in predicting and optimizing business outcomes. Phillips-Wren et al. (2015) argue that big data analytics ofers opportunities for business insights and requires new human skills. Castillo et al. (2021) explore the moderating role of business analytics talent in the relationship between social media cap ability and a firm’s knowledge exploration. However, most of the extant literature considers talent skills as a core dimension of organizational-level data analytics capability, while ignoring its significance in improving employees’ individual performance in the context of big data. In order to fill in the research gap, this study draws upon the adaptation structuration theory for individuals (ASTI) proposed by Schmitz et al. (2016) as an overarching framework to investigate the ante cedents and outcomes of individual-level data analytics skills. We conceptualize big data analytics capability at the individual level by mapping them to the classical ASTI framework (Schmitz et al., 2016), and focus on three salient antecedents. Specifically, we select data-driven culture as a salient organizationa environmental factor, data analytics afordance as a salient technological factor, and absorptive capacity as a salient individual characteristics, to examine their joint influences on employees’ data analytics skills. We select the three antecedents since they are recognized as significant factors driving data analytics capability and performance in the context of big data (Cui et al., 2020; Dremel et al., 2020; Z. Wang et al., 2020). Additionally, we incorporate individual performance as the outcome variable to represent the eficacy of employees’ analytical skills improvement (Ahuja et al., 2003), and divide it into two dimensions, namely task performance and innovative performance based on the previous literature (Goetz & Wald, 2021; Hirs et al., 2018; Janssen & Van Yperen, 2004; Jiang et al., 2019; Kuegler et al., 2015; Ogbanufe & Gerhart, 2020; Tams, 2022). Specifically, task performance is defined as the improvement of employees’ eficiency of task fulfillment, while innovative performance is conceptualized as employees’ generation, promotion, and realization of new ideas in the daily work. In the context of big data, on the one hand, employees need to make efective use of data analytics technologies to accomplish daily tasks and improve work eficacy. On the other hand, employees also need to utilize data analytics technologies to produce new knowledge and ideas for achieving innovative performance (Wu et al., 2019). Thus, we choose task performance and innovative performance as two dimensions of individual performance. The following research question is proposed accordingly:

(1) How do organizational environmental factors, technical factors, and individual factors jointly influence the development of employees’ data analytics skills and employee performance?

Furthermore, the ASTI framework indicates that individual knowledge and experience are important contingency factors in the interaction process of technology and environment (Schmitz et al., 2016). The alignment of technology, environment and individual characteristics is a requisite for efectively improving employees’ data analytics skills. Considering that technology and organizational environment are relatively stable over a short time, individuals’ experience is a salient contingency factor of their data analytics skills formation process. The notion of digital natives/immigrants well explains the distinction between individuals experience of digital technology usage (Hofmann et al., 2014). Prior studies argue that individuals’ perceptions, recognitions, and learning processes of technology usage are contingent upon the generation gap labeled by the “digital divide” (Hofmann et al., 2014; Palfrey & Gasser, 2008; Vodanovich et al., 2010). Particularly, age is considered as a salient criterion to distinguish between Digital Natives (DNs) and Digital Immigrants (DIs) (Prensky, 2001). Individuals born before the 1980s time point are classified as digital immigrants, while individuals born after the 1980s are identified as digital natives (Guo et al., 2008; Palfrey & Gasser, 2008; L. Zhang et al., 2021). Previous literature suggests that there exist significant age-related generational diferences between digital natives and digital immigrants in the context of digital technology usage. For instance, Tams et al. (2018) find that age-related differences in computer experience significantly moderate the interruption-stress relationship in the context of digit workforce. Ghobadi and Mathiassen (2020) identify the digital technology-driven generational diferences in the context of digital workforce in software develop ment. In a recent study, Tams (2022) indicates that age is negatively associated with individuals’ informationprocessing speed and IT-enabled task performance, and

IT experience reduces the negative impact of the decline. Despite the significant role of age being identified within the extant literature, most of the studies focus on technostress and information overload issues, and there is scant literature that examines its moderating efect on the formation mechanism of employees data analytics skills. As such, this study further incorporates age as a salient contingency factor in the theoretical model to explore the specific diferences between DNs and DIs in terms of their data analytics skills. The following research question is proposed accordingly:

## (2) How does age moderate the influences of organizational environmental factors, technical factors, and individual factors on data analytics skills?

To address these questions, we develop a theorydriven research model based on the ASTI framework to investigate the influences of organizational environmental factors, technical factors, individual factors on employees’ data analytics skills and employee performance, and further, to uncover the moderating efects of age on the path relationships. We conduct an empirical field study and collect data from three dataintensive research institutes. Our quantitative data analysis results indicate that environmental, technical, and individual factors are positively associated with employees’ data analytics skills and their task and innovative performance. A multi-group analysis suggests that the influences of three aspects of factors on data analytics skills exhibit significant diferences between digital immigrants and digital natives.

Overall, our study contributes to the conceptualization of data analytic skills by extending organizationallevel data analytics capability to the individual level and investigating the antecedents, outcomes, and boundary conditions of employees’ data analytics skills from the lens of ASTI. The empirical research findings could help organizations identify, deploy, and manage data analytics talents efectively in the digital transformation process, thus achieving the benefits of data analytics practices.

The remainder of our study is outlined as follows: we illustrate the theoretical foundation in Section 2 and develop the theoretical model and corresponding hypotheses in Section 3. Section 4 describes the research methodology, including data collection, instrument design, and statistical analysis. We conclude with discussions, future research directions, and theoretical implications for IS research in the final section.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Data analytics skills

Big data analytics refers to the application of advanced analytical techniques to analyze large amounts and multi-sources of data sets (Wu et al., 2020), which has received tremendous attention in both academics and business in recent years (Akter et al., 2016), and is considered as a critical source for achieving operational benefits and innovation (Lehrer et al., 2018). Nowadays, managers are relying more on data analytics to make strategic decisions (Constantiou & Kallinikos, 2015), catch business opportunities (Liu, 2014), and improve firm performance (Mikalef et al., 2020; Wamba et al., 2017). The performance of data analytics does not rely upon the technology itself, but also depends on the organizational capability to efectively utilize technologies to extract business insights from data (Müller et al., 2018; Schneckenberg et al., 2021). This brings forth the concept of data analytics capability. Drawing upon the resource-based view (Grant, 1991), data analytics capability is described as organizational competence to provide business insights using data management, technology infrastructure, and talent, and transform business into a competitive force (Akter et al., 2016), which comprises of three salient dimensions, namely tangible resources (e.g., technology infrastructure), intangible resources (e.g., data-driven culture), and talent (e.g., data analytics skills). This study focuses on the talent dimension of data analytics capability and defines it as employees’ data analytics skills of applying statistical, processing, and analytical techniques to attain business insights from the captured data.

In the past decade, how to leverage data analytics to enable data-driven decision-making and realize business innovation has aroused the attention of both IS scholars and practitioners (Jha et al., 2020; Mikalef & Krogstie, 2020). One stream of studies examines the relationship between data analytics capability and firm performance from the resource-based view and dynamic capability theory (Côrte-Real et al., 2020; M. Gupta & George, 2016; Müller et al., 2018; D. Q. Chen et al., 2015). For instance, M. Gupta and George (2016) employ a survey of 340 data analytics managers and chief data oficers, and report that data analytics capability is positively associated with organizational market performance and operational performance. In a recent study, Côrte-Real et al. (2020) find that data analytics capability can help achieve competitive advantage in terms of strategic performance and financial performance. While another category of research investigates the actualization mechanism and value creation of data analytics capability from the practice-based view and afordance perspective (Grover et al., 2018; Lehrer et al., 2018; Z. Wang et al., 2020). Data analytics capability depends on the interplay of organizational context, technical infrastructures, and the skills of actors, which are considered as the constituents of a socialtechnical system (Dremel et al., 2020). This classification has been widely adopted and used in IS literature to examine the relationship between big data analytics capability and firm performance (Akter et al., 2016; M. Gupta & George, 2016; Mikalef et al., 2020; Upadhyay & Kumar, 2020).

As the critical users of data analytics, frontline employees’ skills in utilizing the data analytics tools play a significant role in achieving performance (Barton & Court, 2012; Kiron et al., 2014; Phillips et al., 2021). As such, this study focuses on data analytics skills at the individual level and examines its salient antecedents and outcomes from the lens of ASTI (Schmitz et al., 2016).

## 2.2. Adaptation structuration theory

We refer to adaptive structuration theory to depict the process regarding how big data analytics implemented in organizations enhance employees’ skills and performance. AST describes a complete adoption progress of advanced technology by “input-process-output” scheme at the organization level (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). DeSanctis and Poole (1994) suggest that “the major sources of structure for groups as they interact with an advanced information technology are: the technology itself, the tasks, and the organizational environment (pp.128)”. Technology can be defined in two ways: one is the structural features of the advanced technology itself, and the other is the spirit of technology. Task means task knowledge or rules, including facts and figures, opinions, or practice related to the task at hand (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Shao & Li, 2022). While environment represents social knowledge or rules of actions drawn from the organization or society at large. The core idea of AST is structuration and adaptation (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Schmitz et al., 2016), describing a process that brings the structure sources into social interactions where they are applied and reproduced (Schmitz et al., 2016; Shao & Li, 2022).

The AST framework has been widely applied in various research contexts to study the evolutionary usage of advanced information technologies, such as enterprise information systems (DeSanctis et al., 2008; Liang et al., 2015; Peng & Guo, 2019), information and communications technology (Thomas & Bostrom, 2010), and electronic medical record (EMR) system (Ilie & Turel, 2020). Most of the empirical studies focus on organizational or group level usage of advanced technologies (e.g., Liang et al., 2015; Thomas & Bostrom, 2010), and only a handful of studies are conducted at the individual level. Notably, Schmitz et al. (2016) extend the AST framework at the individual level to explicate the adaptive usage of malleable information technologies by ordinary frontline employees. The framework of AST for individuals (ASTI) identifies three categories of factors that potentially afect employees’ technology adaptation behaviors and subsequent performance, including technology structure, task structure, and individual characteristics (Schmitz et al., 2016). Specifically, technology structure manifests in individuals’ interpretations of the technology artifacts when using the advanced technology, including the technical objects, functional afordances, and symbolic expressions (Peng & Guo, 2019; Schmitz et al., 2016). Task structure includes the work process and environment that interacts with technologies (Schmitz et al., 2016). While individual characteristics represent individuals’ personality, knowledge, and experience (Schmitz et al., 2016).

## 3.1. Data analytics skills and employee performance

With enterprises stepping into the digital age, frontline employees are confronted with the challenge of coping with diverse and massive data in daily work, which puts forward an urgent requirement for data analytics skills. Originated from data analytics capability, data analytics skills are conceptualized as individuals’ skills of applying statistical, processing, and analytics techniques to attain business insights from the captured data (Akter et al., 2016; Albergaria & Jabbour, 2020), which enable frontline employees to utilize data resources eficiently to achieve a particular goal, e.g., improvement of performance. Data analytics skills involve a complex skill set of data analytics technology, business process experience, and cooperation capability across the department (Akter et al., 2016; Shamim et al., 2019). Employees who are skilled at data analytics can extract data value by implementing new information management systems and utilizing data analytics infrastructures to create a competitive advantage for organizations (Rialti et al., 2019).

The relationship between data analytics skills and performance has aroused the attention of IS scholars. Data analytics skills are important predictors of employees’ performance by supporting the efectiveness of task fulfillment (Ahuja et al., 2003). Frontline employees who are skilled at data analytics can make better decisions and achieve valuable business outcomes (Gopal, 2021). For example, the largest financial institution in Indonesia, Bank Mandiri, has devoted resources to improve employees’ data analytics skills and help them make better and faster decisions when providing services. Previous studies indicate that employees’ IT skills would advance performance (J. A. Zhang et al., 2022; Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Kane, 2015; Pitafi et al., 2018), and employees should enrich their skills concerning the cutting edge of technologies to improve their performance. For instance, Akter et al. (2016) argue that employees with analytics skills or knowledge can attain databased insights and perform the assigned tasks better in the big data environment. Ghasemaghaei et al. (2017) find that employees’ analytics skills can improve their task achievement and job performance. In the context of big data, employees with higher levels of data analytics skills can make sense of magnanimous data, integrate the data across organizational boundaries, and efectively apply them in their daily work to support the concrete task process (Mikalef et al., 2020). Additionally, employees who are skilled at big data analytics tend to reconfigure their problemsolving strategies based on the identified trends observed in the data, complete the assigned tasks more eficiently and support the decision-making more efectively (Ghasemaghaei, 2019; Mikalef et al., 2020). Thus, we propose that:

## H1: Data analytics skills are positively associated with employees’ task performance.

Data analytics skills could also inspire flexible and agile innovative working ideas through precisely interpreting and suficiently comprehending the findings in the vast amount of data circumstance (Ghasemaghaei, 2019). With the advance of digital technologies, employees who are skilled at data analytics can make efective usage of multi-sources of data, e.g., historical user records and feedback data (Ghasemaghaei, 2019). Given that creative ideas are often sparked by the collision of ideas from diverse thoughts (Kuegler et al., 2015; Moore & Benbasat, 1991), the ability to analyze multi-sources of data is vital for the generation of innovative ideas (Benitez et al., 2018). Particularly, as the frontline service providers, employees who are skilled at data analytics can anticipate users’ needs and even discover unforeseen innovative service patterns to provide better user experiences. Thus, data analytics skills are also considered as critical factors in promoting employee’s innovation performance. The above arguments lead to the following hypothesis:

H2: Data analytics skills are positively associated with employees’ innovative performance.

## 3.2. Antecedents of the development of data analytics skills

Data-driven culture is described as “the extent to which organizational members make decisions based on the insights extracted from data” (M. Gupta & George, 2016, P.1053). Data-driven culture considers data analytics as the premise of strategic operation and decision-making (Z. Wang et al., 2020). Instead of following the subjective decisions made by the top executives and higher-ups, data-driven culture emphasizes the importance of consensus among employees complying with the objective insights observed from the data (M. Gupta & George, 2016; Shamim et al., 2020).

In the context of big data, data-driven culture is identified as a salient factor in facilitating big data analytics capability (Dubey et al., 2019; M. Gupta & George, 2016; Upadhyay & Kumar, 2020; Z. Wang et al., 2020). Organizations with strong data-driven culture are investing more in data analytics skills training for frontline employees (Gopal, 2021). Datadriven culture can afect employees’ expectations, motivations, and positive responses of achieving benefits from data analytics practices (Dubey et al., 2019). Specifically, data-driven culture can encourage employees to make better utilization of data and empower them with decision rights in daily work (Ross et al., 2013). Data-driven culture can promote employees to shift from an instinct-based decisionmaking to a data-driven paradigm, enable employees to be more confident in getting help from data analytics infrastructures to fulfill task activities, and provide employees more autonomy and opportunities to com bine data across multiple departments (Gopal, 2021). This is beneficial to enhance employees’ ability to apply data analytics techniques to obtain business insights and generate innovative ideas from data to support business operations (Ross et al., 2013). For example, Nissan succeeds in the digital world by reconstructing the data support model and emphasizing the importance of data management to employees, in order to stimulate them to have a faster awareness and a deeper understanding of data analytics. Given the theoretical analysis and practical examples, we propose that:

H3: Data-driven culture is positively associated with the development of employees’ data analytics skills.

Afordance stems from the intertwined outcome between the specific utilization environment and actual users and emphasizes the potential outcomes determined by the goals of users (Dremel et al., 2020; Z. Wang et al., 2020). Based on it, technology afordance is relevant with the interaction between users and advanced technologies, indicating users’ possible utilization of the technology (Madsen, 2015). In the context of big data, data analytics afordance refers to employees’ perceptions of the features and functionalities of data analytics technologies that can help them apply and exploit data and information to achieve business goals (Dremel et al., 2020). The concept of data analytics afordance enables us to describe employees’ action potentials obtained from data analytics technologies at the individual level (Dremel et al., 2020).

In the era of digital technology, employees are provided with a variety of data analytics tools to complete tasks and make decisions (Lin et al., 2021). Based on ASTI, data analytics afordance acts as a critical input of skills development by providing employees the features and functions of data analytics technologies (Schmitz et al., 2016). Data analytics technology enables employees to collect multi-sources of data and adopt the analytics methods to find out innovative task solutions and obtain business insights from data (Dremel et al., 2020; M. Gupta & George, 2016; Z. Wang et al., 2020). For instance, in practice, more and more companies are establishing strategic alliances with professional big data analytics and business intelligence providers to discover business insights with big data (e.g., PowerBI and Tableau). This is beneficial to enhance employees’ skills to utilize technologies and tools in completing data acquisition, analysis, and application by themselves. The actualization of data analytics afordance results in the improvement of employees’ analytics skills (Dremel et al., 2020).

Previous studies have examined the relationship between data technology afordance and data analytics skills in empirical studies (Dremel et al., 2020; Lehrer et al., 2018; Z. Wang et al., 2020; Zeng et al., 2020). For instance, Dremel et al. (2020) find that employees’ data analytics skills can be developed by interacting with big data analytics techniques at the actor level. Zeng et al. (2020) indicate that data analytics afordance can activate employees’ usage of data analytics technologies and improve employees data analytics skills to create data-based solutions and innovations. Data analytics afordance enables employees to accumulate more experience in exploiting emerging data analytics techniques and tools. This is beneficial to improve employees’ technical proficiency and analytics skills of utilizing multisources of data to achieve task performance eficiently and efectively (Dremel et al., 2020). Hence, we propose that:

H4: Data analytics afordance is positively associated with the development of employees’ data analytics skills.

Absorptive capacity originates from organizational level literature, which is defined as a firm’s ability to identify, assimilate, transform, and apply external data and knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Liu et al., 2013). At the individual level, absorptive capacity is conceptualized as an individual’s ability to acknowledge and understand the value of new information and apply it for a business purpose (Lowik et al., 2016, 2017). In the context of big data, absorptive capacity enables individuals to assimilate the acquired external knowledge, integrate it with internal resources, and transform knowledge into data analytics skills to exploit new business insights and support task completion and innovation (Leal-Rodríguez et al., 2014).

The concept of absorptive capacity has been widely adopted in the IS literature to describe an individual’s ability to leverage new technology and achieve better performance (Deng et al., 2008; Duan et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2018; Leal-Rodríguez et al., 2014; Majhi et al., 2020). For instance, Majhi et al. (2020) find that individual absorptive capacity can improve their ability to extract optimal solutions by leveraging both internal and external knowledge. Huang et al. (2018) indicate that individuals’ absorptive capacity can significantly facilitate their performance of IT usage. In the context of big data, employees’ data analytics skills are largely dependent upon their absorptive capacity. Given that suficient absorptive capacity would increase employees’ knowledge foundation and further improve their data analytics expertise (Carlo et al., 2012), absorptive capacity is regarded as a salient facilitator of IT capability (Huang et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2013; Malhotra et al., 2005). Specifically, employees who are proficient in identifying and assimilating valuable knowledge are more likely to obtain new and valuable business insights from the combined data using the data analytics techniques (Deng et al., 2008). Hence, we propose that:

H5: Employees’ absorptive capacity is positively associated with the development of data analytics skills.

## 3.3. The moderating efect of age: digital immigrants versus digital natives

Previous literature argues that there exist significant diferences between DIs and DNs in terms of their thinking and learning patterns of digital technologies (Hofmann et al., 2014; Thompson, 2013). The digital natives/immigrants’ generational distinction stems from the notion of digital technology experience (Hofmann et al., 2014). Specifically, DIs are inexperienced and are not familiar with digital technologies. As such, DIs prefer to follow institutional policies and orders when using data analytics tools to perform tasks (Vodanovich et al., 2010). When top executives advocate a data-driven culture within the organization, DIs are more inclined to recognize the significance of data in decision-making and actively utilize data resources to complete the task assignment. On the contrary, DNs are born in the digital age and possess more experience of digital technologies. Therefore, DNs focus more on the usefulness of data analytics tools instead of institutional policies and orders (Castaneda et al., 2007) and prefer to obtain the utility of digital technologies when making decisions (Shao et al., 2020). As such, data-driven culture as an important situational support may exhibit a stronger influence on DIs’ data analytics skills by enhancing their self-eficacy of learning new techniques and tools (Collins-Camargo & Royse, 2010; Warkentin et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2010). The following hypothesis is proposed accordingly:

H6a: Data-driven culture has a stronger influence on data analytics skills for digital immigrants compared with digital natives.

The discrepancy between DIs and DNs would foster their distinct evaluated manners of data analytics tools (Castaneda et al., 2007). Specifically, DNs show positive attitudes towards digital technologies and proactively interact with the emerging technologies (e.g., machine learning and cloud computing) to complete the assigned tasks and business processes. With rich knowledge in digital technologies, DNs exhibit greater potential to learn and accept new technologies (Kesharwani, 2020) and are skilled at utilizing data analytics approaches to obtain business solutions (Vodanovich et al., 2010). For instance, Kesharwani (2020) finds that DNs tend to exhibit higher levels of appreciation and feedback towards the usefulness and ease of use of technology features. In contrast to DNs, DIs are less familiar with the emerging technologies and accumulate less knowledge in data analytics (Kesharwani, 2020; Kirk et al., 2015; Tilvawala et al., 2013; Vodanovich et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2018). As such, DIs may be less afected by the functionalities of data analytics tools when learning and adopting emerging technologies. Based on this logic, we argue that data analytics afordance plays a more significant role in enabling DNs to develop higher levels of data analytics skills by exploiting the emerging features and functionalities. The above analysis leads to the following hypothesis:

H6b: Data analytics afordance has a stronger influence on data analytics skills for digital natives compared with digital immigrants.

## 4. Research methodology and empirical analysis

## 4.1. Instrument design

We refer to the extant literature to design the instrument and use five-point likert scale to measure the items, ranging from 1(totally disagree) to 5(totally agree). Data-driven culture is measured at the individual level as perceived by employees, from the aspects of organizational rewards mechanisms, policies, and business processes (Cao & Duan, 2014; M. Gupta & George, 2016). Items for data analytics afordance are adapted from the measures of IT afordance according to the characteristics of the big data contex (Chatterjee et al., 2015; Dremel et al., 2020). We measure data analytics afordance at the individual level as perceived by employees. Specifically, we conduct a procedure of literature review, expert panel, and content validity test following Straubs (1989) suggestion, and develop three items for the construct to assess individuals’ appropriation of data analytica tools to archive, share, and exploit data and information for achieving business goals. Items for absorptive capacity are designed to capture an individual’s ability to acquire, assimilate, transform, and exploit new or valuable data based on Cohen and Levinthals (1990) and Seo et al.’s (2015) studies. Data analytics skills are operationalized as a second-order reflective construct adapted from Akter et al.’s (2016) study, which comprises of three interdependent and equally important dimensions: technical capability (i.e., employees’ skills to understand and apply the emerging data analytica tools), business capability (i.e., employees’ skills to understand business processes, policy and rules, and structures within the organization), and relationa capability (i.e., employees’ skills to communicate and cooperate with people across multiple business functions). Employee performance is divided into two dimensions: task performance vs. innovative performance. The first dimension focuses on task eficiency improvement, while the second dimension concentrates on innovative performance, such as seeking new ways of doing work (Janssen & Van Yperen, 2004; Kuegler et al., 2015). Table 2 describes the detailed items and references for each construct.

The questionnaire involves three sections. In the first section, we declare the research motivation of this study and provide key definitions of data analytics skills. Then we ask the respondents to truthfully report their evaluations of data-driven culture and data analytical afordance based on their perceptions and selfreport their absorptive capacity, data analytics skills, and individual performance (i.e., task performance and innovative performance). The third section includes the employees’ demographic information, such as gender, age, educational background, and work experience.

We conduct a pre-test and a following-up pilot study before the final data collection. We first invite two professors and four Ph.D. students from a wellknown university in China to check the semantic content of the questionnaire to avoid any ambiguous or unclear expressions. Then we invite 60 employees who work in a data analytics organization to participate in the pilot study. Statistical results suggest that the psychometric properties of all the constructs satisfy the recommended criterion. We elaborate and refine several words based on the feedback and suggestions from the academics and practitioners.

## 4.2. Data collection

We employ a field survey in China to examine the theoretical model. We collect data through collaboration with three data-intensive research institutes that focus on the design, analytics, and management of high-tech products and services during April and May of 2020. The three selected institutes have experienced digital transformation in the past decade and are highly dependent on data analytics in support of their business strategy and decision-making. As such, employees in the institutes are required to use data analytics tools to support business operations and work performance. We randomly select employees from the full employee list across multiple departments (e.g., strategic planning department, IT department, design department, and product development department) of the three organizations to guarantee the diversity of samples in terms of educational back ground, work experience, and work position (Liang et al., 2015; Peng et al., 2018). The research team then sends an email invitation with a questionnaire link to the oficial email address of each selected employee. Particularly, we inform the respondents that their participation in the survey is voluntary, and their personal information would be kept confidential to mitigate the potential social desirability bias. We totally administrator 350 copies of questionnaires to the employees across the three organizations and receive 280 responses one month later. We delete the questionnaires with incomplete or dirty data (e.g., all data is 1 or 7) and finally obtain 258 valid questionnaires. To assess the potential issue of non-response bias, we conduct a t-test between the responsive employees and non-responsive employees in terms of gender, age, education, and work experience and find no significant diferences (p > 0.1).

## 4.3.1. Measurement model analysis

We employ the structural equation modeling (SEM) technique to test the research model since it is capable of examining nomological network relationships among multiple independent and dependent variables simultaneously (Gefen et al., 2000). Specifically, we use the estimator consistent partial least squares (PLSc) in the path modeling, which is suitable for testing the reflective first-order and reflective second-order model (Dijkstra & Henseler, 2015) and is appropriate for confirmatory and explanatory IS research (Benitez et al., 2020a, 2020b; Dijkstra & Henseler, 2015).

We assess the construct reliability and validity of the measurement model by referring to the criterion of factor loadings, rho\_A, composite reliability, and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) of each construct. We first assess the second-order construct of data analytics skills and then assess the reliability, validity, and correlations of the first-order constructs. As shown in Table 4 and Table 5, the factor loadings, rho\_A, and composite reliability are high above the threshold value of 0.7, demonstrating good reliability of the measurement model (Chin et al., 2003; Dijkstra & Henseler, 2015). Additionally, the AVE value for all the constructs has surpassed 0.5, thus indicating an adequate convergent validity of the measurement model (Chin et al., 2003).

We perform the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT) approach to assess the discriminant validity of the measurement model by comparing the ratio of the between-trait correlations to the within-trait correlations. As indicated in Table 5, the HTMT value for every two constructs is less than the threshold of 0.85, indicating a good discriminant validity (Hair et al., 2016; Henseler et al., 2016).

## 4.3.2. Common method bias

Given that our collected data are all self-reported by the respondents in the survey, common method bias (CMB) may be a potential threat resulting from systematic measurement errors (Bagozzi & Yi, 1991; Podsakof et al., 2003). We adopt two commonly used statistical tests to rule out potential alternative explanations from common method bias. First, we perform the Harmon’s single-factor test by examining an unrotated factor solution for all items of major constructs (Podsakof et al., 2003). The analysis results indicate that the first factor explains 18.469% of the total variance. Second, we employ a common method factor and calculate the variance explained by the principal construct and the method construct following Liang et al.’s (2007) study. As shown in Table 7, the ratio of method variance to substantive variance is about 1: 202. To conclude, CMB is not a serious concern in our study.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

Based upon adaptation structuration theory, this study conducts a theory-driven empirical study to address the two proposed research questions. Our results show that three contextualized triggers (i.e., data-driven culture, data analytics afordance, and individual absorptive capacity) have significant efects on employees’ data analytics skills. Specifically, consistent with previous research findings on data analytics capability (M. Gupta & George, 2016) and other technology adaptation studies (Nguyen et al., 2021), we find that absorptive capacity is the most significant antecedent of employees’ data analytics skills, followed by data analytics afordance and data-driven culture. Additionally, data analytics skills exhibit strong influences on both task performance and innovative performance. The results not only confirm the previous findings that data analytics plays a salient role in enhancing individual task performance (Davison & Ou, 2017; Ghasemaghaei et al., 2017), but also extends the extant literature by identifying the role of data analytics skills in improving employees’ innovative performance in the context of big data. The results suggest that data analytics skills could enable employees to extract data insights and generate innovative ideas and solutions.

Furthermore, we introduce age as a salient digital divide factor in the research model and perform a multi-group analysis to explore the contingency influences of organizational environmental, technological, and individual factors on employees’ data analytics skills across digital immigrants and digital natives. We find that datadriven culture exhibits a stronger influence on data analytics skills for digital immigrants than digital natives, while data analytics afordance demonstrates a stronger influence on data analytics skills for digital natives compared with digital immigrants. The empirical research findings address our second research question and support our hypotheses, implying that data-driven culture as an important institutional support plays a more salient role in fostering $\mathrm { D I s } ^ { \prime }$ data analytics skills, while DNs’ data analytic skills are more likely to be improved when they are provided with higher levels support of data analytics infrastructures. We illustrate the theoretical and practical implications in the following sections.

## 5.1. Limitations and future avenues for IS research

Our study has several limitations that leave open to future IS research directions. First, we collect data from three research institutes in China that focus on high-tech products and service design. Although the sample organizations are all data-intensive and can satisfy the requirement of data analytics, our study may sufer from external validity because of industry limits. Future studies can collect data from organizations of other industries to generalize our research findings. Second, we target frontline employees and use survey methods to collect data. Future research can adopt second-hand data analysis (e.g., diary study) and make a 360 feedback evaluation of employees data analytics skills and performance. Third, we classify the overall sample into digital natives and digital immigrants based on their age. Future studies could consider other salient individual characteristics, such as education, digital self-eficacy, and experience, to explore their moderating efects on employees’ data analytics skills development. Fourth, we investigate the efects of data-driven culture, data analytics afordance, and absorptive capacity on employees’ data analytics skills based upon the ASTI framework. Future studies can conduct a longitudinal study and collect data at multiple time points to examine if there exists a reversed relationship between the three antecedents and employees’ data analytics skills, and also, to uncover the causal influence of data analytics skills on employees’ performance over time.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications for IS research

Our study contributes to the extant literature in three aspects. First, to our best knowledge, our study is one of the first that conceptualizes and operationalizes employees’ data analytics skills at the individual level. Despite the great demands of improving employees’ skills to achieve business benefits from big data in practice, previous academic literature mostly focuses on organizationallevel data analytics capability, while ignoring employees’ data analytics skills (Côrte-Real et al., 2020; M. Gupta & George, 2016; Müller et al., 2018; D. Q. Chen et al., 2015). Our study fills in the research gap by adapting the notion of data analytics capability from prior literature and measuring employees’ data analytics skills from three dimensions of technology skills, business skills, and relational skills (Akter et al., 2016). The research findings further broaden the data analytics capability theory from the organizational level to the individual level and provide an avenue for future studies to uncover the antecedents and outcomes of data analytics skills from diferent theoretical perspectives.

Second, we extend the AST framework to the context of big data analytics and develop a nomological network model that links organizational environmental factors (i.e., data-driven culture), technology infrastructure factors (i.e., data analytics afordance), and individual factors $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ absorptive capacity) to data analytics skills at the individual level. Despite the notion of talent skills being mentioned in prior IS literature, our understanding regarding the formation mechanism of individuals’ data analytics skills is still limited (Akter et al., 2016). The ASTI framework is a welldocumented theoretical perspective in understanding the mechanisms of how organizational environmental, technical, and individual factors jointly afect an individual’s adaptation behavior and performance (Schmitz et al., 2016). Particularly, we find that data analytics afordance, data-driven culture, and absorptive capacity exhibit significant influences on data analytics skills to diferent extent. We further uncover the mediation efect of data analytics skills between the three antecedents and two dimensions of performance (i.e., task performance and innovative performance). We find that data analytics skills fully mediate the influences of data-driven culture and data analytics afordance on task and innovative performance, while partially mediate the influence of absorptive capacity on the two performances. The research findings can provide novel insights regarding how frontline employees’ data analytics skills can be improved through the joint influences of both tangible and intangible resources and enrich the extant literature of digital performance in the context of big data.

Third, we explicate the boundary condition of the research model by incorporating the contingency factor of digital age. Specifically, we divide the overall sample into two groups (i.e., digital immigrants vs. digital natives) based on their age categorized by the digital divide and uncover the path relationship diferences between the three antecedents and data analytics skills. Although previous literature has examined the cognitive perceptions and behavioral diferences between digital immigrants and digital natives (e.g., Metallo & Agrifoglio, 2015), few studies pay attention to their data analytics skills discrepancies. Our study fills in the research gap by revealing the contingency influences of organizational environmental factors, technical factors, and individual factors on employees’ data analytics skills in the emerging context of digital technology usage. We find that there exhibit significant diferences between digital natives and digital immigrants regarding the formation mechanisms of their data analytics skills. Particularly, employees of digital natives depend more upon the functionalities and infrastructures of digital technologies, while employees of digital immigrants rely more on organizational policies, rewards mechanisms, and business processes in improving their data analytics skills and achieving business goals. Overall, the research findings can deepen our understanding regarding the digital divide diference issue in the workplace.

## 5.3. Lessons learned for IT and business executives

Our study can provide managerial guidelines and insights to practitioners in two aspects. First, the managers are advised to pay more attention to employees data analytics skills and enhance their skills by implementing data analytics technology infrastructures and facilitating data-driven culture. On the one hand, managers can adopt the emerging data analytics technologies to support organizational business processes and operations. Managers can also hold data analytics workshops and provide regular training sessions to frontline employees. This is beneficial to facilitate knowledge sharing and assimilation within the organization, and further, to help employees better understand the functionalities and features of the technologies. On the other hand, top executives can articulate the significance of data analytics for organizational strategic development, and establish policies and rewards mechanisms supporting data analytics within the organization. This can help facilitate a datadriven culture and encourage employees to utilize data analytics technologies in support of daily work. Additionally, managers should note the salient role of individuals’ absorptive capacity and select individuals who are skilled at learning new knowledge to take the position of data analytics.

Second, managers should also recognize the learning and cognitive diferences between digital immigrants and digital natives and adopt diferent strategies to improve their data analytics skills. Given that digital natives are more interested in the development and application of emerging technologies, managers can provide more learning and training opportunities to them when introducing and implementing data analytics tools and infrastructures within the organizations. As such, digital natives can take the position of internal consultants and provide more support for digital immigrants who are not familiar with the emerging technologies. On the contrary, digital immigrants show little interest in the new technologies and focus more on organizational institutional policies and culture. Therefore, providing technical support for this group of users is not enough. Instead, managers must attend to digital immigrants’ dificulties and obstacles when using data analytics technologies and establish appropriate policies and rules to encourage digital immigrants to actively adopt data analytics tools in support of the business processes. Overall, these tactics are beneficial to enhance employees’ data analytics skills and enable them to efectively utilize big data analytics to improve task eficiency and seek new ways of working.

## 5.4. Concluding remarks

We conceptualize data analytics skills at the individual level and develop a nomological network model to examine three salient antecedents and outcomes of employees’ data analytics skills based upon adaptive structuration theory. A field study is conducted, and a structural equation model technique is employed to test the path relationships among the constructs. Empirical analysis results suggest that data-driven culture, data analytics afordance, and individual absorptive capacity significantly afect data analytics skills to diferent extent, which further, facilitate employees task performance and innovative performance. We perform a multi-group analysis and find that data analytics afordance exhibits a stronger influence on data analytics skills for digital natives, while datadriven culture has a greater efect on data analytics skills for digital immigrants.
