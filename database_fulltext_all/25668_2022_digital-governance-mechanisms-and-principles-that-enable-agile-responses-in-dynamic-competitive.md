# Digital governance mechanisms and principles that enable agile responses in dynamic competitive environments

Giovanni Vaia<sup>a</sup>, Daria Arkhipova<sup>a</sup> and William DeLone<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management, Ca’ Foscari University of Venice, Venice, Italy; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Technology and Analytics, American University, Washington, DC, USA

## ABSTRACT

Being an agile company today is no longer just about timely recognition of early signs of change in order to defend an existing competitive position but rather being able to perpetually transition between a series of short-lived, temporary competitive advantages. While traditional IT governance approaches have lent themselves well to extracting value from a long-lasting competitive advantage, they can become a liability if agile companies need to continuously reinvent themselves in pursuit of a new, “transient” competitive advantage. To that end, a temporal dimension of IT governance (when and how fast to decide) becomes as important as its structural (who decides what) and procedural (how to decide) aspects. In this paper, we adopt a “ternary” view on governance and build on the extant literature in IS, strategy, and organisation design as well as exemplar case studies to explore how traditional approaches to IT governance structures, processes, and relational mechanisms are altered to improve sensing, deciding, and responding capabilities in turbulent business environments. These mechanisms are then summarised into four digital governance principles to guide future research and practice. Those principles are: 1) disciplined autonomy, 2) IS-Business convergence, 3) permeable boundaries, and 4) incremental financial commitment including fast experimentation.

KEYWORDS IT governance; digital transformation; strategic agility; digital governance

## 1. Introduction

Agility is considered an imperative for achieving and sustaining competitive advantage vis-à-vis competitors in fast-changing environments (Overby et al., 2006; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Teece et al., 2016; Van Oosterhout et al., 2006). The assumption is that once achieved, competitive advantage can be sustained over time, provided that a company tailors its resources and capabilities to its unique position within an industry and recognises early signs of change (Porter, 1985, 1991, 1996). Over the past decade, the rise of platforms such as Uber and Airbnb alongside a shift to cloud technologies has blurred industry boundaries, substantially accelerated the pace of change, and compelled companies to react faster to multiple unforeseen threats and opportunities based on limited information and under conditions of high uncertainty (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Day & Schoemaker, 2019; Nambisan et al., 2017).

While organisations were the driving force behind technology innovation in the past, currently organisations are lagging in adapting to rapidly changing technology-driven consumer behaviours. It is precisely this ever-increasing disparity between the rates at which businesses and consumers adopt new technology that continuously threatens organisations’ competitive positions (Kane et al., 2019). Accordingly, being agile today is no longer just about timely recognition of early signs of change in order to defend an existing position but rather being able to perpetually transition between a series of short-lived, temporary competitive advantages (McGrath, 2013, 2019). This novel conceptualisation of agility implies that today’s sources of competitive advantage not only do not determine future growth but can hold companies back from pursuing new opportunities or can lock them into ways of doing business that are ultimately destined to fail. Therefore, researchers and practitioners must seek a more nuanced understanding of what it means to be agile today and how this new type of strategic agility can be achieved.

Strategic agility (hereafter, agility) is a dynamic capability that describes the ability to sense and respond to environmental change (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). While sensing refers to identifying, assessing, and predicting opportunities and threats, responding involves addressing them through mobilisation of resources and execution of operational processes (Roberts & Grover, 2012; Teece et al., 2016). Furthermore, there is a time lag between sensing and responding, which can be reduced through faster and more efective decisionmaking (hereafter, deciding; Overby et al., 2006; Park et al., 2017). Information technology (IT) has the potential to enhance an organisation’s ability to quickly sense and respond to change (Lee et al.,

2015; Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012; Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010; Van Oosterhout et al., 2006). Information systems (IS) research has ofered several potential explanations of how IT can enhance or inhibit agility (see, Tallon et al. (2019) for a comprehensive review). Yet, prior work provides only a partial understanding of the agility-enabling mechanisms of IT because it largely overlooks the fact that the mere presence of IT assets and capabilities does not automatically translate into agile responses unless they are enacted and governed through agile management decisions (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Queiroz et al., 2018). Therefore, IT governance – i.e., organisational choices regarding who makes what IT decisions, how, and when – can either amplify or hinder agility.

IT governance has been widely considered by IS scholars as a “secret sauce” (Tiwana & Kim, 2015) that enables agility by efectively leveraging IT resources and overcoming rigidities imposed by existing organisational structures (Teoh & Chen, 2013). While there is a substantial body of research on IT governance and agility (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010; Zhen et al., 2021), prior work has predominantly relied on conventional assumptions of functional separation between IS and business, hierarchical decision-making structures, vertical authority, and formal IT governance processes within a firm. While such traditional governance approaches have lent themselves reasonably well to extracting value from a long-lasting competitive advantage, they can become a liability if agile companies need to continuously reinvent themselves in pursuit of a new, “transient” competitive advantage (McGrath, 2013). To that end, a temporal dimension of IT governance (when and how fast to decide) becomes as important as its structural (who decides what) and procedural (how to decide) aspects.

For instance, the need to quickly abandon exhausted opportunities in favour of new ones requires having access to a flexible and scalable IT resource base that spans the boundaries of a single firm (Gregory et al., 2018; Tiwana et al., 2013). Agile companies continuously repurpose and redeploy their workforce thereby challenging the idea of a stable, functional separation between business units (BUs) and IT (Gregory et al., 2018). Furthermore, agile companies create systematic and nimble IT innovation processes that allow them to quickly develop and test new ideas in uncontested areas without committing large-scale financial investments upfront. Such a move is often incompatible with traditional assumptions of top-down governance and formal capital budgeting processes, suggesting a need for new thinking on IT governance (Cao et al., 2013a; Magnusson et al., 2020).

However, to date, the research literature has not adequately explored the new IT governance mechanisms that are enabling companies to respond to market turbulence with ease, speed, and dexterity. Therefore, there is a need to uncover these new governance mechanisms and principles that support the new challenges facing strategic agility and that provide a structure to an IT-related decision-making process that enables firms to act faster upon discovering new opportunities despite having few reliable insights about them. Our research aims to respond to this challenge.

To achieve our aim, we adopt a “ternary” view on governance (Wu & Saunders, 2016) and build on the extant literature in IS, strategy, and organisation design as well as exemplar case studies to explore how traditional approaches to IT governance structures, processes, and relational mechanisms are altered to enhance agility (Peterson, 2004). This framework is particularly suitable for discussing new approaches to IT governance because it allows one to logically structure the governance changes occurring along the three core dimensions of IT governance (who, what and how) and to map them onto a temporal dimension that captures the need for strategic agility (when to sense, decide, and respond). We provide several illustrative examples of companies from diferent industries in which new IT governance approaches have manifested themselves.

Our research makes three primary contributions to the next frontier in agility research. First, we discuss how the need for strategic agility challenges conventional assumptions about IT governance that have hitherto dominated IS research. Second, we explore and identify how new “digital” governance mechanisms and principles such as “disciplined” autonomy, alignment of knowledge and decision rights, fast experimentation, and permeable boundaries can improve strategic agility. In doing so, we discuss how these new governance principles can be reconciled and co-exist with traditional governance principles. Despite possible tension between these two sets of governance principles, both approaches can still be complementary. The challenge with reconciling these tensions lies in designing for agility while being open to compromise on coordination and control. Third, we propose new avenues for future research and practice at the nexus of agility and IT governance.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the limitations of traditional approaches to IT governance in a digital context. Section 3 introduces novel and emergent governance mechanisms on the firm and ecosystem levels and discusses their implications and risks for achieving agility. Section 4 abstracts from the practical level of governance mechanisms and develops a set of highlevel governance principles as well as formulates promising future research directions for IS scholars. Section 5 summarises the contributions to research and practice.

## 2. Traditional approaches to IT governance and their limitations

IT governance defines how IT-related decision rights and accountabilities are allocated between the IS department and BUs and is implemented through a set of structural, procedural, and relational mechanisms (Gregory et al., 2018; Peterson, 2004; Tallon et al., 2013). In general, structural mechanisms formally institute liaison roles, lines of communication, and horizontal integration structures between IT and business (e.g., committees and executive councils); procedural mechanisms determine IT decision-making rules and ensure the congruence of IT decisionmaking with strategic goals (e.g., scenario or costbenefit-risk analysis); relational mechanisms establish organisational practices for encouraging IS-business collaboration (e.g., job rotations, colocation, crossfunctional training; Peterson, 2004; Wu & Saunders, 2016). These governance mechanisms are consistent with a view of IS as a separate functional unit that “can be ring-fenced and contained within clear organisational boundaries, assigned a head (i.e., the CIO), given resources and a mandate that is often framed as optimising or maximising a return from any spend, all within an acceptable level of risk” (Peppard, 2018, p. 80). Such perceptions of IT governance reflect conventional assumptions of: (1) top-down authority, (2) functional specialisation, (3) closed organisational boundaries, and (4) financial return maximisation. These assumptions may need to be revisited, however, when trying to plan and deliver strategic agility in today’s fast-paced competitive environment. We next consider each of these assumptions in turn.

## 2.1. Assumption 1. Top-down authority

The degree to which IT decision making authority should be centralised at the top management level as opposed to being delegated to, or shared among, individual BUs or process owners has been extensively studied as a key aspect of IT governance (Andersen et al., 2017; Gregory et al., 2018; Tiwana & Kim, 2015; Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010; Weill & Ross, 2004; Winkler & Brown, 2013; Xue et al., 2011; Zhen et al., 2021). Although decentralised governance is associated with better responsiveness and flexibility, the assumption of vertical, top-down authority is implicit in these studies. Even in “decentralised” governance structures, decision rights are still concentrated in the hands of a single manager at the top of a BU hierarchy (Peterson, 2004). Consequently, granting IT decision-making authority to non-managerial staf has been rarely contemplated and often seen as synonymous with a poorly functioning and uncontrollable “anarchy” (Weill & Ross, 2004).

Today, when consumer adoption of technology often precedes organisational innovation (Kane et al., 2019), advanced consumer understanding allows a company to spot early signs of change before competitors. Consequently, the ability to recognise weak signals of a looming change often resides with nonmanagerial employees who directly interact with consumers but may not be able to pass this information up the hierarchy efectively or expeditiously (McGrath, 2019; Peppard, 2018; Pfefer & Sutton, 2000). Furthermore, consumerisation of IT empowers employees to make their own decisions about the use of tools and software in the workplace (labelled shadow IT) without formal involvement of the IS function. Under such conditions, the classic approach to IT governance rooted in top-down authority and standardised rules not only fails to allow for the heterogeneity of individual needs and behaviours but also inhibits the speed and efectiveness with which employees can independently enhance their business processes (Gregory et al., 2018; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Emmanuel, 2018).

## 2.2. Assumption 2. Functional specialisation

Second, while IS scholars acknowledge the importance of alignment and the need to create a shared understanding between IS and business (Chakravarty et al., 2013; Karahanna & Preston, 2013; Liang et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2015), the idea that the IS function is functionally and organisationally separated from other business functions (e.g., marketing, finance, logistics) has rarely been questioned. Moreover, traditional governance models typically assumed that “decision rights need to be collocated with the knowledge needed to make those decisions” (Tiwana & Kim, 2015, p. 658), thereby favouring functional specialisation between IS and business.

Today, there is increasing evidence that as IT becomes more pervasive, IT knowledge is more widely distributed across and beyond the organisation. First, more IT decisions (e.g., software purchases) are made outside the IS function by BUs (Aubert & Rivard, 2020; Queiroz et al., 2020; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015). Second, more business knowledge from non-IS functions is needed to deliver value from IT (Gregory et al., 2018). Finally, more IT knowledge is required to deliver value in non-IS functions (Nambisan et al., 2017; Svahn et al., 2017). Taken together, this suggests that the knowledge needed to generate value from IT is no longer required to be located within a single IS function (Peppard, 2007, 2018). Therefore, continuing to treat IS as a separate function that is accountable for extracting value from IT, “without having full access and authority over all necessary resources and knowledge” (Peppard, 2007, p. 339), undermines an organisation’s ability to continuously sense and respond to change in a timely manner.

## 2.3. Assumption 3. Closed organisational boundaries

Third, conventional assumptions confine IT governance to an internal IS function within an organisation (Weill & Ross, 2004). This reflects a legacy view that the resources and capabilities underlying a competitive position should be owned by the firm (Barney, 1991). Indeed, when the goal of the firm is to sustain an advantage in the long run, a unique combination of proprietary resources deters rivals’ attempts at replicating the firm’s advantage. However, when competitive advantage is short-lived, having a large resource base becomes an impediment to agility for three reasons. First, managers’ attention is focused on extracting as much value as possible from the existing resources (sunk costs) thus reducing their ability to recognise opportunities that their existing resource base does not readily support (McGrath, 2013). Second, having a large resource base escalates managerial commitment to an “old” course of action and makes them unwilling or unable to respond to change even when signs of change abound (Staw, 1981). Third, rapid technology development (e.g., cloud, mobile, big data analytics) increases pressure on firms to quickly scale up or down in response to fastchanging customer demands by developing new capabilities (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Nambisan et al., 2017).

Since continuously developing new capabilities within the boundaries of a single firm is a time-consuming and resource-intensive endeavour, there is a growing realisation that the pursuit of transient competitive advantage is only possible by leveraging external capabilities through an extended network of specialised third parties (Svahn et al., 2017). Moreover, accessing external talent and innovation through external companies makes more economic sense as “the costs of internal bureaucracy often exceed external transaction costs” (Gulati et al., 2012, p. 575) due to increased connectivity and lower communication costs (Bharadwaj et al., 2013). As a result, the locus of value creation shifts from a single firm to a dyadic relationship between the firm and its suppliers (Cao et al., 2013b) and, more recently, to an ecosystem comprising the firm and its multiple interconnected partners (Chen et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Schreieck et al., 2021).

## 2.4. Assumption 4. Financial return maximisation

Finally, a traditional process to prioritise and evaluate IT investments relied on evaluation of financial criteria (e.g., ROI, NPV, payback; Cao et al., 2013a). Typically, investments in IT are made with the same capital budgeting mindset as other corporate investments and often prioritise eficiency criteria (McGrath, 2019). However, the pursuit of transient competitive advantage in a fast-changing environment neither allows traditional planning and assessment of the return on IT investment nor does it necessitate large-scale lump sum investments upfront. Instead, rapid competitive response requires an agile approach to software development that enables small-scale experimentation and quick testing of new market opportunities when the stakes are still low (Day & Schoemaker, 2019; Vidgen & Wang, 2009). Therefore, there is a growing disconnect between the formal IT funding process that is based on welldefined, fixed requirements, and follows a specific delivery schedule and agile software development that is based on evolving scope, incremental design cost, and flexible scheduling (Cao et al., 2013a). Riskaversion and the slow pace of formal IT investment prioritisation and funding processes results in the emergence of shadow IT innovation practices that are unsanctioned or discouraged by corporate IS managers (Koch et al., 2021; Magnusson et al., 2020).

To summarise, emerging information technologies present opportunities and threats that demand diferent and greater agility from firms in order to remain competitive. Agility, in turn, can be enhanced by IT governance approaches that improve and expedite sensing and responding capabilities. There are early signs of looming “inflection points” for IT governance, that will inevitably require companies to change their approach to governing IT, thereby raising important research questions. For example, as the scope and focus of IT governance transitions from governing firm-controlled resources within a separate IS function to governing staf and shared IT resources that span the boundaries of a single firm (Gregory et al., 2018), should the well-established structural, procedural, and relational mechanisms that worked in the past – as well as their underlying assumptions – be replaced if companies hope to achieve and maintain agility today?

In the next section, we discuss how these four conventional assumptions are being challenged in today’s fast-paced competitive digital environment and consider several examples from practice to identify new, emerging digital governance practices that are being initiated to enhance strategic agility. Our findings highlight who is being empowered to make what IT deployment decisions as well as how those decisions are being made and when they are being made in order to improve the timeliness of those decisions. Our findings are based on a review of the current literature on IT governance and related disciplines as well as on empirical evidence regarding digital governance practices being deployed in several corporate case studies.

## 3. Emerging mechanisms of “digital” governance

Consistent with a ternary view of IT governance, we review emergent structural, procedural, and relational governance mechanisms and discuss how they contribute to enhancing agility at each core stage of sensing, deciding, and responding. In doing so, we distinguish between the governance mechanisms that manifest themselves, (1) internally within the firm (i.e., firmlevel mechanisms) and (2) between the firm and a set of interrelated partners within an ecosystem (i.e., ecosystem-level mechanisms). We support our arguments with empirical evidence from five innovative companies in the banking (anonymised as The Bank and Spunta banking ecosystem), telecommunications (The Telco), and utilities (The Utility and The Gas company) industries. Table 1 lists and briefly describes the companies that have informed our research findings. In the next section, we begin an exploration of emergent firm-level governance mechanisms uncovered in these companies and what this means for strategic agility.

## 3.1. Firm-level mechanisms

## 3.1.1. Structural mechanisms

Structural mechanisms essentially determine who gets to make what IT decisions. To enhance agility, organisations are changing the structure and shifting the locus of IT decision making authority. This manifests itself in: (1) the merging of IS and business functions; (2) the emergence of community-based organisational forms; and (3) the utilisation of crowds and innovation networks.

## 3.1.1.1. Merging of IS and business

As IT becomes more pervasive in organisations, employees within BUs independently rely on consumer IT applications which allow them to operate faster than if they were restricted by corporate IS. In response, IT governance is moving away from standardised rules and rigid processes towards defining a set of broader principles that employees are expected to follow, without precisely specifying which IT assets and applications employees need to use (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Emmanuel, 2018). Furthermore, BUs’ local IT capability is strengthened by redistribution of the IT workforce across diferent non-IS functions. For instance, over 60% of IT employees at the Bank are embedded in non-IS organisational functions. As a result, the BUs have become empowered to make technology decisions independently (Aubert & Rivard, 2020) while IS and non-IS functions responsibilities have started to overlap and merge.

A mix of functional and IT expertise within a BU enables it to sense opportunities faster and prevent internal misalignments that might make it dificult to respond in time (Chau et al., 2020; Roberts et al., 2016). Additionally, granting BUs – and their constituent employees – greater autonomy and accountability for their own IT decisions accelerates their ability to respond to opportunities and to initiate their own projects quickly, bypassing the enterprise IS function and minimising delays associated with corporate approval procedures (Queiroz et al., 2022). This structure is beneficial at a sensing stage when BUs explore new technology opportunities and develop proof of concept of potential business applications, as well as at the responding stage when product, process, and information architecture is designed and implemented. On the downside, the risks of excessive BU autonomy include the lack of cross-BU synergies, control, and coordination that can result in cost ineficiencies, proliferation of diferent standards (Xue et al., 2011), and increased security vulnerabilities (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Emmanuel, 2018).

## 3.1.1.2. Community-based structures

Second, companies are increasingly moving towards self-organising, cross-functional, community-based structures in terms of governing IT within the firm. Most well-known examples of self-organising structures that defy formal hierarchy and authority come from the technology sector. For instance, Spotify’s agile model uses “squads” as basic organisational units which autonomously decide what aspects of a product to build and how (Mankins & Garton, 2017). Similarly, Valve, a leading company in the video game software industry, completely does away with the hierarchy and allows employees to self-select IT projects (Puranam & Hakonsson, 2015). Recently, traditional companies outside the technology sector are adopting community-based structures in governing IT as well. For instance, the Bank structures its digital innovation eforts by means of “hybrid task forces” that self-organise around a particular project idea. The Telco company adopted the Spotify blueprint and organised its digital innovation workforce in squads, tribes, and chapters.<sup>1</sup>

While companies may use diferent terms to refer to self-organising teams, there are several structural characteristics that these community-based governance forms share. First, communities span the boundaries of functional departments and management levels (Kolbjørnsrud, 2017). The communities are formed on a voluntary ad hoc basis by employees with various functional specialisations, diferent levels of seniority, heterogeneous capabilities, and backgrounds. Communities include frontline employees that are close to the issue and have direct contact with customers but are typically excluded from decisionmaking by traditional structures. As a result, communities shift IT decision rights to a level where the necessary knowledge to make these decisions resides. Therefore, in fast-paced environments, community structures permit employees to sense signs of change faster and to decide on appropriate application responses due to the contribution of employees at all organisational levels (Lee & Edmondson, 2017).

Second, even though communities are internal to the firm, they can reach out to external partners (e.g., incubators, startup networks, research centres) to sense new technology trends or to bring in temporary talent to support the development of innovative products or services. For example, the Gas company constantly scouts for startups that can quickly bring solutions to unresolved customer demands and organises hackathons to quickly develop prototypes of solutions that can be converted into actual products.

Third, communities have a structure characterised by the absence of formal hierarchy between members. Instead, individual authority is based on one’s own capabilities and project commitment (Dahlander & Mahony, 2011; Lee & Cole, 2003) and is reflected in the decision rights and responsibilities that an individual assumes (Ferraz & Dos Santos Júnior, 2021). Because decision-making authority is held by individuals that are most knowledgeable about the problem context, communities are more likely to distinguish signals from noise and to eventually make a more informed decision.

Fourth, communities, in all of our cases, are built in an ad hoc fashion around concrete problems to respond to a specific goal or request from the business. By design, communities voluntarily bring together a heterogenous group of individuals who a priori have a common goal. Furthermore, communities are not constrained by formal reporting relationships and control mechanisms (Lee & Edmondson, 2017). Therefore, communities create favourable structural and behavioural conditions for incentive alignment, shared understanding, and goal congruence among community members which, in turn, enhances agility (Dahlander & Mahony, 2011; Kolbjørnsrud, 2017).

For example, in the Utility company, decision structures take the form of hybrid, decentralised communities called “hubs” in all its business lines. A hub is an organisational unit that consists of cross-functional teams that has the responsibility to make small investments (typically €200K - €300K) in digital innovations. Once a hub identifies and evaluates market opportunities and risks, agile rooms take operational responsibility for project development. Thus, hubs are also responsible for defining the strategic investments and standard setting for the diferent lines of business.

## 3.1.1.3. Internal and external crowds

Digital governance relies on structures that build connections and strengthen ties between individuals with diferent mental schemas (Pinsonneault & Choi, 2022). This facilitates sensing of weak signals on the periphery and generates more ideas that serve as input for timely strategic decision-making. For example, the Bank enhances its capabilities to sense new opportunities by leveraging the “wisdom of the crowd” through its Idea Management programme. Idea Management consists of a collaborative improvement platform (both on-line and of-line) that can host all of the organisational projects that require multiple interactions between team members. An individual, also called the sponsor, can launch a business challenge and potentially everyone inside the Bank can contribute to the challenge and enrich it with new ideas. These challenges can result in a real organisational project, with human, technological, and economic resources. Likewise, interaction with the external actors via crowdsourcing is key to increasing agility. For example, LEGO, Fiat, Netflix, and NASA “have largely relied on their internal R&D while using the crowdsourcing initiatives as a complementary way to generate ideas”, to solve problems by accessing multiskilled workforce, and to gain competitive advantage by quickly converting new ideas into products (Nevo & Kotlarsky, 2020, p. 9).

## 3.1.2. Procedural mechanisms

Procedural mechanisms and practices relate to processes of how IT decisions are made in new IT governance structures.

During the sensing stage, multiple actors in crossfunctional communities rely on diferent, often conflicting mental models that can enhance or undermine agility in sensing and interpreting signals from the environment (Tallon et al., 2019). Hence, digital governance processes need to reduce competing cognitive tensions while at the same time encouraging diversity of perspectives. To that end, the merging of IT and business convergence endows BUs with an ability to “tap localised intelligence” (Day & Schoemaker, 2006). While shared understanding of business and IT accelerates knowledge transfer and promotes consensus around emerging opportunities (Chau et al., 2020; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), teams with unique “converged” understanding of a problem lack the benefit of having opposing views (and confrontational discussions) and may miss signs of change, thereby decreasing sensing efectiveness. In order for communities to avoid group-think that might otherwise lock them into a single, “dominant” understanding of an issue, companies are developing new practices that generate and explore multiple hypotheses about the future development of technology and customer demand. This will help to encourage openness and moderate conflict within a team (Day & Schoemaker, 2006).

Conversely, digital decision-making structures (deciding stage) take into consideration input from all community members without necessarily requiring all members to unanimously agree (consultationbased practices). While consensus among the core community members may be required, when communities get larger, reaching consensus takes too long and dilutes responsibility for the decision (Laloux, 2014; McGrath, 2013). An alternative to consensus is consultation-based decision-making when all those afected by a decision are consulted but are not given the power to block the decisionmaking process (Laloux, 2014). As an example of IT and business convergence, the Utility company set up several hubs (overarching structures) linked to each of its core BUs and 90 rooms (including over 1,000 people). Hubs decide on field operational solutions immediately after an agile room is born. As defined by the Utility company, a hub is where top skills of all the teams reside (co-location of knowledge). These units are designed to probe strategies and learn through small investments and testing hypotheses. The basic idea is to create a “balanced portfolio of strategic options” with small investments (Day & Schoemaker, 2006) but to only subsequently expand its range of investments once uncertainty is suitably resolved.

When it comes to action (responding stage), community members take on responsibilities voluntarily and self-select for tasks that the project requires (Dahlander & Mahony, 2011; Fjeldstad et al., 2012). Project roles emerge spontaneously. Individuals may hold one or several diferent roles at diferent points in time. Coordination of actions is achieved through transparency of both task allocation and each member’s contributions. Control is enacted by instilling in members behavioural norms and community values of trust, knowledge sharing, and fast experimentation through a series of small-scale launches. In addition to social forms of control, community members oversee each other’s contributions to prevent misalignment with the project or organisational goals (Kolbjørnsrud, 2017).

Communities are granted considerable autonomy in managing digital projects and are held fully accountable for project outcomes and spending. For example, the Telco company launched five tribes working on diferent technology-related aspects of consumer innovation (e.g., data analytics, AI, digital channels) consisting of 50 squads and over 450 employees. Each squad manages its own budget and is responsible for functional and architectural choices. Investments are allocated in an incremental fashion and are intended for concept testing rather than a large-scale roll-out. The Bank uses smaller-scale investments at the initial stages of community-based development projects. This incremental funding approach speeds up decision making, bypassing formal investment approval procedures, and helping to achieve agility at the initial stages of a project when it is most needed.

## 3.1.3. Relational mechanisms

New governance structures and processes are supported by relational mechanisms of technologyenabled communication transparency, cultural change, information sharing, people empowerment, and competence-based trust.

First, communication transparency and free flow of information are key relational mechanisms during both the sensing and decision-making stages. These are achieved by using technology-mediated tools which enable the public exchange of information in real time, minimise informational asymmetries among community members, and increase the rate of knowledge transfer. For example, the Gas company implemented a collaboration platform accessible to internal and external participants that served as a document repository and document-sharing tool.

Second, transitioning to new digital governance also entails cultural changes. Community structures eliminate cultural barriers that prevent employees from freely interacting with one another regardless of their formal positions and functional “jurisdictions”. Additionally, instilling a culture of experimentation has motivated employees to rapidly test new solutions and to consider failure as an integral part of a learning process. Rotation of personnel and joint training programmes further blur the boundaries between IS and non-IS colleagues, increase alignment among them, and improve shared understanding of problems.

Finally, community participants face two main issues: (1) align their knowledge and goals, and (2) manage social relationships among team members. The main relational mechanism at work to govern these two challenges is competence-based trust (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999) which is grounded in professional credentials, track record, and past experience. Interactions between team-members is facilitated by the exchange of high-quality information and the use of efective communication tools.

## 3.2. Ecosystem-level mechanisms

An ecosystem is “a network of interconnected organisations, organised around a focal firm or a platform, and incorporating both production and use side participants, and focusing on the development of new value through innovation” (Autio & Thomas, 2014, p. 205). An ecosystem includes organisations, customers, suppliers, and other complementary actors (regulators, education, and research institutions) that develop more extensive capabilities using a shared set of digital technologies. Ecosystem members combine their individual contributions into a coherent and customer-focused solution. Collectively they create value that no single constituent firm could do alone (Adner, 2017).

The ecosystem holds the potential for enhancing both sensing and responding agility. As regards the former, ecosystems often include customers (or other partners that work closely with customers), and therefore the ability to correctly identify customer needs ahead of competitors is improved. In fact, when consumers are directly and continuously involved in the ideation and creation process, there is no need to “sense” their needs at all because customers explicitly articulate how they want services to be designed and deployed (Tan et al., 2009). The ecosystem provides the focal firm with access to complementary skills that it might not readily possess in-house. Therefore, once an opportunity is identified, the focal firm can quickly respond to it by mobilising external resources and leveraging thirdparty talent. Reaping agility benefits from an ecosystem is contingent on developing new governance approaches that account for the combined use of proprietary IT assets and an array of partner-owned technologies in a timely and cost-efective manner.

## 3.2.1. Structural mechanisms

Ecosystems defy traditional internal structures based on top-down allocation of individual rights and responsibilities. To that end, the real value of an ecosystem lies in its systematic capacity to access complementary knowledge, resources, and skills possessed by other members that support problem solving and sensing new opportunities (Teece et al., 2016). The search for opportunities is enabled by the distribution of knowledge and information among actors that individually are better positioned to recognise early signs of change in technologies, markets, and society (Nambisan et al., 2017).

At the sensing stage, the notion of orchestration, the matching of problems with potential solutions, or brokering solutions generated by contributors to likely problems or opportunities, becomes crucial (Giudici et al., 2018; Nambisan et al., 2017). Orchestration takes diferent forms in open and closed ecosystems. Open-system orchestration is essentially decentralised, where individual members are free to interact to the extent that they find it beneficial and the orchestrator clarifies the goals and identifies complementarities between contributors (Giudici et al., 2018). Conversely, in a closed ecosystem, orchestration is performed by a central firm that governs the alignment between distinctive capabilities and skills within a coordinated innovation system. In both cases, the ecosystem has the power to discover new business scenarios using heterogenous network resources efectively and quickly.

At Utility, the innovation manager created the Idea Factory to stimulate creative thinking by engaging individuals from outside the company. They experimented with this approach in Brazil where the programme goal was to redesign the business development activities for renewable energy. Since then, the Idea Factory has been introduced in other areas of Latin America. Now digital governance includes an on-demand component where external persons contact the company in response to requests for assistance.

In terms of decision-making (deciding), in an ecosystem context, authority is partitioned between the focal firm and its partners (e.g., developers) in a way that balances flexibility and control (Huber et al., 2017). While an ecosystem governance approach requires the focal company to relinquish some control over its digital business applications (Gregory et al., 2018), giving too much decision-making influence to external partners with multiple goals and divergent perspectives can result in time delays as it may take the partners too long to reach a consensus (Chen et al., 2021). To that end, researchers have advocated for semi-decentralised, “shared” decision structures that enable the focal firm to shape governance by creating structures and processes while giving external actors an ability to refine governance processes (Hurni et al., 2020) and to make application-related development and implementation decisions independently (Tiwana, 2013). Doing so ensures that decision rights are co-located with specialised, complementary knowledge.

Chen et al. (2021) report that semi-decentralised governance structures usually allow ecosystem members to participate in goal setting and decision making. At the same time, there are key parties that ensure efective governance that prevents decision stand-ofs between members, maintain creativity and diversity within the ecosystem, and balance quick response with efective solutions. The key parties make decisions about ecosystem architecture that allow for the coordination and integration of the interdependent activities of multiple complementors into a coherent whole without compromising their diversity and autonomy (Tiwana, 2013).

Similarly, during the responding stage the role of the orchestrator (among multilateral groups of actors) in innovation ecosystems is fundamental as it ensures both coherence and customer orientation (Adner, 2017; Autio & Thomas, 2014; Dattée et al., 2018). Moreover, the orchestration firm can enhance coherent and customer-oriented digital innovations using technology artefacts that match solutions to problems as well as joint sensemaking during the innovation process (Autio & Thomas, 2014).

Utility, for instance, has created diferent initiatives including hackathons and competitions, to engage startup companies in joint development. Utility has also invited suppliers to meet with the R&D staf of their customer firms, thereby leveraging the innovation capabilities of an ecosystem. This collaborative strategy was enabled through the “Open Innovability” platform for crowdsourcing innovative ideas and solutions to global energy challenges. This platform has created numerous partnerships and innovations at all stages of solution development, from theoretical models to full prototypes, ready for the mass market.

## 3.2.2. Procedural mechanisms

Ecosystems are characterised by actors’ mutual dependence because the final outcome is contingent upon interdependent activities performed by the multiple actors collectively. This characteristic has implications for the processes of sensing, coordination, control, and financing within an ecosystem.

The interdependent nature of ecosystem innovation makes it impossible to predict with certainty the type and timing of innovation outputs of the participant firms within a network. In an open ecosystem, efective entrepreneurial innovation depends on a firm’s sensing capabilities which can be strengthened by an orchestrator though deliberate processes of building collaborative engagement, facilitating member’s introspection, encouraging members’ exploration of complementarities, and fostering dispersed collaboration (Giudici et al., 2018).

Knowledge mobility, specifically the ease with which knowledge is shared, acquired, and deployed within the network, is another enabler of ecosystem innovative outcomes (Dhanasai & Parkhe, 2006). In a closed ecosystem, a hub firm orchestrates the valuable knowledge residing at diferent points in the network and can arrange for its transfer to other points where it is needed. It is important to note, however, that the transfer of knowledge does not mean that those entities will act on that knowledge, even if there is a realisation that knowledge is important to their welfare (Pfefer & Sutton, 2000).

When it comes to deciding, making a financial commitment in an ecosystem is complicated by the inherent uncertainty about technology trajectories and by unclear resource commitments from the complementary actors. In such conditions, the space of possible alternatives is so vast that it limits a decision-maker’s ability to distinguish meaningful signals from noise. Therefore, decision-makers prefer to proceed in incremental steps which require decomposing projects into smaller, sequential stages which are executed and funded sequentially (Tiwana, 2013). Cheaper and faster iterations create two primary benefits for agility. First, even if actors realise they have pursued the wrong opportunity, it is easier to stop the project early on because the investment risk and financial commitment are “capped” to the completed stage only. Second, each stage reduces uncertainty about technology, narrows down the scope of viable alternatives and allows for a quick pivot to the right opportunity when the signals become clear (Dattée et al., 2018; Day & Schoemaker, 2019).

For example, initially the Spunta project proceeded with small investments (about €25,000 per bank) to start the experiments on blockchain. After a few years, the ecosystem became a space for testing and using many business applications.

Ecosystem governance places greater emphasis on coordination. In this case, governance becomes “a multilateral one-to-many” issue (Hurni et al., 2021, p. 237) and cannot be achieved by means of a series of bilateral contracts for two main reasons. First, mutual independencies require the focal firm to co-govern other actors collectively so that they achieve high performance (Krancher et al., 2022). Second, as the number of ecosystem actors grows substantially larger, contracting with each actor “bilaterally” will increase governance costs and fail to accommodate all possible contingencies (Wareham et al., 2014). To reduce governance costs and accelerate multilateral decision-making, coordination of actions occurs through standardised rules which universally apply to all actors within an ecosystem (Huber et al., 2017). To that end, relationships among actors take place through a platform interface that automates elements of coordination, control, and monitoring procedures (Yoo et al., 2010). Technically, this is supported by modular software architectures that allow components of a single product to be produced by multiple partners with little coordination (Jacobides et al., 2018; Tiwana et al., 2010).

For example, for Spunta the coordination of activities was assured by five diferent structures (Business Network, Business Network Governor, Business Network Designer, Business Network Operator, and DLT Network Provider) that have the responsibility of setting up, coordinating, and managing the network of adherents. These structures also facilitate consensus on functional and governance rules.

Control, monitoring, and compliance within an ecosystem tend to eschew conventional contractual and relational mechanisms (Krancher et al., 2022). On the one hand, exploring diverse technology configurations necessitates more autonomy and freedom for ecosystem actors (Wareham et al., 2014). On the other hand, the complementary nature of each actor’s contributions requires a certain degree of control to ensure that diferent components evolve at a proper pace and can be easily integrated when completed (Tiwana, 2013). As a result, during the responding stage, control shifts away from process-based towards outcomes-based controls that motivate the actors to reach a common objective (Gregory et al., 2018; Krancher et al., 2022). Monitoring and compliance are non-coercive and are based on providing the actors with facilitative resources (e.g., manuals, mockup, and testing tools) that enable actors to autonomously check if their contributions adhere to the standards, fix errors early in the progress, and minimise rework (Tiwana, 2013).

New IT platform governance research ofers some promising answers to the efective management and control of digital ecosystems. Empirical research by Zhang et al., 2022) found that strict platform access control (gatekeeping) enhanced complementors’ value creation activities and knowledge sharing among complementors while lax gatekeeping rules reduced knowledge sharing. O’Mahony and Karp (2022) found that collectively developed platform governance mechanisms led to increased participation by, and leadership from, ecosystem members.

For instance, the infrastructure for the Spunta project included the creation of a functional space to host other use cases. Two main areas of control were established: 1) the Business Zone which comprises a set of banks belonging to one or more Business Networks enabled by a common blockchain/DLT technological infrastructure and organised according to governance rules shared by the members; and 2) ABILabChain – The Business Zone of ABI (Italian Banking Association) associates for which ABI Lab plays the role of coordinator.

Innovation artefacts, design guidelines, standardised development methods, and shared infrastructure act as orchestration tools that help customeroriented digital innovations to materialise. These innovation artefacts complement the organisational actors as orchestrating entities for matching solutions to problems and joint sensemaking (Autio & Thomas, 2014).

Lastly, Lyytinen et al., (2016) identify the process of a cognitive translation as a generative process where innovation knowledge is identified, produced, refined, integrated, and evaluated through digital means. During this process, ecosystem members use digital tools and digital representations “to assist in creating and refining innovation knowledge by mapping the knowledge into representations and sharing them with others in the network” (Lyytinen et al., 2016, p. 55).

## 3.2.3. Relational mechanisms

In addition to automated and formal controls, the focal firm instils shared values and promotes cultural norms which guide the behaviour of ecosystem actors (Huber et al., 2017; Tiwana et al., 2010). As discussed above, the orchestrator supports a certain degree of openness and heterogeneity among actors and facilitates the flow of innovative ideas through the sharing of information. Mutual trustworthiness is a means to lower relational barriers and to share knowledge and information with others more freely (Giudici et al., 2018). During sensing activities, mutual trustworthiness enhances self-awareness and positive emotional experiences.

Utility created an ecosystem programme named the “ReShape challenge”, to generate innovative solutions to two energy-related social problems: namely, 1) the sustainability challenge focused on digitising scalable recovery plans for local markets, and 2) the equity challenge focused on developing connectivity and digitalisation that combats social inequality. The motivation for this programme is to join forces and create teamwork that creates a win-win-win collaboration for the corporation, the startup, and the planet.

In an ecosystem, shared values, norms, and peer controls take time to develop naturally because of the lack of prior history of interactions between actors, high levels of churn, and diversity of partners (Tiwana, 2013). Emergence of shared values and norms can be accelerated by actively scouting for complementors that already represent a cultural fit or centrally controlling the admission of “fit” actors through a gatekeeping process (Tiwana, 2013).

While in a closed-system, orchestrators are responsible for defining roles and coordinating eforts and contributions of its members to a collective goal, an open-system requires “a lighter touch” to preserve the inherent entrepreneurial spirit and spontaneity required to scout new business opportunities (Giudici et al., 2018, p. 1392). Furthermore, Nambisan et al. (2017) claim that socio-cognitive sensemaking facilitates digital innovation processes and lowers barriers when innovation is hard to understand by any single innovator given diferent cognitive frames in diferent participants. Socio-cognitive sensemaking takes place when “the technology is being made sense of simultaneously in an individual innovator’s cognition and the innovator’s social system of collectives of organisations and individuals” (Nambisan et al., 2017, p. 228).

To enable decision-making agility (deciding), ecosystem governance should enable the focal firm to orchestrate actors’ interactions in a way that all available information is gathered, communicated, and leveraged in the most time-eficient manner (Chen et al., 2021). In a larger ecosystem, communications are mostly platform-based (e.g., online forum) and facilitate transparent knowledge exchange. In addition, socialisation events (e.g., developer conferences) can expedite the exchange of ideas, information, and knowledge and, consequently, enhance collective decision-making capabilities. It is important to mention that knowledge exchange is supported by the cultural values discussed above. Therefore, encouraging collaboration, promoting mutual trustworthiness, and preserving “the positive energy of network interactions” are efective relational mechanisms that can support agility within an ecosystem (Giudici et al., 2018, p. 1393).

## 4. Towards new frontiers of agility-enabling digital governance

As previously discussed, the IT governance literature has traditionally focused on four basic assumptions: top-down authority, functional specialisation, closed boundaries, and financial return maximisation. In Section 3, we have identified new digital governance mechanisms that improve an organisation’s ability to quickly sense and respond to rapid changes in the environment. These new governance mechanisms challenge the conventional assumptions about IT governance that have dominated IS research for the past two decades.

Challenging these assumptions creates theoretical lacunae and triggers a need for transformative theories of IT governance that are more representative of the present realities confronting organisations. IS scholars are encouraged to broaden the scope of their research on IT governance in order to address these gaps and explore the new frontiers of digital governance.

In Section 3, organisational structures, procedural mechanisms, relational dynamics from extant literature, and case observations point to enablers of cultural and organisational change that facilitate (or alternately inhibit) agility. In today’s technologydriven competitive environment, organisations are challenged to adopt governance approaches that will accelerate decision making and reduce the delay from sensing to responding and yet not compromise the relevance and veracity of decision outcomes. According to Tallon et al. (2019), the presence of sense and response capabilities per se does not answer the question “of how long it takes for a firm to sense new market threats and opportunities, how long it takes to translate sensing into competitive actions or how the pace of decision-making afects the transition between sensing and responding” (p. 231). The reality is that IT governance, as traditionally defined, can delay the path from sensing, to decision-making, to reacting. New forms of IT governance can not only accelerate the time it takes to sense, decide, and respond but can also reimagine the actors in that process and the structures within which they operate.

We have studied changes in organisational IT governance decision making behaviours that avoid potential decision-making bottlenecks. Case observations included in this study highlight agile companies committed to digital transformation and strategic agility. These companies cultivated conditions that have led to new culture-driven governance approaches (Kane et al., 2019), and thereby become more responsive to dynamic environments. Below, we abstract from our company observations a series of new digital governance principles that enable strategic agility based on the emerging governance mechanisms reported previously in Section 3.

## 4.1. Principle 1. Disciplined autonomy (vs top-down authority)

Decision-making with cumbersome or timeconsuming hierarchical approval or socialisation processes are discarded in modern organisations that pursue short-term, dynamic competitive advantage. New agile, autonomous forms of digital governance such as communities and teams can function in the absence of a formal hierarchy. Community empowerment is enabled by allocating decision rights authority based on one’s abilities and expertise and not based on one’s managerial position. Community members are not necessarily appointed but, in some cases, can join and leave communities voluntarily. Top management’s governance role is limited to providing high-level guidance, while awarding communities substantial autonomy to operate through transparent and technology-mediated communication tools and to selforganise using “peer-based monitoring” practices (Kolbjørnsrud, 2017). Top-management strategic direction does not disappear, but rather it focuses on assessing the potential impact of proposed new products and services on competitive advantage.

The adoption of cross-functional teams is unlikely to achieve agility unless those teams have some degree of autonomy (Kane et al., 2019). Autonomy is supported if it meets certain criteria such as focused productivity, shared values, performance control, and rules of engagement. Autonomous governance structures require that managers adopt new ways of leading teams, and that team members understand and focus on company strategic goals. Yet, decision autonomy should be combined with formal, output-based controls on how third-party applications should perform and be integrated into the core system (Tiwana, 2013). Excessive autonomy may result in a lack of synergies between BUs, proliferation of multiple incoherent IT standards, and inability to achieve corporate level eficiency benefits (Queiroz et al., 2022). To capture the need to balance the tension between autonomy and control, we use the term “disciplined autonomy”.

In the emerging new frontier of digital governance, our case observations found that governance in these agile organisations is becoming less organisationcentric and more governed by empowered individuals working in flexible/agile/liquid teams and ecosystems that are driving faster, more comprehensive, and more successful responses to marketplace changes. Fast forming teams or task forces are supporting the growth of experimentation-based problem solving within high trust environments. Governance communities are instituting faster investment approval processes, bypassing formal asset allocation systems thereby achieving agility at the initial response stage when it is most timely and reducing delay between sensing and responding. Agile organisations are also empowering decision-making at lower levels of the organisational hierarchy, thus granting individuals authority based on situational knowledge rather than a formal position in the organisational hierarchy. This observed “disciplined autonomy” principle raises several important questions for future research including:

● Will placing IT decision rights closer to employees who are in a better position to sense problems/opportunities result in faster, more appropriate responses?

● Will all industries be conducive to IT governance at the community or team level or only to industries operating in fast changing, technologydriven business environments?

● What are the impacts of intra-firm communitybased, digital governance on agility?

## 4.2. Principle 2. IS-business convergence (vs functional specialisation)

New digital governance mechanisms demonstrate that the organisational boundaries between the IS function and business functions, that were fundamental to traditional IT governance models (Weill & Ross, 2004), are becoming blurred. IT knowledge and business knowledge are merging across the organisation. Since most of today’s business innovations are technology-based, IT projects are increasingly being managed by non-IS business functions (Gregory et al., 2018). The convergence between business and IS implies that roles that were traditionally held by the IS department are now formally embedded in business functions. In allocating decision rights, new governance will require less emphasis on functional separation and more emphasis on individual and collective knowledge.

Prior research suggests that decentralised governance enhances agility only in combination with greater peripheral knowledge of both IS and BUs (Tiwana & Kim, 2015). The main argument is that greater IT knowledge in the BU will contribute to agility by ensuring the shared understanding of business needs and IT capabilities, by faster knowledge transfer, and by more easily reaching consensus on how to react to emerging opportunities and threats (Chau et al., 2020; Preston & Karahanna, 2009; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). The convergence principle is consistent with the idea that achieving alignment between IS and business is fundamental for agility, while it also extends this line of argument by suggesting that agility can be further increased if functional separation between the two functions ceases to exist.

The collapsing of traditional functions within a single locus of decision-making and action enables teams to manage diferent situations and test many options concurrently, thus reducing the time to devise and deliver ideas for how to respond to change. Additionally, since these new teams are composed of employees from diferent backgrounds, they are able to bring diferent perspectives, experiences, and thinking, thereby increasing the level of creative problemsolving. Firms are investing in the training and rotation of employees so that functional employees gain more IT knowledge and IT specialists obtain a better understanding of the business and its customers.

The purposeful convergence and colocation of business and IT knowledge is fostering decisionmaking and innovation as well as fail-fast experimentation where IT-driven solutions are critical to meeting new environment opportunities and challenges (Tiwana & Kim, 2015). With these converged digital governance models, IT application decisions are not experiencing communication delays across separate organisational units that speak a diferent language thereby resulting in faster managerial consensus and decision-making related to business challenges and solutions.

This observed “IS-Business convergence” principle raises several important questions for future research including:

● How should companies reorganise to foster the convergence of IT and business knowledge to improve sensing, deciding and responding capabilities?

● How can merged IT and business knowledge be leveraged to enhance experimentation and responding capabilities?

## 4.3. Principle 3. Permeable boundaries (vs closed boundaries)

An organisation can enhance its agility if it relies on the expertise of external actors who have “skills and an appreciation of client needs” that the focal organisation alone might not possess (Tiwana et al., 2010, p. 675). Social media and new digital communication tools are supporting direct cross-communication among customers, suppliers, and other partners, reconfiguring work based on transparency, connectivity, and sharing. Clients ofer prompt feedback to companies on products and prices, while companies engage customers in a continuous process of value creation. Further, suppliers and partners are digitally engaged and integrated into operations and the product development process, fostering collaborative relationships that result in operational and strategic innovation outcomes.

Today, companies with short-lived competitive advantage need to continuously monitor their customers and innovate products, processes, and business models, drawing new ideas from broad digital ecosystems by accessing flexible skill inventories adaptable to changing needs. Evolving ecosystem governance requires organisations to relinquish some control over their IT resources and capabilities to efectively enable communities and BUs to experiment fast, pursue promising projects, and discard bad ideas.

However, strategic agility depends on how agile the ecosystem partners are (Tallon et al., 2019). The challenge of organising ecosystem governance for agility becomes how to create conditions for the creativity and generativity of external actors without losing control over quality of output and value appropriation while at the same time maintaining an aggressive timeline (Tiwana et al., 2010; Wareham et al., 2014).

The creation of multi-stakeholder, ecosystem IT governance mechanisms holds much promise. Governance ecosystems can provide faster access to complimentary knowledge for improved sensing and responding capabilities where there is a common understanding of shared strategic goals (Jacobides et al., 2018). Ecosystem actors that are “aligned” because they efectively complement one another and mutually depend on each other for survival (Adner, 2017), whose investments cannot be redeployed elsewhere without costs (Jacobides et al.,

2018), and who possess shared understanding, have the potential to reduce delay between sensing and responding (Teece et al., 2016). Ecosystems that foster faster understanding of other perspectives (such as customer needs) also have the potential to generate faster reactions to those needs (Tan et al., 2009).

Our case observations find that relational governance mechanisms are providing stronger sensing and responding capabilities than more traditional formal structural mechanisms. IT-based collaborative, virtual technologies, such as “crowd sourcing”, are facilitating faster, interactive problem identification, and problem-solving capacities among a variety of knowledge domains (e.g., business, IT, customer, suppliers, etc.).

New digital governance envisions future organisations that are able to:

● test various ideas and, through the support of the new digital tools, see what works and what does not in real-time, thus avoiding recurrent errors

● retain knowledge acquired through experiments and have a propensity for self-learning

● collectively balance the facts, the potential future drawbacks, risks, and opportunities in order to make informed IT decisions

● collaborate intensively across organisational units and across organisational boundaries in order to create new forms of value

This observed “permeable boundaries” principle raises several important questions for future research including:

● How do companies transition from their existing IT governance approaches to ecosystem governance? What role does decision-making agility play in this transition?

● How should companies balance the agility benefits of ecosystems with the loss of administrative control?

● How can companies manage ecosystem sensing, decision-making, and responding activities for improving the speed of response rather than delay action?

● What methods or tools can be used to enable decision-making agility in ecosystems?

## 4.4. Principle 4. Incremental financial commitment (vs financial return maximisation)

New digital governance implies that there is enough autonomy at the bottom to develop and test new ideas with locally allocated resources without going through a lengthy, bureaucratic IT approval process. Agile companies use an experimentation approach, looking for the right balance between testing, failing fast, and successful experiments. The goal is to produce more options for diferent challenges, to test and learn from them, and to immediately scale the best digital-based innovations. This iterative process requires continuous verification, adaptation, and decision activities.

At the Bank for example, the portfolio of IT projects and investments has evolved from large-scale, million-dollar projects to small, experimental projects. Top managers are still responsible for approving and managing digital/business innovation investments with large trajectories, but the initiation of digital innovation projects is delegated to local governance structures. The Bank’s internal venture capital funds are invested in team-initiated digital initiatives. Those local teams must also identify project challenges and which projects are potentially transformational. Some other firms, as reported by Kane et al. (2019), reinvest savings from other projects to support new initiatives and consequently increase new, team-based innovation opportunities.

This observed “incremental financial commitment” principle raises several important questions for future research including:

● How do companies measure the success of a fast, incremental investment approach to IT project development vs. a traditional capital investment governance approach?

● How are upper and middle level managers reacting to this re-allocation of IT investment authority?

## 5. Conclusion

In summary, our research makes four main contributions to IT governance and its influence on strategic agility. First, we identified four key assumptions that drive traditional IT governance and discussed how those assumptions limit a firm’s ability to react quickly to the changing competitive environment. Second, based on the extant literature and several case studies, we introduced four new digital governance principles: namely, disciplined autonomy, IS-Business convergence, permeable boundaries, and incremental financial commitment that enable strategic agility. Third, we described how traditional IT governance practices and new digital governance mechanisms can coexist within a single organisation but be applied to diferent IT decision domains. Finally, we envisioned the next frontier of digital governance practices and related future research challenges.

An important research question pertains to reconciliation of traditional and digital governance principles. Since not all IT resources carry an equal risk of obsolescence, companies may adopt a combination of traditional and digital governance mechanisms depending on scope, maturity, and the organisation’s goals (exploration vs. exploitation; Andersen et al., 2017; Xue et al., 2011; Zhen et al., 2021). Additionally, there are relatively stable industries where technological changes and shifts in consumer demand occur at a slower pace. In such industries, companies risk being “trapped” into thinking that their competitive advantage is permanently secure and continue to operate as usual. As a result, these companies underestimate the importance of agility for their future business. Even when changes are less frequent, they are nevertheless likely to take place. Therefore, it is important for all companies to employ governance approaches that discern meaningful signals and spot the signs of industry “tectonic shifts” that might render even the most successful business models obsolete. In the future, companies must adopt digital governance mechanisms that can identify market changes and facilitate the sharing of responses across traditional organisational and sectoral boundaries. The digital governance principles outlined in this paper enable firms to be better prepared for inevitable change.
