# Patient health locus of control: the design of information systems for patient-provider interactions

<sup>a</sup>AmeriPlus Select Services; <sup>b</sup>University of South Florida

## ABSTRACT

Patient locus of control (LOC) is a strong determinant of health outcomes, yet healthcare systems rarely use patient LOC to devise treatment plans. Our clinical research applies action design research methods to design and evaluate healthcare information systems to improve paths and outcomes of patient care. Socio-technical synergies between the interior (technical) and exterior (socio) designs support efective human-computer interfaces and interactions. Rigorous focus group evaluations of the systems for capturing LOC information and using that information in treatment plans are performed and system refinements are implemented. The practitioner organisation has future plans for full implementation of the designed systems.

## 1. Introduction

In clinical settings, evidence suggests that patients sense of control over their situation has a meaningful influence on the outcome of care. Locus of control (LOC) is such a strong determinant of outcomes that two patients with the same condition, acuity, and care plan can experience very diferent results (Norman, 1995). Healthcare organisations and insurers recognise the opportunity to positively influence outcomes but are challenged to implement a systemic approach for integrating patient LOC into treatment plans.

Locus of control is defined as the manner in which an individual frames accountability and manages a challenge, issue, or problem. This locus is further defined as either internal, that is, accepting personal responsibility for achieving a desired outcome, or external, identifying chance or powerful others (e.g., God, a parent, or other authority figure) as primarily responsible for their situation (Rotter, 1966).

The impact of LOC on clinical outcomes has been documented widely, with most studies identifying the improved eficacy of internal versus external LOC (Cvengros et al., 2007; Rajamma & Pelton, 2010; Richard et al., 2011). However, these studies are limited to specific disease categories, patient demographics, and types of healthcare systems. Few prior studies generalise or operationalise their findings to general patient populations. Thus, design research is needed to fully integrate LOC into clinical healthcare systems.

Our research team of experienced healthcare practitioners and academic researchers worked closely with a nationally known healthcare organisation to design and build two key health information systems for assessing patient LOC and for using this information to structure an eficacious treatment plan for patients. The research goals are to identify and manage a patient’s LOC to support improved medical outcomes across a wide range of health conditions.

The paper begins with brief reviews of the literature on patient LOC and socio-technical healthcare information systems design with a focus on interior and exterior modes of humancomputer interaction. We apply action design research (ADR) methods to build the LOC identification and treatment systems. We conclude with findings, implications, and future research directions.

## 2. Locus of control in healthcare information systems

An essential component of all healthcare treatment plans is the individual’s active participation in the course of care. Efective treatment plans are often compromised by a patient’s lack of adherence or compliance with the recommended treatments. Reasons for this vary, including access to resources, financial capacity, and perspective of responsibility, that is, internal or external LOC.

We reviewed the literature on LOC in healthcare treatment plans. Based on this review, the following key requirements for integrating LOC in clinical healthcare information systems are:

● Means for assessing sub-components of LOC (i.e., internal, chance, powerful others; Cohen et al., 1976; Hiroto, 1974; Wallston et al., 1976).

● Means for identifying context-specific factors that interact with LOC (e.g., age, gender, clinical condition; Grafeo & Silvestri, 2006; Lau, 1982; Luszczynska & Schwarzer, 2005; Schneider et al., 2006).

● Measures of key LOC determinants (e.g., health value, peer attitudes, learned helplessness, selfeficacy, socio-economic background, religiosity; Abella & Heslin, 1984; Armitage et al., 2002; Lau, 1982; Lau & Ware, 1981; Masters & Wallston, 2005; Norman, 1995; O’Hea et al., 2005; Saudia et al., 1991; Wallston, 2005; Weiss & Larsen, 1990; Wrightson & Wardle, 1997).

There exists an expansive research literature on healthcare information systems and IT solutions (Fichman et al., 2011; Ho et al., 2019; Tortorella et al., 2020). Current research directions largely focus on the use of cutting-edge technologies such as blockchain, data analytics, and AI in healthcare applications and infrastructures. There are few rigorous studies on design of human-computer interactions between patients and providers to improve the quality of healthcare (Hevner & Wickramasinghe, 2018).

## 3. Design modes of human-computer interactions

Human interaction with IT has become a pervasive element in private, organisational, and societal contexts. In an increasingly digitised and rapidly transforming world, humans constantly need to adjust their daily routines and work practices to the latest technological developments. Interdisciplinary research on human-computer interaction (HCI) focuses on advancing the knowledge base concerning how humans interact with information, technologies, and tasks in socio-technical systems (Sarker et al., 2019).

The design of HCI can focus on the interior mode of an information system technical design and/or on the exterior mode of an information system usage design (Adam et al., 2021). The interior mode focuses on how to construct a HCI artefact for a given problem space. Here, primary HCI artefacts constitute computing interface constructs, models, methods, and their instantiations. In contrast, the exterior mode focuses on how individuals use the artefact in its environment (Simon, 1996). Human interaction constructs, models, methods, and their instantiations constitute primary HCI artefacts in the exterior mode. This framework distinguishes between whether the researcher participates in constructing the artefact “first-hand” (the interior mode) or whether the researcher constructs and studies interaction artefacts to support stakeholders in efectively using the artefacts as deployed in the field (the exterior mode).

## 4. Action design research methods

The author team, in close collaboration with the healthcare organisation, committed considerable time and resources to the study of how best to integrate patient LOC information into treatment plans to improve patient outcomes. The decision to design, build, and intervene with a digital IT-based solution led the research team to employ Action Design Research (ADR) methods (Mullarkey & Hevner, 2019; Sein et al., 2011). A fuller description of the elaborated ADR methods used in this research is provided in Appendix A. The clinical research project covers a Diagnosis Cycle and two Design Cycles in the following three sections. Figure 1 provides an overview of the research methods.

## 5. Diagnosis cycle – problem space

The initial research cycle of diagnosis focuses on a full understanding of the patient interaction processes that address opportunities for efective integration of required LOC patient-provider interactions. Two key socio-technical systems are determined essential.

(1) Identification and assessment of existing patient LOC (internal, external, chance, powerful others) with contextual demographic influences.

(2) Determination of treatment plans to manage patient LOC and clinical interventions to move patients from external to internal locus of control.

The artefact of the diagnosis cycle is the LOC Management Protocol as seen in Figure 2.

The protocol artefact was evaluated via a series of interviews with healthcare professionals, practice personnel, and patients (n = 19) in four physical locations (St. Petersburg FL; Boston MA; Bradenton FL; Newton MA) and three virtual locations (Tampa FL; Jacksonville FL; Washington DC) to confirm the goals, efectiveness, and feasibility of the LOC constructs and their systemic requirements. Qualitative feedback was highly positive with many comments on the importance of LOC and current lack of artefacts for understanding and applying LOC in patient treatment plans.

Results of the evaluation interviews supported clear foundations for the two subsequent design cycles. Particular attention pointed to challenges on the need for well-defined patient-provider interaction processes that address:

Who: What healthcare professional is best positioned to collect the information from the patient and convey the recommended clinical interventions to the patient in order to encourage candour, trust, and objectivity?

When: At what point in the care sequence is patient demographic and attitudinal data collected to optimise clarity and avoid bias? Additionally, at what point in the care sequence are the clinical interventions optimally communicated to the patient?

How: By what means are patient demographic and attitudinal data best collected and clinical interventions best communicated to minimise anxiety and maximise engagement?

## 6. Design cycle 1 – LOC identification and assessment

The first design cycle builds and evaluates the interior instruments and the exterior interaction processes for identifying a patient’s LOC and assessing options for mitigating potential issues when external forms of LOC are found.

## 6.1. Patient LOC identification

We adapt a multidimensional LOC scale (Wallston et al., 1978) as the most widely applied instrument for measuring LOC. This questionnaire measures LOC on three mutually exclusive dimensions of internal, chance, and powerful others (the latter two being external), and further segments powerful others into doctors and other people.

In close collaboration with our practitioner organisation, we modified and produced a new instrument as shown in Figure 3. We included an expanded set of demographic questions in order to better understand the patient’s treatment options.

## 6.4. Focus group evaluation

We performed a rigorous evaluation of the three design artefacts via an IRB-approved focus group (Tremblay et al., 2010). The participants were five licenced nurse care managers and one Ph.D. behavioural scientist. An independent moderator with substantial healthcare experience was recruited to assist with conduct of the focus group.

The focus group protocol consisted of a tenminute review of literature and a set of pre-defined questions addressing the Who, When, and How of patient data collection and risk assessment of the data. The group discussed three vignettes that covered diferent patient scenarios. These vignettes were (a) a myocardial infarction (heart attack) patient, (b) a hip replacement surgery patient, and (c) a Type 2 diabetes patient.

The focus group discussions were recorded and transcribed using Otter.ai software. The transcription was verified for reliability and faithfulness to the discussion by the lead author and the independent moderator. The resulting data were coded in NVivo qualitative analysis software. The details of the coding and data analyses are provided in (Wallace, 2021).

In summary, the focus group provided the following key evaluative insights:

1.The Patient LOC questionnaire was efectively designed to influence patient advocacy, that is, to impact emotional investment by a patient in their health outcomes. Focus group members felt that the questionnaire could influence in a positive direction how patients feel about participating in their course of care with improved belief and trust in the care treatment plan.

2.The Patient LOC questionnaire was adequately designed to influence patient engagement, that is, to impact a patient’s orientation to action. Members felt that the questionnaire could impact participation and engagement, and focus patient interaction.

3.Nurses were better positioned to administer the questionnaire rather than doctors and care managers.

4.Digital presentation was the optimal method for administering the questionnaire.

The focus group reviewed the patient risk assessment instrument and provided feedback on how best to communicate this information for clinical decision making to those responsible for building patient treatment plans. After reviewing the focus group comments, it was determined the next design cycle of patient-provider engagement would require another focus group evaluation to deal more fully with LOC diagnostic and treatment artefacts.

## 7. Design cycle 2 – patient-provider engagement with LOC risks

The second design cycle builds and evaluates the interior instrument and the exterior interaction processes for engaging with the patient in a treatment plan that addresses a patient’s unique LOC risk profile.

Drawing from the patient’s LOC assessment and resulting risk profile, the healthcare organisation will match risks with efective control theories and practice interventions. To illustrate the types of LOC risks, Table 1 provides a sampling of nine potential LOC risks with grounding controls and mitigating interventions.

## 7.1. Patient LOC treatment artefact

The interior design of the patient LOC treatment artefact supports a parsimonious communication of a patient’s risk profile to the providers responsible for formulating a treatment plan. Working closely with the sponsoring healthcare organisation, we designed a concise presentation of the patient’s treatment options based on their unique risk profile as seen in Figure 6. The organisation identified seven key actionable risk measures that are highlighted as to severity (Red = Strong; Yellow = Moderate; Green = Low). Initial actions are indicated for the provider to consider in formulating the patient’s treatment plan.

The structure and content of the engagement artefact were informed by the previous diagnosis and design cycles and co-design activities with the organisational practitioners. From the insights gained in the first focus group, it was determined that clinical intervention may be inefective for certain conditions; these conditions were generally identified as circumstances in which patient engagement has negligible impact on the treatment plan. Specifically, these were noted as conditions primarily treatable by drug regimens for which medication adherence is the sole treatment plan.

On deeper examination, we established that LOC is clearly a factor in medication adherence, especially where patient health value and self-eficacy were concerned. This suggested more broadly that the presentation of clinical interventions should be ordered by (a) substantive actions and (b) palliative actions. Thus, the clinical intervention tool was organised to identify substantive actions, namely, those that contribute to patient health value and self-eficacy, and palliative actions that contribute to the patient’s sense of wellbeing.

Scepticism was expressed by the first focus group that clinical interventions could be broadly prescribed. A recurring suggestion was that “there are just too many factors” to consider in an individual case to elevate the clinical recommendations to the prescriptive level of a care treatment plan. Therefore, like the indications and contraindications of medication use, it was determined that a proviso in the clinical intervention tool identifying the actions as items of consideration would be helpful in positioning them correctly with the healthcare provider in the overall care treatment plan.

The issue of mass customisation is challenging for all businesses, and none more so than healthcare. Resources difer greatly between healthcare markets and vary materially within markets, as healthcare professionals develop practice and referral patterns unique to their manner of care delivery. For this reason, it was determined that the clinical intervention artefact should be customised in such a way as to allow the provider to refer to a preferred resource or consult a default resource for the user (e.g., a health insurer). This was determined to be incorporated as a customisable option of the clinical intervention artefact.

## 7.3. Focus group evaluation

The question of how best to convey the LOC patient intervention options into treatment plan decisions was explored initially with healthcare professionals in interviews during the Diagnosis cycle. Key challenges arose concerning the possible resistance of physicians to include LOC interventions into a patient treatment plan. Another significant issue was the acceptance and adherence to the LOC interventions by the patient. The identification of these challenges early in the project informed the evaluation activities of this design cycle. In addition, insights gained from the first focus group refined the evaluation goals to address more pointedly reinforcement of patient engagement through customised intervention.

The objectives of the second design evaluation focus group shifted to confirming the format, medium, and delivery mechanism for the Clinical Engagement artefact. Similar to the first iteration, the following questions were addressed:

Who: What healthcare professional is best positioned to discuss the recommended actions to optimise patient engagement? Possible candidates were again identified as care managers, nurses, physician assistants, and physicians.

When: At what point in the care sequence are the clinical actions best presented to optimise patient engagement? Possible scenarios were identified as pretreatment or admission, discharge, and postdischarge.

How: How much guidance should the providers exercise to best communicate the recommended actions? Should the actions be required and set up for the patient, ofered for referral, or simply discussed?

Consistent with the study protocol, the same research participants from the first focus group were engaged for the second focus group. Potential bias related to the functional focus of the participants was considered (all were nurse care managers with one Ph.D. behavioural scientist; no physicians participated), however, no material conflict was identified in the analysis of results from the first focus group.

The focus group protocol consisted of a ten-minute review of the first focus group findings and six questions addressing the who, when, and how of presenting the clinical interventions. Three diferent vignettes were discussed to study variance of the recommended actions. The vignettes were (a) a hip replacement surgery patient, (b) a myocardial infarction (heart attack) patient, and (c) a thyroid disease patient being treated via an intensive drug regimen.

As in the first focus group, discussions were recorded and transcribed using Otter.ai software. The transcription was verified for reliability and faithfulness to the discussion by the lead author and the independent moderator. The resulting data were coded in NVivo qualitative analysis software. The details of the coding and data analyses are provided in (Wallace, 2021)

The focus group discussed the interior Patient Engagement artefact and the exterior Patient-Provider Interaction artefact. The group identified several critical insights:

1. The instrument for patient engagement was adequately designed to be used by providers to influence patient receptivity to change. Participants felt that the instrument could be used to improve how a patient’s need or desire to participate in their course of care would impact their willingness to perform the recommended actions.

2. Focus group members felt strongly that the instrument could be used by providers to improve the efectiveness of patient involvement by providing information that facilitated defining and communicating the treatment plan. Further, it was felt that the instrument could be used to enhance motivation, willingness, belief, trust, and emotional investment of patients in the treatment plan and would be most successful if family and other support elements were made aware of the actions presented.

3. Focus group members suggested that verbal delivery of the recommended actions was essential to clinical success, with direct conversation and face-toface reviews the best methods. It was also suggested that printed material to review and provide to the patient was material to its success.

4. Participants felt that doctors would be most efective in presenting the LOC actions as part of the clinical treatment plan. Participants also confirmed that immediately preceding or subsequent to discharge was the best time to present the LOC actions, and that integrated implementation was also necessary, with optimal utility arising from all members of the care management team being apprised of the recommended clinical actions.

5. The focus group members emphasised the importance of education and training of providers on LOC risks and mitigating actions. Clear explanations of the recommended actions were identified as areas requiring further research, and the medium of recommended actions was also reconfirmed as digital presentation.

6. With regard to the clinical engagement artefact, feedback from the focus group clarified and confirmed the presentation and order of items on the form. In initial consideration of the tool, items were in order of the actions associated with the respective influence factors measured on the Patient LOC Questionnaire. Further, in the initial instantiation, there was no information conveyed to the treating physician about the weight or significance of the associated clinical action. After reviewing the focus group comments, it was determined that presenting the information in a stoplight format, corresponding to the level of impact of the associated action, and ordering the questions in decreasing level of impact was optimally suited to accommodate the information burden and medical justification of the treating physician.

7. Similarly, feedback from the focus group helped determine when the Patient Engagement instrument should be administered. In the initial instantiation of the clinical intervention tool, it was assumed that the recommended actions provided to the patient at the time of diagnosis would sufice. On review of focus group feedback, however, it was determined that communication from the respective medical professionals would necessarily be delayed for emergent conditions.

Thus, communication of the clinical treatment plan was determined to occur at the first available time after the later of diagnosis or discharge for the underlying medical condition.

## 8. Discussion and implications

The importance and utility of patient LOC for improving healthcare outcomes are well documented in prior research. However, like diagnosing an illness without prescribing a cure, knowing a patient’s LOC is only part of the healthcare challenge. Identifying and employing methods that influence LOC for improved clinical eficacy is of greater value. The incorporation of LOC in patient assessment, clinical decision making, and the development of patient treatment plans are critical design questions that have not been widely studied in existing healthcare research. Via three eADR cycles, we built and evaluated two healthcare information systems for the assessment of patient LOC and the subsequent patient-provider engagement to develop efective treatment plans to mitigate LOC challenges.

For practice implications, the LOC Management Protocol in Figure 2 is an innovative approach to leverage our design contributions into actionable guidance for healthcare practitioners. First, the healthcare organisation must identify a patient’s baseline LOC. Use of a comprehensive patient LOC Questionnaire (Figure 3) along with demographic information from the electronic medical record can be collected via technology-enabled web and mobile devices. Case managers and nurses are well positioned to identify patient circumstances and influences that can augment the baseline LOC measures. Second, the organisation must assess the patient LOC information by building a detailed risk assessment profile for each patient. The profile is calculated using a novel survey tool and presented to the physician electronically to support decisions on specific clinical actions for possible inclusion in the patient treatment plan. Third, the desired goal is to better engage the patient in understanding and commitment to a treatment plan. Specific activities in the plan will encourage the patient to move from external to internal LOC to support better health outcomes.

The novel design of human-computer interactions and interfaces in the two information systems of LOC assessment and engagement provided a clear structure to the research project. Further, the distinction between the interior artefacts (interface instruments) and the exterior artefacts (interaction processes) supported relevant and rigorous research activities leading to important contributions to both healthcare practices and research. Research implications include:

(1) Patient LOC manifests across a continuum from internal orientation to external orientation. The external orientation is further divided into chance – holding fate, genetics, or factors beyond their control responsible or powerful others – holding God, the physician, parents, or anonymous others responsible.

(2) Four categories of factors shape patient LOC and impact clinical outcomes. These are: demographic factors such as gender, race, education, or income; personal factors such as health value, self-eficacy, religiosity, or behavioural health; environmental factors such as condition severity and progression or procedural complexity and intensity; and societal factors such as availability of social support.

(3) A number of factors associated with LOC can be leveraged to address patient engagement. Chief among these are cognitive dissonance, receptivity to treatment, expectation of health outcomes, and adjustment to illness or recovery. Identified factors can be influenced to modify the efects of patient LOC, thus improving health outcomes.

(4) Our study revealed a substantial gap in practitioner knowledge with regard to patient LOC. The consensus view that virtually no time or resources are invested in the understanding of patient LOC in practitioner education is astounding given its relative impact. This insight reinforces the need for further eforts to expand practitioner education on this important subject.

## 9. Contributions, limitations and future directions

Existing healthcare information systems research largely studies the use of emerging technologies (e.g., blockchain, AI) to improve the efectiveness and eficiencies of medical decisions and infrastructure. Solutions that address the social issues of human interactions with healthcare information systems are understudied in research and underserved in practice. To remedy this gap, our clinical research on patient LOC and patient-provider interactions provides the following novel contributions to the field.

(1) We design and evaluate two novel healthcare information systems for patient LOC assessment and patient-provider engagement to support improved treatment outcomes.

(2) Contributions are made on both the interior mode of IT interface design and on the exterior mode of IT interaction design. The synergies in each of the two systems between the interior and exterior design modes provide a novel IS research contribution.

(3) The clinical research reported in this paper provides a rigorous and novel application of ADR methods in a healthcare information system context.

(4) Two rigorous Focus Group evaluations of the interior and exterior artefacts were performed (Tremblay et al., 2010). The application of focus group evaluation methods to healthcare information systems practice and research is rarely reported in the literature.

(5) Based on the results of this study, the participating healthcare organisation is committed to the next steps of implementation of proposed system designs.

Two key research limitations are noted. First, we performed this research with a single large healthcare organisation. The practitioners and focus group participants are employees of this organisation. While these individuals are experienced and expert healthcare providers, they reflect a single organisational culture which may not be reflected in other healthcare organisations.

A second limitation is the use of the same participants in both evaluation focus groups. We felt that the continuity of expertise and knowledge across the two groups provided needed understanding of how the two information systems worked together. However, the selection of only six focus group members from a single organisation is a limitation (Carlsen & Glenton, 2011). Five of the six focus group participants were nurse care managers. While nurses are critical members of the integrated care team, they are one of at least three categories of team members, care managers, nurses, and physicians, who directly interact with the patient in the care treatment plan.

Further research is required in the area of presentation formats for the instrument (interior) artefacts and interaction (exterior) artefacts to optimise the efectiveness of the information presented to both the patients and the healthcare providers. Additional cycles of artefact designs and evaluations will create and test improved means of assessing and engaging patient LOC criteria.

Improved LOC education and training for both patients and providers is essential for organisational success. As part of this research direction, due consideration should be given to the information burden already placed on clinicians and care providers (especially physicians), and appropriate changes to the LOC artefact designs must strike the appropriate balance between concise and convincing information and intervention justification and priority. This will require further research in the best methods of presenting and communicating LOC research in the various medical curricula and continuing medical education.

As a clinical support tool, the LOC Management Protocol and associated components are only as good as their accepted use by the provider and patient. Recognising segmentation of prior LOC research by demographic, severity, and other dimensions, a comprehensive human subject study should be conducted including both surgical and non-surgical interventions, with varying degrees of clinical and procedural intensity.

Future implementations will require a Phase I FDA clinical trial of the protocol as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) with necessary biomedical IRB approvals. Reimbursement for clinical applications like the LOC Management Protocol vary based on the type of healthcare information system, health plan, and payer. Additional research should be conducted on variation of efectiveness by patient age, clinical severity, and procedural intensity, as well as interaction with simultaneous treatments, contraindications, and other relevant factors. Such research will serve as the basis for regulatory approval, training, and recommended use of the LOC Management Protocol as a Class I, II, or III software medical device.
