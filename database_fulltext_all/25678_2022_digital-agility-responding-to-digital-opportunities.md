# Digital agility: responding to digital opportunities

Varun Grover

Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Little Rock, Arkansas, USA

## ABSTRACT

In a rapidly changing digital environment, companies must sense weak signals and respond to them. This essay focuses on how agile companies can be more responsive in a pervasively digital context. To build our argument, we discuss the ontological diference of digital (vs. IT) and then describe four tenets that characterise agile organisations: modular design and packaged capabilities, use of platforms over pipelines, ability to foster concurrency and agency through data, and a digital culture that promotes ambidexterity

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 14 April 2022 Accepted 22 June 2022

KEYWORDS Agility; digital agility; digita ontology; digital response; digital environment; agile company; ambidexterity; weak signals

## 1. Introduction

The description of agility as the ability to sense and respond (Roberts & Grover, 2012) has gained traction in the literature. Environmental sensing requires the ability to both recognise potentially useful signals and give them credence. In today’s dynamic digital environment, the challenge is to make sense of weak signals, especially given the conundrum of agility – people who sense a weak signal don’t get the big picture of what the signals mean, and people at the higher level who do get the big picture are unable to sense weak signals (Pinsonneault & Choi, 2022). Companies with good sensing abilities can draw information from proximal and distal social networks, be alert to data streams, and can analytically recognise important patterns in the data that have actionable implications. However, agility requires the ability to respond efectively to these signals once their importance has been recognised. In this article, we address the respond part of sense and respond and describe how organisations should address this.

Our framing is set in a pervasive digital environment (DE), which we characterise as qualitatively different from a traditional information technology (IT) supported environment. Organization’s that have evolved or are evolving into a DE environment either as digital natives or that are transitioning towards DE can exhibit far more pliability in their responsiveness to signals. To build our argument, we first describe agility followed by a discussion of the ontological diference of digital (vs. IT). This sets the stage for digital agility by describing four tenets that characterise agile organisations: their modular design and packaged capabilities, use of platforms over pipelines, ability to foster concurrency and agency through data, and digital culture that promotes ambidexterity.

## 2. Agility: responding after sensing

Earlier approaches to orchestrating responses to environmental change are heavily vested in the concept of “capabilities” where IT investments are complemented with organisational resources (IT-enabled resources) to create emergent capabilities (Kohli & Grover, 2008; Melville et al., 2004). Models of IT value relied on these emergent capabilities as mediators between IT and value (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). The case for efective organisational responses is based on the “logic of positioning” and the “logic of leverage”. The former emphasises bargaining power in the firm’s extended network of buyers and suppliers, while the latter emphasises the inimitability of the IT-based initiative by competitors. Most responses rely on coordination and execution of operational processes (Dove, 2002).

More recent approaches have moved from a static to a dynamic view of rapid responses in a hypercompetitive environment, where windows of opportunity (product-market gaps) are narrow and ephemeral (McGrath, 2013). Using “dynamic capabilities” as the theoretical lens (Teece, 2007), the focus is on building, leveraging, and reconfiguring capabilities to facilitate responsiveness and competitive advantage. Correspondingly, some recent work has used the “options” lens. This lens recognises that many IT assets could be latent positioning investments that secure the option for additional investments to be realised when the timing is right. Under the dynamic view, responses often rely on a flexible IT infrastructure (Roberts & Grover, 2012; Tafti et al., 2013). So, while earlier studies focused on building IT-based capabilities, later studies placed more emphasis on dynamic capabilities and digital options (Sambamurthy et al., 2003).

From an IT business value perspective, response models are largely initiated with IT investments which in turn build required capabilities which result in business value (Kohli & Grover, 2008). The objective is to align IT with organisational resources and strategy in order to realise value. With the increasing malleability, pervasiveness, and openness of IT, we argue that the script is being flipped. Rather than “IT -> capabilities -> value”, it is increasingly possible to envision solutions to market opportunities and then determine the business capabilities required to realise it (Kohli & Grover, 2008). IT resources can then be used to build these required capabilities (i.e., how to IT-ize or digitalise these capabilities). So, a prototypical response sequence would be initiated by a business vision, where “vision-> required business capabilities -> configuration of IT and other resources -> value”. This vision is particularly important in a pervasively digital environment where the constraints on feasibility are largely bounded only by imagination.

## 3. The ontological digital diference

The term “digital” is now being routinely used to refer to digital-x, where x could be terms that were associated with IT – like alignment, strategy, innovation. However, is the substitution of the term IT for the term digital merely an issue of nomenclature – or is there a substantive ontological diference? We claim it is the latter, and while digitisation, the conversion to binary form, has been around since the first digital computer, value is no longer limited by what takes place “in a box”. We are largely changing from a box world to one where digits freely flow on our networks. Boxes of hardware and software, named DSS, ERP, MIS – are moving to the background, while the flow of digits (bitstrings) is in the foreground. Faulkner and Runde (2019) recognise this and define digital objects as objects of bitstrings that can be combined with other material and nonmaterial components. These digital objects could be software and data files and could just as well be a hybrid object, such as an Echo device or an iPhone. Piccoli et al. (forthcoming) describe a subset of digital objects as digital resources if they satisfy three conditions: they (1) encapsulate something of value (e.g., a digital service like the processing of payments), (2) are modular in that they can be combined with other resources, and (3) can be accessed via a programmatic interface. Teubner and Stockhinger (2020) indicate that digital is a new concept because it homogenises the digital representation of diferent types of information which removes the tight coupling between information representation and the devices required for processing, storing, and transmitting it. We see this as “liquification” of the box as the digits can flow and gain salience as needed (on demand) on a variety of devices. Bygstad (2017) refers to a vertical liquification as a modular, layered architecture that is hierarchically organised in homogenous technical layers and, vertically, in functional modules. Defined interfaces then allow for quickly adding or replacing components, as well as for (re-)combining them. A modular, layered architecture also paves the way for what is called a “lightweight infrastructure”, which embraces the flexible integration of mobile applications, end-user devices, and cloud-services. Mikalef et al. (2021) also indicates that moving towards modular forms increases the possible combinations of outcomes, while simultaneously enhancing flexibility in adaptations.

Our concepts and practices of agility need to evolve. Agile companies are ones that can best create digital resources and/or orchestrate them by leveraging digital infrastructures (i.e., the Internet). Creating digital resources requires digitising unique processes, products or services and selling them through a platform. Orchestrating simply implies integrating other external and internal digital resources to generate and expand the company’s value proposition. Weight Watchers provides an instructive example. They have codified their proprietary processes into an application which is distributed on a platform that allows users to participate in Weight Watcher programs and to track their food and weight. The company can also orchestrate other digital resources (e.g., exercise apps) and use their digital interface to expand their value proposition for customers. So, agile organisations are those that can create the right development environment and governance structures to manage what Gartner would call “packaged business capabilities” as building blocks for novel customer experiences.

## 4. Key tenets of digital agility

Below, we describe four key tenets of (digital) agility. Based on the literature and observations of several digital enterprises, it is our contention that efective agility requires companies to both nurture and leverage these four precepts.

## 4.1. Agile companies use modular designs and can package capabilities

To be agile, companies need to be able to follow a modular approach in developing capabilities – so they can quickly combine these modular capabilities to create new value propositions and satisfy a need sensed or respond to a sensed opportunity. This requires the ability to “build” and “configure” capabilities. In a digital world, software can encapsulate a capability and applicationprogram-interfaces (APIs) can facilitate quick configuration of both internal and external capabilities. Agile companies encapsulate key capabilities into bundles or stacks that can be creatively combined with other capabilities to enhance the value delivered. Weight Watchers, for instance, has taken a proprietary process and created a software product that can be integrated with other capabilities (software services) through the API. So, the expertise needed in a company is a combination of application developers and business experts that can work in teams on these configurations. Less agile companies that rely on carefully honed processes (e.g., insurance claims processing) will have a harder time adapting to a changing market. This is because tightly coupled processes are far less adaptable than loosely coupled capabilities, especially if the latter are encapsulated as modular digital products.

Fortunately, contemporary technological products greatly facilitate API based software development. Krancher et al. (2018) describe Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS) and how its rapid elasticity and abstraction (in the cloud) can facilitate agility by creating afordances like continuous feedback and self-organising for software development. Model-based PaaS services, such as Force.com and Mendix, provide cloud-based application environments that allow software by configuration, without necessarily writing code. At Amazon, for instance, engineers launching a new product ofering are less involved in coordinating across departments and more with something resembling the assembly of Lego bricks; they mix and match capabilities for new oferings that fit the changing marketplace (Greeven et al., 2021). Many modular digital capabilities (e.g., payment services) are available in the marketplace and can be integrated into a valuable ofering. Similarly, in many cases, the modular capability developed, can be made accessible (through an API) at a price to other companies that are configuring solutions to their problems or opportunities. Therefore, agile companies need to architect (package) the inbound and outbound flow of digital capabilities.

## 4.2. Agile companies use platforms, not pipelines

Platforms provide the ability to respond to opportunities expediently. They do this by providing integrated systems from commoditized IT components that can quickly scale and adapt to meet a diverse range of business needs (Richardson et al., 2014; Tiwana et al., 2010). They can reduce the cost of application sharing by allowing IT architects to create new ways for component modules to work together. Thet can also enable distributed innovation to occur in a digital ecosystem. Ecosystems can encompass individuals, corporations and communities that are individually autonomous but connected through an underlying technical system.

In a (traditional) pipeline model, products are created, distributed, and serviced. Such a model requires companies to develop good processes that are then improved, or even reengineered, over time. The value is largely based on the quality and quantity of these processes and their outcomes. However, the coupling and dependencies in the pipeline model limit its flexibility. Platform models, in contrast, focus on exchanges between participants (complementors), and the platform owner nurtures the quantity and quality of these exchanges. So, the platform model allows for innovation through participation as well as enhancing the value of the interaction (e.g., Facebook’s information sharing services or LinkedIn’s recruiting interactions). Unlike the pipeline model where processes lead to outcomes, processes and outcomes in a platform model are shaped, and are being shaped, by each other.

Having a platform model does not necessarily mean that all companies should be platform owners. However, every company should have a platform strategy – which could include ownership and use of a digital platform to distribute products and services but could also include participation in other platforms based on their encapsulated digital capabilites. As a platform owner, the challenge is to keep all participants involved in their value proposition, while delivering a unified face to the customer. Salesforce is a platform owner that has a core digital product that can be enhanced through complementors participating on the platform. The challenge for Salesforce is to efectively integrate their complementors’ products with the core product, minimise competition between their core product and complementors, and provide a unified and comprehensible face to their customers (Staub et al., 2021). The company can be agile in modifying its product through the orchestration of capabil ities. LinkedIn set up their platform initially as a professional social media site. In 2011 they added the “apply with LinkedIn” button as a plugin to facilitate participants’ packaging of their job interests that allowed companies to find them. By adding this “value” layer to the interactions, today LinkedIn is one of the largest recruiting sites in the world.

The value lies not in the capability itself but in the resource reconfigurations they facilitate – structure, communication and skills – each with ability and repeatability. El Sawy et al. (2010) call this the “holistic confluence among environmental turbulence, dynamic capabilities, and IT systems – and fused interactions unfolding as an ecosystem”(p835). The loose coupling ofered by platform models allows these resource configurations to occur rapidly. Karimi-Alaghehband and Rivard (2019) discuss IT as becoming a “platform for agility”. Tan et al. (2019) describe agility as coming from managing interdependencies on platforms, and Li and Chan (2019) describe a dynamic digital platform capability as a way to provide novel value creation paths and access to external resources and capabilities. Agile companies use platforms as the vehicle to orchestrate a solution to a problem – and modularise their oferings to participate in other platforms (i.e., the recursive nature of platform competition).

## 4.3. Agile companies foster concurrency and eficiency through data

The bridge between sensing and responding in agile firms often lies with data. Sensing signals tend to rely on user-generated data and social media. The extent to which companies can create concurrency and eficiency between sending and responding, the more efective they will be in their agility. Concurrency represents the idea that there is data in the “sensing” that can provide guidance for “responding” and so the linearity between the two can be minimised and their relationship be more iterative. Customers can not only identify problems and opportunities but can also be a source of solutions. Eficiency indicates how quickly the response can be automated or build into the company’s oferings. The key to both concurrency and eficiency is data – and while much has been written about the value of big data and analytics, its role in fostering agility has been understated. If data can be viewed as an afordance that ofers potential for action (Krancher et al., 2018), then data analytics can influence agility – through various value mechanisms (transparency, prediction, experimentation, optimisation, customisation, crowdsourcing) that can target processes, decisions, products/services, and customer experiences (Grover et al., 2018).

Agile companies need to set up mechanisms to foster both concurrency and eficiency in a digital environment. By analysing online consumer product reviews and consumer discussion forums, a firm can identify frequently reported product issues as well as consumer desired features which, in turn, provide insights for product and service innovation. Mechanisms could involve innovation (crowdsourcing) platforms, where customers can engage early in the development cycle as co-creators of products and services (Roberts & Grover, 2012). The advantage of digital platforms is that responses can even be calibrated through digital experiments (e.g., a ride-sharing company examining demand changes based on surge pricing), which engenders confidence in causality and, consequently, the efectiveness of the response. Similarly, if analytics can be used to predict customers’ responses to customisation, the customisation itself can be rapidly deployed based on the data. Alternatively, if an IoT based product generates data for failure prediction, can an agile company initiate pre-emptive action or better still, can this concurrency in sensing and responding itself be automated? Tallon et al. (2019) indicate that real-time streaming data can be valuable if companies are setup to act upon what they see. Airlines need to be agile on pricing and their reservation eco-systems are well suited to have dynamic price changes ripple through the system concurrently and eficiently. Walmart also uses various mechanisms to foster concurrency and eficiency through data. The company has the world’s largest private cloud and analytics hub known as Data Café, which pulls information from 200 diverse sources. Walmart’s analytic algorithms are designed to scan through the data in microseconds and to come up with a real-time solution for a particular problem. It is also pre-emptively agile as it uses a ML-based predictive analytics platform for target marketing (Grover et al., 2018). Agile companies set up mechanisms that use data concurrently (with sensing) and eficiently (through automation).

## 4.4. Agile companies have a digital culture that promotes ambidexterity

One of the biggest challenges for companies, particularly traditional ones, is to change to a digital mindset. Packaged digital capabilities, platform orchestration, and the efective use of data will only work in a company if it can embrace a value system or a culture that fosters innovation on all these fronts. While many definitions of organisational culture have been ofered, at the centre is the idea of shared values across the members of an organisation (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). The term “digital culture” has been described as a distinct type of culture that reflects this mindset. Tech giants, such as Google, Meta, Apple, etc., have shown that firms that embrace digital culture support risk-taking, foster innovation, and encourage the development of collaborative work environments (Kane et al., 2018). The value and norms in a digital culture guide how humans interact, and they also guide the general belief that “digital” can be configured to foster agile responses and create new visions. Westerman et al. (2019) describe key elements of a digital culture as one that reflects “impact” (constant innovation), “speed” (move fast and iterate), “openness” (engage with others and share rather than keeping things proprietary), and “autonomy” (allow people discretion). This leads to behaviours like experimentation, fluid collaboration, and data driven decisions. Pu et al. (2022) describe digital culture as having four archetype companies, with most representing a blend of the four: digital adhocracy (flexibility and innovation), digital clan (scalability, collaboration), digital market (data, experimentation), and digital hierarchy (security, precision).

To address agility, two aspects of digital culture are noteworthy. First, while agile companies must be able to pivot in response to sensed information, they also must be able to fight the social structures (and people)

that have been in place, possibly for many years. Most company failures can be attributed to the victory of these rigidities over agility. Responding requires both leveraging of capabilities and mitigation of rigidities. This has been referred to as ambidexterity (Chan et al., 2019). For example, Lucas and Goh (2009) describe Kodak’s failure in digital photography as directly attributable to their inability to manage their middle management rigidity<sup>1</sup>. Henderson (2021) argues that years of research indicate that ambidextrous firms combine the ability to manage conventional operationally focused organisations with dynamic fast moving ones, rooted in a shared vision. Teubner and Stockhinger (2020) also suggest that IT ambidexterity should be seen as a capability that enables companies to meet competing demands of exploration (focus on new opportunities) and exploitation (while building on existing systems). This requires smart deployment where agile teams can create “minimally viable solutions” rather than end products that can be launched and enhanced over time. In a similar vein, Queiroz et al. (2018) states that agility depends not only on the ability of firms to quickly build or buy IT applications but also on their ability to act rapidly to discontinue inflexible applications that are creating rigidities. They describe IT application orchestration as a key mechanism through which firms enable IT-based responses to market changes (e.g., by adding new applications to the IT portfolio) and eliminate barriers to agility (e.g., by divesting legacy applications).

Second, digital culture should also be reflected in the organisation’s value proposition. Agile companies should be willing to transform themselves. This does not suggest that every sense and response sequence is necessarily transformational but the willingness is a cultural attribute that fosters a constant search for innovative solutions. Wessel et al. (2021) suggest that the diference between digital transformation compared with IT-enabled organisational transformation is that the former involves a new organisational identity while the latter enhances an existing organisational identity. Therefore, agile companies must have culture that embraces the value of digital in fostering change.

## 5. Responding in a digital world

Teece (2007)’s visionary work recognising dynamic capabilities as the ability to sense, seize and reconfigure tangible and intangible assets, is manifestly being realised in the digital environment. This is reflected in organisational (digital) agility which, we argue, has four major tenets:

(1) Packaging Digital Capabilities

(2) Orchestrating Platforms/Ecosystems

(3) Using Data for Concurrency and Eficiency

(4) Fostering a Digital Culture

Figure 1 schematically illustrates the representation of digital agility. The key ideas here are concepts of building digital capabilities and using them as part of many value-creation paths on a digital platform with data and analytics-driven feedback loops, all evolving in a culture that fosters digital innovation. In responding, the company can envision a solution based on a sensed problem, or the customer can drive the solution. For instance, Uber ofers value through the vision of a platform that integrates a problem (need for transport) and a solution (excess capacity in private vehicles). Similarly, a company’s website (platform) or app are instantiations of their digital capabilities and ofer vehicles for delivering value to customers. A customer can receive products and services “on demand” by activating other digital objects through these instantiations (e.g., adding purchases to a shopping card, paying by credit card, seeing a bank statement on an app, printing a concert ticket, watching a video file, etc.). Customers provide copious data based on the choices they make in interacting with the company. Agile companies are those that can respond to signals (e.g., their customer’s changing value proposition) and deliver value eficiently (even automatically) and efectively through their infrastructure of digital objects. In many cases, the “response” may itself involve the creation of a new value proposition.

The value creation process can be activated when needed by the customer. The opportunities to build products and services using digital layering of objects will increase. Just as we see application mashups today (e.g., Google Maps mashed with a Restaurant app to give location data), in the digital world we will see a proliferation in the possibilities for mixing digital objects. For instance, services could be built with a baseline set of services (e.g., a website listing product to sell) but then be layered with a “socialization” layer that provides social connections (e.g., community shopping) and layered with a gaming layer that allows competition (e.g., who buys more for a grand prize). This combinatorial digital layering provides a host of multiplicative possibilities for adding layers of value.

## 6. Concluding comments

Digital agility requires organisations to have a “digital first” orientation. Metaphorically this indicates a change from “IT first” where boxes of IT hardware and software which are traditionally in the foreground with the digital bitstreams in the background now give primacy to digital objects while the IT moves to the background. The ongoing COVID-19 pandemic is clearly an accelerant for the switch to digital first, as companies enhance their digital infrastructure and development environment, increasing design flexibility and scalability as they rapidly move to cloud operations (Greeven et al., 2021). Advancing all four tenets described in this article is necessary for agility. However, we recognise the challenge, particularly for non-digitally native companies, when setting up the digital objects/resources, platforms, data, and the linking mechanisms for agility.(Ting L., & Chan,Y. 2019) We also recognise the arguably bigger challenge of getting the culture right. Moreover, in the spirit of dynamic capabilities, constant re-evaluation and revisioning of these tenets and the value pathways is the new normal in this exciting digital world.
