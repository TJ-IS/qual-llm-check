# Conceptualising and defining DevOps: a review for understanding, not a framework for practitioners

Aymeric Hemon-Hildgen <sup>a</sup> and Frantz Rowe <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>ESSCA School of Management, LEMNA-University of Nantes; <sup>b</sup>LEMNA-University of Nantes

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 14 April 2022; Accepted 22 June 2022

KEYWORDS DevOps; systematic literature review; critical review; realist review; qualitative research

## 1. Introduction

In their abstract, Gall and Pigni (2022) claim that the information systems and software engineering literature “fails to convey what DevOps is”, and that “the lack of homogeneous and clear conceptualization of DevOps is considered a major obstacle to the difusion of this methodology”.

We strongly disagree with the first statement, and we also feel that the call for a homogeneous and clear conceptualisation of DevOps may be too demanding given the industrial stance of the paper. It does not mean that relevant definitions do not exist, nor does it mean that DevOps constitutes a methodology. Second, we also strongly disagree with the claim that their review is a critical review. It seems that the authors perform a review for understanding (Rowe, 2014), or more precisely for characterising DevOps, but they don’t do this in an especially critical way. They also use other qualifiers for their review: systematic and realist. We also have some reservations about these. Third, the authors believe that their review and the resulting framework will provide guidance and help organisations transition to DevOps. We raise some doubts about this objective, about the orientation of the review and about the framework concerning practitioners. The authors may find our critique unfair (Rowe & Markus, 2018). Therefore, we start this response paper by recognising several merits of the Gall & Pigni paper, including the identification of most of their main research paths, and ofering a few complementary ideas.

## 2. What is the merit of this paper and what to do next?

The merit of the authors’ work relates mainly to the inventory of the conceptual elements for understanding DevOps that are studied in the academic literature. In addition, they propose definitions of the three dimensions they retain for DevOps that they qualify as continuous: continuous culture, continuous automation and continuous monitoring. Their framework stimulates debate on continuity. They present avenues for future research which we share and complete, notably insisting on considering the environment.

## 2.1. Continuity

We agree with the authors on the proposed definitions, but we difer from their interpretation of the principle of continuity, which constitutes a lateral dimension in their model. The principle of continuity is reflected in each of the dimensions; therefore, it should not be “lateral” but central to the model and integrated into each of the dimensions. DevOps has been designed to ensure continuity of flow, notably with continuous delivery (Humble & Farley, 2010) and continuous deployment (Kim et al., 2016). Fitzgerald and Stol (2017) ofer a holistic view of this principle of continuity in software engineering. The principle of continuity is consubstantial, intrinsic, and therefore central to DevOps.

## 2.2. Future avenues of research

In the research agenda section, the authors propose six main research paths. We find them relevant and nuance them. To enrich their framework and explore relationships between identified elements we propose to extend the authors’ proposals by not limiting the analysis of roles and relationships between elements of the framework but also within the three dimensions and with the environment. The authors invite researchers to create a taxonomy of the diferent DevOps tools, practices, and methods. We caution that, like the wealth of definitions, the abundance of taxonomies may make DevOps adoption more confusing. Finally, because of the speed of technological changes, of the accelerated digitisation of our society, the continuity principle could be examined from a new perspective and with new parameters.

The authors’ framework presents an inspiring source of questioning. However, many research questions derive from the contextualisation of DevOps, the understanding of its environment, and the relationships between its constituent elements, which are not present in their framework.

Several studies show how challenging the environment for the DevOps approach could be (Ebert et al., 2016; Riungu-Kalliosaari et al., 2016). This dynamic relationship can be impacted by many frictions (Davis & Daniels, 2016) within a DevOps team as well as outside it: with the immediate environment, within the organisation itself, with external stakeholders, e.g., customers, end users, service providers, subcontractors, external regulations, etc. Considering more numerous environmental constraints than agile teams, DevOps teams exhibit greater sensitivity to contradictions (Hemon-Hildgen et al., 2020). Notably, the analysis of the technological environment makes it possible to appreciate how a constituent element of DevOps could become counterproductive if poorly managed. For instance, a higher level of automation will modify risk perception by DevOps teams (Hemon-Hildgen et al., 2020).

Other environmental dimensions, i.e., human, political, legal, can impact the spread and proper adoption of DevOps. This is particularly true in the case of highly regulated software development environments. Laukkarinen et al. (2017) clearly show how certain standards are obstacles for continuous delivery and integration. To overcome this dificulty, the use of the framework proposed by the authors will not be suficient. It will be necessary to understand and appreciate how the elements and dimensions of the framework interact, especially with the environment. How can DevOps be orchestrated in a highly regulated environment? If hyper-regulated, it will be necessary to adapt some DevOps practices or create new ones (Morales et al., 2018). What could be the guidance to successfully adopt the DevOps approach with standardised software engineering constraints such as ISO/ IEC 12207 or 29110? In summary, the DevOps approach should be tailored to environmental constraints (Ebert et al., 2016).

The environment could also be a source of opportunities. What about Artificial Intelligence use in DevOps? Can we trust AI test and security management? Which elements of the framework would be impacted and how? The addition of the environment to the framework with the understanding of relationships between DevOps’ “components” leads to new questions. How can governance and orchestration of resources be dealt with when DevOps needs to be implemented at scale? How can links between elements in the framework and the environment be managed, for instance, in a platformization context where “Cloud platforms link multiple stakeholders [. . .] enabling new forms of collaboration and sharing among consumers, suppliers and governments” (Benlian et al., 2018, p. 738)?

Finally, when this framework is contextualised, and when relationships between its components are identified, what will happen to these relationships when the environment changes? For instance, how sustainable could this DevOps conceptual framework be considering the increasing demands on information systems related to the acceleration of the world’s digitisation? How can the dynamic within this framework be sustained knowing that orchestration in a DevOps context like Continuous Delivery requires very high availability of resources?

Beyond their updated literature review, albeit one that is restricted to the scientific literature, and their stimulating framework, we disagree with the authors on several points presented hereafter.

## 3. Is there no common basic understanding and no clear proposed definition(s) of devOps?

While we agree that there is no precise shared conceptualisation or definition of what DevOps is, many definitions exist in the literature. However, the existence of several definitions and conceptualisations does not imply that there is no common basic understanding of the phenomenon.

First, ever since Debois coined the term Debois (2008), practitioners have highlighted the need to develop practices that bring software development and operations closer together. Hüttermann (2012, p. 4) recognised the necessity of tailoring definitions to the needs and requirements of each job or role in the overall development and operations process. Several definitions have been proposed from diferent perspectives, the developers (Hüttermann, 2012), the software architect (Bass et al., 2015) or the Ops (Le-Quoc, 2011).

While there are good practical definitions for different stakeholders, as Willis (2012), one of the fathers of DevOps, says “we’ve been doing DevOps for years”. We were lacking then “a good canonical definition for DevOps”. In short, we contend that good practical definitions, adapted to diferent jobs, existed from the first years of DevOps and that those highlighted the collaboration between development and operations. A common basic understanding of what DevOps is existed for practitioners but needed academic conceptualisation.

Second, academics engaged with the phenomenon and provided definitions, like those identified by Gall and Pigni (see Appendices B and C). Lwakatare et al. (2016) proposed a definition of DevOps and presented and defined five dimensions of DevOps. More recently, Hemon-Hildgen et al. (2020, pp. 477–478) clarified and synthesised DevOps’ definition as a “set of a principles for collaborative work implemented between the IS design and development function and the IS operations function, along with potentially other constituencies (stakeholders), which is founded on the sharing of culture, goals, measures, automation tools and automated processes towards continuous delivery of software”.

Hence, we agree with the part of the authors’ argument that states that the literature presents a multitude of definitions. However, we disagree with their conclusion: the existence of multiple definitions does not mean that there are misunderstandings or a common misunderstanding of DevOps or that there is no conceptualisation. This only means that DevOps is a relatively new, rich and complex approach related to diferent conditions: a diverse array of stakeholders, environments, and technologies that must be adapted to the implementation context. These diverse interactions create the possibility of developing a plurality of perspectives on this phenomenon; some perspectives focus more on certain principles than others. However, DevOps’ core principles tend to remain the same (e.g., the Culture, Automation, Measurement, Sharing (CAMS) pillars; the principle of continuity) and are embedded in recent definitions and conceptualisations (e.g., Hemon-Hildgen et al., 2020 or Gall & Pigni, 2022).

## 4. Is DevOps a methodology? What does this mean?

We find problematic that Gall and Pigni believe that DevOps should be considered a methodology without discussing the term. If we understand that a methodology is a set of principles that support some goal or approach, we would tend to agree with their use of the term. But not if, as it is often used, the term simply means a method. The Merriam-Webster dictionary defines a methodology as “a body of methods, rules, and postulates employed by a discipline: a particular procedure or set of procedures”. Then, the method is defined as “a procedure or process for attaining an object: such as a(1): a systematic procedure, technique, or mode of inquiry employed by or proper to a particular discipline or art; (2): a systematic plan followed in presenting material for instruction b(1): a way, technique, or process of or for doing (2): a body of skills or techniques”. Again, there is no DevOps procedure or process that is systematically applicable to all organisations. Willis (2010), who formulated the CAMS model supporting DevOps, reminds us that:

“Devops is not a plan, it’s a reaction (. . .) Devops is not a product. If a vendor tells you that they have a Devops product or a Devops compliant product, then you will know immediately that they don’t have a clue of what Devops is”. Similarly, Hüttermann (2012) emphasises that DevOps is not a catalogue of recipes where there would be a DevOps-by-the-book method that is applicable everywhere and in a systematic way. Finally, Humble and Molesky (2011) highlighted that DevOps is an approach undertaken through CAMS principles. While we may ofer guidance for transitioning to DevOps, we cannot conceptually propose a methodology to get there.

## 5. What type of review?

So where is the problem? Gall and Pigni indicate that we lack an understanding of the conceptual elements of DevOps. To identify DevOps’ conceptual dimensions and related constituent elements, the authors stated that they performed a systematic literature review (SLR). The review clearly follows the SLR method as defined by Kitchenham and Charters (2007), but we contend that it neither fully translates into a systematic review in the sense given by Paré et al. (2016), nor into a critical review (Paré et al., 2015).

## 5.1. Lack of systematicity

This paper and its appendices report what the authors did in very transparent way (Paré et al., 2016). However, we have some issues with the systematicity and the soundness of the execution of the review. According to Paré et al. (2016, p. 496), “Systematicity is defined as a disposition towards organized, methodic, and orderly inquiry that uses various methods and processes to search, screen, assess, analyse and interpret relevant information with a view to achieving a set of specific research goals”. While the authors selected several criteria for their inclusion/exclusion policy, they did not apply these criteria systematically, and sometimes they did not justify their choices.

## 5.1.1. A lack of systematicity related to type and time period of sources

One inclusion-exclusion criteria mentioned by the authors is “only academic literature (A-level journals and conferences as listed in Table 3 – relevant academic sources)”. Consequently, the authors eliminated many evidence-based sources such as books written by practitioners because they are nonacademic. However, in the founding article on Evidence-Based Software Engineering (EBSE), Kitchenham et al. (2004, p. 8) make several recommendations, one of which relates to the integration of practitioner publications.

The authors’ personal interpretation of Kitchenham led them to discard a significant part of the literature and this methodological choice is not appropriate for DevOps. For comparison, it is as if we were trying to define agile development while excluding practitioner publications, in particular, the founding texts practitioners wrote such as the Agile Manifesto, which is conceptual and is born from practice. DevOps was created by and for practitioners. From their expertise and their field experience of software engineering and development, these practitioners can deliver evidence-based content to the scientific community (e.g., Debois, 2008; Hüttermann, 2012; Kim et al., 2016). Such literature exclusion probably impacted the richness of their results and their understanding of DevOps; moreover, excluding this literature contradicts the authors industry stance and their desire to create guidance for practitioners and to provide this guidance to them.

Another criterion is selecting relevant academic sources themselves. The authors ignored several academic publications (evidence-based ones), like the book written by Bass et al. (2015) which, more than definitions, contains rich content and perspectives on DevOps. So, when the authors claim to “identify significant contributions using journal databases within and outside the specific domain;” we must wonder, why not books?

Another criterion focused on A-level journals and conferences. It is quite easy to understand what an A-level journal is, but it is more complicated to identify a top-level conference in Information Systems integrating DevOps. Several conference ranking systems exist such as Conference Ranks based on Australian ERA and CORE, Brazilian Qualis and American MSAR. The authors focused mostly on AIS conferences, some IEEE conferences and some conferences on Software Engineering. This led to omission of frequently cited papers such as Dyck et al. (2015), which was published in a good conference with peer reviewing. In addition, the authors did not consider topic-related conferences like the XP Conference, a world-class agile software development conference, or DevOpsDays, the first and main conference on DevOps in the world, or conferences combining research and practice like the IEEE-RELENG conference on Release Engineering. These conferences are peer-reviewed and both practitioners and academics contribute to these conferences.

Finally, the authors excluded all the literature before 2012 without justifying this choice. Why not consider publications from before January 2012 knowing that DevOps first appeared in 2008? Several good papers and books were published by Debois, Humble and Molesky or Kim before 2012.

## 5.1.2. Systematicity and application scale

We understand the authors’ concerns about the possibility of generalising the results between a study carried out at a small “unrealistic” scale to large-scale corporate software applications. However, “It is well known that there is no one-size-fits-all method to software and information systems development” (Karlsson & Agerfalk, 2009, p. 300). The fundamental pillars of DevOps remain conceptually the same regardless of the “realism” of the scale of study and regardless of the size of the organisation trying to adopt DevOps. The exclusion of studies conducted on “unrealistic” scales is not justified; it rather constitutes a lack in the systematicity of the SLR, especially if the literature review is realistic.

## 5.1.3. Consequences: some inaccuracies due to the lack of systematicity

Some inaccuracies appear due to the lack of systematicity. First, the authors claim that “The existing literature uses terms such as ‘continuous deployment’ and ‘continuous delivery’ interchangeably (Rodríguez et al., 2017) without providing a clear definition”. There are very clear definitions of continuous delivery (Chen, 2015; Humble & Farley, 2010; Laukkanen et al., 2017; Virmani, 2015) and continuous deployment (Leppänen et al., 2015; Olsson et al., 2012; Rodríguez et al., 2017; Savor et al., 2016) in the literature. Second, the authors state that “The study identified four conceptual elements of DevOps: collaboration, automation, measurement and monitoring (Lwakatare et al., 2015)”. This is not the study by Lwakatare et al. (2015), which identifies these four conceptual elements. Indeed, it was two practitioners, John Willis and Damon Edwards, who conceptualised the four DevOps pillars after the first US-based DevOpsDays in Mountain View, California on Friday, 25 June 2010 (Willis, 2010).

## 5.2. Not critical enough and not realistic

“A critical review primarily aims to analyse the extant literature on a particular phenomenon to reveal contradictions, controversies or inconsistencies (e.g., Sylvester et al., 2013; D’Arcy & Herath, 2011; Keutel et al., 2014). [They] seek to problematize a particular area of investigation” (Paré et al., 2016, p. 3).

First, the coding scheme of the papers is somewhat limited. To create a powerful and novel conceptual framework would require some conceptual innovation. When examining the data structure (Appendix D), all the high-level categories already exist in the literature as pillars or are put forward as the major goal of DevOps. Actually, the authors recognised that they confirm what has already been observed in their discussion. There is little in the way of an abduction or theorisation process.

Second, the authors collect heterogenous material and criticise the whole set of papers for their heterogeneity, but they build essentially if not solely on it to establish their model with little novelty (i.e., extraneous ideas or material). Why say that this heterogeneity is problematic if a) more encompassing definitions of the essential elements could be obtained from it, and if b) this heterogeneity could also refer to diferent perspectives or to diferent environments?

Third, the authors claim their review is also realistic. It is certainly not a realist review as defined by Pawson et al. (2005) and as understood in the literature (Paré et al., 2016; Templier & Paré, 2018). “The main goal of a realist review is to unpack the mechanisms of how ‘complex interventions’ work in particular contexts” (Templier & Paré, 2018, p. 5). First “Realist reviews, which provide an explanatory analysis aimed at discerning what works for whom, in what circumstances, in what respects and how, cannot use a protocol-driven approach” (Paré et al., 2016, p. 7). Indeed, like Paré et al. (2016), we consider that Kitchenham and Charters (2007) use such an approach, precisely the one adopted in this paper. Second, realist reviews are for explaining, not for understanding, and as defined by Templier & Paré, it is dificult to have a review that is both critical and realist. The paper considered is neither.

This is not to say that to advance knowledge on DevOps, we should not consider performing a critical or a realist review. Unlike what was implicitly suggested by Gall and Pigni, we do not think that adopting DevOps is always relevant for organisations as a whole or for all types of software projects. We need a realist review to explain when DevOps works. Second, we believe that software development research, and DevOps in particular, would benefit from more critical views on what collaboration means in this context, what values it serves.

## 6. Is this conceptual framework relevant to practically guide a transition to DevOps?

The authors regularly claim a lack of guidance. We believe that the framework and the definitions proposed by the authors provide an understanding of DevOps but do not constitute guidance of how to adopt or evolve towards DevOps. The act of guidance is to provide a guide, to control the course of an object or an idea, to direct or to influence towards a particular end or goal.

Two fundamental elements are missing from the framework presented by the authors: 1) contextualisation of DevOps in its environment; 2) precision regarding the relationships between the diferent elements and the diferent dimensions. These two points are fundamental to writing potential guidance for DevOps. The success of such guidance for DevOps will depend on the ability to orchestrate these elements and the resources attached to them, and the ability to do so in idiosyncratic contexts (Hemon-Hildgen et al., 2020).

In addition, while representing a good academic description (still remote from practice) of the dimensions and elements that constitute DevOps, the fineness of the framework is not suficient to guide a transition towards DevOps successfully. The framework remains a fixed description that does not integrate the DevOps dynamic elements. It does not show the underlying mechanisms that drive these elements, either the levers to be activated to move towards DevOps in a given situation. There is a lack of abstraction, of recontextualization, of crossing with other models and theories; this would have made it possible to create new knowledge that could be used for a successful transition to DevOps. Notwithstanding the quality of their description, the proposed framework remains static and is of little use as a practical guide to transitioning to DevOps. We are still in need of a multifaceted and complex theory for explaining how to transition to DevOps.

Is the conceptual framework new? The authors themselves lucidly explain that “While the conceptual dimensions are not new, we precisely define them, identify and discuss their constituent elements, and state what the goal of an organisation should be when implementing DevOps”. The authors’ modelling sums up the literature but doesn’t bring much novelty to it.

We agree with the authors on the building blocks of DevOps. However, the framework does not establish relationships, or loops between the diferent constituent elements as well as between each of the dimensions. For example, what does communication or collaboration mean in a DevOps approach? What elements of communication and collaboration interact within DevOps? To obtain elements of an answer, readers may go back to the literature to discover some diferences between agile practices and DevOps practices (Jabbari et al., 2016) or the diferences in collaboration within a DevOps team (Hemon et al., 2020).

## 7. Conclusion

Many questions remain unanswered: should we restrict DevOps communication and collaboration to interactions between development and operations (Erich, 2018)? Should we consider that collaboration and communication extend beyond that and integrate architects and design (Bass et al., 2015; Jabbari et al., 2016)? Humble and Molesky (2011, p. 7) highlight that “DevOps is about aligning the incentives of everybody involved in delivering software, with a particular emphasis on developers, testers, and operations personnel”. Hence, DevOps may invite collaboration far beyond; it integrates business, UX designers, architects, and customers. The proposed framework is limited because it does not provide information on how it works in a real environment; it is not possible to see, for example, how the collaboration item is articulated with other elements in a prescriptive or explanatory model, but also with fundamental elements outside the model (e.g., customers, service providers, constrained environments).

In conclusion, we agree with the three dimensions and identified elements in the framework proposed by the authors. We agree they performed a transparent literature review, but neither systematic nor critical. Consequently, they created misconceptions about DevOps, i.e., as a methodology. They also missed rich insights provided by practitioner and expert literature. Notably, omitting the context as well as the environment where DevOps is adopted or implemented does not allow addressing DevOps orchestration challenges. Finally, we believe they mainly clarified some elements of DevOps without providing guidance. To sum up all these deficiencies, in this review we miss the industry or practitioner flavour DevOps has had from its inception. This proposed framework is nevertheless interesting as an updated picture of DevOps, a framework source of stimulation and debate. Despite our diferent interpretation, we agree with authors on their research agenda and suggest extending the proposed research avenues.
