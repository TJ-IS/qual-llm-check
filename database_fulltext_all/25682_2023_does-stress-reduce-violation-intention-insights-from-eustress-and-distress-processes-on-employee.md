# Does stress reduce violation intention? Insights from eustress and distress processes on employee reaction to information security policies

Adel Yazdanmehr <sup>a</sup>, Yuan Li <sup>b</sup> and Jingguo Wang <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Paul H. Chook Department of Information Systems and Statistics, Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, The City University of New York; <sup>b</sup>Department of Accounting and Information Management, Haslam College of Business, University of Tennessee, Knoxville, TN, USA; <sup>c</sup>Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, University of Texas at Arlington College of Business Administration, Arlington, TX, USA

## ABSTRACT

Past research suggests that the demands of information security policies (ISPs) cause stress upon employees, leading them to violate the policies. It emphasises the distress process but overlooks a possible positive process that may arise from the ISP demands (i.e., the eustress process) and motivate employees to reduce ISP violations. This study explores both the distress and eustress processes. It proposes that the challenge and hindrance aspects of ISP demands induce these processes and subsequently afect ISP violations. Besides, employees’ ISP-related self-eficacy may facilitate or impede these processes. To test the research model, a survey was conducted on 375 employees in the U.S. The results show that the challenge aspect of ISP demands elicits a positive psychological response of employees, which in turn triggers their planful problem-solving to deal with these demands. In contrast, the hindrance aspect of ISP demands provokes a negative psychological response that triggers employees’ wishful thinking about ISP demands. Meanwhile, employees’ self-eficacy strengthens the efect of positive psychological response on planful problem-solving. Subsequently, planful problem-solving reduces employees’ intention to violate the ISP, while wishful thinking increases their intention. This dual-process view sheds new light on the connection between ISP demands and ISP violation intention.

KEYWORDS Information security policy violations; information security policy distress; information security policy eustress; wishful thinking; planful problem-solving; information security policy related self-eficacy

## 1. Introduction

Companies develop information security policies (ISPs) to guide employees’ information technology (IT) use and protect the companies’ digital assets. Violating these policies elevates a company’s security risks and its vulnerability to cyberattacks (Li et al., 2018). It was found that insiders were responsible for 22% of security incidents in 2021, up about 68% from 2020 (Verizon, 2021). An estimated \$15 million were spent annually on insider threats, an increase of 34% from 2020 (Proofpoint, 2022). Employee security missteps accounted for approximately 85% of all data breaches (Hancock, 2020).

To understand the reasons for employees’ ISP violations, researchers explore factors that influence their responses to ISP demands. It is a common belief that ISPs, like other organisational policies, demand efort and time from employees, which causes a great deal of stress on them. To cope with the stress, employees may apply emotion-focused coping, which uses strategies such as moral disengagement, to alleviate the stress and rationalise ISP violations (D’Arcy et al., 2014). The Insider Threat Mitigation Guide (CISA, 2020) also notes that employees who feel stressed are more likely to engage in risky behaviour and eventually pose threats to organisational information security. Subsequently, the literature has focused on the distress process and examined the efect of ISPrelated (i.e., security-related) stress on ISP violations (D’Arcy et al., 2014, 2018; D’Arcy & Teh, 2019; Hwang & Cha, 2018).

Studies on stress and coping (Folkman & Moskowitz, 2000; Podsakof et al., 2007), nevertheless, show that in addition to the distress process, job demands may induce a positive process called eustress (using the prefix eu- which means “euphoria”) that elicits afirmative responses from employees and reduces their policy violations. For example, studies on workplace behaviour show that some stressors related to job demands, such as role uncertainty, role conflict, resource inadequacies, administrative hassles, and job insecurity, may generate adverse outcomes, while other stressors such as workload, work pace, job complexity, and job responsibility may evoke motivation for problem-solving and lead to desirable outcomes (Cavanaugh et al., 2000; Mende et al., 2017; Podsakof, 2007; Rodell & Judge, 2009). In information systems (IS) research, Ahmad et al. (2012) show that employees view work overload and uncertainty surrounding IT demands as challenges that could enhance organisational commitment. Srivastava et al. (2015) show that stressors due to IT use (e.g., overload, complexity, uncertainty) may be perceived by employees as an opportunity for job engagement. These examples illustrate an alternative view of work-related stress that allows for a positive appraisal of job demands that may motivate employees to meet the demands and achieve desired organisational outcomes.

ISP demands are inevitable in today’s digital workplace, and these demands are coupled with employees daily duties. A systematic understanding of the distress, as well as the eustress processes in ISP violations may help managers design interventions to boost information security. For instance, employees may feel pressured to handle an increasing daily workload because of ISP demands (e.g., encrypting data, saving data in password-protected folders), which causes distress. However, they may also expect to find peace of mind and a sense of security after completing required ISP tasks, which causes a eustress response. Such pressure may encourage employees to develop new skills (e.g., data encryption) or learn new security tools to better comply with ISPs while fulfiling their daily duties. Those who see ISP requirements as part of their responsibility to protect their organisations may feel obliged to fulfill ISP demands because doing so is considered an important part of their duties (Burns et al., 2019). Accordingly, we propose a model that explores these possibilities and ofer a dual-process view of how ISP demands elicit both the distress and eustress processes and influence employee intention to violate the ISP.

Thus, our research question is: How do the distress process and the eustress process work in employees’ response to ISP demands? We integrate the transactional model of stress and coping (TMSC; Glanz & Schwartz, 2008; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984b) and the holistic stress model (HSM; Calif et al., 2020; Simmons & Nelson, 2007) to study employees’ responses to ISP demands. The TMSC depicts a process in which environmental stimuli trigger diferent coping responses of employees (e.g., emotion regulation, problem-solving) that influence their behaviour to deal with the stimuli (such as ISP violations); it also depicts the roles of coping resources (such as self-eficacy) in the process. The HSM supplements this process by specifying how the diferent coping responses are chosen based on an employee’s positive and negative psychological responses to the stimuli (Calif et al., 2020). Integrating both theories, we disentangle the distress and eustress processes underlying employees’ responses to ISP demands.

First, we argue that employees appraise ISP demands as both a challenge and a hindrance. Like other job demands such as safety policies (Clarke, 2012), employees may “appraise” the demands as challenging (i.e., challenge demands), which triggers the eustress process. This generates a positive psychological response (e.g., feelings of motivation and achievement) and encourages employees to address the issues at hand in a planful way. Employees may also “appraise” ISP demands as obstructing (i.e., hindrance demands), thus inducing the distress process. This generates a negative psychological response (e.g., feelings of anxiety and distress) and triggers eforts to alleviate the feelings.

Subsequently, drawing upon the TMSC, we argue that two types of coping responses, namely, emotionfocused coping and problem-focused coping, are triggered based on the diferent psychological responses of the employees. These two types of coping responses are widely studied in the literature (see Appendix A). The former involves directing one’s focus, energy, and efort to create an illusory interpretation of the environment and avoid the stressor without changing it. The latter involves directing focus, energy, and efort towards changing or solving the problem (Chen & Liang, 2019; Folkman et al., 1986; Liang et al., 2019). Given the ISP violation context, we operationalise these coping responses as planful problem-solving and wishful thinking (see Appendix C for detailed discussion). We propose that a positive psychological response facilitates planful problem-solving (i.e., deliberate problem-focused eforts to alter the situation; (Folkman et al., 1986)), while a negative psychological response facilitates wishful thinking (i.e., wishing that the situation would go away or somehow be over with; (Chen & Liang, 2019)). These coping responses result in diferent behavioural outcomes, such as lower or higher levels of ISP violations. Further, we argue that planful problem-solving decreases employees’ intention to violate the ISP, while wishful thinking increases this intention.

We further argue, based on the TMSC, that the choice of the coping responses is contingent on employees’ self-eficacy in dealing with ISP demands. Self-eficacy refers to individuals’ judgements of their ability to perform a task (Bandura, 1977). In our context, it means the ability to meet ISP demands (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). The TMSC suggests that selfeficacy could influence one’s coping ability and thus act as a stimulating or bufering mechanism against stressful situations (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Bakker et al., 2010; Cooper et al., 2001). For example, employees self-eficacy afects how they cope with job demands by shaping their attitudes towards the tasks (Tarafdar et al., 2015). It also determines employees’ level of engagement in computer-related tasks (e.g., information security requirements) to the extent that higher self-eficacy is associated with higher computer engagement (Compeau & Higgins, 1995b; H Liang & Xue, 2010; Ng et al., 2009) and lower computer-related anxiety (Compeau & Higgins, 1995a; Pham et al.,

2016). We thus argue that when employees are psychologically triggered by ISP demands, those who believe in their self-eficacy are more likely to handle these demands proactively using planful problemsolving, and less likely to use wishful thinking.

To test the research model, we conduct a survey of 375 employees in the United States. This study makes the first case for examining not only the distress process that causes employees to violate the ISP but also the eustress process that reduces the violation intention. The dual-process view expands past literature on employee responses to ISP demands, and shifts the paradigm from examining the distress process alone to the examination of dual processes in ISP violation research. It empirically demonstrates that employees may interpret ISP demands as both challenges and hindrances, resulting in both positive and negative psychological responses. This finding unveils the mechanisms that drive the eustress and distress processes, explaining how these processes unfold and influence ISP violations. In addition, the study incorporates both planful problem-solving and wishful thinking as coping strategies to manage the positive and negative psychological responses and illustrates the moderating role of ISP-related self-eficacy in enhancing planful problem-solving to deal with ISP demands. The findings suggest that employees’ ISPrelated self-eficacy should be enhanced to increase the use of planful problem-solving, which ofers a way for managers to curb employees’ ISP violations.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical background

## 2.1. Studies on employee responses to ISP demands

An important topic in behavioural information security research is to understand how employees respond to ISP demands and how their responses influence ISP violation intention (Ament & Haag, 2016; D’Arcy et al., 2014; D’Arcy & Teh, 2019). From a technostress perspective, the literature shows that security-related stress caused by ISP demands is a critical determinant of ISP violations. For example, D’Arcy et al. (2014) conceptualise security-related stress as a construct reflected by overload, complexity, and uncertainty. They show that the stress induces emotion-focused coping, which in turn, contributes to the intention to violate the ISP. Their findings were later replicated by D’Arcy et al. (2018).

In another study, D’Arcy and Teh (2019) examine security-related stress produced by role conflicts, role ambiguity, and work impediments due to ISP demands. They show that such stress leads to frustration and fatigue of employees, which in turn neutralise their intention to violate the ISP. More recently,

Hwang and Cha (2018) introduce security-related role stress (i.e., role conflict and role ambiguity due to information security requirements). They show that security-related stress reduces organisational commitment both directly and indirectly via security-related role stress, which decreases ISP compliance intention. All these studies emphasise the distress process due to ISP demands. Nevertheless, there are exceptions: Ament and Haag (2016) show that some stressors related to ISP demands may positively influence ISP compliance intention. However, there is no further investigation of the efect.

The prior literature’s overt focus on the hindrance aspect of ISP demands and the subsequent distress process limits researchers’ understanding of how ISP demands drive the security-related behaviour of employees. Little is known about how ISP demands can motivate employees to achieve desired outcomes such as ISP compliance. Moreover, previous studies have mostly focused on employees’ emotion-focused coping response that moralises and justifies ISP violations (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2014; D’Arcy & Teh, 2019). However, these studies ignore potential problemfocused coping that may help meet ISP demands and reduce ISP violations. Further, they have not investigated individual coping resources, such as self-eficacy, that can alter the way employees cope with ISP demands.

To fill these gaps, we intergrade the transactional model of stress and coping (TMSC; Glanz & Schwartz, 2008; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984b) and the holistic stress model (HSM; Simmons & Nelson, 2007). In the literature on technostress and information security (see Appendix A, Table A.1 for a review), the TSMC is a popular theoretical foundation for studying the stress caused by a broad range of IT events such as IT implementation (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016) and interruptions (Galluch et al., 2015). This model has been extended to specific stress contexts such as technology threat avoidance (L. Liang & Xue, 2009) and ISP violations (D’Arcy et al., 2014), showing its value in guiding this line of research. We further incorporate the holistic stress model (HSM; Simmons & Nelson, 2007) to account for individuals’ psychological responses to ISP demands and to examine both the distress and eustress processes in ISP violations. Integrating these theories allows us to explore the distress and eustress processes underlying employees responses to ISP demands and the role that selfeficacy plays as a facilitator or inhibitor of these processes.

It should be noted that the stress-coping perspective is not the only approach to ISP violation research (Cram et al., 2019). Other studies examine the efect of personal traits such as dominant decision styles, general security awareness, and general security orientation on ISP compliance (Donalds & Osei-Bryson,

2017, 2020). While these factors contribute to the ISP compliance literature, they do not answer the question of how employees respond to security-related stress, which is the focus of this study. Thus, the additional factors were not included in this study, the limitation of which is discussed in Section 5.3.

## 2.2. Theoretical background

## 2.2.1. The Transactional Model of Stress and Coping (TMSC)

The TMSC suggests that individuals’ responses to environmental stimuli such as ISP demands may difer depending on their cognitive appraisal of the stimuli (Glanz & Schwartz, 2008; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984b). Through the appraisal, they evaluate whether a certain encounter with environmental stimuli is significant or meaningful to their well-being and in what ways (Folkman et al., 1986). This process includes a primary appraisal and a secondary appraisal. The primary appraisal refers to an individual’s evaluation of the stimuli with respect to the potential impact of the stimuli (i.e., harm or benefit), based on which a valence (i.e., challenge or hindrance) is assigned to the stimuli (Cavanaugh et al., 2000; Pearsall et al., 2009). The appraisal of the stimuli leads to the perception of the stimuli as a challenge or a hindrance stressor. The challenge–hindrance taxonomy has been analysed in various organisational contexts (Boswell et al., 2004; Cavanaugh et al., 2000; Lepine et al., 2004; Podsakof, 2007; Podsakof et al., 2007). Appendix B provides detailed categories of these stressors.

The secondary appraisal refers to an individual’s evaluation of the availability of options and resources to respond to the stressful situation. In the secondary appraisal, individuals assess their resources and capabilities to determine their potential to change the stressful situation, either by minimising the harm or maximising potential benefits (Folkman et al., 1986; Glanz & Schwartz, 2008). Self-eficacy is a key component of the secondary appraisal.

In response to the primary and secondary appraisals, employees undertake various coping responses to change their cognition or behaviour (e.g., problemsolving, emotion regulation; Glanz & Schwartz, 2008). The TMSC holds that individuals who have confidence in their self-eficacy when facing stressful situations are more likely to employ coping responses such as planful problem-solving and information seeking, and they are less likely to employ responses based on emotion regulation such as wishful thinking (Glanz & Schwartz, 2008). These coping responses drive subsequent behaviours. The whole process is illustrated in Figure 1.

With regards to the appraisal of the stimuli, in this study, we focus on four challenge stressors and five hindrance stressors from the ISP perspective (Table 1). These stressors are selected based on prior literature (c.f., Lepine et al., 2005; Podsakof et al., 2007), especially those that have been empirically tested in information security research. For instance, past research has examined ISP-induced workload, work pace, job complexity, job responsibility, role uncertainty, role conflict, and resource inadequacies (D’Arcy et al., 2014; Hwang & Cha, 2018; Pham et al., 2016). To expand the list of hindrance factors, we further include administrative hassle and job insecurity, which are highlighted in the literature (Lepine et al., 2005; Podsakof et al., 2007). The list of factors shown in Table 1 is by no means exhaustive. As perceived challenges and perceived hindrances of ISP demands are conceptualised as reflective constructs in the literature (D’Arcy et al., 2014; Lepine et al., 2005; Podsakof et al., 2007), the included items are suficient to justify the measurement validity of the challenge–hindrance stressors.

In response to these stressors, individuals assess the situation to determine how to manage them (i.e., they develop coping responses; Folkman & Lazarus, 1986). Problem-focused coping and emotion-focused coping are two common ways of dealing with stress (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984a). They coexist in stressful encounters (Folkman & Lazarus, 1980, 1985; L. Liang & Xue, 2009), which has been illustrated in a number of empirical studies (see Appendix A, Table A.1). It is therefore theoretically and practically justifiable to study both coping responses in this context.

Problem-focused coping aims to change the objective reality by directly working on the causes of the stress in order to reduce it (L. Liang & Xue, 2009). Employees may engage in problem-focused coping when a situation is perceived as being beneficial and manageable (Lazarus, 1991a). With this approach, they exert efort to seek information or learn new skills to help them better meet the ISP demands. Emotionfocused coping does not change the reality but allows employees to reappraise the stressors and form a false perception (L. Liang & Xue, 2009; Wang et al., 2017). When a situation is deemed negative, static, or out of control (Lazarus, 1991a), employees may adopt emotion-focused coping to withdraw from dealing with the stressful situation, wishing it would resolve itself or disappear on its own. In particular, wishful thinking as an emotion-focused coping strategy suggests that employees try to alleviate stress caused by IT threats without resolving the threats (Chen & Liang, 2019). With this approach, employees deal with stress by relying on miracles (e.g., the stressor will miraculously be resolved), faith (e.g., God will intervene), or denial (e.g., the cause of the stress is not important or does not exist). For example, those who perceive policies regarding email use as confusing and unclear may experience frustration. As a result, they may conclude that these policies are meaningless to them or that any efort to follow the policies is unnecessary. They may wish the emails they send without following the ISP would not be compromised. Therefore, they are more likely to ignore and violate the ISP (c.f., D’Arcy et al., 2014). Given the context of ISP violations, we adopt planful problem-solving and wishful thinking to operationalise the two coping responses; the rationale is elaborated in Appendix C.

Last, the TMSC suggests that coping responses are contingent on coping resources and capabilities. Employees assess their ability to change a stressful situation (Folkman et al., 1986; Glanz & Schwartz, 2008). One manifestation of the ability is self-eficacy, which refers to employees’ degree of confidence in their own ability to fulfill a task (Bandura, 1977). A strong self-eficacy belief is an indicator of one’s ability to successfully handle stress (Holahan & Moos, 1991; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984b). Thus, employees with strong self-eficacy beliefs feel empowered (Shen, 2009) and prefer to use direct action to handle stress (Holahan & Moos, 1987). As a result, these employees are more likely to engage in problemfocused coping rather than emotion-focused coping (Den Brande et al., 2020; Srivastava & Sager, 1999). In contrast, a weak self-eficacy indicates the inability to manage stressful situations. Thus, those with weak self-eficacy beliefs are discouraged from solving the problem. They are more inclined towards emotionfocused coping to regain their emotional stability and find relief from their negative feelings (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984b). Adapting to our research context, we focus on ISP-related self-eficacy, which is employees’ perception of their ability to fulfill ISP demands.

## 2.2.2. The Holistic Stress Model (HSM)

The HSM describes how occupational stress occurs as a result of employees’ interactions with their work environment (Simmons & Nelson, 2007). The model suggests that the stress may have positive or negative impacts on employees, hence the name “holistic”. For example, in the technostress context, it is shown that employees perceive and respond to both the “bright side” and the “dark side” of technostress (Calif et al., 2020). This model is composed of four major components including environmental stimuli, stressors, psychological responses, and outcomes that are linked by the appraisal, the decision, and the performance stages (Calif et al., 2020; Simmons & Nelson, 2007). The model is illustrated in Figure 2.

The appraisal stage links environmental stimuli to stressors. Based on the appraisals, the stimuli may be perceived as stressors if they cause stress on employees (Hargrove et al., 2013). Like the primary appraisal in the TMSC, employees appraise the impact of environmental stimuli and determine whether these stimuli constitute challenges or hindrances.

The decision stage links the stressors to the psychological responses. Specifically, the challenge stressors are associated with positive psychological responses (e.g., hope, positive attitude, engagement), while the hindrance stressors are associated with negative responses (e.g., negative attitude, hostility, anxiety; Calif et al., 2020; Hargrove et al., 2013; Simmons & Nelson, 2007). Diferent psychological responses trigger diferent behaviours to cope with the stressors and then influence organisational outcomes.

In the performance stage, psychological responses cause behavioural and organisational outcomes, such as strain (Ayyagari et al., 2011), burnout and engagement (Srivastava et al., 2015). In general, a positive psychological response causes desirable outcomes, while a negative psychological response causes undesirable outcomes. In the study by Calif et al. (2020), a positive psychological response to technostress results in job satisfaction, while a negative psychological response results in attrition and turnover intention.

A central claim of the HSM is the role of the positive and negative psychological responses in driving individual behaviour. The HSM suggests that stress is a naturally occurring mind–body response to stressors that place demands on individuals (Quick et al., 1997). Stressors do not influence behavioural outcomes directly; instead, they afect the outcomes through the psychological responses of employees (Hargrove et al., 2011; Simmons & Nelson, 2007). Employees are likely to have positive psychological responses to stressors that ofer opportunities for career growth, learning, and achievement. They enjoy challenging tasks and thrive while performing them (Selye, 1982), and they feel more involved and satisfied (Campion & Mcclelland, 1991; Carpenter et al., 2017) regardless of the initial impact of the stressors on their well-being (Mccall et al., 1988). In contrast, employees are likely to have negative psychological responses to stressors that pose threats to their career growth and achievement (Cavanaugh et al., 2000; Lazarus, 1991a; Rodell & Judge, 2009). These positive and negative psychological responses constitute the basis of the eustress and distress processes (Calif et al., 2020; Simmons & Nelson, 2007).

## 2.2.3. The distress–eustress model of ISP-related stress

We suggest that the TMSC and HSM can be integrated to better understand the impact of ISP demands on employees’ ISP violation intention. The TMSC does not specify how psychological responses play a role in driving coping responses. The model assumes that the primary and secondary appraisals are adequate to determine employees’ coping responses. This account may be insuficient because coping responses are chosen by employees based on their psychological responses caused by job demands (c.f., Simmons & Nelson, 2007). One’s psychological response could be necessary in channelling the efects of ISP demands on diferent ways of coping. Although HSM acknowledges that employees choose diferent strategies in the eustress and distress processes, it does not go further to describe what kind of coping responses they adopt and how (e.g., the influence of self-eficacy). As we argued earlier, employees adopt a combination of planful problemsolving and wishful thinking to deal with ISP-related stress, so both coping responses should be studied in tandem, led by their psychological responses. In addition, self-eficacy, an important secondary appraisal factor, is not emphasised in the HSM. Due to its role in driving coping responses as specified in the TMSC, self-eficacy should also be considered.

Integrating the TMSC and HSM, we propose the distress–eustress model of ISP-related stress (see, Figure 3). We argue that employees’ reaction to ISP demands could be explained via two processes: the ISP-related eustress process and the ISP-related distress process. We suggest the following courses of action when employees encounter ISP demands: (a) Employees appraise the impact of ISP demands and assign a valence (i.e., challenge and/or hindrance) to the demands. (b) Depending on this appraisal, employees develop positive and/or negative psychological responses to the stressors. (c) As a result, they undertake various coping responses to deal with the psychological responses. (d) They do so by evaluating whether they possess the ISP-related self-eficacy to cope with the ISP demands. (e) These coping responses result in behavioural outcomes (i.e., whether the employees violate the ISP). In the eustress process, employees appraise ISP demands as challenges, psychologically respond to them positively, adopt problem-focused coping (i.e., planful problemsolving), and exhibit less intention to violate the ISP. In the distress process, employees appraise ISP demands as hindrances, psychologically respond to them negatively, adopt emotion-focused coping (i.e., wishful thinking), and exhibit increased intention to violate the ISP. We develop a research model to empirically test these claims.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. From ISP demands to psychological responses

ISP demands can produce a positive psychological response of employees when the demands are appraised as challenges. First, employees may perceive that ISP demands increase the volume of their work (i.e., their workload). This may ofer an opportunity for employees to find alternative or innovative ways (e.g., learning new skills) to accomplish their tasks and fulfill ISP requirements simultaneously. Maintaining a heavier workload may be both intrinsically and extrinsically rewarding: Those who can both finish their tasks and fulfill ISP demands are more likely to feel a sense of accomplishment (i.e., an intrinsic reward), and they may also receive a better evaluation from their organisation (i.e., an extrinsic reward). For example, encryption is more labour-intensive for employees, but encryption requirements also create the opportunity for an employee to learn how to protect files (Ament & Haag, 2016). Meeting such data encryption requirements makes employees feel more protected from a cyberattack or from the aftermath of information security incidents (e.g., demotion, negative performance reviews).

Second, ISP demands may cause employees to work faster to meet their job responsibilities. Therefore, they may consider ISPs as an opportunity to boost their skills and techniques to become more eficient. For example, when an employee processes data that must be encrypted, the employee must complete other tasks faster to make up for the time taken by the encryption. This may lead to the development of new skills that allow the employee to work more eficiently. In another scenario, the employee may be required to complete several steps in order to connect to the organisation’s intranet from an external workstation (e.g., secure login with a password, second-factor authentication), or the employee may be required to use a virtual private network (VPN), which can be time-consuming. In both cases, the employee is forced to work faster. The anticipation of working more eficiently as a result of meeting ISP demands could be seen as rewarding and thus exhilarating, leading to positive feelings.

Third, the complexity (i.e., the variety and breadth) of employees’ tasks may require expansion to meet ISP demands. This could come in the form of acquiring new knowledge or skills, and it often leads to better performance, a deeper understanding of tasks, and innovation (Hackman, 1980). All these improvements can engender positive feelings and higher job satisfaction among employees (Fried & Ferris, 1987). For example, employees who rely heavily on email for their daily tasks may find it rewarding to learn how to identify phishing emails and understand security warnings when opening email attachments (Goel et al., 2017). The knowledge developed by spending time identifying malicious email attacks could be seen as rewarding because it helps employees avoid the aftermath of information security breaches due to such attacks.

Finally, ISP demands may make employees take more responsibility not only for their own work but also for the whole organisation. Employees who feel responsible for their organisation may safeguard its information assets through actions such as maintaining the health of their work computers or safeguarding the data stored on company-issued mobile devices. This often makes employees feel that they play a significant role in the organisation, which in turn increases their dedication and positive feelings about their jobs (Podsakof, 2007). Accordingly, we propose:

H1: The perceived challenge of ISP demands is positively associated with a positive psychological response to ISP demands.

ISP demands may provoke a negative psychological response if employees perceive that the excessive or unpleasant constraints in the ISP demands are ambiguous, conflicting, or disruptive; or if the demands cause administrative hassle or threats to their job security. First, ISP demands that include uncertain role-specific instructions may distract employees from their day-to-day tasks and cause confusion. For instance, employees may be uncertain about their roles in information security incidents (e.g., phishing attacks, antivirus warnings) and thus may be uncertain about whether they should report these incidents or resolve them. Such uncertainty causes distress.

Second, if ISP demands conflict with employees daily tasks, the demands create disruptions that eventually lead to negative feelings or distress. For instance, scheduled security maintenance (e.g., software updates, virus scans) may interfere with an employee’s current tasks (D’Arcy et al., 2014). This can cause a tremendous amount of distress when the employee is struggling to meet a deadline.

Third, a lack of resources for meeting ISP demands often leaves other tasks in limbo. For example, when ISPs are mandated by laws or regulations, employees without proper technical support or ISP training may be intimidated or confused. For example, an organisation may seek to enact a new encryption rule for transmitting data as mandated by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). In such a scenario, employees may be disciplined if they do not have the necessary resources or tools to follow the rule, which leads to distress. In fact, security resources may act as a bufer to burnout due to ISP demands.

Fourth, employees sometimes feel that ISP demands are unnecessary procedures that hinder the completion of tasks. For example, if employees lack the necessary access permissions on a work computer, they must spend time contacting IT staf by email, phone, or through interofice documents. Then, they are forced to wait for IT staf to instal the needed software (D’Arcy et al., 2014) or give permission to do so. Sometimes, based on the IT department’s response time, employees may miss a deadline (Pham et al., 2016). This incites a negative feeling such as frustration (D’Arcy & Teh, 2019).

Finally, the perception of job insecurity due to the fear of not meeting ISP demands appropriately may also cause distress. For instance, employees may fear losing their jobs because they find it dificult to meet new ISP demands. In such situations, employees may think of ISP demands as an uncontrollable burden that threatens their job security. Accordingly, we propose:

H2: The perceived hindrance of ISP demands is positively associated with a negative psychological response to ISP demands.

## 3.2. From psychological responses to coping strategies

The positive psychological response caused by challenging ISP demands means that dealing with such demands may be beneficial for the employees and their organisation. Therefore, employees may be motivated to adopt strategies that help them take advantage of the positive feelings. Those who are charged with expectations of benefits and gains are more likely to make deliberate, planful eforts (Folkman et al., 1986) to deal with ISP demands, such as learning the ISP requirements or finding innovative ways to meet those requirements while fulfiling their jobs. For instance, employees who feel the responsibility to protect organisational information assets or protect their organisation from data breaches or other security incidents may feel obliged to make plans, seek advice, learn new skills, or double their eforts to meet the ISP requirements. Meanwhile, they can advance their careers in the organisation by learning new skills and boosting their ability to deal with security threats in the long run. These planful problem-solving eforts may lead to both intrinsic and extrinsic rewards, such as feelings of accomplishment and positive job evaluations. Accordingly, we hypothesise:

H3: A positive psychological response to ISP demands is positively associated with employee engagement in planful problem-solving with regard to ISP demands.

On the other hand, heightened negative feelings could drive employees to find ways to regain their psychological control by alleviating these negative feelings (Calif et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2019; D’Arcy et al., 2014; D’Arcy & Teh, 2019). To achieve this objective, employees may attempt to reinterpret the situation (i.e., imagine an alternative reality; Lazarus, 1991b) in a way that helps them downplay any possible repercussions from failure to meet ISP demands (D’Arcy & Teh, 2019). As a result, these employees are likely to wishfully think the situation would resolve itself or somehow be over with (Rodell & Judge, 2009). Accordingly, we expect that employees who have a negative psychological response are more likely to engage in wishful thinking (Folkman et al., 1986). This idea is also consistent with prior literature (D’Arcy & Teh, 2019): ISP demands may cause negative feelings (e.g., frustration, fatigue) that in turn cause employees to utilise wishful thinking. Thus, we hypothesise:

H4: A negative psychological response to ISP demands is positively associated with employee engagement in wishful thinking with regard to ISP demands.

## 3.3. The moderating role of self-eficacy

In regard to ISPs, self-eficacy implies that employees have the ability to deal with ISP demands (Ng et al., 2009; Workman et al., 2008). It indicates control over the situation, which enhances employees’ desire to exploit the source of their positive feelings (Cram et al., 2019) and, as a result, to find ways that benefit them. This is in line with the notion that those who are competent in using IT are more likely to be encouraged by, and thus benefit from, exploratory uses of IT (Tarafdar et al., 2015). So, employees with higher ISP-related selfeficacy who have a positive psychological response to ISP requirements may feel more capable of planning their actions, such as learning new security features or using security tools to meet the requirements. In other words, among employees who are galvanised by the challenge of ISP demands, higher self-eficacy helps strengthen their belief that the ISP demands are more manageable. Therefore, these employees will utilise strategies designed to meet those demands, thus boosting the efect of their positive psychological response and triggering more planful problem-solving.

Meanwhile, higher self-eficacy allows employees to control the efect of their negative feelings on their coping responses such that they are less likely to feel the need to alleviate such feelings by wishful thinking. Since wishful thinking does not help employees solve the problem, employees may experience repercussions in the long run due to the rising risk of security incidents. Thus, higher self-eficacy prevents employees from engaging in wishful thinking when they are in distress. In sum, because of their confidence in their ability to meet ISP demands, employees with high selfeficacy may feel more comfortable handling such demands when they are psychologically triggered by ISP demands. As a result, they are less inclined to ignore ISP demands through wishful thinking. Accordingly, we hypothesise:

H5a: Employees’ ISP-related self-eficacy positively moderates how their positive psychological response to ISP demands afects their planful problem-solving with regard to ISP demands, and this efect is stronger among those with a stronger perception of self-eficacy.

H5b: Employees’ ISP-related self-eficacy negatively moderates how their negative psychological response to ISP demands afects their wishful thinking with regard to ISP demands, and this efect is weaker among those with a stronger perception of self-eficacy.

## 3.4. Coping strategies and employee ISP violations

Employees who perceive benefits from dealing with ISPs, and thus engage in planful problem-solving, often strive to find information and methods, or to plan actions, that help them comply with ISP demands. This means that such employees are less likely to violate the ISP. For example, the act of encrypting data before copying or transferring the data to another device adds complexity to the task. However, those who see this new step as an opportunity to learn a new skill may feel encouraged to try (and eventually master) data encryption. Therefore, such employees who spend the time and resources to enhance their knowledge and skills related to compliance are less likely to violate the ISP. We hypothesise:

H6: Planful problem-solving is negatively associated with an employee’s intention to violate the ISP.

On the other hand, those employees who engage in wishful thinking are more likely to ignore or devalue ISP requirements since, for them, wishful thinking helps reappraise or escape reality, thus thwarting the urgency of meeting such requirements. This reappraisal of reality helps them feel a sense of ease, which facilitates maladaptive actions (Chen & Liang, 2019; Folkman et al., 1986) such as wishing that ISP demands may simply resolve themselves without taking action. This makes it easier for such employees to justify their intention to violate the ISP (Chen & Liang, 2019; Folkman et al., 1986). For example, those who perceive policies concerning emails as confusing and unclear may experience frustration. As a result, they may conclude that these policies are meaningless or any efort to follow them is not productive. They may simply hope that emails sent without following the ISP will not be compromised. Therefore, they are more likely to violate policies on email use (c.f., D’Arcy et al., 2014). Accordingly, we hypothesise:

H7: Wishful thinking is positively associated with an employee’s intention to violate the ISP.

## 4. Research methodology, analysis, and results

## 4.1. Research method and data collection

We conducted an empirical test to verify our research model. To collect the relevant data, we designed and created an online survey to capture employees’ appraisal of (and responses to) ISP demands. To ensure that the content of the questionnaire is valid, we revised items based on suggestions from several faculty members who were active in information security research. Then, we conducted a pilot study with 50 graduate students at a major university in the southwest United States. After refining the survey based on the pilot study, we distributed it to the U.S. industrial panel using the Qualtrics Research Suite.

Figure 4. Research model.

Online survey platforms have been suggested as a preferred approach for studies that have invasive contexts or controversial topics (e.g., computer abuse, employee violations of organisational policies) due to concerns about social desirability bias (Berinsky et al., 2012). These platforms also host a diverse set of respondents in terms of education, age, ethnicity, industry, and tenure, among other factors. To mitigate concerns regarding online survey data, we followed the suggested remediations of Lowry et al. (2016) to enhance the validity of the study.

Having received IRB approval, first, we asked for participants’ consent. We then terminated the survey for those who were either part-time employees or whose jobs did not require ISPs. We also restricted our pool of participants to employees who work in the United States. During the survey, we first presented participants with a definition of ISP along with descriptive examples. Then, we asked participants to provide their appraisals of the ISP in their organisations, how they psychologically react to such policies, how they deal with the ISP, and the likelihood that they would do the same thing (i.e., commit an ISP violation) as the individuals in several hypothetical scenarios. We excluded respondents whose response time was unusually short or unusually long. We used attention questions to ensure the accuracy of the responses. We also paid participants the equivalent of the federal minimum wage. We collected a total of 375 valid responses. Appendix D, Table D.1, provides the profile of the sample.

## 4.2. Measurement and questionnaire design

To ensure reliability and validity in measurements, we adopted existing instruments from prior research to our context. All the key constructs were measured as reflective constructs with multiple items. The list of items is provided in Appendix D, Table D.2.

First, the measurements of challenge and hindrance ISP demands were adopted from Podsakof (2007), which is a refined version of the seminal work by Cavanaugh et al. (2000). We reworded the items to reflect ISP demands. For instance, we rephrased the item “I have to work at a rapid pace to complete my job” to “Because of ISP demands, I have to work at a rapid pace to complete my job”. Then, we used items developed by Calif et al. (2020), which were originally adopted from the technostress context, to measure positive and negative psychological responses because of the verified psychometric properties of the items. We revised the wording of the items to fit our context, mostly by replacing the term technostress with terms relevant to ISPs. For example, we rephrased “When faced with technology-related stress, how often do you find that the pressure makes you more productive at work?” to “When faced with stress due to information security policies, how often do you find that the pressure makes you more productive at work?”.

For coping responses, we adopted unidimensional measures of wishful thinking and planful problemsolving to capture employees’ responses (Chen & Liang, 2019; Folkman et al., 1986). All the items were rephrased to match the context of ISP compliance. For instance, when participants were asked to describe their reaction to concerns about ISP violations, the questionnaire posed questions such as “I fantasized that information security policies compliance issues would go away or somehow be over with”. for wishful thinking and “I came up with a couple of diferent solutions to resolve information security policies compliance concerns”. for planful problem-solving.

Next, we adopted the self-eficacy items from Bulgurcu et al. (2010), which measure the construct in terms of information security. The wordings of these items were also modified to fit ISP demands. For instance, one item reads: “I have the necessary competencies to fulfill information security policies”.

We measured employees’ intention to violate the ISP using five scenarios adopted from D’Arcy et al. (2014) and Guo and Yuan (2012). The use of scenarios to measure ISP violation intention has been widely adopted in behavioural information security research because the scenarios create a mock environment for capturing – nearly realistically – employees’ behaviour when they indirectly report their intention to violate the ISP (Vance et al., 2015). This reduces social desirability bias in the responses (D’Arcy et al., 2014). We included two questions immediately after each scenario to measure the respondent’s intention to violate the ISP. This design is considered more pragmatic and less antagonistic (Siponen & Vance, 2010).

For each scenario, we measured and controlled for the level of realism based on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “highly unrealistic” to 7 = “highly realistic”; D’Arcy et al., 2014). The average scores for the five scenarios range between 4.7 and 5.5, which suggests that the scenarios were realistic and applicable to business settings. We also included deterrence, a wellestablished construct in predicting ISP violations (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Willison et al., 2018), as a control variable. Deterrence reduces the intention to violate the ISP (Herath & Rao, 2009a, 2009b).

Finally, we measured and included other control variables for violation intention: demographic factors (e.g., age, gender), education, position and tenure in the organisation, and organisation size.

## 4.3. Measurement validation

We used SPSS AMOS 24, a popular covariance-based structural equation modelling (CBSEM) tool, to perform the data analysis. CBSEM was chosen because it yields unbiased parameter estimates by explicitly modelling the measurement error. This is more suitable for confirmatory analysis and for theory testing than the alternative partial least squares (PLS) SEM (Gefen et al., 2011). This method is also more beneficial when using strong theoretical bases and wellestablished measurement items (Gefen et al., 2011), which is true for our study. See Appendix E for a detailed justification of the method.

Before testing the research model, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of the measurement model (Kline, 2005). Following Hu and Bentler (1999), we used the following fit indices to assess model fit: Chi-square $( \chi ^ { 2 } )$ with degree of freedom (df), the non-normed fit index (NNFI), the comparative fit index (CFI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardised root mean square residual (SRMR). These indices and their thresholds are shown in Appendix F, Table F.1. The analysis produced an acceptable measurement model.

To verify reliability, we used composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha. All constructs met the minimum threshold of 0.7 (see Appendix F, Table F.2). To test convergent validity, we followed Hair et al. (2009), who suggested three criteria: the model fit must be acceptable, the factor loadings (λ) must be significant and greater than 0.5, and the average variance extracted (AVE) must exceed 0.5. All three criteria were met (see Appendix F, Tables F.1, F.2, and F.3). Later, we adopted the test from Fornell and Larcker (1981) to establish discriminant validity. This test requires that the square root of the AVE of each construct must be greater than any inter-construct correlations. All constructs satisfied this requirement. Appendix F, Table F.3, shows the loadings and crossloadings. No cross-loadings were observed.

We employed various procedural and statistical techniques to control for common method bias. The results of our tests indicate that common method bias is not a concern (see Appendix G for more details).

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

This study takes the first step to investigate the eustress and distress processes in employees’ responses to ISP demands. It shows that ISP demands are perceived by employees as both a challenge and a hindrance: The former leads to a positive psychological response that increases planful problem-solving, while the latter leads to a negative psychological response that increases wishful thinking. The efect of the positive psychological response on planful problem-solving is further strengthened by employees’ self-eficacy in complying with the ISP. Finally, planful problemsolving reduces employees’ intention to violate the ISP, while wishful thinking has the opposite efect. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to look beyond the detrimental efect of ISP demands.

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

This study extends the behavioural information security literature in four ways. First, this study is the first to examine both the eustress and distress processes in employees’ responses to ISP demands. This is significant, as until recently (c.f., Burns et al., 2019), the behavioural information security research has focused on the distress process caused by ISP demands but has not considered the eustress process. This study brings in the eustress process in complement to the distress process and unveils their underlying mechanisms. It expands the view on employees responses to ISP demands. The findings ofer new insight into the processes that drive employees’ ISP violation intention. This dual-process view may be helpful in examining not only ISP violations but also other security behaviours of employees in organisations. For example, it may be applied to examine employees’ balanced responses to security incidents or other security threats (c.f., Liang et al., 2019).

Second, rather than assuming that ISP demands are always hindering and causing ISP violations, our study illustrates both challenging and hindering aspects of the demands (i.e., Table 1). This is considerably different from studies such as Ayyagari et al. (2011), which only contextualised the distress aspect of technology characteristics. Moreover, it difers from similar categorisations of IT demands in contexts such as healthcare IT in Calif et al.’s (2020) study, allowing for the testing of more pertinent antecedents to ISP demands. As employees’ perceptions of environmental stimuli may difer, testing more pertinent antecedents would provide a more accurate account of their psychological responses to the stimuli, such as the positive psychological responses to work overload and job complexity. This is in alignment with safety policy literature (Clarke, 2012) but in contrast to Calif et al.’s (2020) claim that work overload and job complexity are hindrance stressors that cause the negative psychological response to healthcare IT.

We show that ISP demands can be challenging due to the impacts on job workload, work pace, job complexity, and job responsibility. This follows the same conceptualisation of job demands commonly found in the vocational behaviour and psychological stress literature (Boswell et al., 2004; Cavanaugh et al., 2000; Podsakof, 2007). These perceptions contribute to a positive psychological response of employees and trigger the eustress process that helps reduce ISP violations. While it is important to reduce the negative psychological response (e.g., security-related distress; D’Arcy et al., 2014), it is also equally important to utilise the positive psychological responses that can help boost ISP compliance and reduce violations.

Third, this study illustrates the critical roles of coping responses through which the positive and negative psychological responses diferentially influence ISP violation intention. Indeed, previous studies have focused on emotion-focused coping to the stress caused by ISP demands and the subsequent ISP violations (see Appendix A) but barely examined the role of problem-focused coping that reduces the ISP violations. This study considers both coping responses in tandem. We show that employees’ positive psychological response to ISP demands motivates them to adopt planful problem-solving to meet the demands, which leads to fewer ISP violations. This sheds new light on information security research: Employees make an efort to meet ISP demands. The findings fill the gaps in prior literature and suggest that both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping are important in influencing the intention to violate ISP. The intermediate roles of coping responses explain the mechanism through which positive and negative psychological responses (or, more broadly, the eustress ad distress processes) are converted into action. As mentioned earlier, HSM does not specify the roles of coping responses but considers that the psychological responses influence behaviour directly. Our findings suggest that the coping responses are necessary elements in associating psychological responses with behaviour, diferent from Calif et al. (2020).

Fourth, this study recognises an important conditional factor that drives planful problem-solving: ISPrelated self-eficacy. It illustrates that employees who have a higher ISP-related self-eficacy are more likely to convert their positive psychological response to ISP demands into planful problem-solving, which helps them to address ISP demands more productively and eventually alleviate ISP violations. This is diferent from Calif et al.’s (2020) study that treats selfeficacy as a control variable without exploring its broader theoretical implications. Our study highlights the importance of ISP-related self-eficacy by showing its moderating efect on the association between psychological responses and coping responses. This finding expands the ISP violation literature, and it also highlights a mechanism to intensify the eustress process. This has implications for research to enhance employees’ ISP-related self-eficacy and further reduce their ISP violations.

## 5.2. Practical implications

For organisations, the main takeaway is that – similar to other work demands (c.f., Simmons & Nelson, 2007) – ISP demands can elicit a mixed bag of positive and negative psychological responses from employees. A dual-process view that encompasses both the eustress and distress processes of ISP demands can help organisations design and deploy ISPs more efectively. First, practitioners may use these findings to enable job design and other interventions that not only reduce the hindrance aspects but also enhance the challenge aspects of ISPs. For example, our study suggests that ISP demands may lead to situations in which employees work harder and faster to meet ISP demands and boost their job performance. To maximise such a positive efect (i.e., eustress), organisations should promote positive feelings such as a sense of accomplishment, peace of mind, and the security that comes with meeting ISP requirements.

Managers should also find out why ISP demands are perceived as beneficial or problematic and, based on the findings, proactively engage in strategies, procedures, or technologies to accentuate the benefits and reduce the problems associated with ISP demands. On the one hand, organisations could enhance the job responsibility of their employees by involving them in ISP-related matters such as designing and implementing new ISPs. This involvement could convey to employees that securing the organisation’s information with ISPs is everyone’s responsibility. Organizations could also implement awareness programms that emphasise the responsibility of employees to not only perform their daily tasks but also protect organisational information assets in order to ensure the smooth operation of the organisation. Further, to make employees feel involved, organisations should consider implementing a feedback mechanism in which employees receive continuous feedback from other employees regarding any potential pitfalls of the ISP or possible ways to improve it.

On the other hand, organisations should streamline their ISPs (i.e., reduce role conflicts, ambiguities, and bureaucracy) to minimise the possible hindering efect of ISPs. For instance, organisations can benefit from the new trend in the industry to abandon periodic, mandatory password changes (Goodin, 2019), which is a policy often perceived as an administrative hassle. ISPs should also clearly define employees’ responsibilities for protecting and accessing organisational information assets with respect to their roles. For instance, ISPs should clearly state employees’ responsibility for reporting information security incidents (e.g., stolen passwords, phishing attacks, system glitches) regardless of their roles, and ISPs should provide a method for reporting these incidents. Further, given the rise of shadow IT in the workplace (e.g., employees finding supposedly better data-sharing solutions that are actually prohibited by the IT department), organisations should strike a balance between (a) allowing employees to use the solutions that help them most and (b) ensuring that access to organisational data is securely managed. Organizations should automate security procedures so they can free up their employees’ time to concentrate on more day-to-day tasks.

Organizations could take advantage of new approaches such as gamification to help employees adjust to complex ISP requirements (e.g., data loss prevention procedures) and make compliance more gratifying. A game is a problem-solving activity approached with a playful attitude (Schell, 2008). In gamification, organisations design tasks (e.g., ISP requirements) to be “play-like”, which makes meeting ISP demands more enjoyable and thus fosters engagement. For example, scoreboards in gamified information systems trigger a positive spirit of competition among employees, encouraging them to manage the organisation’s data properly so they can earn higher scores and climb up the board (Dickson, 2016). Organizations could integrate gaming concepts and mechanics into day-to-day security procedures and practices (Dickson, 2016), since more engagement can enhance positive emotions and consequently increase motivation and performance (Bakker & Bal, 2010; Bakker & Demerouti, 2008).

Given the constructive role of planful problemsolving in reducing ISP violations, organisations should exert more efort to create a climate that values problem-solving behaviours as employees seek to learn and explore. Organizations can help their employees learn skills for planful problem-solving to better deal with ISP demands.

Finally, our findings indicate that employees who have a stronger sense of self-eficacy when facing ISP demands are more likely to respond to positive feelings by utilising planful problem-solving. Therefore, organisations should help develop employees’ selfeficacy beliefs when dealing with ISP demands. This can be achieved through supportive interventions such as employee training and peer support.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This study has a few limitations. First, other than those listed in Table 1, it does not account for other possible stressors recognised in the organisational literature (Podsakof, 2007) or other IT-related job characteristics (Carpenter et al., 2017). Future research may test these stressors in the context of ISP violations. Second, this study examines how employees react after they perceive challenge and/or hindrance ISP demands, but individual diferences in the primary appraisal of the stressors fall out of the scope of the study. Future studies may examine individual diferences in decision styles, general security awareness, and general security orientation (Donalds & Barclay, 2021; Donalds & Osei-Bryson, 2017, 2020) to determine whether they afect the challenge and/or hindrance perceptions of ISP demands. Third, we tested only one emotion-focused coping strategy (i.e., wishful thinking) and one problem-focused coping strategy (i.e., planful problem-solving). Although the rationale behind our coping strategy selections was explained in Appendix C, future studies should go beyond our model and test the impacts of alternate strategies on ISP violations. Adding the scope of coping strategies (and other antecedents to ISP violation intention) may also increase the variance explained for the violation intention.

Fourth, this study focuses on individual coping responses to environmental stimuli but overlooks how the social structures and group eforts can lead to elevating the power of individuals in coping with ISP. The empowerment literature (Gutierrez, 1994) suggests that employees who feel helpless to impact the existence of stressors may feel psychologically empowered (i.e., adjust their emotional response so that stress no longer causes them to be paralysed) and engage in covert behaviour. This literature holds that group interaction and collective action are crucial elements of changing the situation, which explains why some individuals in stressful events may be motivated and become active in the aftermath (Gutierrez, 1994). Future research should consider employees in their social context and, in turn, acknowledge the critical role that social interactions play in shaping employees responses to stress.

Fifth, our sample was drawn exclusively from employees residing in the United States, and 86% of them came from the same ethnic group. The findings are thus limited to the Western, educated, industrialised, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) societies (Henrich et al., 2010). It is unclear whether the proposed model can be applied to non-WEIRD workforces or operate diferently in other cultures (Hovav & D’Arcy, 2012). For example, certain cultures may convey a stronger signal about conformity or individuality. Future studies should be conducted crossculturally to uncover if national cultures could afect employees’ psychological responses to ISP requirements, coping strategies, and intention to violate the ISP.

Lastly, our sample consists of respondents from various industries such as IT, financial services, and healthcare. Features unique to the ISPs in each industry could also afect the generalisability of our results. Future studies should investigate and compare the efect of ISP demands on the intention to violate the ISP in each specific industry. Moreover, the crosssectional data provide minimum support for causality. Future research may use longitudinal methods or controlled lab or field experiments to address this limitation. They may also consider using more objective measures of ISP violations (e.g., logs, manager scores) to avoid bias in self-reported data.

## 5.4. Conclusion

Employees do not inherently oppose ISP demands. Some embrace them as a challenge and engage in planful problem-solving to meet ISP demands, especially those with a high ISP-related self-eficacy. We empirically test this eustress–distress view of ISP demands and find robust results. This view expands past literature on ISP demands and ofers new directions for examining ISP violations. Researchers may follow this view in related studies.
