# Balancing information privacy and operational utility in healthcare: proposing a privacy impact assessment (PIA) framework

Rachida F Parks<sup>a</sup>, Rolf T Wigand<sup>b</sup> and Paul Benjamin Lowry <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Computer Information Systems, Quinnipiac University, Hamden, Quinnipiac, USA; <sup>b</sup>Emeritus College at Arizona State University, Scottsdale, Arizona, USA; <sup>c</sup>Pamplin College of Business Department of Business Information Technology, Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University, Blacksburg, Virginia, USA

## ABSTRACT

One needs to look only at recent data breaches to be reminded of the severe and far-reaching damage caused by privacy threats. In light of these threats, global healthcare leaders are striving to understand how to protect patient information without the loss of benefits (utility) that results from privacy-preserving mechanisms. Consequently, our study examines the relatively unexplored issue of simultaneously responding to information privacy threats and maintaining utility in a healthcare privacy compliance context. Counterintuitively, we also identify a symbiotic relationship between these two focal and interdependent eforts. We adopt an interpretive qualitative research method leveraging the value-focused thinking (VFT) approach which results in two major contributions: (1) the development of a valuedriven framework presented as a means-end objective network providing a list of 16 means objectives and seven key fundamental objectives enabling higher-quality privacy decision making vis-à-vis privacy and utility. Our second and central contribution (2) is a theoretical framework of privacy impact assessment (PIA) emphasising the interplay and balance between making appropriate decisions in responding to information privacy while not hindering healthcare operations. This work provides the foundation for proposing four compelling propositions for future healthcare privacy research.

## 1. Introduction

The healthcare industry has made dramatic strides in the development and deployment of information technology to cut costs and save patients’ lives, and this process continues at a strident pace (Kohli & Tan, 2016; Kruse et al., 2018). Patient information gathered and generated via electronic health records (EHRs) holds great promise for saving patients’ lives, improving healthcare quality and reducing costs (Powers et al., 2018). Using EHRs, however, also raises pressing patient security and privacy issues (Entzeridou et al., 2018; Shen et al., 2019).

One needs to read only about the organisational data breaches recently highlighted in the media to be reminded of the extensive damage that can be caused by privacy and security threats. Data breaches reported in the United States since 2005 exceed 11 billion records (PRC, 2021). Although the actual organisational consequences of privacy breaches are challenging to quantify, several organisations have been fined millions of dollars (Culnan & Williams, 2009; Harjala, 2019). The 2018 IBM Security and Ponemon Institute data breach study reported an average cost of US\$3.6 million per data breach, with an increase of 6.4% from the previous year (Ponemon Institute, 2018).

Previous research has advocated the implementation of a variety of safeguards to reduce privacy and security threats to protect health information (Angst et al., 2017; R. Parks et al., 2017). Although some privacy scholars have pointed to the inefectiveness of existing privacy safeguards (e.g., Al-Muhtadi et al., 2019; Gabriel et al., 2018; Pandey & Litoriya, 2020; Wall et al., 2015), little research has identified the reasons for these failures. Some research has suggested that a key reason for these failures is the implementation of privacy safeguards without consideration of how they may negatively influence operations and strategies (R. F. Parks et al., 2011; Stahl et al., 2012). This is further supported by Angst et al. (2017) in a study that emphasised institutional factors that support the integration of IT security and routines:

Organizations are much less likely to experience breaches when they are more responsive to their organizational characteristics and embedded in environments that place inherent value on substantive adoption. Therefore, policymakers need to branch out beyond investments in IT security and consider institutional factors when developing directives for securing personally identifiable information and other forms of sensitive data.. (p. 911)

Healthcare executives strive to balance privacy demands with the need to provide care for patients eficiently. For healthcare professionals, this objective can be potentially self-contradictory at times. We argue that to ensure the successful alignment of information privacy countermeasures and their business impacts, a set of objectives that represent stakeholders values must be identified. Determining these values before selecting a privacy response increases the chances of selecting the most appropriate countermeasures. As Bond et al. (2008) state in a study examining the ability of decision makers to generate self-relevant objectives for consequential decisions:

If a decision maker overlooks a key objective, then information that could be used to distinguish the options on this dimension may be ignored, increasing the likelihood of an inappropriate decision. (p. 57)

To develop these objectives, we use the value-focused thinking (VFT) approach to assess stakeholders’ values, which serves as the basis for developing and identifying objectives (Dhillon & Torkzadeh, 2006; Keeney, 1992). These objectives will provide guidelines for healthcare executives to respond to information privacy threats in a way that does not hinder business operations.

We investigate the issue of simultaneously responding to information privacy threats and maintaining operational utility in healthcare. We contribute to the literature in two substantial ways. First, we use a qualitative VFT methodology to generate objectives for strategic decision makers. The VFT method resulted in 16 means-end objectives and seven fundamental objectives that we organised in a directional relationship through the means-end objective network. Second, based on this interpretive analysis, we propose a privacy impact assessment (PIA) framework to guide healthcare executives in establishing an efective balance between protecting privacy and maintaining operational utility at the organisational level.

This paper proceeds as follows: We first introduce the concepts of privacy and utility. We focus on the organisational challenges of ensuring privacy in a manner that does not obstruct work practices. We then introduce the VFT approach and present our research methodology, followed by a discussion of the results and analysis. Next, we present the meansend objective network framework. We conclude by discussing this study’s implications for research and practice and by noting key limitations and future research opportunities.

## 2. The challenges of balancing information privacy and business operations

Because we conducted interpretive, qualitative research, our review of the literature was guided by our data analyses and findings – not by a postpositivist approach of being literature-review driven. We followed methodological guidelines that call for qualitatively analysing the data and then comparing the findings to the extant literature (Eisenhardt, 1989; Miles & Huberman, 1994). Our literature review focused on the emerging themes from the interviews and analyses to increase the understanding and context of the literature.

Healthcare data breaches, which occur when a patient’s protected health information is inappropriately accessed, have been increasing over the years (HIPAA Journal, 2021). This negative trend has pressured healthcare providers to adopt various privacy and security measures, such as encryption, two-factor authentication and employee privacy training (Abouelmehdi et al., 2018). However, these privacy measures create unique interoperability and utility challenges (Shrivastava et al., 2021).

Although information privacy problems have been extensively researched, the concomitant utility problem has received little attention, except within specific applications (Valdez & Ziefle, 2019). Privacy and utility have competing objectives; that is, “utility is maximal when the user gets full information flow and reduces when the flow of certain information is reduced by the restriction or addition of noise” (Sankar et al., 2010, p. 2). The existence of a trade-of between privacy and utility is also supported by Brickell and Shmatikov (2008), who stated that “even modest privacy gains require almost complete destruction of the data mining-utility” (p. 1). Privacy loss is “quantified by the adversary’s knowledge gain about sensitive values of specific individuals”, and utility loss “is measured by the information loss about the sensitive values of large populations” (Li & Li, 2009, p. 518). Prior research has demonstrated that utility is hampered in the presence of influences that negatively afect business operations (Shrivastava et al., 2021). Our study attempts to gain a better understanding of the efects of these negative influences. Therefore, we define operational utility as the business impacts in which operational challenges, such as unavailability of information, disruption of workflow, usability and operational feasibility issues, are minimised. These challenges are discussed further below.

## 2.1. Unavailability of information

Patient care and treatment increasingly depend on the availability and accuracy of patient information, often making a crucial diference in a patient’s survival. One of the most widely used countermeasures to protect patient information is role-based access control, which restricts information access to authorised users. This countermeasure may harm patient treatment when a clinician’s access to patient information is impeded (Tembhare et al., 2019). If the information needed by healthcare professionals to make critical clinical decisions is unavailable due to stringent access-control countermeasures, patients may be treated incorrectly. Hence, the unavailability of information may have dire consequences for patient care quality.

## 2.2. Workflow disruptions

The implementation of privacy safeguards and countermeasures in healthcare has changed clinical workflow patterns. Healthcare professionals can face work pressure from IT changes, which subsequently leads to dysfunctional workflows. These changes may involve encrypting documents before emailing them, multiple logins due to timeout features, and so on. Consequently, users may not always react positively to implemented changes, especially when these changes disrupt their work routines. In a study titled “Do you want my password or a dead patient?”, Koppel et al. (2015) leverage an ethnographic study of a medical workplace to investigate how healthcare workers manage to work around their computer access to help patients. Although privacy and security measures are intended to protect patient information, several studies have revealed that integrating privacypreserving mechanisms within real-world clinical workflows may lead to workflow disruptions and possibly cause workarounds that may jeopardise patient safety or care (Blijleven et al., 2019a, 2019).

## 2.3. Usability issues

Usability drawbacks arise primarily from the privacyenhancing technologies used by ordinary users. One of the most frequently used definitions of usability is that of the International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO), which defines the term under the ISO 9242–11 standard as “the extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with efectiveness, eficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use” (ISO, 2018). A study by Dhillon et al. (2016a, 2016b) investigates the relationship between security and usability and resulted in objectives that can be used as a basis for assessing and balancing security and usability. Most researchers agree that usability is context-dependent and based on the interactions between tools, problems and people. In the healthcare industry, understanding the interplay between usability and privacy is essential but still poorly understood. For example, privacy-safeguard technologies, such as biometrics, pose several usability issues due to the influence of temperature, humidity and dirt (Flores Zuniga et al., 2010). Unlike the human–computer interaction (HCI) field, the healthcare IT community lacks research that investigates usability issues resulting from the enactment of privacy safeguards.

## 2.4. Operational feasibility issues

The focus of operational feasibility in this context is on how much the enactment of privacy measures fits an existing business environment. Prior research has reported a misfit when certain privacy protection technologies, such as security protocols, are implemented, resulting in a degradation of performance (R. Parks et al., 2017). Current research places greater emphasis on investigating the business impact of privacy and security regulations (Martin et al., 2019a, 2019b); however, it ofers little insight into how safeguards influence the operational feasibility of healthcare practices.

Few studies have focused on the outcomes rather than the antecedents of privacy safeguards at the organisational level. This gap in privacy research has been recognised by a handful of information privacy and security researchers (Belanger & Crossler, 2011; R. F. Parks et al., 2011; Lowry et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2011). A key study explored the results of unintended negative consequences outweighing the intended benefits of privacy-safeguard enactment (R. Parks et al., 2017). This creates an imbalance challenge that, if not addressed, leads to the adoption of workarounds and potential privacy compliance penalties.

Our review of the literature shows that unavailability of information, workflow disruptions, usability issues and operational feasibility issues are factors shown to influence business operations. However, the literature lack clarity in explaining how the healthcare industry can respond to information privacy threats without undermining operational utility.

## 3. Theoretical and methodological considerations

VFT is a compelling philosophy that advocates starting with one’s values to create decision-making opportunities (Dhillon & Torkzadeh, 2006; Keeney, 1992). The VFT approach has a strong theoretical basis in the means-end chain (MEC) theory and the laddering technique (Sheng et al., 2010). The MEC theory seeks to explain how the selection of a particular alternative facilitates the achievement of the desired end state (Gutman, 1982). The laddering technique is a one-onone interviewing methodology used to clarify how users translate product attributes into personal values (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988; Rugg et al., 2002). Laddering uses probing questions to determine the links between the attributes of a product, and the consequences and the values associated with using the product (Sheng et al., 2010). These links or laddering serves as the basis for product diferentiation.

A recent literature review of VFT summarises three aspects that are beneficial to the decision-making process: decision-making model innovation and selection, company strategy and defence acquisition (Zhou et al.,

2018). VFT has been used to assess users’ privacy and security concerns (Barrett-Maitland et al., 2016) and to assess how privacy influences internet behaviour (Dhillon et al., 2018). VFT has been applied to various contexts, such as healthcare (Lipson-Smith et al., 2019), education (Gao et al., 2018), risk management, sustainability (Alencar et al., 2017; Benkert, 2019) and business intelligence systems (Dooley et al., 2018).

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Using the VFT Approach

This study adopts an interpretive research method to answer research questions about responding to privacy threats in a way that does not hinder business operations. Specifically, we use the VFT approach, a decision technique developed by Keeney (1992) and powerfully demonstrated in an information systems (IS) organisational security context by Dhillon and Torkzadeh (2006), to provide a systematic way to identify what is important to users and how to achieve their objectives. VFT identifies values – that is, what decision makers care about when facing dificult problems; awareness of such values will enable better and more intelligent organisational decisions (Keeney, 1992). ## 4.2. Participating informants

We interviewed 30 consenting informants from 21 healthcare organisations. The latter included a representative sample of diferent types of US hospitals, which included small, medium and large hospitals. Although we initially only targeted hospitals, our data collection and analyses revealed the need to revisit and include other healthcare organisations and entities, such as the United States Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), healthcare professional associations, healthcare IT providers and healthcare privacy consultants (see Appendix A). We conducted semi-structured interviews, lasting between 40 and 90 minutes. To be eligible to participate, informants were required to be privacy experts who held key positions in healthcare organisations, such as chief information oficer (CIO), chief privacy oficer (CPO), chief medical information oficer (CMIO), chief executive oficer (CEO) or HIPAA privacy compliance oficer. Appendix A summarises the informants’ titles, organisations and educational backgrounds. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

## 4.3. Data collection process

Initially, all interviews were conducted face-to-face at each informant’s location. This approach accounted for the sensitivity of the topic of privacy (Kotulic & Clark, 2004) and the contextual settings (Schultze & Avital, 2010). Once we built more comfort and familiarity with the interview process, we expanded to several regions in the United States via phone interviews. We used “snowball” sampling to identify additional informants (Lincoln & Guba, 1985), and data collection continued until we reached saturation (Corbin & Strauss, 2008).

To illustrate the process, during the first interviews, the concepts of operational feasibility and unauthorised access emerged. This discovery led us to include more probing questions in subsequent interviews to determine whether and to what extent these two new emerging concepts were worthwhile to include in our final analysis. This process was applied to other concepts/themes. Some concepts were sustained as worthwhile, while others were not. By the time we interviewed the $1 5 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ informant, the protocol was finalised for the remainder of the data collection. By the $2 2 ^ { \mathrm { n d } }$ informant, no new concepts/themes emerged, suggesting that we were approaching theoretical saturation (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Heath & Cowley, 2004). After interviewing 30 informants, all concepts were confirmed and no new ones emerged; therefore, we stopped collecting data.

## 4.4. Data coding and analysis

Four steps were used to identify and organise stakeholders’ values concerning how to respond to privacy threats without impeding utility: (1) identifying values by questioning informants via interviews, (2) structuring values and developing objectives, (3) distinguishing between means and fundamental objectives and (4) establishing a means-end objective network that models the interrelationships among the means and objectives (Keeney, 1992). Figure 1 illustrates these steps.

## 4.4.1. Step 1: identifying values

Identifying a comprehensive set of objectives with the goal of responding to privacy threats in a manner that does not obstruct business operations requires considerations that transcend the implementation of technical measures, policies and administrative processes. We argue that, rather than linking privacy threats to the implementation of countermeasures, the values of healthcare executives must be identified to develop meaningful privacy countermeasures.

Figure 1. Research approach.

The process of identifying values began during the interviews. As stated in the Data Collection Process Section, we used semi-structured interviews with probing questions. These questions became more specific the further we advanced in our data analysis. Probing questions and examples were used as techniques that resulted in the emergence of several values. In terms of probing questions, Dhillon and Torkzadeh (2006) recommended using probing words such as “trade-ofs”, “consequences”, “impacts”, “concerns”, “fair” and “balance” in helping informants express their values. Finally, in our semi-structured interviews, we explored informants’ values via scenarios and illustrative examples, as suggested by Keeney (1992). For example, if the informant stated, “minimise the use of social media” as a possible value in the context of information privacy management, this could have led to the emergence of the value expressed as “protecting the organisation’s reputation”.

The process of identifying values continued after all interviews were transcribed verbatim and required several months of intensive efort. The transcribed interviews were imported to NVivo, a computeraided qualitative data analysis software tool. We coded the transcriptions according to the latest standards found in IS qualitative research (e.g., Clayton et al., 2012; Koch et al., 2013). NVivo did not automatically code the transcribed interviews but organised the diferent identified values. NVivo supported conducting diferent stages of analysis, including setting up objectives and subobjectives and providing some data analysis capabilities for searching, grouping and relating values.

Interpretive researchers assume that reality is a social product in which humans make sense of their perceived worlds. Therefore, interpretive researchers investigate phenomena via the meanings assigned by the people they are studying (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). To avoid the possibility of bias, three qualitative researchers (one professor and two senior Ph.D. students) reviewed the interviews and coding. The authors then worked closely with each value that emerged, discussed and revisited the values and made adjustments when discrepancies emerged (Corbin & Strauss, 2008).

## 4.4.2. Step 2: structuring values

In the second phase, we performed a process of structuring and converting the identified values into objectives. This step involved combining similar values under a unifying theme or objective and removing duplicates for a deeper understanding of what healthcare executives care about when responding to information privacy threats. This step can be compared to axial coding (Corbin & Strauss, 2008), which allows values (codes) to be developed further into objectives (themes; Urquhart et al., 2010).

An objective is defined as “a statement of something that one desires to achieve. It is characterised by three features: a decision context, an object, and a direction of preference” (Keeney, 1992, p. 34). For example, one objective found in our study is “maximise monitoring”. The decisionmaking context for this example is information privacy, the object is monitoring and the direction of preferences is that more monitoring is preferred to less monitoring.

## 4.4.3. Step 3: organising objectives

In the third step, similar objectives were clustered into groups of objectives. It is important to distinguish between fundamental objectives and means-end objectives. Fundamental objectives are essential reasons in the decision-making context, whereas meansend objectives are a means of achieving fundamental objectives (Keeney, 1992). We applied the “Why Is That Important” (WITI) test that (Keeney, 1992) recommends applying the diferentiation between means-end and fundamental objectives. In diferentiating these objectives, the authors raised the question, “Why is this objective important for maximising information privacy without hindering business impact”? If the response was that the objective was important because of its implications for another objective, the objective was classified as a means-end objective. If the objective was important because it is essential to the overall objective, it was classified as a fundamental objective.

To illustrate this process, one objective of this study was to “maximise role-based access control”. The authors followed up with WITI. The reply was to “minimise unauthorised access”. This was followed by “why?”, to which the answer was to “maximise the privacy of our data”. Thus, “maximise role-based access control” and “minimise unauthorised access” were categorised as means-end objectives.

## 4.4.4. Step 4: constructing the means-end objective network

Finally, in Step 4, we established a means-end network in which all objectives were organised in a directional relationship. Sixteen means-end objectives and seven fundamental objectives and directional relationships were validated by two industry experts (a CIO and a CEO). The finalised version of the proposed meansend objective network framework appears in the Results section.

## 4.5. Rigour of the study

Our interpretive research uses triangulation, member checking, an inquiry audit and authenticity as evaluative criteria (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Golden-Biddle & Locke, 2007; Ibrahim et al., 2012; Klein & Myers, 1999). First, this study used multiple methods and sources, such as single-person interviews, group interviews and data collection across diferent sources (e.g., hospitals, government, consultants and IT designers), to ensure triangulation and credibility of the findings. Triangulation was also achieved by using information from supplemental workshops, roundtables and other documentation. Member checking was achieved by seeking feedback from the informants. Rather than conducting an inter-rater reliability test, an inquiry audit was conducted. This was done because interpretive research assumes that each researcher will have a unique interpretation of findings and that inter-rater reliability therefore cannot be applied (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). An inquiry audit was performed by one professor and two senior graduate students (trained in qualitative research) to examine and assess the inquiry process and to review the interview transcripts, coding sheets and data analysis. Authenticity was achieved by presenting the findings and framework in industry and academic meetings to check the extent to which the researchers’ interpretations were reasonable and accurately reflected the data.

## 5. Analysis and results

Several means-end and fundamental objectives emerged from the analysis of the interviews. The initial list contained 74 means objectives and 20 fundamental objectives. The authors carefully reviewed the objective list and removed any redundant objectives. A final set was shared with three hospital executives for further validation. The final set of 16 means objectives and seven fundamental objectives is presented in Tables 2 and 3. More qualitative support for the creation of the means objectives and fundamentals objectives is provided in our online Appendix B.

2) Maximise information availability: This study provides evidence of privacy leaders’ struggles when facing the efects of privacy protocols on information availability. This challenge was described by a CIO:

We don’t want to keep information out of the hands of people who need it. If we develop something that is too stringent . . . they can’t do their job the right way.

The challenge of maximising information availability by minimising barriers to accessing patient data was illustrated by a healthcare executive:

The fact of the matter is that [a] lot of dificulties [go] back to this idea of access, we can’t overly restrict access, because when you do so, what you end up doing is getting in the way of delivering care.

3) Minimise usability issues: Understanding the interplay between usability and privacy is essential in the healthcare industry. The data revealed that healthcare employees, including clinicians, will not use privacy tools and features that are not easy to use and/or disrupt their workflow. Patient care is their core mission, and everything else, if it does not support this mission, stands in the way. One healthcare executive commented on the usability issues related to using multiple applications and systems:

With the privacy and security in healthcare it’s the need for speed. I don’t want to log in twice . . . . If it is not usable to them [clinicians], they won’t use it.

4) Minimise workarounds: Workarounds have been recognised in both the IS and health informatics literature (Blijleven et al., 2019a, 2019). Some of the healthcare executives we interviewed expressed their concerns about the use of workarounds to bypass privacy safeguards. For instance, in an emergency, a clinician might use someone else’s login to view patient information. A striking example was provided by a CPO, who stated that clinicians sometimes bypass privacy safeguards to do their job, which involves saving lives

I would much rather happen to explain to the ofice of civil rights why somebody inappropriately accesses information than explain to a family why their loved one is dead, and they wouldn’t have been dead had the information we had in our possession wasn’t accessible to the people treating that patient.

The concern surrounding inappropriate methods of information access is that the same access that saved life could hinder privacy compliance. Therefore, healthcare executives can make better and more intelligent decisions when they know the outcomes of their privacy safeguards, as one physician asserted:

If I’m a doctor and I am in the emergency room and it times out on me while I’m critically working on a patient . . . and I have to go in and put my password in, that’s not a good thing. But it’s something that we can work around. We just have to document it.

5) Maximise operational feasibility: Maintaining operational feasibility when deploying new privacy technology or processes has emerged as an important factor in the healthcare domain. A CPO expressed this concern:

My biggest concern time again comes down to operational feasibility and whether what’s being asked can either be operationalised or is it going to be detrimental to the patient’s best interest and there is balance [to achieve].

6) Maximise the use of resources: The availability of resources influences how organisations can invest in their privacy responses. From hiring the appropriate workforce to buying and maintaining technical solutions, the resources needed to handle these initiatives are not insignificant. A CIO reported:

Healthcare is not flush with money because of the reimbursements . . . you do your due diligence; you don’t overspend, and you don’t underspend. You make the case for what is needed, how it matches up with laws and how it matches up with how you pick one security safeguard to protect your data and patients’ rights.

7) Maximise leadership commitment: This study reveals that organisations handle privacy management diferently depending on how much support they receive from their top management. Cultivating a culture of privacy starts with support from an organisation’s top executives and survives through employees buy-ins. Such sponsorship from organisational leadership is key to the success of privacy initiatives within an organisation, and it translates mainly into generously allocated resources to uphold privacy. The importance of top management’s support was described by a CPO:

If you don’t have that [buy-in from leadership], no matter what you implement, you are not going to have the resources in the first place.

## 6. Privacy impact assessment (PIA) framework & theoretical propositions

The data analysis revealed a risk assessment rationale that privacy leaders undertake when responding to information privacy threats. This risk assessment involves factors such as evaluating appropriate processes and policies or listening to patients’ and clinicians’ needs. One CPO commented:

What I try to do is listen to . . . the clinicians, physicians and patients . . . try to understand what the perspectives are and try to develop systems that, in processes and policies, are reasonable based upon our needs to access information, as well as to allow our patients to feel comfortable that their information is being protected.

Organisations undertake risk assessments in two major areas: responses to privacy threats and the influence of privacy-safeguard enactments. First, the analysis showed that healthcare organisations assess and account for means objectives when responding to privacy threats. Privacy assessment involves evaluating each means objective separately and then all means objectives as they interact.

One CPO commented:

You always do what the law says. That’s a risk that’s not worth taking. But then you have to look at other kinds of risks, too, for what you may do or may not do and what the risk of reputational harm or financial harm or all those other kinds of things, how that comes into play.

The second area of assessment includes evaluating the actual outcomes of privacy safeguards in which organisations engage. Such assessments encompass fundamental objectives that healthcare organisations need to account for when enacting privacy safeguards.

The concept of the PIA emerged as a representation of how healthcare organisations respond to privacy threats to diferent means objectives, fundamental objectives and the implications of privacy-safeguard enactments. The emergence of this analytical concept helped in understanding that responding to privacy threats is not as static and straightforward a process as it seems, nor is it as simple as investing in a few countermeasures. This process involves more wellthought-out processes, and it was efectively described by a consultant in healthcare privacy and security:

There were some that went out and bought an expensive tool thinking that would solve the problem for them. But because they didn’t understand the concept of risk analysis, having the tool is not going to help you . . . if you don’t understand the concepts of cabinetry or woodworking, going out and buying an expensive Mitre saw is going to be worthless, because you are not going to know what to do with it.

The emergence of the PIA concept facilitates understanding of the processes healthcare organisations undertake to respond to privacy threats while accounting for operational utility. These processes consider the diferent objectives influencing an organisation’s actions but also account for the implications of information privacy-safeguard enactments. This concept aligns with HHS’s definition of a PIA (HHS, 2019):

An analysis of how information is handled to 1) ensure handling conforms to applicable legal, regulatory, and policy requirements regarding privacy; 2) determine the risks and efects of creating, collecting, using, processing, storing, maintaining, disseminating, disclosing, and disposing of PII in an electronic information system; and 3) examine and evaluate protections and alternate processes for handling information to mitigate potential privacy concerns.

Our qualitative study provides evidence of the struggles that privacy leaders often face when they consider enacting privacy safeguards in their organisations. Not surprisingly, previous studies have used the term trade-of to describe the relationship between privacy and utility (Zhu et al., 2017). Likewise, Anderson et al. (2017) enumerate this privacy tension that is replete in healthcare and other settings because of the importance of accessing information to provide quality care, as well as the need to avoid violating patients’ privacy. The dynamics inherent in medical practices, such as scheduled and unscheduled patient visits, clinicians unscheduled shifts and the workforce needed at unexpected times and locations, often conflict with privacy role-based access safeguards and therefore negatively afect healthcare operations. In summary:

P1: The enactment of privacy safeguards in a healthcare setting is negatively associated with operational utility.

The emerging theme of PIA focuses on a risk assessment approach that encompasses the identification of privacy safeguards and the assessment of their efects on business operations. Using the VFT approach, we identified both means objectives that serve as tools to achieve information privacy and fundamentals objectives to assess the results of privacy safeguards. Our findings suggest that a PIA is crucial to healthcare organisations because it influences the safeguards these organisations would likely implement to mitigate privacy threats. Hypothetically, a careful combination of technical, physical and human safeguards and organisational processes makes sense. Nonetheless, organisations continue to face devastating privacy breaches (e.g., De Aguiar et al., 2020; Lowry et al., 2017; Tripathi & Mukhopadhyay, 2020; Wall et al., 2015). This is especially concerning in the healthcare context in which these breaches have multiplied in recent years because of increased digitalisation and complexity, remote healthcare and telemedicine, platform-based computing and a greater variety of systems and processes such as mobile devices, cloud computing and the internet-of-things (De Aguiar et al., 2020; Al-Muhtadi et al., 2019; Gabriel et al., 2018; Pandey & Litoriya, 2020). This highly concerning trend concurs with Culnan and Williams’s earlier claim that “existing compliance programs are not efective” (Culnan & Williams, 2009, p. 678). Our data clearly demonstrate that when healthcare organisations assess diferent threats while identifying means objectives, they are likelier to enhance the likelihood that efective safeguards will be implemented and subsequently ensure information privacy. In summary:

P2: The practice of assessing means objectives in healthcare organisations is positively associated with information privacy.

In the healthcare industry, the need to implement appropriate privacy safeguards is as crucial as the need to protect patient privacy, confidentiality and the integrity of EHRs. However, some of the major challenges faced by healthcare organisations are establishing information privacy safeguards that are in harmony with daily activities (Anderson et al., 2017; Blijleven et al., 2019a; El Emam et al., 2020). This, too, was a recurring theme in our data.

Much of the research on privacy safeguards overlooks operational utility, while healthcare executives emphasise that hospital operations and services provided to patients should not be interrupted. This begs for daily conflicts between medical practice and privacy. Regarding utility, our findings identified more factors than those addressed in the literature. Through fundamental objectives, the healthcare executives provided many examples of how business operations should be accounted for: (1) ensure business continuity, (2) maximise information availability, (3) minimise usability issues, (4) minimise workarounds, (5) maximise operational feasibility, (6) maximise the use of resources and (7) maximise leadership commitment. Our study shows that these fundamental objectives are not operating in a vacuum but are processed as part of PIA that accounts for means objectives and influences the privacy safeguards to be implemented. Based on these findings, we propose that fundamental objectives are positively related to utility. In summary:

P3: The assessment of fundamental objectives in the healthcare industry is positively associated with operational utility.

Research and practice have established PIA as a leading risk management tool used to assess the use of privacy safeguards (Culnan & Williams, 2009; Makri et al., 2020; Vemou & Karyda, 2019; Wadhwa & Rodrigues, 2013; Wright & De Hert, 2011; Zibuschka, 2020). In fact, the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) has largely made PIA mandatory for high-risk settings, such as healthcare (Wadhwa & Rodrigues, 2013; Zibuschka, 2020). Likewise, the United States Department of Homeland Security defines PIA as a decision-making tool used to identify and mitigate privacy risks at the beginning and throughout the development life cycle of a programme (DHS, 2010). In accordance with the guidelines set forward in the e-Government Act of 2002, the HHS began promoting PIA as an assurance mechanism for the adequate protection of patient information (HHS, 2015). Although our proposed PIA serves the interests of diferent health agencies (e.g., the Food and Drug Administration and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention), it can also serve the interests of healthcare organisations, such as hospitals. Thus, it is essential to assess the privacy risks associated with this practical impact in adequate protection of patient information. A better understanding of how to assess these privacy risks will further enhance the theoretical understanding of privacy in healthcare and its impact on healthcare practices.

Once a PIA is conducted, a variety of privacy responses are implemented to reduce the negative consequences of a privacy risk. Studies have focused more on the safeguards themselves than on a privacy approach that considers responses and outcomes. Thus, perhaps the existing frameworks are not cohesive enough to explain how and why organisations respond to privacy issues and threats to their patients or customers (Gerlach et al., 2019; Lowry et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2011), which is especially true in healthcare settings.

A better understanding of organisational responses and their operational influences allows for a better information privacy operational model in which PIA can be an integrative part of the model. Therefore, we propose the mediating role of PIA in moderating the negative relationship between information privacy and operational utility as follows:

P4: PIA in healthcare organisations positively moderates the relationship between information privacy and operational utility.

In summary, our PIA framework, which is inspired by our informants’ feedback and industry-conducted PIAs, goes a step further than merely acting as a simple risk assessment and privacy mitigation tool. Our PIA framework is the result of a thorough analysis and the development of a value-driven framework presented as a means-end objective network that provides a list of 16 means objectives and seven key fundamental objectives enabling higher-quality decision-making vis-à-vis privacy and utility. Such a framework transcends the industry approach to PIA by engaging organisational leadership to identify and undertake an indepth analysis of their means objectives and fundamental objectives and how they map out to contribute to a customised assessment of their privacy.

Our PIA framework not only inductively emerged based on healthcare leaders’ needs and actions but also responds to scholarly calls for a model that assesses risks, factors and relationships. This is illustrated by a recent study:

Privacy risk assessments need to go beyond traditional security assessments to consider the nature of the risks arising from the processing of personal data . . . This necessitates adopting a risk model that defines the key risk factors that have impacts on the privacy of data subjects and establishes a conceptual relationship among these factors. (Alshammari & Simpson, 2018, p. 86)

## 7. Contributions to research and practice

This study contributes to the limited literature on privacy and operational utility in several ways. Our first contribution to the extant IS and health informatics research literature resides in focusing on the symbiotic relationship between the two focal and interdependent elements of responding to information privacy threats while maintaining utility. Our focus on the healthcare domain is not arbitrary. The healthcare domain faces stringent regulations, such as the HIPAA, which requires rigorous protection of patient health information (PHI). Some privacy countermeasures can stand in the way of healthcare delivery and thus suggest a struggle between saving lives and protecting PHI.

Our second contribution resides in the identification of several characteristics related to information privacy and operational utility. These characteristics, presented in the form of means objectives, indicate what healthcare executives care about when dealing with this issue. Using the systematic VFT methodology, we identified the key objectives associated with strategically responding to information privacy threats without impeding operational utility. Our results identify a means-end objectives network with 16 means objectives and seven fundamental objectives that executives must consider when balancing between privacy and operational utility.

Finally, we propose a PIA theoretical framework that can help healthcare executives find the right balance between privacy and operational utility. Current PIA studies focus more on how to implement a PIA than on considering its influence on utility (Ahmadian et al., 2018; Vemou & Karyda, 2019). The limited literature that has considered both PIA and utility focuses more on technical influences, such as anonymisation and location privacy-preserving mechanisms (Mohammady et al., 2018; Riaz et al., 2018). Most extant studies focusing on privacy and utility (and typically using the privacy calculus model) are executed at the individual level. By contrast, our study contributes to this nascent research stream at the organisational level.

## 7.1. Implications for practice

With recent regulatory pressures and public media exposure, healthcare organisations are more engaged than ever in achieving information privacy. However, if this stands in the way of practicing medicine, privacy does not prevail. The findings of this study are important and have useful practical implications for healthcare organisations.

The means-end objective network points out some negative outcomes of implementing privacy safeguards, such as information unavailability, usability issues and workarounds. Thus, healthcare leaders need to account for these objectives to make sound decisions about privacy safeguards. A careful analysis of privacy safeguards because of their impact on day-to-day operations would be a helpful tool for avoiding staf workarounds. This study analysed how healthcare organisations respond to information privacy threats, including the assessment of the business impacts of privacy safeguards. This analysis was accomplished by identifying and explaining the different objectives and their relationships, producing a parsimonious, elegant and useful framework.

An appropriate practical extension of this study would be to identify how healthcare executives could implement the means-end objective network. This study demonstrates the need to reach a balance between mitigating information privacy threats and maintaining operational utility. Future studies can achieve this balance by developing systematic instructions for how to conduct an assessment in healthcare settings, given the objectives identified in this study.

## 7.2. Limitations and future research

In this research, several limitations related to data collection and data analysis can be addressed by future studies. Regarding data collection through interviews, there was a possibility of the interviewer’s bias. Walsham and Sahay (Walsham & Sahay, 1999, p. 77) distinguished between the “outside observer” and the “involved observer”. The authors’ roles in our study can be described as that of outside observers. Consequently, potentially relevant knowledge might not have been captured. However, the first author, who conducted the interviews, has several years of industry experience in the healthcare environment. Such expertise, we argue, helped mitigate some of these potential limitations. Although our qualitative study approach provided rich data, future research should build on these findings by conducting further empirical and generalisable studies. Future studies may include informants’ educational backgrounds as a possible moderator of the privacy countermeasure[s] being implemented within an organisation.

Future research could extend these findings in several promising directions. The means-end objectives network highlights the importance of maximising information availability for patient treatment and care. Future studies could build on these objectives to increase generalisability. Finally, our research could benefit from further empirical testing in contexts other than healthcare.

## 8. Conclusion

Motivated by the significant increase in breaches and damages caused by privacy and security threats, the primary goal of this article is to examine the relatively unexplored issue of simultaneously responding to information privacy threats and maintaining operational utility in the context of healthcare. Using the notion of ascribing executives’ values in managing privacy threats, we used the VFT method to develop a value-driven framework presented as a means-end objective network. The analyses resulted in the emergence of 16 means objectives and seven key fundamental objectives. This study represents an initial step in understanding how organisations can leverage the VFT approach to foster a balance between maximising information privacy and maintaining operational utility. More broadly, this study advocates a shift to examining how – rather than with what tools – decision makers can strategically respond to information privacy threats and establish an information privacy agenda for their organisations. We developed a theoretical framework, PIA, that can serve as a bridge between privacy safeguards and operational utility to help healthcare executives find the right balance between privacy and operational utility. This work provides the foundation on which we ofered four propositions for further research in this area.
