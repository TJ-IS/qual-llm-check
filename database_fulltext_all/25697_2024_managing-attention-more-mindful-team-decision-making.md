# Managing attention: more mindful team decision-making

Valerie L Bartelt <sup>a</sup> and Alan R. Dennis <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Business Information and Analytics, University of Denver, 2101 S University Blvd, Denver, Colorado, USA; <sup>b</sup>ODT Department, Kelley Schoo of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana, USA

## ABSTRACT

Prior research has found that teams using computer-mediated communication (CMC) often make poor decisions because team members fail to pay attention to the information they receive from others. We developed a process designed to focus team members’ attention on one aspect of decision-making at a time. We divided the group decision-making process into three phases: 1) an initial period to contribute information, 2) a middle period of silence (i.e., no typing) to read others’ contributions, and 3) a final period to discuss the information. We designed a lab experiment to compare the information processing and decision-making of teams using this text-based CMC process to teams using text-based CMC with no intervention. Teams that used this CMC process contributed more information, recalled more information, committed fewer errors, and made better decisions. The efect sizes were large, about two times greater than the average efect sizes for the use of CMC. An examination of this new process suggests that it improved decision quality mostly by helping teams avoid errors.

KEYWORDS Mindfulness; attention management; computermediated communication; collaboration; information exchange; time

## 1. Introduction

Prior research has found that teams using text-based computer-mediated communication (CMC) often make poor decisions because team members ignore information they receive from others (Curtis et al., 2017; Dennis, 1996; Minas et al., 2014; Schmidt et al., 2001; van Knippenberg et al., 2004). This has been called the common information problem (Gigone & Hastie, 1993) because team members focus on common information known to all team members before the discussion begins (Cramton, 2001; Dennis, 1996; Stasser, 1992). They are not mindful of the contributions of other team members (Curtis et al., 2017; Weick & Roberts, 1993).

Many businesses today rely heavily on teams due to growing complexities within businesses (DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus, 2010; Salas et al., 1992). Our paper is especially relevant due to the recent COVID pandemic when remote work and CMC (both asynchronous and synchronous) have become essential (Hacker et al., 2020; Herath & Herath, 2020). Employees are increasingly using online discussions due to pandemic workfrom-home mandates and employee preferences. Much IS research on remote work during COVID-19 has been published, including IS opportunities and challenges (Fink, 2020) due to technologies available (Hacker et al., 2020; Herath & Herath, 2020; Waizenegger et al., 2020; Wrycza & Maślankowski) while based on “the new normal”, (Pan & Zhang, 2020). This topic has been researched at the organisational level (Conger, 2020; Mykytyn, 2020), in public administration (Gabryelczyk, 2020), and in the midst of government mandates (Waizenegger et al., 2020). The need for remote work driven by the COVID pandemic has increased the importance of research focusing on virtual teams and virtual team decision making (Hacker et al., 2020)

Our focus is on one way to redesign CMC-based work processes to improve team decision-making by reducing errors, increasing information contributed, and improving information recall. When teams communicate faceto-face, team members often fail to share important information (Gigone & Hastie, 1993; Stasser & Stewart, 1992; Stasser & Titus, 1985; Stasser, 1992; Thompson, 1991), likely because production blocking (the need to take turns speaking) and cognitive inertia (the tendency for sequential statements to focus on the same information) limit information sharing (Dennis, 1996). In contrast, when using text-based CMC, teams exchange more information than when they work face-to-face (Chidambaram, 1996; Dennis, 1996; Dennis et al., 1997-1998; Hedlund et al., 1998; Hightower & Sayeed, 1996; Schmidt et al., 2001), but teams’ decisions are seldom better in the text-based environment (Dennis et al., 2001). Teams fail to attend to new information because members engage in monologues – broadcasting their own information – rather than mindfully paying attention to others (Curtis et al., 2017).

In this paper, we propose a simple and easily implemented technique to improve team mindfulness. In a team context, mindfulness is a pattern of behaviour that shows “heedful interrelating” with other team members (Curtis et al., 2017; Weick & Roberts, 1993); it is paying attention to the contributions of others and integrating their information into one’s own mental model as well as contributing appropriate information back to the discussion that aligns with the needs of the team (Curtis et al., 2017). Paying attention to others is often challenging (Thatcher et al., 2018), so this research examines one approach designed to manage team members’ attention on contributing and attending to others’ information.

We argue that a process that better manages team members’ attention to balance between contributing information and attending to others’ information will lead to better quality decisions, due to increasing information that is contributed, increasing new information recalled, and reducing errors. In this paper, we examine a three-step process: 1) a time period for contributing information, 2) a time period for attending to others contributions (a period of silence in the middle of the discussion to focus on others’ contributions), and 3) a time period for discussing. We theorise that this threestep process will reduce information overload and lead to better quality decisions because this process promotes more mindful contribution of information and more mindful consideration of others’ information. This simple process is also novel: When was the last time you convened a face-to-face or online meeting that included a period of silence in which no one could talk or type? Although this is possible, it is not the norm. Thus, our research question is:

Does using a three-step process with separate steps for contributing information, attending to others’ information, and discussing information lead to better quality decisions (involving increased contributed information, more new information recalled and reduced errors) in comparison to a typical CMC decision-making process?

We used a lab experiment with 32 five-person student teams to investigate the efects of this three-step process. Teams that used this process contributed more information, recalled more information, committed fewer errors, and made better decisions. The efect sizes were large. Thus, we conclude that the use of this process significantly and meaningfully improves team decision making. The following section details the theory development leading to the hypotheses. We then present the methodology and the results of the experiment. Finally, we discuss why the threestep process is beneficial based on our findings, including implications for future research as to how this process could be expanded further and for practice as to how this process could be implemented successfully in real-world situations.

## 2. Prior theory and research

One of the most enduring models for examining team behaviour is the input-process-output model (Marks et al., 2001; Reiter-Palmon et al., 2017). This model has been instantiated in diferent forms across diferent studies (e.g., Dennis et al., 2001; Kozlowski & Ilgen, 2006; Nunamaker et al., 1991; Reiter-Palmon et al., 2017), but in essence, it argues that the outcomes of teamwork are influenced by inputs (e.g., group, task, context, technology) and the processes used by the team (Nunamaker et al., 1991). Processes include both social processes (e.g., communication) and individual cognition processes (e.g., how individuals update their mental models) (Reiter-Palmon et al., 2017). Thus, some elements of the process are inherently group-level phenomena (e.g., participation equality, information exchange), and other elements are individual-level phenomena (e.g., satisfaction, trust) (Reiter-Palmon et al., 2017). Much initial research focused on team-level tasks that result in goals, or outcomes (e.g., a decision, product development, team commitment) (Marks et al., 2001), while more recent research has focused on how diferent processes influence both team-level and individuallevel outcomes (Marks et al., 2001; Raghuram et al., 2019; Reiter-Palmon et al., 2017).

Much research has examined teams using technology over the past four decades (Dennis et al., 2001; Fjermestad & Hiltz, 1998; Raghuram et al., 2019). Raghuram et al. (2019) identified three distinct, rather separate clusters of research: computer-mediated work (e.g., teams using diferent work processes or diferent types of collaboration technology), telecommuting (e.g., individuals working remotely), and virtual teams (e.g., team dynamics (e.g., conflict and trust)) in teams separated in time and space). Our work falls into the computer-mediated work cluster, which focuses on consequences of using computermediated communications (CMC) and other media for communication processes, problem-solving, decision-making, and productivity. There are many possible outcomes of interest, but the most common studied triumvirate is efectiveness, eficiency, and member satisfaction (Dennis et al., 2001). Research in this cluster has often examined the factors that afect outcomes, most commonly efectiveness using measures such as decision quality and idea generation (Raghuram et al., 2019).

In this paper, we focus on the efectiveness of teams performing ad-hoc multicriteria decision-making tasks using text-based CMC. The primary outcome of interest is decision quality – due to contributed information, information processed, and errors – but, as we note later, we also include team member satisfaction and perceptions. Multicriteria decisionmaking is a common type of task (e.g., personnel selection, project selection, investment) assigned to teams because teams have access to more information than any one individual acting alone (Hackman, 1990). We focus on ad-hoc or non-routine decisionmaking because routine decisions are often standardised with well-understood prescribed processes (Briggs et al., 2009; De Vreede & Briggs, 2018; Santanen et al., 2000). Much work by teams (Duhon & Hoch, 2017) and much prior research (Dennis et al., 2001; Fjermestad & Hiltz, 1998) involves teams performing non-routine tasks.

Members of ad-hoc teams often bring some unique information to the team, such as functional knowledge, regional expertise, detailed information about a possible alternative, or simply diferent stakeholder representation in the decision (Bell & Kozlowski, 2002). This diversity of information and viewpoints can lead to better decisions because the team as a whole has access to more information (Hackman & Kaplan, 1974; Hackman, 1990). This situation, in which team members possess common information plus unique information, has been called a “hidden profile” task (Stasser & Stewart, 1992). Team members form their initial decision preferences based on both the common and unique information that they individually possess, so team members often begin the discussions with diferent initial preferences.

During multi-criteria decision tasks, team members contribute their own information and preferences, learn the information and preferences of others, and work together to make a shared team decision from a range of options. The exchange of information is important because this is the basis for an integrated team decision (Curtis et al., 2017; Stasser & Stewart, 1992; Stasser & Titus, 1985). Sharing unique information is important because it has the greatest potential to change the team member’s pre-decision preferences (Curtis et al., 2017; Myers & Lamm, 1976; Vinokur et al., 1975), yet teams often overlook it (Dennis, 1996; Gigone & Hastie, 1993).

## 2.1. The common information problem

The common information problem refers to the issue that information known to all team members is likely to be more influential than information known to only some team members (Cramton, 2001; Gigone & Hastie, 1993; Kerr & Tindale, 2004; Reimer et al., 2017). The common information problem has been well documented in teams without technology (Gigone & Hastie, 1993; Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch, 2009; Stasser & Stewart, 1992; Stasser & Titus, 1985) as well as those using CMC (Curtis et al., 2017; Dennis, 1996; Hightower & Sayeed, 1996; Minas et al., 2014; van Knippenberg et al., 2004). This issue has also been researched at the organisational level, including how individuals optimally share knowledge within a firm (Grant, 1996; Moorman & Miner, 1997). This issue often has a more positive connotation in organisational research because common information enables team members to work together (Bechky, 2003; Carlile, 2002; Grant, 1996; Spender, 1996).

Extensive research shows that when communicating face-to-face without technology, team members prefer to focus on common information known to all team members and tend not to contribute the unique information known only to them; however, often this is the information that is important to the decision (Gigone & Hastie, 1993; Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch, 2009; Mohammed & Dumville, 2001; Reimer et al., 2017; Stasser & Stewart, 1992; Stasser & Titus, 1985). There are plausible theoretical reasons that could explain this tendency for the team discussion process to focus on “what everybody knows” (Sunstein & Hastie, 2014) rather than mindfully contributing and considering new information.

CMC ofers several process advantages over faceto-face communication that theory argue should help overcome the common information problem because it addresses some of the underlying causes (Curtis et al., 2017; Dennis, 1996; Nunamaker et al., 1991). The parallelism of CMC removes production blocking because no one is prevented from speaking at any point (Nunamaker et al., 1991). All CMC comments are recorded, which reduces attention blocking because participants can disengage from the conversation to think without missing the comments of others (Nunamaker et al., 1991). CMC enables anonymity, so social pressure to conform to the opinions of the group majority is reduced (Nunamaker et al., 1991), but anonymity does not address the social signalling of what information is important. CMC also does not address information recall.

A long history of empirical research has shown that the common information problem also exists when teams use CMC (Staples & Webster, 2008; van Knippenberg et al., 2004; Wang & Haggerty, 2009). One of the first studies to investigate information exchange in CMC was that by Dennis (1996). He found that teams using CMC shared more information, but team members usually ignored the information from others and, thus, made no better decisions than teams working face-to-face.

This pattern of ignoring information from others leading to poor decisions has been noted in numerous subsequent CMC studies (e.g., Hightower & Sayeed, 1996; Hilmer & Dennis, ; Minas et al., 2014; van Knippenberg et al., 2004). Thus, teams often experience the common information problem when they use CMC, but the central cause is the opposite from faceto-face discussion. In face-to-face discussions, team members often fail to contribute unique information, so it cannot be considered (Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch, 2009). In contrast, during CMC discussions, team members often contribute unique information, but it is not considered (Curtis et al., 2017; Dennis, 1996).

CMC communication often devolves into “multiple monologues” with many people contributing and few people mindfully considering others’ comments (Dennis & Valacich, 1994). Yet, information exchange and mindful consideration of information from others is critical to good decision-making (Curtis et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2012; Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch, 2009; Robert et al., 2008). A failure to exchange information mindfully is likely a primary reason why meta-analyses find that CMC use does not materially improve decision-making (Dennis et al., 2001; Fjermestad & Hiltz, 1998).

The key question is why do team members interacting through CMC tend to ignore information from others? And perhaps more importantly, what can we do about it?

## 2.2. Attention to others’ information

We believe that one important piece of this puzzle lies in how team members choose to focus their attention. Team members need to perform two tasks for efective, mindful discussions: they need to contribute the information they have and attend to the information from others in order to integrate new information into their mental models (Curtis et al., 2017; Heninger et al., 2006; Stasser, 1992). Attending to and integrating new information from others into one’s mental model has been called “representing” (Curtis et al., 2017), so we will use this term. These two tasks (contributing and representing) are naturally separated during faceto-face communication because only one person can speak at once. When someone speaks, you cannot contribute your information – you must wait, and social norms suggest that you pay attention to the person speaking while you wait (Heninger et al., 2006).

When team members use a traditional text-based CMC process, there is no forced separation of these two tasks. One can contribute information or read others’ information as desired (Nunamaker et al., 1991). One theoretical benefit of text-based CMC is the ability for team members to disengage from discussions – to pause and think about the information from others without missing newly contributed information as would happen in face-to-face discussion (Nunamaker et al., 1991; Walther, 1996). It is possible to introduce a pause in face-to-face teams during decision-making; however, we argue that this may be easier to implement in the CMC setting where the tool can enforce this process. Further, the traditional textbased CMC discussion that has been used in organisations and countless prior research studies commingles contributing and representing, thus enabling individuals to decide how to allocate their attention (e.g., Curtis et al., 2017; Dennis, 1996; Minas et al., 2014; Schmidt et al., 2001).

Individuals cannot efectively perform two tasks at one time (Kolfschoten, 2011; Meyer & Kieras, 1997a, 1997b; Pashler, 1994; Shaft & Vessey, 2006), so they must choose how to allocate their attention to these two competing tasks (Heninger et al., 2006). Empirical research shows that team members using text-based CMC usually focus their attention on contributing the information they have and devote less attention to representing (Dennis, 1996; Hightower & Sayeed, 1996; Hilmer & Dennis,). Heninger et al. (2006) compared the performance of individuals who thought they were members of a team (and were asked to both contribute and read the discussion) to individuals who were instructed only to read the discussion. Individuals who thought they were members of a team performed both tasks, remembered less information and were less likely to change their decision from their incorrect pre-decision preference.

We conclude that individuals using text-based CMC have an attention problem. CMC provides the capability to disengage from discussions and think about the information from others, but empirical evidence shows that team members do not take advantage of this. When enabled to perform these two tasks (read or write) concurrently, team members typically chose to focus on contributing their information rather than mindfully attending to others’ information. The net result is a failure to integrate new information from others into their mental models and overreliance on information received prior to the discussion, leading to poor decisions (Heninger et al., 2006).

## 2.3. Managing mindful attention

Like any resource, attention can be managed – at least to some extent (Davenport & Beck, 2001; Stanko & Beckman, 2015). We often think of attention as voluntary, or something that is dificult to control (Beck, 1993; Davenport & Beck, 2001). Yet, attention can be deliberately captured and directed at a target (Biocca et al., 2007; Dunn et al., 2011; Stanko & Beckman, 2015). For example, the movie previews in a theatre before the start of the show direct our attention to other films (Davenport & Beck, 2001). Managing mindful attention may be more challenging. Although theatres can direct our attention to movie previews, we do not have to watch them mindfully. However, managing mindful attention is common in education settings, where instructors strive to manage students’ attention to focus on important material (Mayer & Moreno, 2003).

Mindfulness is heedful interactions with one’s surroundings and attention to new information (Weick & Roberts, 1993). It is observing the environment and refining understanding based on new information (Thatcher et al., 2018). This can include an awareness of multiple perspectives and openness to new information (Langer & Moldoveanu, 2000; Sternberg, 2000; Sun et al., 2017; Thatcher et al., 2018; Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutclife, 2006).

In a team context, mindfulness is paying attention to information from others and integrating it into one’s own mental model as well as contributing appropriate information back to the discussion that aligns with the needs of the team (Curtis et al., 2017). Mindful interaction is characterised by understanding others’ perspectives (“representing”) and aligning one’s contributions with other team members (“contributing”) (Curtis et al., 2017). Representing is the attentive consideration of others’ contributions and the integration of that information into one’s mental model, so team members have a shared mental model that can be used to make the decision. Developing a shared mental model is dificult when using text-based CMC (Miranda & Saunders, 2003), but it is important because team members will be unable to align their contributions unless they understand the task in the same way (Curtis et al., 2017).

Contributing to a team using CMC primarily involves conveying information and preferences in a way that is aligned with the team’s needs. For example, team members often mirror each other’s words, syntax, grammar, and themes (Branigan et al., 2000). The aligning of contributions is an empathetic activity-one that is carried out as individuals mindfully attend to other team members and the needs of the task (Curtis et al., 2017). Aligning contributions is a mindful behaviour that is resilient and responsive to others rather than routine and mindless (Butler & Gray, 2006). Thus, mindful team interaction includes two separate behaviours: attention to other members’ information and the contribution of information aligned to the team’s needs (Curtis et al., 2017).

Attention management is perhaps easier in a textbased CMC environment than in life outside of technology because, in a CMC environment, the user’s attention is focused on what the technology presents (Stanko & Beckman, 2015). Davenport and Beck (2001) argue that technology can be designed to protect and manage attention, minimising information overload. They argue that technology can reduce the flow of information by filtering out information. Filtering out unimportant information is useful, but designing information-filtering rules for ad hoc team decision-making can be challenging.

We take Davenport and Beck’s (2001) primary argument (managing attention) but adopt a diferent strategy: directing attention to diferent parts of the task at diferent times. This strategy is also due to the limited capacity assumption from the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, which argues that people have limited processing capabilities (Mayer, 2005). Thus, we propose that managing members attention rather than allowing them to make poor choices in allocating attention between contributing and representing (Heninger et al., 2006) will improve team decision making.

We build on the work of Marks et al. (2001), who argue that teamwork consists of multiple episodes (input-process-output cycles) that run sequentially and/or simultaneously. Each episode is goal directed and is usually identifiable by the nature of the inputs, processes and outputs associated with it (Marks et al., 2001). These episodes may be long or short depending on the task. An episode that starts with strategy formulation, moves to action, and considers interpersonal processes may improve team performance (Marks et al., 2001). Other research has also explored time pacing during the action process (Krancher et al., 2018; Massey et al., 2003; Waller et al., 2002).

We argue that a decision process with specifically designed episodes (input-process-output cycles: Marks et al., 2001) will improve team performance. We build on Media Synchronicity Theory, which argues that teamwork is comprised of two fundamental communication processes: the conveyance (including consideration) of information, and the convergence on its meaning (Dennis et al., 1997-1998). We propose that a process with a dedicated time period for contributing one’s own information plus a dedicated time period for representing others’ information (conveyance), and a dedicated time period for discussion (convergence) will reduce information overload, improve mindfulness, and lead to better decisions. By separating these two tasks and managing team members’ attention to focus on each task one at a time, we theorise that text-based CMC discussions will become more mindful. This results in a three-step episode-based process which we call a Management of Attention (MOA) process: 1) a time period for contributing information, 2) a time period for thinking about the contributions of others, and 3) a time period for discussing the information to arrive at a consensus (see Figure 1).

The MOA process begins with a step devoted to contributing information. Team members are instructed to share the information they have with other team members. We argue that a separate step dedicated to the contribution of information will reduce attention blocking and improve contributing. It will provide time for team members to recall all the information they have better, potentially addressing biased information recall that contributes to the common information problem. It will also reduce the social pressure against contributing information that challenges the group majority and the implicit bias that the majority is right because the process explicitly directs team members to contribute what they know. Taken together, these three efects should result in team members contributing more information to the discussion. Thus:

Figure 1. Management of attention (MOA) process.

H1: Teams that use the Management of Attention process will contribute more information than teams that use a traditional CMC process.

The second step is representing (i.e., mindful attention to the information from others). We theorise that a separate step dedicated to representing will result in more mindful attention to others’ information and better integration of new information into one’s mental model, so that the team members develop a shared mental model. Team members often immediately reject information that challenges their existing mental model (Minas et al., 2014). However, when individuals have more time to consider such information that might be initially rejected, the information is often more persuasive and results in a revision to the mental model (Kumkale & Albarracin, 2004; Nemeth, 1986; Pratkanis et al., 1988). This “sleeper efect”, by which a message that is initially discounted becomes more persuasive after time passes (Pratkanis et al., 1988), has also been observed when team members holding minority opinions are given time to persuade others to change their views (Nemeth, 1986).

Traditionally, the revision of one’s mental model to include new information from others has been measured by asking team members to recall information after completing the task. This change in one’s mental model is measured by counting the unique information recalled that was not known prior to the discussion because the only way one could have obtained this information is by reading and remembering it (Heninger et al., 2006). This is not a perfect measure, but it is a widely used proxy because all information recalled must have been read and thought about enough to remember it even though not all the information read and thought about is recalled (Dennis, 1996). Thus:

H2:Teams that use the Management of Attention process will recall more unique information than teams that use a traditional CMC process.

Mindful behaviour is indicated by a lack of errors (Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutclife, 2006) because errors are more likely when attention is not focused (Weick & Sutclife, 2006). When mindfulness breaks down, team members make errors by failing to contribute needed information and failing to represent the information they receive from others in their mental models (Curtis et al., 2017). Communication is shaped more by each member’s pre-discussion knowledge and preferences than shared facts (Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutclife, 2006). Attention is focused internally (in this case, the information known before team discussions began) and not to the global situation facing the team (in this case, the information from others) (Weick & Roberts, 1993). Key information and activities are overlooked (Chidambaram & Tung, 2005; Streufert & Streufert, 1978), and there is less understanding of the unfolding discussion (Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutclife, 2006). This may lead to a focus on internal information leading to error-prone emotional decision-making that is disconnected from the actual information available to the team (Weick & Sutclife, 2006).

Errors in team decision-making are often due to a failure to communicate efectively, either because a person fails to share enough information or because a person fails to pay attention to the information contributed by others (Curtis et al., 2017). When team members interact mindfully, there should be fewer miscommunications and fewer errors. Therefore, we theorise that teams using this MOA process will display more mindful behaviour as indicated by fewer errors in contributing and representing. Thus:

H3: Teams that use the Management of Attention process will make fewer errors in contributing and representing than teams that use a traditional CMC process.

The ultimate objective of the MOA process is to improve decision quality. Improved contributing and representing improves the quality of team decisions (Curtis et al., 2017) as does fewer errors (Sun et al., 2017; Sunstein & Hastie, 2014; Thatcher et al., 2018; Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutclife, 2006). However, more information does not always produce higher quality decisions (Chidambaram & Tung, 2005; Scheidel, 1986).

It is common for team members to have slightly diferent information (Robert et al., 2008; Stasser & Titus, 1985; Stasser, 1992; Sunstein & Hastie, 2014). Some information is common and known to all team members, but teams are often comprised of members from diferent functions, regions, responsibilities, expertise, and so on, so each team member brings some unique information known only to them (Robert et al., 2008; Stasser, 1992). Sharing and understanding this unique information is critical to making the best decision (Curtis et al., 2017; Robert et al., 2008; Stasser & Titus, 1985; Stasser, 1992). When team members are not mindful (i.e., they fail to share information and/or fail to think about the information they receive from others), this unique information will not be considered, and thus teams will make worse decisions than teams whose members share, read, and think about information (Curtis et al., 2017; Dennis, 1996).

We theorised above that the MOA process will result in improved contributing and representing as well as fewer errors. Teams will exchange more information and be more likely to read and think about more information – steps that are essential to successful decisions on hidden profile tasks, where each participant receives some but not all of the relevant information that will lead to the correct choice (Curtis et al., 2017; Dennis, 1996; Stasser & Titus, 1985; Stasser, 1992). Thus, teams that use text-based CMC with the MOA process will make better decisions than teams that use a traditional process:

H4: Teams that use the Management of Attention process will make better quality decisions than teams that use a traditional CMC process.

The usual triumvirate of team outcomes is efectiveness, eficiency, and team member satisfaction (see Dennis et al., 2001). Our focus was efectiveness (decision quality), and we held eficiency (time taken) constant. As an exploratory analysis, we also assessed satisfaction with the decision-making process.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Participants

Participants were undergraduate business students from a large US state university. One hundred and sixty participants worked as members of 32 five-person teams that were randomly assigned to the two treatments (i.e., 16 teams in the traditional CMC control condition and 16 in the experimental treatment that involved the MOA process). We chose five-person teams because teams of this size are common in organisations and have been commonly used in past research (Dennis et al., 2001; Fay et al., 2000;

Raghuram et al., 2019). Random assignment of participants to teams and random assignment of teams to treatments controlled for between subject diferences (e.g., intelligence, motivation, knowledge of the task or other team members). About 33% of participants were female. Their ages ranged from 18 to 23 with a mean age of 20.0; 31% were freshmen, 28% sophomore, 11% junior, and 29% senior. About 81% were Caucasian, 12% Asian, 4% Black, and 3% other.

## 3.1.1. Task

The task asked participants to choose students to admit to the university from a set of five possible candidates. This type of task was selected because the application process is familiar to students as they have successfully navigated it to gain admission to the university. This task has often been used in prior research (e.g., Dennis, 1996; Robert & Dennis, 2005). To encourage discussion, no more than three of the five candidates could be allowed admission to the university. The task was designed as a hidden profile task following the normal procedure established by Stasser and Titus (1985) that has been replicated in hundreds of studies (e.g., Dennis, 1996; Robert & Dennis, 2005). This type of task is commonly faced by ad-hoc teams in organisations (Robert et al., 2008). In a hidden profile task, each team member has a subset of the total information available, reflecting the fact that in organisations, diferent team members have more information about some aspects of the task and less about other aspects. Most team members think about the task before the first meeting, and in the process of thinking about the task, form some initial preference (s) about the outcome before the team meets.

Therefore, in this study, each team member was given incomplete information and asked to make a decision based on it. The incomplete information consists of both common information, which is known to every participant, and unique information, which is known to only one participant. This incomplete information will lead team members to develop pre-discussion preferences that may vary from others because they each have received diferent information (no information was in conflict); some of these preferences may be the correct decision, but some may not. When team members discuss the information and make a team decision, they must consider essentially all of the information to reach the correct decision.

The task information was based on the information and standards used for admissions at the university the participants attended. We worked with three assistant directors in the university’s undergraduate admissions ofice to develop the task and ensure the information for candidates designed to be admitted and rejected was appropriate. After the task was designed, the director of admissions reviewed the task materials and identified which candidates would be admitted and which would be rejected. These matched the decisions that the materials were intended to produce and were used as a scoring key for determining the teams’ decision quality.

Eighteen pieces of information for each candidate (90 in total) were distributed among the team members. Consistent with prior research, each participant was provided with six pieces of common information: the candidate’s name, gender, GPA, verbal SAT score, maths SAT score, and intended major (see Dennis, 1996; Robert & Dennis, 2005). The common information does not lead the participants to the correct admissions decision. Similarly, consistent with prior research (Dennis, 1996; Robert & Dennis, 2005), two unique pieces of information were provided to each of the five participants (including high school class rank, quality of the high school, parents’ connection to the university, commitment to their major, place of residence, motivation to attend, missing courses, letters of recommendation, grade trend over the years, and extracurricular activities). Two facts contained in the unique information should have led to a denial of two applicants (one student was missing two courses and another student had a lower class rank from a poorquality school).

Participants were first asked to read and learn facts about the candidates whose information would be shared. Participants had four minutes to read and come to an individual decision based on incomplete information designed to lead participants to a suboptimal decision (individual decisions were recorded). Then, 15 minutes were allowed for participants to work as a team to share, read, and discuss their information in order to reach a team decision. Prior to participating in the team decision, all participants were informed that they had received incomplete information and that each participant had information that was known only to them so that it was important to share the information they had and read the information from others to reach good decisions.

## 3.1.2. Treatments

There were two treatments. Participants in both treatments communicated through Gmail’s Google chat software similar to software used in prior studies (e. g., Curtis et al., 2017) and those available from other vendors, such as Slack or the chat feature in Microsoft Teams; see Figure 2. Latency on our network was about one second. Participants were seated in separate cubicles, and no verbal discussion occurred.

The first treatment was a control condition using a traditional process that has been used in prior studies of CMC. Participants were allowed 15 minutes to discuss the task with their team members and reach a team decision. They were instructed to share their information, read and think about the information they received from other team members, and discuss the information to reach the best decision.

The experimental treatment was the MOA process with its three steps: 1) contributing, 2) representing, and 3) discussing. The total duration of time was the same in both treatments; however, a specific amount of time was allocated for each of the three steps in the MOA treatment. We selected five minutes for the first step, four minutes of silence, and up to six minutes for discussion to reach a team decision. As a manipulation check, we examined the typed transcripts to ensure that the teams used the MOA process as designed. All teams followed its design, so the manipulation was successful.

It is dificult to provide a theoretical rationale for the choice of specific times for each step, so two pilot tests were used to gauge the appropriate length for each step. No one who participated in any of the pilot tests participated in the main study. We initially designed a 12- minute process split into three steps of contributing for five minutes, representing for three minutes, and discussing of four minutes. We tested this with five teams in the control treatment and seven in the MOA treatment. The resulting mean decision quality for the control was 3.20 and the MOA 4.14; we did not formally code the two mindfulness variables, but visual inspection suggested that the teams in the MOA treatment contributed more information and recalled more unique information. We asked participants to comment on whether the length of time was suficient for each step using an open-ended survey question, and multiple comments asked for more time to discuss.

Based on the first pilot test, we increased the total time to 15 minutes. We kept the contributing step at five minutes and increased the representing step to four minutes and the discussion step to six minutes. We conducted a second pilot test of the revised MOA process using five teams with the sole objective of determining whether the increased time was perceived to be suficient. Responses indicated the revised time was appropriate with no comments about needing more time.

## 3.1.3. Dependent variables

There were four primary dependent variables plus three perceptual measures. The first was decision quality, which was measured at the team level. It was the number of correct decisions to admit or reject each candidate. There were five candidates, so decision quality could vary from 0 to 5. Teams were instructed to admit at most three candidates; one team admitted only two candidates, but all other teams admitted three.

The second dependent variable was the amount of information contributed to the discussion, also measured at the team level. Team discussions were coded to count the number of pieces of common and unique information that appeared in the transcript. Only correct information was counted. Two raters independently coded all the transcripts. Inter-rater reliability was adequate (Cohen’s kappa=.84).

The third dependent variable was individual information recall, measured at the individual participant level. Participants were asked to fill out a questionnaire with a series of five open-ended questions (one question for each candidate) and write all the information they remembered about each candidate. Two raters independently coded this data to count the number of correct pieces of information recalled. The raters counted the unique information unknown to the participant at the start of the task because this measures the extent to which participants recalled information received from other participants during the discussion. The raters also coded common information recalled (which we used in a manipulation check). One rater coded all the data and the second coded data from 11 teams (approximately one-third) in order to assess inter-rater reliability. Interrater reliability was adequate (Cohen’s kappa=.94).

The fourth variable was the number of incidents displaying breakdowns in mindfulness leading to errors, measured at the team level. We analysed the process used by each team, focusing on the two primary mindfulness components of contributing and representing; that is, is there evidence that members committed errors related to a breakdown in mindfulness pertaining to contributing and/or representing?

Identifying errors during the discussion began by understanding what opinions participants had immediately before the discussion began. Prior to the team discussion, each participant read their own initial information and recorded their choice of which candidates they would admit based only on this incomplete information. We needed to control for these prediscussion preferences because they are not afected by which process the teams used (control or MOA) as they are formed prior to the start of discussion.

We examined the individual team members’ prediscussion choices and computed a projected baseline decision for each team before the discussion following the approach of Stasser and Titus (1985). When a majority of team members (three or more) reported they would admit or reject a candidate, we coded that as the projected baseline decision. This resulted in most teams’ baseline admitting three candidates, although seven teams had a baseline admitting four candidates and four had a baseline admitting only two.

Following the approach of Stasser and Titus (1985), we compared the baseline predicted decision for each team and with the final team decision to identify the diferences that might be due to the team discussion.

We identified 38 cases where the team’s final decision was diferent from the baseline projected decision, which might have been caused by statements made by team members during discussion or could be completely unrelated to these statements. We examined the transcripts to code team members’ statements that were related to each of the 38 diferences. Since our focus is on mindfulness, we coded the pattern of statements as suggesting mindful behaviour, suggesting a breakdown in mindfulness (errors), or being unrelated to mindfulness. We identified five categories; see Table 1. One category suggested mindful behaviour. Two categories were mindfulness breakdowns based on Curtis et al. (2017): a contributing breakdown (when teams failed to contribute information) and a representing breakdown (when teams ignored information contributed to the discussion). We also identified breakdowns due to a misunderstanding of the criteria and other breakdowns that did not fit the other four categories. Two raters independently examined the transcripts and coded the reason for each of these 38 diferences using the coding scheme above. Inter-rater reliability was adequate (Cohen’s kappa=.86). The four disagreements in coding were resolved by discussion.

There were three perceptual measures, all measured at the individual participant level. The first was perceived attention to others’ information, which was measured with three items from McNamara et al. (2008) (alpha = .71; see Table 2). The second was perceived integration of others’ information (i.e., representing), which was measured with six items from McNamara et al. (2008) (alpha= .78; see Table 2). The third was participant satisfaction with the decision-making process, which was measured with five items from Dennis (1996) plus a new item (alpha = 0.83; see Table 2).

## 3.1.4. Procedures

The experiment took place in a behavioural research lab. Upon entering the lab, participants signed the consent form. Next, they were seated at a computer in a cubicle, so they could not easily see other participants. The experiment began by showing the participants how to use the chat tool. Then, the participants performed a warm-up task. In the terms of Wheeler and Valacich (1996), this was training on the process.

The experiment task began by giving participants four minutes to read the pre-decision information, which contained both unique and common information about the candidates. They were then asked to make their individual decision to admit no more than three candidates to the university and complete a questionnaire indicating their decision.

Next, participants worked with their team under either the control or MOA conditions to share, read, and discuss the information in order to arrive at a team decision. The researcher guided the teams through the process by informing them either of the control instructions or the MOA instructions delivered at the three points in time as the process unfolded. Wheeler and Valacich (1996) would call this facilitation to improve the faithful appropriation of the process.

## 5. Discussion

We found that team decision making can be improved by using a simple three-step process designed to pace team work (Mayer, 2005), based on the concept of managing attention (Davenport & Beck, 2001; Stanko & Beckman, 2015) by implementing an explicit episode pattern (input-process-output cycle: Marks et al., 2001), The process, which we called a Management of Attention (MOA) process, follows Media Synchronicity Theory (Dennis et al., 1997-1998) by separating the episode into conveyance (one step devoted to contributing information, and one step committed to reading others’ information), and convergence (a final step allotted to discussing information to form a consensus). This episodebased process significantly and meaningfully increased the amount of information contributed, increased the amount of new information recalled, reduced the number of errors and improved decision quality. The efect sizes were large – larger than the efect size of selecting the right technology in the first place. Table 5 summarises the hypotheses and the results.

The structures and social norms of face-to-face communication generally promote mindful attention to others by alternatively guiding participants to speak or listen and naturally guiding participants to listen more than speak (Curtis et al., 2017). Face-to-face information processing in groups is driven by both social and epistemic motivation (De Dreu et al., 2008). Text-based CMC lacks these structures and social norms; team members are free to allocate their attention to either contributing or attending to others (Nunamaker et al., 1991; Walther, 1996) and often choose poorly, allocating less attention to considering others’ information mindfully than they do to contributing their own information (Curtis et al., 2017; Dennis, 1996).

The MOA process was designed to alter this imbalance by allowing for an explicit input-process-output episode (Mark, et al., 2001) intended to manage team members’ attention to focus solely on contributing and then solely on representing. This improved both contributing and representing, so we conclude that the MOA process was successful in managing participants’ attention. The episode (Marks et al., 2001) that we designed followed the recommendations of Media Synchronicity theory (Dennis et al., 1997-1998) to focus on the fundamental conveyance and convergence processes. We believe that this MOA process can be applied successfully to online teamwork as well as incorporated into online collaborative learning settings (Arbaugh & Benbunan-Finch, 2009) in tools such as Microsoft Teams or Slack.

One of the goals of the MOA process is to increase mindfulness. Errors – and the reduction of errors – were the genesis of research on mindfulness (Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutclife, 2006). Our research examined errors where teams made worse decisions than what an initial majority would predict. In about half the cases when a team in the control condition deviated from the predicted decision, one or two individuals lobbied for their pre-discussion choices, and information challenging those choices was not contributed or overlooked. These errors can be attributed to breakdowns in mindfulness. Team members focused on the common information known to all and overlooked the unique information. In contrast, the MOA process reduced these errors by encouraging team members to be more mindful – to share their information and think about others’ information rather than focus on their own preferences and common information.

## 5.1. Limitations

This research sufers from the usual limitations of laboratory research. We studied undergraduate participants working on an artificial task for a short period of time, which is diferent from collaboration with organisations that often involves professionals working on larger tasks for longer periods of time. Laboratory research has often been criticised for the use of students because students can difer from the general population (Compeau et al., 2012). Undergraduates tend to be more intelligent and come from richer households than the general population (Gordon et al., 1986) because they are in training to be professionals (Compeau et al., 2012). Thus, business students are not unlike business professionals, who tend to be college-educated, more intelligent, and come from richer households (Byington & Felps, 2010).

Research also suggests there are few diferences in behaviour between students and professionals (Fréchette, 2015, 2016). When diferences are found, the most commonly cited reason is lack of familiarity with the experimental task (Gordon et al., 1986). Professionals often work on tasks that are novel to them but are usually familiar with the context of the task. Thus, Gordon et al. (1986) recommend that experimental tasks be selected so that student participants are familiar with the decision context. Our task was selected to be familiar to our participants – all were familiar with college admission and understand the information present (e.g., GPA, SAT, extracurricular activities). Our analysis showed that most participants had a reasonable understanding of the relative importance of these criteria as used at their university, but even if their perceptions of the relative importance were diferent from those used at the university, these diferences only influenced one out of the 38 changes we observed.

A related issue for studies of technology is familiarity with the technology used. We used a commercial tool (Google Chat) that is commonly used by our student participants as well as teams in organisations. Although commonly used, Google Chat may not be an ideal platform for decision-making. Nonetheless, we believe it was appropriate in enabling our student teams to work together with little training.

## 5.2. Implications for future research

First and foremost, there is a need for more research to extend and test these initial findings with other tasks, participants, and types of CMC to understand the boundary conditions of these results better. For example, would this process produce similar benefits for asynchronous text discussions, such as email, or for synchronous meetings that use video and audio in place of or in combination with text discussion? It is also possible that our findings better fit certain decision-making tasks that do not involve multiple convergence cycles.

From a theoretical perspective, we need to focus more on the processes of team collaboration and the dance between contributing and representing (Curtis et al., 2017). We argued that one primary theoretical mechanism at work in text-based CMC is the ability of team members to choose to devote their attention to contributing or representing. This ability to choose breaks the usual balance that we see in face-to-face communication with its social norms that direct team members to take time away from contributing so they may listen to others (Curtis et al., 2017). Our MOA process manages members’ attention by directing them to focus attention on contributing and representing, thus returning to a balance more typical of face-to-face communication. This separate, more balanced attention to contributing and representing led to fewer errors and improved the quality of team decisions

Future research may be able to direct attention by changing the technology to embed a process. Conversely, it may be possible to use digital nudges (Schneider et al., 2018) to influence attention more subtly. For example, acknowledging the receipt of a team member’s message may be helpful in encouraging team members to direct their attention to the information they receive from others. We need new theory, new processes, and new technical interventions that directly address this balance between contributing and representing.

This also raises the larger issue of individual cognition during teamwork (Kolfschoten, 2011), which has received far less research than its social interaction aspects (see Dennis et al., 2001; Fjermestad & Hiltz, 1998). Curtis et al. (2017) compared two diferent collaboration technologies and found that use of the diferent technologies had a medium-sized efect on mindfulness. Our research shows it is possible to use a process to manage the individual attention of team members (with large efect sizes). We need more research on individual cognition in teamwork to determine ways to influence individual cognition deliberately to influence the outcomes of teamwork.

Our process analysis suggests that the primary reason for the improved decision quality in the MOA process is that teams avoided errors. Discussions using a traditional CMC process actually reduced decision quality from the initial predicted majority decision. In 17 out of 25 cases where the final team decisions deviated from the initial projected decisions, the changes could be traced back to errors related to breakdowns in mindfulness, either because information contradicting a change was never contributed to the discussion or because it was ignored. One or two persuasive team members led the team to their preferred choices, which turned out to be wrong because they did not consider the information that other members had. In contrast, we traced only two of 13 cases in the MOA process to errors related to breakdowns in mindfulness. We need more research on errors in decision-making and how we can prevent them, including the potential afect other interpersonal factors, such as gender or personality, on identifying errors. We also need more research on other ways to promote mindfulness in teams (e.g., see Thatcher et al., 2018).

We chose times for each of the three steps in the MOA process that appeared to be appropriate and revised them based on a pilot test. It is quite possible that these times are not optimal. Likewise, we used fixed, pre-determined times, and it is possible that a more flexible approach to time allocation would lead to better (or worse) decisions. It may be beneficial for team members to be able to self-regulate the duration of contributing and representing. For instance, buttons could be added to the software to indicate when team members felt ready to end contributing or representing. Future research should investigate other ways to manage the time devoted to contributing and representing.

Our research focused on the design and use of only one episode, because the task was a short-duration task, as commonly used in lab experiments. Marks et al. (2001) note that teams are likely to have multiple sequential and simultaneous episodes as they work on large projects, because large projects involve many subtasks. Thus, one important question for future research is how to best design and implement work processes using the MOA processes for larger projects that include multiple episodes.

New communication technology should be considered for future research in improving users’ decisionmaking. Technologies such as Jira and GitHub, which track views and ofer comments and recommendations to team members, should be examined to determine the degree to which each platform dissuades confirmation bias. Based on our findings, we believe that feedback on these platforms may be used primarily to justify one’s current opinions. However, it is possible that these platforms could be adjusted in such a way to minimise confirmation biases and encourage the MOA process – possibly by adjusting the timing in which confirming and disconfirming content is displayed.

Our study examined ad-hoc decision-making rather than routine decision-making. Research on the design of technology to support routine decisionmaking is often called collaboration engineering (Briggs et al., 2003; De Vreede & Briggs, 2018). Thus, we can make no claims about the efectiveness of the MOA process for routine decision-making teams. However, much prior research on mindfulness has focused on routine decision-making because it is very susceptible to breakdowns in mindfulness (Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutclife, 2006). Thus, we need more research on the use of the MOA process for routine decision-making.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

The primary implication for practice from our findings is that using a process with separate periods devoted to contributing information and to reading information from others is likely to reduce errors and improve performance. Perhaps the most challenging and counterintuitive part of this process is purposefully setting aside time during a meeting in which no one is permitted to talk or type. What would your colleagues say if in the middle of the next meeting you asked everyone to be quiet and think? Although plausible, silence is not normal. Pausing to think is not normal. Stop reading and take a moment to think about that.

Pausing to think is the antithesis of multi-tasking. Focusing attention on one task at a time enables us to be more mindful and to perform the one focal task better without distraction (Sun et al., 2017; Thatcher et al., 2018; Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick & Sutclife, 2006). This multi-step process has been alluded to in educational settings, suggesting that controlled pacing can improve learning in certain conditions (Mayer & Moreno, 2003). Prior research has found that those who are mindful act in a more ethical manner, especially during decision-making (Ruedy & Schweitzer, 2010). This may be due to the heightened awareness of the present that may deter typical unethical mindsets, including self-serving cognition (Epley & Caruso, 2004; Ruedy & Schweitzer, 2010) and unconscious biases (Bazerman et al., 2002; Ruedy & Schweitzer, 2010).

Implementing a process with time for typing and a period of silence to focus on others is simple. It enables people to be more attentive, aware, and in the moment. It has a meaningful efect on decision quality by helping teams avoid errors related to breakdowns in mindfulness. Thus, the central implication is simple: managers should develop agendas for mindful team decision-making that have separate times for contributing and silence to read and think about others’ contributions. Current CMC tools could be used to incorporate this process, including Slack or Microsoft Teams. The time allotted for each part of the decision-making process could be based on similar ratios that we used in this study (e.g., 35% contributing, 25% reading, 40% discussing), although more research is needed to identify ideal distributions. For instance, it is possible that complex business problems (e.g., an environmental emergency), would require a higher percentage of time in discussing the problem and resolution in comparison to the reading process. In this experiment, more time was allocated for initial contributing than reading because typing is slower than reading. Such time ensures that members can thoughtfully express themselves in writing. The most time was allocated for the discussion to ensure that members had time to reach consensus.

## 6. Conclusion

Our research found that managing team members attention during text-based CMC improved decision quality. Building on the idea that attention can be managed (Davenport & Beck, 2001; Stanko & Beckman, 2015), we developed a process that divided team interaction into three steps: 1) contributing information, 2) reading others’ comments, and 3) discussing. We termed this the Management of Attention, or MOA, process. We used a lab experiment to investigate whether the MOA process improved team decision-making compared to the control treatment. We found that the MOA process led teams to contribute more information, recall more information, make fewer errors, and make better decisions. The efect sizes were large.

Thus, the teams in which team members took time to pause and read others’ contributions – which can often be neglected during CMC communications (Curtis et al., 2017)—were the most successful in improving decision quality and minimising errors. The findings ofer practical and easily implemented implications for companies using CMC, which has become more popular due to the pandemic. CMC users can adopt this MOA process during their interactions, especially if they need to make team decisions during the pandemic while working from home. Our findings are also important for design science, which could assist in designing online communication platforms, such as Teams or Slack, to accommodate different activities in which users intend to engage, including the decision-making process.
