# Exploring physicians’ continuous use of clinical decision support systems

Mohamed Abouzahra<sup>a</sup>, Dale Guenter<sup>b</sup> and Joseph Tan<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>College of Business, California State University, Monterey Bay; <sup>b</sup>Department of Family Medicine, McMaster University; <sup>c</sup>DeGroote Schoo of Medicine, McMaster University

## ABSTRACT

Today, studying continuous clinical decision support system (CDSS) use is critical as continuity is a prerequisite to sustaining successful CDSS implementations. Previous health information technology (HIT) research has focused on HIT acceptance via information system-related constructs. Theoretical models explaining HIT use are limited and obfuscated other phases of HIT, such as continuous use in a clinical context. This study seeks to fill this gap by integrating context-related factors with IS factors in a theoretical framework that explains the antecedents of continuous use. To validate this model, data were collected from physicians after six months of using CDSS as well as system usage data from system logs. Threats to physician professional identity surpassed the intention to continue using CDSS, influencing its use by physicians in the first six months, and patients afected by the system influenced physician satisfaction with the system and, hence, their use of the system. This study reveals physicians’ continuous use of CDSS and explains the efects of healthcare contexts on physicians’ behaviour.

## 1. Introduction

Health Information Technology (HIT) refers to the electronic systems used by care providers and patients to manage healthcare data. Examples of HIT include electronic medical records (EMR) and clinical decision support systems (CDSS). While early studies investigated clinician acceptance of new HIT systems (Hossain et al., 2019; Mun et al., 2006; Pynoo et al., 2013) with few emphasising clinician continuance (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2011). This focus was justified given the relatively recent adoption of information systems (IS) in healthcare (Berner et al., 2005). Today, studying the continuous use of HIT is encouraged by several shifting factors. More government and payer incentives are tied to the efective use of HIT which implies longstanding use. For example, the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health act passed by the USCongress encourages “meaningful use” of HIT (Lin et al., 2019; Mennemeyer et al., 2016) implying sustained use. Furthermore, the current wide deployment of healthcare data analytics applications and precision medicine requires abundant patient data. HIT sustained and continuous use can create the data required by these applications while expanding their efectiveness.

Drawing on prior continuance behaviour research (Saleem & Herout, 2018; Varpio et al., 2015), this study explores the key factors influencing continuous use of health information systems and technology. However, past HIT continuance research has overlooked physicians’ professional identity and their relationships with patients. Professional identity refers to patient-related qualities and values acquired via training and interactions in the medical community, which can impact physicians behaviour and motivation to use the best tools for patients’ needs. Investigating the role of contextual healthcare factors such as professional identity in driving HIT use is essential as previous researchers (Benbasat & Barki, 2007; Holden & Karsh, 2010; Venkatesh et al., 2011) have noted that technology adoption theories such as the technology adoption model (TAM) were created for the business context, leaving room to include more contextual factors when studying other IS types. Furthermore, physicians’ professional identity and their obligation towards patients have been found to influence physicians’ decision to abandon the use of healthcare systems after accepting them (Alohali et al., 2020; Dyb et al., 2021). Accordingly, this study examines physicians professional identity and patient relationships as fundamental contextual factors vis-à-vis continuous HIT use.

Significantly, we integrate the IS and healthcare perspectives of HIT to study physicians’ continuous use of a knowledge-based CDSS (KB-CDSS). KB-CDSSs, a sub-category of CDSS, facilitate decision making by applying evidence-based clinical guidelines based on patients’ medical information. For example, the pain management KB-CDSS in this study proposes a course of action based on patients’ age, weight, and X-ray images vis-à-vis published clinical guidelines. KB-CDSSs are widely used to improve care quality while reducing medical errors; hence, further understanding physicians’ use of these systems is vital (Belard et al., 2017; Cheerkoot-Jalim et al., 2019). Accordingly, the goal is to perform theory-based research with not only traditional IS constructs such as usefulness and ease of use but also contextual factors impacting medical professionals’ attitudes and HIT use.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the extant literature, including CDSS and professional identity. Section 3 details past research models leading to the theoretical framework followed by the study methodology in Section 4, including data collection and analysis.Section 5 presents the study results and discusses the key findings. Finally, Section 6 ofers insights into the theoretical and practical impacts as well as study limitations and future prospects.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Continuous HIT use

Continuous use is key to sustaining a beneficial lifespan ofIS (Bhattacherjee, 2001); indeed, organisations will not benefit with prevailing user resistance. Users are shown to abandon HIT after acquired by healthcare organisations (Barrett, 2018; Davidson et al., 2018; Kohli & Tan, 2016), especially physician users who highly value their professional identity in terms of autonomy and patient relationships (Tallis, 2006). Despite its usefulness, physicians are likely to resist if HIT appears to threaten their professionalism (Doolin, 2004). Currently, scant research emphasisingmostly business-oriented IS, such as corporate IS (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004; Bhattacherjee, 2001; Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010) and e-commerce applications (Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006), have limited generalisability to the HIT domain given the dependence of factors influencing IS continuance on the context of these systems(Venkatesh et al., 2011a). Hence, we build a novel framework to study physicians’ continuous KB-CDSS use. This model integrates the widely accepted attitude-behaviour (A-B) model (Eagly & Chaiken, 1993) with the DeLone-McLean IS success (D&M) model (DeLone & McLean, 1992).

The A-B model, unlike the TAM and other acceptance theories, includes constructs on the continuity of behaviour such as the “attitude towards the object”, reflecting attitude not towards the behaviour but the object (in our case, the HIT). This attitude is framed by one’s experience; it influences one’s decision to use the system sustainably. While the A-B model includes constructs already studied in the IS literature, such as utilitarian outcomes (perceived benefits [PB]), it also includes constructs surrounding the role of context in HIT use (normative outcomes). Moreover, the

A-B model is stable as it has been applied successfully to explain behaviour in diferent contexts (Maio et al., 2018). In explaining continuous HIT use in more depth, the A-B model addresses the gaps identified in prior HIT continuance research.

## 2.2. The role of CDSS in health care

CDSS is an information-communication system that assists users with timely, accurate, and relevant knowledge for care delivery (Greenes et al., 2018) with two essential features: (a) giving access to key patient information, enabling more informed and optimised decisions, and (b) including clinicians (physicians/ nurses) and patients (plus families) among users. Additionally, CDSS may be applied to patient monitoring and costly resource control, including sending alerts and reminders to clinicians.

Over time, CDSSs have gained acceptance and momentum for many reasons. First, technology advances and other HIT types, such as EHR, ofer a context in which to actualise CDSS benefits (Kawamoto et al. 2005). Second, HIT brings overwhelming information for physicians, requiring a filter for the most relevant information, especially with personalised medicine (Shortlife & Sepúlveda, 2018). CDSSs can provide targeted information to establish patient treatment regimens. Third, healthcare quality has been frustrated by increased medical errors, leading to approximately 98,000 preventable deaths annually in the US (Pham et al., 2012). CDSSs, especially used to alert physicians of possible overdose efects, augment care quality while reducing medication errors (Bates et al., 1999; Singer & Vogus, 2013). Finally, with patient-centred care calling for active patient involvement in their own treatment (Barry & Edgman-Levitan, 2012), new CDSSs, traditionally oriented towards clinicians, have been redeveloped to allow for shared physician-patient decision-making (Belard et al., 2017).

Conceptualised as both e-health and knowledgemanagement systems, KB-CDSSs may benefit users as (a) an evidence-based knowledge repository (Damiani et al., 2010); (b) a decision aid that draws on clinical guidelines and evidence-based medicine (Buenestado et al., 2013); and (c) a digital channel to integrate guidelines with patient information to enhance care quality.

## 2.3. Physicians’ professional identity (PID)

Physicians’ PID and dedication to patients (Real et al., 2009) have deepened patients’ respect for physicians assertive position in society, trusting them on matters linked directly to patient health and well-being. Key aspects of physicians’ PID include autonomy (Blumenthal, 2009), decision-making and judgement, and their focus on the patient’s best interest (Roland et al., 2011). These aspects are nurtured via medical education through ongoing communication in the physician community (Freidson, 1994).

This study examines PID and CDSS use via identity theory. Identity (Stryker, 1987) and social identity theory (SIT) (Abrams & Hogg, 1990) are complementary theories (Stets et al., 2000) defining the individual and social meaning of one’s identity – as distinct from others in identity theory (Stryker et al., 2000) and as part of a group or collective in SIT (Abrams & Hogg, 1990). Underlying these theories is the concept of selfcategorisation. While identity theory proposes that one categorises oneself by one’s role (one’s position or function) in the community, SIT describes selfcategorisation as associating oneself with a specific group. The outcome of self-categorisation is associated with meanings and expectations based on the individual’s role or group membership (Stets et al., 2000).

Physicians view their role in society as caring for patients and making decisions in their best interests (Tallis, 2006). Physicians regard these values as symbolising their commitment to patients beyond their “employment contract’ (Tallis, 2006). Resistance to new HIT is associated with threats to this role, such as negative efects on physician-patient communications (Mishra et al., 2012), threats to autonomy (Walter & Lopez, 2008), and threats to control over the care-taking process (Bhattacherjee et al., 2007). Conceptually, physicians’ PID has changed as a response to changes in patients’ awareness and role in the care process, PID changed from patients following physicians’ advice without questioning, to patients being empowered to make decisions about their own health (Johnson et al., 2018; Kilbride & Jofe, 2018) using innovative CDSSs to support patient-centred care. Hence, physicians must reconsider patient autonomy and expectations and work with patients to make decisions jointly. This level of coordination requires improved physician-patient communication to empower patients while maintaining the required control over decision-making (Ubel et al., 2017). This study examines the role of physicians’ autonomy with respect to external influence (influence other than patients’ autonomy) to capture the change of patientphysician autonomy.

Likewise, physicians identifying as members of the medical profession view this community membership as a major factor in afirming their autonomy and independence (Freidson, 1988). Evidently, the importance and power of the medical community membership in influencing physician behaviours and decisions cannot be overemphasised. Both PID and medical community membership are investigated as contextual factors influencing HIT use.

## 2.4. Physicians’ perceived quality of care improvement (PPQI)

Patient satisfaction in physicians’ decisions to adhere to clinical guidelines is an important factor that impacts physicians’ behaviour (Grol, 1990). However, patient satisfaction research involving HIT use is lacking. Past research (Sebo et al., 2015) shows that patients are generally very satisfied with their physicians. Here, the indirect efect of patient satisfaction on system use is measured by physicians’ perceptions of patients’ feedback on the care process. This feedback can be direct via communication or indirect through better adherence to advice. Based on this feedback, insight into patient satisfaction may be studied as an important contextual factor in physicians HIT use.

## 2.5. Is continuance & attitude

Extensive prior attitude research has led to several attitude-based theories such as the theories of reasoned action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1977) and planned behaviour (TPB) (Ajzen, 2011). While these theories underpin most IS adoption models, such as the TAM (Davis, 1989), the attitude construct was later dropped from adoption models (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2003). However, as evidenced in models explaining physicians’ guideline adoption with attitude as a key predictor of adoption (Pathman et al., 1996) and attitude being a key predictor of HIT and guideline use in healthcare studies (e.g., Cabana et al., 1999; Solà et al., 2014), healthcare researchers must reconsider attitude as a key factor in determining physicians adoption of guidelines (via HIT).

Physicians’ PID has a significant impact on their behaviour (Alohali et al., 2020; Dyb et al., 2021), and there are repeated calls by healthcare researchers and practitioners to involve patients in care processes because of the positive impact of this involvement on clinical outcomes (Bao et al., 2020). However, how PID and patient involvement influence HIT adoption received little discussion in HIT adoption literature. Accordingly, this study focuses on continuous CDSS use via the lens of physicians’ PID and the impact of patients’ attitudes towards the system by validating a theoretical model based on the A-B model.

## 3. Theoretical model

As the widely accepted TRA/TPB in prior research focus chiefly on cognitive beliefs but ignore identity and prior behaviour (Maio et al., 2018), we incorporate contextualised PID constructs by partly incorporating the A-B model. The A-B model was developed to fill the gaps of TRA/TPB by including normative and identity outcomes as attitude predictors (Maio et al., 2018). This model also considers previous behaviour as predictive of future behaviour, making it suitable for studying continued CDSS use. The A-B model was successfully used in studying behaviour in diferent contexts including IS (Suseno et al., 2021), healthcare (Yoo et al., 2021), and marketing (Verma et al., 2021).

While the A-B model serves as the primary model to encapsulate concepts of identity and normative and utilitarian outcomes (Eagly & Chaiken, 1993), it is nonetheless parsimonious and excludes the antecedents of these constructs. Indeed, both A-B and TPB ignore specific external beliefs influencing the model constructs (Ajzen, 2011; Maio et al., 2018), for which Ajzen (2011) proposed including context-specific factors. IS researchers (e.g., Gefen et al., 2003; Hung et al., 2012; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006) include such external beliefs to extend their theoretical models.

In our study, following a comprehensive literature review, five focus groups of family physicians were queried on external beliefs associated with HIT use. The external beliefs of system quality (SQ), information quality (IQ), and patient perceived quality of care improvement (PPQI) emerged, for which the D&M model was used to include these constructs.

The D&M model (DeLone & McLean, 1992) proposes that IS success is determined by SQ, IQ, use, satisfaction, and benefits. In 2003, DeLone added SQ as another IS success dimension (Delone & McLean, 2003), suggesting that these dimensions are not independent but rather interrelated as they work together to predict IS success (DeLone & McLean, 1992).

Here, we adopted some of the D&M model constructs to represent external beliefs (Ajzen, 2011) related to SQ and IQ to represent the antecedents of PB and satisfaction with system use as these constructs are already part of the D&M and A-B models. We followed several researchers in using the D&M model to represent external beliefs related to system and information quality. For example, Venkatesh et al. (2008a), when developing version 3 of TAM, used the D&M model and included system quality as a predictor of usefulness and information quality as a predictor of ease of use, Xu et al. (2013) used the D&M model to predict usefulness and ease of use of e-services, and Seddon (1997) used the D&M model to predict system use in a business system. Hence in this study, we adopted some of the constructs of the D&M model to represent the antecedents of perceived benefits and satisfaction with system use since both constructs are already a part of both the D&M model and A-B model.

## 3.1. Intention to continue using CDSS

Intention captures the motivational factors driving behaviour. Empirical research confirms a strong intention – behaviour relationship (Ajzen, 2011). In fact, the TPB, TAM, and A-B models have intentions as behaviour-predictors. Intention has been used as a surrogate of adoption in a large body of IS adoption research (e.g., Amoroso & Lim, 2017; Prakash & Das, 2021) Thus,

H1: The intention to continue using CDSSs is positively related to continuing to use them.

## 3.2. Attitude towards continuing CDSS use

Attitude towards using CDSS may be (un) favourable (Ajzen, 2011; Maio et al., 2018) depending on how physicians evaluate their tendency to continue CDSS use. As validated empirically in past IS studies (e.g.,

Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006), TPB proposes that one’s intention to perform a behaviour is influenced by one’s attitude towards this behaviour (Ajzen, 2011). Thus,

H2a: Physicians’ attitudes towards continuing to use CDSSs are positively related to their intention to continue using them.

Beyond the attitude-intention relationship, prior research (e.g., Ajzen & Fishbein, 2000; Glasman & Albarracin, 2006) has also shown that attitude, when formed by direct experience with the behaviour object, yields a direct relationship with the behaviour. In the continuous-HIT-use context, physicians having directly experienced the system via initial use form a direct relationship between their attitude and continuous system use. Thus, we propose that

H2b: Physicians’ attitude towards continuing to use CDSSs is positively related to continuing to use them.

## 3.3. Satisfaction with CDSS

Satisfaction refers to positive attitudes towards the system (object) based on one’s cognitive-emotional appraisal of its performance (Wixom & Todd, 2005). In the A-B model, satisfaction represents the attitude towards the target factor. Previous research (e.g., Ain et al., 2019; Jasperson et al., 2005; Hung et al., 2012) validates the relationship between attitude towards the IS and towards system use. Researchers have also found a significant relationship between satisfaction (object-attitude) and behaviour-attitude. Accordingly, we propose that, if physicians are satisfied with the system, they will have a positive attitude towards their interactions with the system, yielding a positive relationship between physicians’ satisfaction with the system and attitude towards using it (Zhang, 2013).

H3a: Physicians’ satisfaction with the system is positively related to their attitude towards using it.

In addition, satisfied system users are likely to perceive system outcomes as beneficial (Petter & McLean, 2009), relating it positively to perceived benefits (DeLone & McLean, 1992). Perceived benefits are the improvements in eficiency and quality of care physicians get when using the system. Perceived benefits are result of physicians’ role identity classification process. When physicians identify themselves as care givers, the benefits they perceive from the system are related to this role. This agrees with the sense-making theory proposing that users’ perceived benefits depend on their perception of the system as related to their role (how they make sense of their role) (Jensen et al., 2009).

Hence,

H3b: There is a positive relationship between physician satisfaction and the perceived benefits of using the system.

## 3.4. Perceived benefits (PB)

Upon experiencing system perceived benefits, physicians evaluate their system interactions as positive, yielding a positive attitude towards continuing system use (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Hence,

H4a: There is a positive relationship between perceived benefits and attitude towards using the system.

In this study, while the A-B model does not propose a direct perceived benefits-intention relationship, a positive relationship has been established via several IS use models, including the TAM (Davis, 1989). This relationship was verified by numerous IS adoption studies (e.g., Ray et al., 2019; Cheng et al., 2021) Hence,

H4b: There is a positive relationship between physicians’ perceived benefits using CDSSs and their intention to continue using them.

## 3.5. Perceived influence of medical community membership

The efect of group belonging (group membership) may be explained as follows. First, as individuals seek to link with others, they value behaviours that help them create and maintain these relationships (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Second, SIT proposes that, as one identifies with a group, one’s perceptions are influenced by this identification (Abrams & Hogg, 1990; Trepte & Loy, 2017), which further motivates one to behave in ways accepted by the group. For physicians, the influence of a medical community membership is significant given their perception of the membership as fundamental in their profession and how they associate with such a community more than with their employer (Freidson, 1994; Touati et al., 2019). Tallis (2006) discusses medical professionalism as “Medical professionalism signifies a set of values, behaviours, and relationships that underpins the trust the public has in doctors”. (Tallis, 2006; p. 9). Hence, the relationships physicians form through their membership in the medical community has a significant impact on their professionalism and values. Medical community membership creates shared values among physicians and hence influence professional integrity, motivates physicians’ continuous improvement and seeking excellence in providing service to patients, and work with other members of the medical community to care for patients (Tallis, 2006). Therefore, we argue that physicians highly value each other’s opinions and experiences because of their shared education and life-long training (Hilton & Slotnick, 2005; Tallis, 2006; Touati et al., 2019). They are willing to behave in peer-accepted ways. If CDSSs are widely accepted by medical society, this is likely to boost physicians’ positive evaluation of system use, positively influencing their attitude towards the system.

Thus,

H5a: The perceived influence of medical community membership will have a positive relationship with physicians’ attitude towards continuing CDSS use.

Moreover, when individuals value their membership in a community, they engage in behaviours that maintain this membership. For example, in a social media context, a sense of belonging to the social media community motivates users to contribute to this community (Pan et al., 2017; Ren et al., 2012). Hence, as physicians perceive that the medical community favours system use, they will see this use as a way to improve their community belonging, positively impacting their intention to continue system use (Eagly & Chaiken, 1993; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Hence,

H5b: The efect of the medical community membership will have a positive relationship with physicians intention to continue using the system.

## 3.6. Perceived threat to professional identity (PID)

The efect of barriers to use on IS use has been well documented. Users may resist a system if it might negatively influence the outcome of their work (Marakas & Hornik, 1996). With a system implemented, users naturally evaluate its impact on their work to determine if they can cope with the changes created by the system. If the barrier (cost) is greater than the opportunity (benefit), users will resist (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Liang & Xue, 2010). For example, Anderson and Agarwal (2011) found that threats to patient privacy negatively impacted physicians’ HIT use. Previous healthcare research found that physicians may resist new guidelines (Dowswell et al., 2001) or HIT use (Doolin, 2004) because this system threatens their autonomy by requiring them to follow imposed guidelines or processes. As physicians perceive themselves as care providers, they form their expectations of system use based on this perception and may label the system as “identitychallenging” (Tripsas, 2009). If the threat to physicians autonomy and patient relationships outweighs the benefits, physicians will resist adopting EHR systems (Walter & Lopez, 2008). Whenever CDSSs become challenging or threatening to physicians’ PID, physicians will evaluate their system use negatively. Thus,

H6a: Perceived threat to PID has a negative relationship with physicians’ attitudes towards continuing CDSS use.

Moreover, as physicians consider the negative consequences of system use on their identity, they are likely to be less motivated to continue using the system. Hence,

H6b: There is a negative relationship between threat to PID and the intention to continue using the system.

Finally, the A-B model proposes a direct relationship between social outcomes (threat in this case) and behaviour. Even if physicians intend to use the system, threats to PID may surpass this intention and directly influence their system use. Hence,

H6c: Threat to PID has a negative relationship with system use.

## 3.7. Perceived information quality (PIQ)

PIQ is the usefulness and desirability of the information produced by the system, such as its accuracy and timeliness (Wixom & Todd, 2005). For CDSSs, the PIQ reflects the accuracy and timeliness of the clinical guidelines and alerts provided as well as the suitability of the information format to physicians. The D&M model proposes a positive relationship between PIQ and system benefits and satisfaction (Petter & McLean, 2009). In CDSS, PIQ is directly related to CDSS perceived benefits as the system can only be useful if the information it produces accurately reflects diseasemanagement guidelines. Hence,

H7a: PIQ has a positive relationship with physicians PB.

Information is also the main outcome of CDSS use. Thus, physicians’ satisfaction with using the system is likely to depend on their perception of the quality of information they receive. Hence,

H7b: PIQ has a positive relationship with physicians’ satisfaction with CDSS use.

## 3.8. Perceived system quality (SQ)

Perceived CDSS SQ refers to subjective evaluation of such use measures as reliability, and flexibility of the system (DeLone & McLean, 1992). Similar to PIQ, the D&M model proposes a relationship between SQ and system benefits and satisfaction. For CDSS, when physicians perceive the system to be high-quality, they are likely to be satisfied using it and more likely to perceive system outcomes as useful. Hence,

H8a: Perceived SQ has a positive relationship with PB from system use.

H8b: Perceived SQ has a positive relationship with physicians’ satisfaction with CDSS use.

## 3.9. Physicians perceived quality improvement (PPQI)

PPQI refers to the physician’s subjective evaluation of an enhancement in patients’ care quality from using CDSSs, a concept that has not been quantitatively studied. While threat to professional identity represents, in part, physicians’ perceptions of how using the system threatens their autonomy, the PPQI construct infers the role of patients’ responses to system use and how these impact physicians’ perceptions of the system. As patients are more educated and empowered, comanaging their care plans and sometimes even refusing to adhere to the physician’s advice, patients are asserting more of their autonomy and their right to make decisions (Ubel et al., 2017). Physicians must consider patients’ autonomy in their decision-making. Research has shown that patient care outcomes improve when physicians consider their patients’ autonomy and satisfaction with care decisions (Williams et al., 2005). Hence, we argue that, in keeping with the physicians role and beliefs that they should provide the best care for their patients (Freidson, 1994; Touati et al., 2019), when physicians believe using the system enhances their ability to support patients’ autonomy and improve patient satisfaction and outcomes, physicians will be more satisfied with the system and perceive it as beneficial to their work.

A parallel may be drawn between this construct and the SQ construct in the modified D&M model (Delone & McLean, 2003), where the service provided by the system directly influences users’ outcomes and satisfaction. Thus,

H9a: There is a positive relationship between PPQI and perceived benefits.

H9b: There is a positive relationship between PPQI and physicians’ satisfaction with the system.

## 3.10. Control variables

This study focuses on two main control variables. The first is the user’s position – whether the system user is a physician, resident (medical trainee), or nurse practitioner (a nurse authorised to make medical decisions). Previous studies (Engel et al., 2014; Steininger & Stiglbauer, 2015) have argued that, as residents are less experienced than physicians, they may not have established workflows. Hence, residents’ vs. physicians’ attitudes and behaviours towards HIT use may difer. The second control variable is the clinic, which reflects the organisational factors that may influence physicians’ system use. In this study, we recruit system users from three clinics and examine how the diferences between these clinics impact system use. Prior studies indicated that organisational factors, including management support (Hung et al., 2012) or the existence of a champion (Gui et al., 2020), may influence HIT use.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Study context

This investigation focuses on the continued use of the McMaster Pain Assistant (MPA), a KB-CDSS developed by the Department of Family Medicine at McMaster University to provide clinicians with recommendations for managing patients with lower back pain (LBP) and neuropathic pain (NeP).

Despite the acknowledged value of LBP-NeP guidelines on patient outcomes (Chou & Hufman, 2007; Fritz et al., 2007), previous studies have reported low adherence to these guidelines (González-Urzelai et al., 2003). Moreover, knowledge translation models propose that the use of electronic tools can enhance the application of guidelines in practice (Rycroft-Malone & Bucknall, 2010). The MPA not only gives physicians LBP-NeP guidelines; it also ofers tools to facilitate their practical application. The sample included both family physicians and residents working across three academic clinics in Southwestern Ontario, Canada. All participants oversaw patients with chronic and acute LBP or NeP. As part of a larger study evaluating the MPA, the participants recruited for the larger study also agreed to complete all the surveys, focus groups, and assessments for this study. To avoid social desirability bias arising from peer pressure (physicians completing the surveys just to satisfy their peers), all recruitment, deployment of surveys, and follow-ups were performed by research coordinators without the researchers’ intervention. Indeed, previous research (Fisher & Katz, 2000; Fisher, 1993) has shown that social desirability bias is unlikely to influence selfreported surveys focusing on personal outcomes and perceptions, which also applies to physicians given their training and PID (Ting, 2013). Therefore, this bias was not considered an issue in this study.

## 4.2. Study phases

## 4.2.1. Initial focus groups

Five (5) focus groups of physicians and healthcare researchers participated in this study with the goal of establishing the external beliefs that impacted A-B model constructs and developing the questionnaire used in this study.

## 4.2.2. Pilot test

Pilot testing is often used to validate questionnaire instruments (Boudreau et al., 2001; MacKenzie et al., 2011). Therefore, proposed questionnaires were sent to 21 participants (11 family physicians and 10 residents) to refine and validate the content.

The main study procedures were as follows. Study participants, including physicians, residents, and nurse practitioners, were recruited from three academic clinics. ## 4.3. Construct operationalisation

Previous research (MacKenzie et al., 2011; Segars & Grover, 1998; Sethi & King, 1991) signified the importance of construct operationalisation as a prerequisite for generating a valid and reliable measurement instrument. This step is essential to specify what the construct represents (or does not represent) and diferentiate it from other similar constructs (MacKenzie, 2003). Hence, the quality of the measurement model depends on the accurate operationalisation of its constructs.

System use operationalisation (Venkatesh et al., 2008) has been conceptualised as the frequency, duration, and intensity of system use. However, unlike Venkatesh et al. (2008), these system use attributes are captured from system logs and not via self-reported questionnaires. System access frequency is captured by computing how often users save system forms in the system. System use duration is measured by how many fields within each form are completed. Use intensity is measured as the number of diferent forms (sub-modules) within the system that were used. Using diferent dimensions to operationalise system use allows the study to capture the complexity of this behaviour and follows the suggestions of Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007), who proposed individual system use as a construct involving a user, system, and task.

Conducted to operationalise threat to professional identity, a literature review resulted in a list of potential threats, which were discussed with participants in the five focus groups alongside several interviews. Three dimensions of perceived threat to professional identity were selected: (a) consumption of visit time, (b) negative influence on physician-patient communications, and (c) loss of control over decision-making.

To conceptualise the PB construct, a list of potential benefits, as noted in the literature, was created and discussed with focus group participants. Three dimensions of benefits perceived as important to family physicians were selected: improving (a) patient management, (b) knowledge of pain management, and (c) diagnosis eficiency.

This study follows Wixom and Todd (2005) on how to select IQ and SQ dimensions by surveying the literature to identify the potential dimensions of both constructs. Running five focus groups with potential system users helped prioritise and select the most important dimensions of both constructs. Users were allowed to select other SQ dimensions if they were not on the list. Three IQ dimensions – accuracy, currency, and completeness – and four SQ dimensions – ease of use, flexibility, accessibility, and comprehensiveness – were selected.

To operationalise PPQI, the focus group (as discussed above) yielded three dimensions: (a) physicians’ perceived improvement in patient experience, (b) perceived improvement in patients’ perception of reliability, and (c) perceived improvement in patients perception of care responsiveness to their needs.

## 5.2. Reflective constructs

For reflective constructs, the Götz et al. (2010) process was adopted. First, the constructs’ convergent and discriminant validity are computed to determine whether any items should be removed due to loading or cross-loading issues. As Table 3 shows, all the reflective indicators loaded most highly on their assigned constructs with a minimum threshold of 0.50 (Gefen et al., 2000). As system use was derived from actual use data, it was excluded from this analysis. PB, clinical guideline adoption, threat, SQ, IQ, and PPQI were not included in this analysis because they are formative constructs (Petter et al., 2007).

Moreover, Gefen and Straub (2005) proposed that indicator loadings on their theoretical constructs should be higher than on other constructs by at least 0.10. Table 3 shows the indicator loadings (the relationship between an indicator or survey item and a model construct). As observed, this condition holds for all reflective items in the model, implying that no indicator is removed.

Next, Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability with thresholds of 0.70 and 0.60, respectively, were adopted to assess the reliability of reflective constructs (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988; Bernstein, 2010). As shown in Table 4, both were greater than 0.90, indicating construct reliability.

The convergent construct validity was evaluated using average variance extracted (AVE). The AVE value should exceed the variance resulting from the measurement error of the construct, that is, AVE >0.5 (Vinzi et al., 2010). As Table 4 shows, this criterion is met for all reflective constructs.

For acceptable discriminant validity of a construct, the square root (SR) of the AVE should be greater than the correlation between this and other constructs (Barclay et al., 1995). Table 5 presents the correlation matrix for the constructs with the AVE SR, represented as diagonal elements. All reflective constructs meet this criterion.

In all reflective constructs, the AVE SR is greater than their correlation with other constructs, and the loading of construct items is greater than crossloadings by at least 0.1, meeting both requirements for construct validity.

AVE is not meaningful for formative constructs as the requirement of covariance of the formative construct items is not assumed (MacKenzie et al., 2011). Therefore, the AVE SR of the formative constructs was not computed.

## 5.5. Key findings

The first significant finding is that, while threat to professional identity was the strongest predictor of system use with a path coeficient of −0.436, physicians’ system continuance intentions did not significantly afect actual continuance behaviour. This contradicts the conventional assumption that intention is a strong predictor of behaviour (Egea & González, 2011; Gefen et al., 2003; Melas et al., 2011). To the best of our knowledge, prior HIT adoption studies using a theoretical model did not extend beyond intention. Thus, the intention-use relationship in the healthcare context has been neglected. This result may be attributed to how physicians value their professional identity as care providers and their relationship with patients (Tallis, 2006). Physicians are willing to stop using systems if they fail to support their professional identity. Therefore, it can be proposed that, when physicians perceive threats to their professional identity, intention becomes insignificant. In other words, the influence of threat from system use surpasses that of intention.

To validate this explanation, the theoretical model was re-analysed after removing the relationship between threat and behaviour (H6c) to determine if intention would significantly impact system use.

As Figure 3 shows, when (H6c)was absent, the efect of intention on use became significant (0.316, p = 0.022), and the explained variance in system use decreased from 22.3% to 11%. This result shows that physicians adopting a caregiver professional identity via their training and education will stop using the system despite its usefulness and their prior intention to use it if they perceive a threat to their role, confirming that threat to role identity surpasses physicians’ intention to continue using the system.

Another interesting result is the influence of the medical community membership on attitude (H5a), intention (H5b), and threat (H5c). After six months of use, the medical community membership had no significant efect on intention or attitude. The absence of this efect may be attributed to the independent nature of medical training and education. Physicians are trained to use all available information to make independent judgements (Sackett et al., 1996; Touati et al., 2019). Hence, physicians’ decisions to use the system are made independently and not influenced by belonging to the medical community. While it may be argued that this result may be attributed to the short duration of this study (6 months) which may limit the impact of membership influence, the influence of medical community membership is associated with the shared values among physicians. These shared values are created throughout the physicians’ careers and education. Hence the lack of impact of medical community membership is not influenced by the duration of the study.

Conversely, because medical community membership influences physicians professionalism and shared values of excellence and continuous improvement (Tallis, 2006), when physicians observe that the medical community has accepted the system, their perception of threat decreases. They perceive the system as less threatening to their PID because others with similar values and objectives do not perceive the system as threatening. This result agrees with the extant IS research finding social norms to have an indirect efect on user behaviour (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). This is also confirmed by healthcare research focused on guidelines as, when the guidelines are developed by physicians, physicians view them as less threatening (Cabana et al., 1999).

In terms of (H7a) (positive PIQ-PB relationship) and (H7b) (positive PIQ-system satisfaction relationship), these relationships were not significant. This may be because the MPA was designed by the Family Medicine Department, and the guidelines were selected by a group of family physicians. Prior research showed that guidelines are more acceptable if physicians are involved in their development (Cabana et al., 1999).

Another explanation for physician acceptance of the IQ provided by the MPA may be the series of awareness sessions provided to physicians on pain management guidelines. Hence, they were aware of the quality of the user guidelines even before the system was deployed. This result is interesting because it difers from the findings of most IS studies (Petter & McLean, 2009). This shows that physicians trust the medical community. This trust makes IQ a given when the system is designed by other physicians and disables its efect on satisfaction and PB.

Unlike PIQ, the relationships with SQ-PB (H9a) and SQ-satisfaction (H9b) were positive. This result agrees with numerous studies using the D&M model (Petter & McLean, 2009).

The PPQI in this study accounts for the influence of patient condition improvement that physicians perceive as a result of using the system. Rarely studied in IS or healthcare literature, this construct accounts for patient influence on physician decisions to use the system. PPQI positively influenced satisfaction with the system (H10b), but its influence on PB was not significant (H10a). This was an interesting result as patients are at the centre of care (Kilbride & Jofe, 2018). Hence, one would think that PPQI would have a direct influence on the benefits physicians perceived from using the system. This result may be attributed to how physicians clearly diferentiate between their satisfaction with the system and their PB from the system. PB are related to the efect of the system on how physicians work – for example, improving eficiency or as a guideline reference. The benefits received by patients are implicit and not directly considered by physicians. This perception of benefits stems from physicians’ perceived roles as caregivers. All of their work is directed at improving patient conditions (Freidson, 1988). Hence, if their eficiency or efectiveness improves, this will reflect on the patient, and PPQI does not have a direct influence on PB.

In contrast, physicians’ satisfaction with the system is influenced by the PPQI. Physicians, as caregivers, try to do their best for their patients. However, they are sometimes met by resistance from patients or have dificulties convincing patients to adhere to their advice (Ubel et al., 2017; Zolnierek & DiMatteo, 2009). These dificulties have been found to influence physicians’ decisions to adopt clinical guidelines (Lau et al., 2007). Physicians are satisfied with the system if it helps alleviate these dificulties by convincing patients to follow their advice or by getting physicians more involved in their care. Hence, the PPQI has a direct relationship with physician satisfaction with the system.

To assess the PPQI-PB and PPQI-satisfaction relationships, we conducted a focus group of seven physicians after the survey analysis. Physicians diferentiate between factors influencing their perceptions of system benefits and their satisfaction with the system. The system is perceived as beneficial if it provides the information necessary to aid physicians in diagnosing and prescribing medication more eficiently. They were satisfied if the system improved patient adherence and communication. Indeed, one participant noted a very creative use of the system. Patients with pain usually ask for stronger opioids. This participant used the system to show the patient that opioids were not recommended for his case vis-à- vis the pain-management guidelines, convincing the patient to adhere happily with what the physician prescribed. This shows the promising use of the system for managing patients’ autonomy.

## 5.5.1. Control variables

Two control variables were studied: position and clinic. First, the user’s position was found to influence satisfaction with the system and system use. From the focus groups, physicians and residents valued the system diferently. While residents focus more on learning and building their knowledge base, physicians are more concerned with eficiency in managing their patients and the system’s relative advantage over current tools. Furthermore, physicians have already developed workflows and tools to manage patients with pain while residents are still building their workflows. Hence, residents are more open to trying the MPA while developing their workflows. Moreover, they are more likely to be satisfied with the system even if it only partially satisfies their needs. This positive efect of position on system use and satisfaction was supported by the end-of-study focus group we conducted at the end of this study. Physicians reported having “memory tools” to remind them of guidelines and were reluctant to switch those tools for the MPA. Conversely, residents lacking such tools were more open to experience with the system.

There are other potential explanations for the efect of the position control variable on system use. For example, previous research has shown that new resident physicians are expected to be able to use HIT (Meehan et al., 2018). Hence, they are more likely to learn and use these systems, especially if their digital capabilities are higher than those of older physicians (Clarke et al., 2016). Alternatively, residents have a more positive attitude towards clinical guidelines than more experienced physicians (Kilaru et al., 2014); hence, they are more likely to use a system that facilitates practical guideline application. Finally, residents are more exposed to medical errors than physicians (Gurley et al., 2018; Singh et al., 2007). Hence, they are likely to be more satisfied with the system if it provides guidelines that can protect against medical errors.

In this study, with only 10 nurse practitioners, their position were not included in the analysis. The few nurse practitioners in our sample prevented us from including them in the “position” analysis.

The second control variable was the clinic where the users worked, which yielded surprising results. Despite similarities in the training sessions and physician population among clinics, the clinic was found to influence both system use and PB. To explain this, diferences among clinics were investigated with the only diference being the existence of a champion and the activities of the champion in each case. In clinics, champions act as a source of knowledge. Other users asked them questions about system specifics and how to use system features. For two of the clinics, two of the study investigators worked at these sites and acted as champions. For the third site, no physician acted as a champion. Prior research has shown that champions could play an important role in encouraging system use and demonstrating system benefits to users in a HIT context (Ash, 1997; Ludwick et al., 2009; Sittig et al., 2010).

## 6. Conclusion

As detailed below, this study has several theoretical contributions and practical implications.

## 6.1. Theoretical contributions

Although HIT adoption and use have been widely studied, this body of research has been limited in terms of theory (Abouzahra et al., 2015; Holden et al., 2011). Moreover, continuous HIT use after initial adoption has rarely been studied. This work extends the HIT literature by using an integrative model that incorporates not only IS-based constructs but also contextual factors such as physicians’ PID, the role of the medical community membership, and the influence of patients on HIT use. Our first contribution is in demonstrating the importance of physicians PID in their decision to continue using HIT. Most prior studies considered intention to be the main predictor of system use (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Shemesh & Barnoy, 2020). Hence, the theoretical models considered stopped at the intention and scarcely included actual use behaviour as part of the model. This current study, however, shows that, although intention influences continued use, this influence is surpassed by the influence of threat to physicians’ PID. Even in previous IS research that considered both intention and threat as part of the theoretical model (e.g., Nicolaou & McKnight, 2006; Walter & Lopez, 2008), this research did not include actual use; therefore, the influence of threat on use was not captured. The significance of this finding is that it identifies the role of context in the study of IS use and the importance of including user (physician) identity in future research. This finding also supports previous calls (Benbasat & Barki, 2007) to include contextrelated constructs in IS use models.

Another discovery is the relationship between the medical community membership and the threat to professional identity. While past studies have examined the influence of the medical community on intention (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2011), this study found no direct impact of the medical community membership on physicians; instead, the efect is fully mediated by threat to physician PID, reflecting the importance of context in HIT studies and the complexity of medical community-use relationships. This result agrees with previous studies that found medical community membership to impact physicians’ professional identity (Tallis, 2006).

While the role of patients has been noted to motivate physicians’ guideline use (Hoque et al., 2017; Winston & Medlin, 2011), this role has rarely been conceptualised in HIT-adoption studies. With the current focus on patient-centric care (Barry & Edgman-Levitan, 2012; Boland et al., 2019; Sperl-Hillen et al., 2016), our study conceptualised the influence of patients on physician HIT use in the construct “PPQI”, which captures how physicians perceive patient attitude towards care due to system use. This concept, found to be theoretically distinct from other model constructs like $ { { ^ \circ } } \mathrm { P B } ^ { \mathcal { P } } ,$ , has a strong influence on physician satisfaction with the system but not their PB.

We found that residents were more likely to use KB-CDSSs than physicians, probably because of the previous experience of physicians and their established workflows as opposed to the less-experienced residents. This is confirmed by the finding that residents found the system to be more useful than physicians.

While the role of a champion in encouraging use is well documented in the IS literature (Rezvani et al., 2017; van Laere & Aggestam, 2016), the HIT literature has not emphasised this with our study afirming that a champion can significantly improve system use in clinics.

## 6.2. Practical considerations

Key considerations to practice should also be noted, including data to back up the recommendations of prior qualitative research while clarifying context. First, developing systems useful to physicians is just the first step; these systems must integrate seamlessly with existing workflows to be accepted by physicians. As noted, when it comes to HIT use, threat surpasses intention, making it necessary to integrate system use into workflows. For example, KB-CDSSs may be integrated with systems currently used by physicians so that they surface when needed without interrupting physicians’ work. KB-CDSSs should also present information vis-à-vis the natural flow of patient visits.

Second, the system implementation should consider significant physician involvement. Here, physician involvement improves confidence in the IQ provided by the system. IQ was considered a given and did not influence the system perceptions of physicians. Prior research (Mishra et al., 2012) found that physicians consider other physicians to be less threatening than system developers, such as insurance or pharmaceutical companies, and should be included in the system-deployment phase. Evidently, if the medical community accepts a system, physicians perceive it to be less threatening and are more likely to use it. Interestingly, pharmaceutical companies use a similar approach to market new drugs. They involve key figures in the target medical area even before the drug is fully approved, and they participate in conferences to gain buy-in from physicians and from the medical community (Smith, 2014). A similar approach for KB-CDSS development and deployment would improve system adoption and use.

Finally, physician satisfaction with the system depends on improved patient care and system use. Briefly, KB-CDSSs should contain components that directly benefit the patients, which enables patients autonomy, making the patients more satisfied with the care process and motivating the physicians to continue system use. In using the MPA, physicians tied their satisfaction during the focus group to develop components that allow patients to complete pain questionnaires prior to visits. They also suggested developing mobile apps to guide patients with pain via pain management exercises while facilitating patient adherence to physician advice.

## 6.3. Concluding remarks

This study addressed key research gaps in the continuous KB-CDSS use and the disintegration of healthcare context-related factors in KB-CDSS use research. It extended the current literature by combining IS and healthcare context-related constructs to explain physicians’ continuous KB-CDSS use, yielding several interesting results. First, physicians highly value their PID as caregivers to the extent that the efect of this identity surpasses their intention to use the system. With time, physicians’ PID plays an important role in defining their attitude towards using the system. Finally, the results of this study signify the importance of context in understanding IS user behaviour and calls for the inclusion of context in future IS-use research.

## 6.3.1. Limitations

First, this study was conducted at a clinic in southwestern Ontario. Hence, caution should be exercised when generalising results to other geographical areas and within diferent healthcare cultures. However, the study outcome can be applied to other developed countries due to the similarities in educational, training, and healthcare systems across these countries.

Second, the study was conducted in academic clinics. While physician education is very similar in academic and non-academic clinics, both residents and physicians in academic clinics may be more ready to apply guidelines in practice. Hence, caution should be exercised when generalising the study results to non-academic settings.

Third, this study did not control for the patient’s condition. Physicians’ decisions to use medical guidelines (or CDSSs) may be influenced by the patients conditions as well as their ability to aford care (Cabana et al., 1999). Unfortunately, we had no access to patient information in this study; hence, we were unable to control for this variable.

Finally, due to the small number of nurse practitioners involved in the study, we were unable to control for their positions. Nurse practitioners are an interesting category of medical professionals. Their position enables them to see patients, diagnose them, request tests, and prescribe medications (Naylor & Kurtzman, 2010). They have the same autonomy in decision-making as physicians, and their role in the healthcare system improves the quality of care (Brown & Grimes, 1995; Mundinger et al., 2000). However, they do not have the same training as the physicians.

## 6.3.2. Future works

First, patient-related constructs have often been ignored in research on CDSS use. There is abundant research on how HIT can afect patient outcomes; however, we found no studies examining the efect of patient outcomes or satisfaction on physician HIT use. This efect can logically be deduced given how physicians perceive their role in society as caregivers and their mission as making decisions for the benefit of their patients. The lack of research in this area may be because patients usually rate their physicians high on satisfaction surveys (Streiner et al., 2014). Therefore, there may be no motivation to study the efect of PS on HIT use. While this study employed the PPQI as a proxy for patient-related constructs, future work should consider patient-related constructs. This could lead to the development of research projects that focus on understanding the influences of patient perceptions and satisfaction on physician HIT use. These research projects can be useful in enabling patients’ autonomy and improving patient-physician relationships.

Second, this study should be extended to nonacademic settings. Although the results are expected to hold in other settings, it would be interesting to determine whether other factors influence physician continuance of use in these contexts. This is especially important because research comparing academic and non-academic environments in the HIT-use context is relatively scarce.

Finally, nurses were an interesting group. Unfortunately, there is little research on HIT use in nursing and the efect of nurses’ training on their attitudes towards using HIT. Future research should compare nurses’ IS use with physicians and nurses.
