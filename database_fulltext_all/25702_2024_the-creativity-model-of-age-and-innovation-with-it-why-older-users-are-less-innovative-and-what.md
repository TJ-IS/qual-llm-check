# The creativity model of age and innovation with IT: why older users are less innovative and what to do about it

Stefan Tams and Alina Dulipovici

Department of Information Technologies, HEC Montreal

## ABSTRACT

To realise the full potential of their information technology (IT) infrastructure, successful firms encourage users to innovate with IT. However, older users are much less innovative with IT than younger ones. This situation is alarming because the workforce is ageing rapidly at the very same time as organisational technologies are proliferating. Using the concepts of mediation and moderation, the present investigation proposes the creativity model of age and innovation with IT. This model postulates that the age diferences in user innovation are due to age-related changes in the perceived ability to be creative with IT. It also postulates that the negative efect of age on user innovation can be mitigated by three factors: job control, IT support, and user enjoyment. Two studies were conducted to test the model. The first one was a large-scale survey of IT users who work with various technologies, and the second one was a more specific survey of knowledge workers who use electronic knowledge repositories. The results largely support the model. Thus the model provides a well-founded theoretical framework, not only for explaining why older users are less innovative with IT, but also for determining what managers and systems designers should do about it.

## 1. Introduction

Changes in self-concept traits such as self-eficacy can occur with age and have a significant impact on the ability of older users to innovate with IT at organisations. When users are less innovative with IT, this means that the goal of finding new ways to apply existing IT systems to their work tasks is pursued with less intensity. Consider the case of 61-year-old Bonnie, a marketing manager who constantly uses IT to perform job tasks. She relies heavily on electronic knowledge repositories like Microsoft SharePoint to collaborate with her co-workers and to share information with them. Like many older workers, Bonnie has a positive attitude towards IT but has dificulty taking full advantage of it. She rarely tries to find new ways to apply IT features to her work, even though she knows that doing so would improve her job performance. In contrast, Bonnie’s younger co-manager Clyde constantly discovers new ways of exploiting available IT to do his job better. For instance, he recently tried tagging metadata to SharePoint folders in order to propagate it automatically. Thus, he discovered a way to find the right files faster.

This case illustrates two trends that are present in OECD countries: (1) the greying of the workforce and (2) the inescapable growth of IT at organisations (Ghasemaghaei et al., 2019; OECD, 2022). The workforces in OECD countries are rapidly growing older. Indeed, the number of older workers (i.e., workers who are 60 years of age or over) has increased by more than 50% during the last decade (OECD, 2022). According to the OECD, this trend will increase during the next 30 years (OECD, 2013).

At the same time as the workforce has aged, the IT used to accomplish work tasks has become more complex. It has evolved from simple word processing tools to highly complex applications such as knowledge management systems with hundreds of features. To ensure that these complex applications are fully utilised, successful firms such as Microsoft, 3 M, and Nike encourage their employees to innovate with IT (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005). This tendency to innovate with IT, also called user innovation, comes into play when workers actively seek to identify new ways of applying IT to their work tasks in order to increase their job performance (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Tams et al., 2018). User innovation occurs most often through the self-initiated discovery of new features. This is often taken into consideration when software is updated. For example, the SharePoint 2019 update improved team collaboration by adding features such as user-generated communication sites that most users only discover through self-initiated learning. If users are not motivated to innovate, these features can easily go unnoticed.

Unfortunately, older users are much less innovative with IT than younger ones (Lee et al., 2010). Often, they continuously apply the same narrow band of features when they do their job tasks and make little or no efort to discover new ways of using workplace

IT to improve their performance. Indeed, research in fields such as human-computer interaction and marketing intelligence has shown that older users often limit themselves to a few core features and that, as a result, their use of IT remains qualitatively diferent from younger persons’ use of IT (Beck, 2017; Knowles & Hanson, 2018; Lee et al., 2010; Mariano et al., 2022; Tams, 2022; Wan et al., 2022).

Research has yet to develop an in-depth explanation of the relation between age and user innovation. Until now, it has focused on the general connection between these two constructs, that is, on the direct efect of age on user innovation, and it has provided empirical evidence that because of their age, older users are in fact less innovative with IT (Hauk et al., 2018; Knowles & Hanson, 2018; Lee et al., 2010). This direct efect of age has fundamental implications for IS researchers. It is a pioneering discovery about the relation between two important IS variables: age, which is considered a “key demographic variable” (Ghasemaghaei et al., 2019; Tams et al., 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2003, p. 469), and innovation with IT, which is an important dependent variable (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Jasperson et al., 2005). However, it is time to conduct a thorough examination of the scientific basis of the efect of age on user innovation. In other words, it is time to bring into focus the structure of the relation between the age of users and their innovative behaviour with IT. A much more nuanced explanation of this relation is needed, one that will clarify the precise nature of the observed efect of age.

To address this issue, two interrelated research objectives need to be examined. The first one concerns the causes of the lower innovativeness of older users, for research has yet to provide an adequate explanation of the relationship between age and user innovation. Thus the first goal of the present article is to develop an explanation for why older users do not innovate with IT as much as younger ones do. This means that mediation concepts and analyses will be necessary to identify the mechanism through which age impacts user innovation. The mechanism examined in this investigation is the perceived ability to be creative with IT (i.e., creative IT self-eficacy and personal innovativeness with IT). Industrial and organisational psychology has proposed this concept in a more general form as an explanation of the relation between age and innovation (Binnewies et al., 2008).

Moreover, as Tams et al. (2014) have argued, knowing why older users innovate less is of limited value if nothing can be proposed to help them improve their ability to innovate. Consequently, the second goal of the present article is to determine whether use conditions like having more job control can moderate this problem faced by older users, a task that will require moderated mediation analysis.

Both these research objectives concern pressing issues for organisations with ageing workers, and it is also worth noting that IS researchers have been calling for a detailed examination of the relation between age and IS outcomes for some time. In particular, they have emphasised the need to identify the underlying influential mechanisms producing the observed age efects that constitute the mediating variables (e.g., Morris et al., 2005; Tams et al., 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2003, p. 469). Therefore, it is essential to ask the following research question:

RQ: Can the relationship between age and user innovation with IT be explained by diferences in the perceived ability to be creative with IT among younger and older users, and does this indirect relationship depend on certain use conditions like having more job control?

The present article makes two important contributions to the literature on IS use, and it also draws attention to research in other fields such as human-computer interaction and marketing intelligence that has previously examined the (general) relationship between age and user innovation. As a first contribution, this article presents a mediation model that improves understanding of the connection between age and user innovation by replacing broad explanations and stereotyping by a more detailed account of the underlying mechanisms that come into play when older workers use IT. By doing so, it provides valuable support both to managers and to systems designers. Second, by examining use conditions like having more job control as moderators, this article is able to identify ways of helping older users improve their ability to innovate with IT. In a word, by providing an indepth explanation of the relation between age and user innovation, this article improves understanding of the impact that age has on the ability to use IT.

Moreover, although user innovation has been of interest to IS researchers for a long time, there is still insuficient empirical and theoretical understanding of the role that users play in driving it (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Tams et al., 2018; Thatcher et al., 2018). IS researchers recognise that users play a critical role, but they tend to give much greater emphasis to the technologies than to the human beings that use them (Tams et al., 2018). The present article expands the research horizon by theorising and empirically evaluating the role that age plays in user innovation with IT.

The article is structured as follows. The next section discusses the creativity model of ageing from industrial and organisational psychology, showing that it is relevant to the relation between age and user innovation and that it can be used to frame a parsimonious research model for age diferences in user innovation. The third section uses this model to develop the research hypotheses. The fourth section reports on two complementary studies that were conducted to test the hypotheses. The goal of the first one was to establish generalisable results, and the goal of the second one was to replicate the results of the first one using diferent measures in a population of users who work with one specific technology. The final section discusses the implications of the results for researchers, managers, and systems designers.

## 2. Theoretical background

Prior research has provided ample evidence that older persons have greater dificulty using IT than younger persons (Brown et al., 2010; Morris & Venkatesh, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2012, 2014). Focusing on the direct link between age and the ability to use IT, researchers have been able to show that age influences how efectively people use IT. For example, in the context of self-service checkouts, Lee et al. (2010) find that user innovation decreases dramatically with age (β = −0.18, p < 0.01). Researchers such as Venkatesh et al. (2014) and Niehaves and Plattfaut (2014) have greatly improved understanding of the relation between age and IS adoption by providing evidence that older adults are less likely to use technology. More recently, others such as Abouzahra and Ghasemaghaei (2022) have corroborated this earlier evidence.

As Tams et al. (2014) and Venkatesh et al. (2003) have argued, current research needs to build on these important insights by undertaking an in-depth examination of the underlying causes of the dificulties that older workers have using IT. In other words, the “underlying influential mechanism” that leads to these dificulties must be brought to light (Venkatesh et al., 2003, p. 469). This means that current research needs to find a precise theoretical explanation for the relation between age and user innovation with IT. Ofering broad speculations and/or focusing on sensemaking will not sufice. Moreover, it is important to build on past research by exploring the moderators that are the boundary conditions for the relationship between age and user innovation with IT (Abouzahra & Ghasemaghaei, 2022; Tams et al., 2014). It is time to move away from the search for general explanations for this relationship and to focus instead on providing detailed accounts of the specific factors that determine it.

As a foundation for the study of the efects of age on IT use, IS research has primarily used IS theories and general theories from cognitive psychology. For example, it has drawn on the unified theory of the acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) or on social cognitive theory (Brown et al., 2010; Lam & Lee, 2006; Niehaves & Plattfaut, 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2012). These theories are very helpful for explaining userelated behaviours in general, but because age is outside their scope, they are unable to lay the ground for a precise explanation of the observed efects of age on

IT use. Therefore, it has been dificult to specify the ways that age diferences are associated with the use of IT.

A more nuanced theory with a specific focus on ageing would have the potential to provide deeper insights into the relationship between age and the post-adoptive use of IT (Tams et al., 2014). We argue that the creativity model of aging (Binnewies et al., 2008) is especially relevant to the problem of reduced user innovation among older workers. The central thesis of the creativity model of aging is twofold: (1) Increased age in adulthood is associated with a decrease in creative behaviour and innovation; and (2) This decrease is moderated by contextual factors, especially job control and support for creativity (Binnewies et al., 2008). This means that age afects user innovation indirectly via perceptions about one’s ability to be creative and that this indirect efect only manifests under certain conditions such as when users have less job control or less support for creativity.

This theoretical framework can be adapted to IS research using the approach recommended by Truex et al. (2006), an approach that is consistent with the seminal work of (e.g., Murray & Evers, 1989). When adapting a theory, researchers must take into consideration four criteria:

(1) The fit between the selected theory and the phenomenon under study;

(2) The theory’s underlying assumptions;

(3) The theory’s impact on the choice of research methodology; and

(4) The contribution of the theorising to cumulative theory.

As regards the first criterion, the creativity model of ageing is applicable to the phenomenon under study because it has age as its independent variable and creativity as its dependent variable (Binnewies et al., 2008). Creativity can be defined as the generation of ideas that are both new and useful (Amabile, 1988). In the context of the contemporary workplace, such ideas can be about how to use IT in novel ways to perform job tasks more quickly and eficiently. Creativity is a prerequisite for innovation in general and for user innovation in particular (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Amabile, 1988). Indeed, when workers innovate with IT, creativity is more important than in other areas of IT use (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005). This means that the creativity model of aging is relevant to the first goal of the present article, which is to lay the ground for a theoretically adequate explanation of the relationship between age and the post-adoptive use of IT.

According to the creativity model of ageing, certain factors can mitigate the problem of the reduced creative behaviour of older users. The two most important mitigating factors are job control and support for creativity (Binnewies et al., 2008). Although IS research has not yet hypothesised or empirically examined these factors as moderators of the relationship between age and user innovation with IT, the creativity model of aging suggests that they are highly relevant to the second goal of the present article, which is to improve understanding of what managers can do to help older users innovate. It is clear, therefore, that the constructs and relationships proposed by the creativity model of aging fit the phenomenon under study.

Concerning the second criterion, it is important to emphasise that the underlying assumptions of the creativity model of ageing do not conflict with the theoretical framework of IS research. The model has two underlying assumptions: (1) The relationship between age and idea creativity is moderated by contextual factors; and (2) Coworkers, supervisors, and older workers themselves often hold negative age stereotypes that fuel perceptions of older workers as less efective, less creative, and less flexible (Binnewies et al., 2008). These two assumptions are both consistent with the theoretical framework of IS research. As regards the first assumption, IS researchers have long acknowledged that it is important to take into consideration contextual factors when studying the relationship between age and IT use (Lam & Lee, 2006; Tams et al., 2014). Concerning the second one, it is true that older workers are generally perceived and evaluated as less efective, less creative, and less flexible when they use IT (Schloegel et al., 2018). Moreover, they are seen as less adaptive to new IT systems and as less innovative with IT than their younger colleagues (Schloege et al., 2018). It follows that the underlying assumptions of the creativity model of aging are consistent with IS research.

The third criterion concerns the research method and the data-analytic procedure. Here it is important to note that the ontological and epistemological presuppositions of the creativity model of ageing as well as the methods and data-analytic techniques used to test its predictions are consistent with the traditions in IS research. The model is tested using survey research as well as moderation analysis that is based on OLS approaches such as regression analysis in SPSS (Binnewies et al., 2008). In IS research, there is also a long tradition of quantitative methods, and moderation analysis is frequently used. Thus the method and data-analytic techniques used to test the predictions of the creativity model of aging are consistent with the IS research tradition.

As for the fourth criterion, it specifies that any application of a theory, in this case the creativity model of ageing, must ofer new possibilities for extending current knowledge. Past research has demonstrated that age has a direct efect on user innovation, but it has not explained why older workers innovate less with IT such as electronic knowledge repositories (EKR), and what factors can mitigate this problem (e.g., Lam & Lee, 2006; Lee et al., 2010). The creativity model of aging provides a viable approach for addressing these important questions.

In a word, the creativity model of ageing is a broad theoretical framework that can be used to explain the relationship between age and innovation in the specific contexts where older workers use IT. Since the creativity model of aging is still evolving as an explanatory framework for investigating a recent phenomenon, it has not yet emerged as a complete theory with specific assumptions and tight logical arguments. However, even if it is not yet a fully developed theory, this model provides a useful conceptual lens for investigating the complex relationship between age and user innovation with IT.

We use the creativity model of ageing to conduct an in-depth examination of the impact of age on user innovation. Drawing on the model’s constructs and relationships to specify our own research model, we adopt a theory-driven approach to the examination of age-related diferences in user innovation. In this approach, the constructs of the creativity model of aging are adapted to the context of IS-enabled work.

## 3. Hypotheses development

Using the creativity model of ageing, we propose a moderated mediation model of age, user innovation, creativity, and contextual factors. The research model hypothesises that age causes a decline in the perceived ability to be creative with IT and that this reduces user innovation (see Figure 1 and Table 1). The model also proposes that age-related efects on user innovation are mitigated by three contextual factors: job control, IT support, and user enjoyment. To develop a formal representation of this model, we first conceptualise the causal link between age and user innovation (Research Objective 1). We then refine this baseline model by elucidating the mitigating factors that managers and systems designers need to consider in order to control age-related efects on user innovation (Research Objective 2).

## 3.1. Perceived ability to be creative with IT as an underlying mechanism (Baseline mediation model for research objective 1)

When applying the creativity model of ageing to the study of user innovation, it is possible to formulate the hypothesis that advanced age leads to erosions in users’ perceived ability to be creative with IT, and this hampers the efort that older users make to innovate with IT.

The perceived ability of users to be creative with IT is a self-concept trait that reflects the extent to which users believe that they can use technology in creative ways to get their work done more efectively (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998; Rahrovani & Pinsonneault, 2014, 2015, 2020). This self-concept trait can take diferent forms, such as creative IT self-eficacy or personal innovativeness with IT (Binnewies et al., 2008). These constructs are directly related to creativity and innovation, which is the focus of this investigation and of the creativity model of ageing. By contrast, related constructs such as IT self-eficacy or computer competence are much broader and thus less relevant to this investigation.

IT self-eficacy is defined as the extent to which workers believe that they can use IT successfully to accomplish work tasks (Compeau & Higgins, 1995). It is a general construct that has no direct relation to creativity or innovation. In fact, the breadth of this construct has allowed IS researchers to adapt it to various technologies, such as Internet self-eficacy (Lam & Lee, 2006), M-health self-eficacy (Fox & Connolly, 2018), and Digital self-eficacy (Shao et al., 2021). Similarly, computer competence has limited ties to creativity or innovation because it is broadly defined as a learned behaviour involving some combination of cognitive and motor processes in order to maximise performance when working with a computer (Marcolin et al., 2000). The next section provides further analysis of the creativity model of ageing and specifies the hypotheses that formalise our baseline mediation model.

## 3.1.1. Connecting age to the perceived ability of users to be creative with IT

Based on the creativity model of ageing (Binnewies et al., 2008) and the literature on technology and aging (Schloegel et al., 2018), we use the concept of negative age stereotyping to explain the connection between the age of users and their perceived ability to be creative with IT.

Age stereotypes are socially-shared beliefs about the abilities and behaviours that members of a specific age group have in common (Fiske et al., 2002). Age stereotypes are mostly negative because older workers are viewed as low in competence (Fiske et al., 2002). Negative age stereotypes are very common, especially in the context of IT-related work (Schloegel et al., 2018). One persistent and widespread negative age stereotype at organisations is that older workers are less innovative (Lee et al., 2010; Ng & Feldman, 2013). There is some truth to this stereotype: it parallels findings showing that older users are less innovative with IT than their younger counterparts (Lee et al., 2010). This does not mean that every older worker is less innovative than every younger worker but that older workers are less innovative with IT on average, as an aggregate. It is crucial to note that older workers are not less innovative per se. Instead, the stereotype that older workers are less innovative holds true because it is a self-fulfiling prophecy (Ng & Feldman, 2013; Robertson et al., 2015; Wandke et al., 2012), a false prediction that evokes corresponding beliefs and thereby causes the originally false prediction to become true. Specifically, since older users are faced with a great deal of negative stereotypes and since they have usually even held these stereotypes themselves long before becoming older, over time they have started to believe that these stereotypes are true (Robertson et al., 2015; Wandke et al., 2012). This means that – due to negative age stereotypes – workers generally expect their creative potential to decline as they age, with a corresponding erosion of their perceived ability to be creative with IT (Binnewies et al., 2008; Rietzschel et al., 2016). This is in line with recent evidence showing that “increased negative age stereotypes (and stereotype threat) regarding creativity and innovation with age suggest negative efects of ageing on creative and innovative behavior” (Rietzschel et al., 2016, p. 107).

Indeed, negative age stereotypes are learned early in life through socialisation and reinforced later in life through co-workers. This means that the confidence of older workers in their own abilities is reduced, with sometimes dramatic consequences. For example, older persons who have held more negative age stereotypes earlier in life are significantly more at risk to develop Alzheimer’s disease (Levy et al., 2016). Due to their reduced confidence in their own abilities later in life, they demonstrate significantly steeper brain losses than those who do not have reduced confidence in their own abilities.

The creativity model of ageing maintains that – in response to negative age stereotypes – older workers have a less positive perception of their ability to be creative than younger workers do. In the context of user innovation, this means that older workers likely have a lower perceived ability to use IT in creative ways to get their work done. Therefore, it is plausible to assume the following hypothesis:

H1a: Age is negatively related to a user’s perceived ability to be creative with IT.

## 3.1.2. Connecting the perceived ability of users to be creative with IT to user innovation

The creativity model of ageing and seminal research on creativity indicate that the perceived ability to be creative has strong ties to creative behaviour in general and to innovation in particular (Amabile, 1988; Binnewies et al., 2008; Tierney & Farmer, 2002). This is because a greater perceived ability to be creative motivates workers to make a greater efort to innovate. Creative behaviours like innovation require an internal, sustaining force that pushes persons to persist in the face of the various challenges that are native to creative work, and persons with a greater selfperceived ability to be creative are more likely to persist when encountering these challenges (Bandura, 1977; Tierney & Farmer, 2002).

The relationship between perceived ability and use behaviour has been examined in the IS literature as well (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Marakas et al., 1998; Zhang & Venkatesh, 2013). For example, IS research has argued that computer self-eficacy helps individuals make a greater efort towards the efective use of technologies and that it allows them to be more persistent in the face of challenging use situations like trying to innovate with IT (Marakas et al., 1998). Such research has also indicated that individuals with high computer self-eficacy set higher goals for themselves regarding the use of technologies and that they are more committed to reaching those goals. Since we conceptualised user innovation as a goal-based construct, it is clear that greater levels of goal commitment have direct impacts on it. If this inference holds true for computer self-eficacy, it should hold true even more for constructs like creative IT self-eficacy (Rahrovani & Pinsonneault, 2014, 2015, 2020) and personal innovativeness with IT (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998; Venkatesh et al., 2014), which are more specific and relevant to user innovation. Elevated levels of selfconcept traits like users’ perceived ability to be creative with IT can also lead to changes in how users approach cognitive tasks such as trying to innovate with IT. These changes include a broader information search and a greater memory recall, both of which improve innovation (Tierney & Farmer, 2002). Therefore, it is plausible to assume the following hypothesis:

H1b: A user’s perceived ability to be creative with IT is positively related to the user’s goal to innovate with IT.

3.1.3. Connecting age to user innovation through the perceived ability of users to be creative with IT As regards the causal order of the constructs, it is apparent that age cannot be expected to cause decrements in user innovation directly. As Binnewies et al. (2008) make clear, the reduction in the perceived ability to be creative causes declines in innovation and age is responsible for this reduction. This underscores the fact that the perceived ability to be creative is conceptually closer to innovation than age is and that it plays the fundamental role of mediating the efect of age on user innovation.

We use the framework of proximal and distal individual diferences to examine the causal order of the constructs more closely (Luszcz et al., 1997). Individual diferences characterised as proximal are conceptually closer to the dependent variable, whereas those characterized as distal are conceptually further away from it. Depending on whether they are cognitive or non-cognitive predictors, individual diferences such as age and the perceived ability to be creative can be either proximal or distal with respect to user innovation (Luszcz et al., 1997; Salthouse, 1991). Noncognitive predictors are distal with respect to innovation, whereas cognitive ones are proximal with respect to innovation. This has been demonstrated in studies observing non-cognitive and cognitive predictors concurrently (e.g., Nyberg & Backman, 1996). It can also be inferred from research confirming that cognitive predictors substantively reduce or even eliminate agerelated variance and that non-cognitive ones are not nearly as efective in doing so (Hultsch et al., 1993). Age is a demographic characteristic of users, which makes it a non-cognitive predictor. By contrast, the perceived ability to be creative is a cognitive one because it reflects a psychological state (Binnewies et al., 2008). Therefore, the perceived ability to be creative with IT is conceptually closer to user innovation than age is.

In a word, proximal cognitive predictors like the perceived ability to be creative often mediate the impact that more distal non-cognitive ones like age have on innovation (Binnewies et al., 2008; Luszcz et al., 1997). This means that the perceived ability to be creative with IT can provide a precise explanation for the efect that age has on user innovation. Therefore, it is plausible to assume the following hypothesis:

H1c: The perceived ability to be creative with IT mediates the negative efect of age on trying to innovate with IT.

## 3.2. Mitigating factors (1st stage moderated mediation for research objective 2)

According to the creativity model of ageing (Binnewies et al., 2008), certain factors can mitigate the innovation problem for older users. These mitigating factors include job control and support for creativity. Perceived enjoyment is also relevant because it increases the likelihood that users view their own abilities regarding technology use and creativity as more positive. Therefore, we suggest that job control, perceived enjoyment, and IT support can provide a bufer against the negative consequences of age-related declines in the perceived ability to be creative with IT.

The creativity model of ageing maintains that these three mitigating factors moderate the relation between age and creativity. This is because these factors can counteract negative age stereotypes about creativity so that they weaken the efect of age on creativity perceptions (Binnewies et al., 2008). Thus we argue that they moderate Path a, the first stage of the mediation process that we hypothesise. In other words, the moderation operates on the efect that age has on the perceived ability to be creative with IT. There are two reasons that Path b, the second stage of the mediation process, is not moderated. First, the relation between the perceived ability to be creative with IT and user innovation is not based on negative age stereotypes about creativity, and this means that the moderators, which were selected based on the creativity model of aging, are less important for this path. Second, given that the relation between age and user innovation and the relation between perceived creativity and user innovation are foundational premises of our model, examining moderators of them would be less interesting and would reduce the parsimony of the research model that is developed here.

## 3.2.1. Job control as a mitigating factor

A high degree of job control indicates to employees that they are believed capable of successfully fulfiling their work tasks. This indication runs counter to the negative age stereotypes that older workers often hold against themselves. In doing so, it casts doubt on perceptions of reduced abilities, so that the negative efect of age on the perceived ability to be creative with IT should be weakened.

This notion is consistent with seminal research on control. For example, Karasek (1979) points out that control represents an opportunity to exercise judgement, which boosts workers’ feelings of self-eficacy. Moreover, job control is especially important for older workers as their capacity for primary control decreases. In other words, the capacity of persons to adapt their external world to their personal needs declines with age (Schulz et al., 2015), and this means that enhancing the control striving of older workers through control on the job is likely to be a particularly potent motivating factor for them.

This idea is in line with the results of Binnewies et al. (2008), who found that job control moderates the relationship between age and creativity so that age is positively related to creativity when job control is high and that it is negatively related to creativity when job control is low. In addition, IS research has shown that control is a major factor in the context of trying to innovate with IT (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005). Therefore, it is plausible to assume the following hypothesis:

H2a: As mediated by the perceived ability to be creative with IT, age has an indirect efect on trying to innovate with IT that is weaker when the user has more job control.

## 3.2.2. Perceived enjoyment as a mitigating factor

Perceived enjoyment is a core element of intrinsic motivation, which is fundamental to the creative process (Amabile, 1988). In fact, intrinsic motivation and enjoyment are the most important factors in creative idea generation. They are even more important than experience and expertise (Amabile, 1988). The basic premise is that workers are less likely to develop negative perceptions of their own abilities if they are intrinsically motivated to perform a behaviour. In the context of IT-related work, seminal research on perceived enjoyment and technology acceptance indicates that hedonic motivation and especially user enjoyment are strongly related to IT usage and to a user’s goal to innovate with IT (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000; Davis et al., 1992; Van der Heijden, 2004; Venkatesh et al., 2012). This means that users are likely to view their own abilities regarding technology use and creativity with IT as more positive when they enjoy using technology.

At the same time, enjoyment is relevant to ageing because human beings become increasingly selective as they age. Indeed, socioemotional selectivity theory maintains that older persons seek out positive emotional experiences a lot more than younger persons do (Carstensen et al., 2003; Mettler et al., 2021). As a result, they invest themselves more fully in goals and activities that maximise positive emotional experiences like enjoyment. In other words, aging leads to the desire to increase enjoyment in life, and if older persons enjoy an activity, they are more likely than younger persons to embark on it fully.

Taken together, these arguments indicate that older workers who enjoy using an IT are less likely to develop negative self-perceptions about their ability to be creative with it. Even in the presence of negative age stereotypes, they are more likely to seek out enjoyable and stimulating experiences like developing new ideas about how to use an IT. Therefore, it is plausible to assume the following hypothesis:

H2b: As mediated by the perceived ability to be creative with IT, age has an indirect efect on trying to innovate with IT that is weaker when the user has more perceived enjoyment.

## 3.2.3. Support for creativity as a mitigating factor

Organizational support in general could have a negative relationship with creativity if all that were asked of employees is to stick to established routines and learned behaviours (Amabile et al., 1994). However, support for creativity will have positive implications for employee creativity if it motivates employees to go beyond established routines and learned behaviors. The creativity model of ageing maintains that receiving a high level of support for creativity signals to workers that developing creative ideas is valued. This increases their motivation to be creative.

In the context of this investigation, IT support is a relevant motivating factor. It can take various forms such as user guides, access to super-users, and technical support through an IT helpdesk that can assist with numerous problems such as password resets, printer configurations, and new software installations. These forms of IT support are often combined to increase their efectiveness (Sykes, 2015).

Based on the creativity model of ageing, we hold that IT support signals to workers that developing new ideas about how to use an IT is valued. This signal counters the negative age stereotypes that older workers often hold against themselves, especially regarding the use of technology. Thus, the negative relationship between age and the perceived ability to be creative with IT should be weakened.

Moreover, Amabile (1988) suggests that support can foster perceptions of empowerment in workers, which increases self-perceived abilities. This means that greater IT support may foster perceptions of empowerment in users and that empowered users feel that they are autonomous and that they may have an impact on how IT is used for their jobs and for the organisation. This likely creates more positive perceptions of their ability to be creative with IT. As a result, the negative efect of age on the perceived ability to be creative with IT should be weakened.

Furthermore, IT support can create a climate of psychological safety that encourages workers to generate new ideas about how to use an IT. For example, generating new ideas about how to use an IT is more doable and less risky when users have access to super users or to technical specialists who can help. This will encourage older workers to have a more positive perception of their ability to use IT in creative ways (Tierney & Farmer, 2002). Therefore, it is plausible to assume the following hypothesis:

H2c: As mediated by the perceived ability to be creative with IT, age has an indirect efect on trying to innovate with IT that is weaker when the user has more IT support.

## 4. General method

We conducted two complementary studies. This approach helped improve internal validity because it enabled convergent validation. In addition, Study 1 focused on external validity and Study 2 on ecological validity. Study 1 consisted in a large-scale survey of IT users who work with various technologies. Its primary goal was to test whether our model is generalisable across job positions and technologies. Study 2 consisted in a survey that made it possible to accomplish two additional goals. The first goal was to replicate the findings from Study 1 with a diferent set of measures to demonstrate that these findings are real and not measurement-specific. The second and especially important goal was to extend the findings from Study 1 to a population of users who work with one specific technology. This means that Study 2 developed the results from Study 1 further by highlighting the specific role of information systems in the relation between age and user innovation. The systems examined in Study 2 were electronic knowledge repositories. Our two-study approach enhanced the contribution of the present investigation by producing results for organisational technologies in general and for electronic knowledge repositories in particular.

## 4.1. Study 1

The objective of Study 1 was to test whether our model is generalisable across job positions and technologies. Thus Study 1 was a large-scale survey of users of various technologies.

## 4.1.1. Study 1 sample and measures

Data were collected from workers of varying ages who use organisational IT. To satisfy the sample frame requirements, the following screening question was used: “At the organization where you are employed, do you currently use Information Technology (IT) to accomplish your work tasks?”

Following prior IS research (e.g., Ayyagari et al., 2011) and recent methodological guidelines (Lowry et al., 2016), a market research company was used for data collection. These companies ofer many advantages for survey research, including generalisability (Lowry et al., 2016). We used Empanelonline, which performs sophisticated panel management, stressing verification and data quality. As part of the panel member recruitment process, Empanelonline verifies that prospective panellists are who they say they are, including the verification of respondents against third-party databases, the validation of email and postal addresses, and the testing of whether respondents’ responses are sound (e.g., Empanelonline uses data mining methods to compare respondents’ stated income levels to their professions).

Empanelonline also ensures through careful maintenance of its panel that respondents are qualified to answer surveys. For example, it removes unserious survey takers and implements knowledge-based questions that define whether a respondent is qualified to take a survey. To obtain the highest level of data quality, Empanelonline incorporates numerous security features such as SurValidate Digital Fingerprinting and Double opt-in requirements.

We took additional steps to ensure data quality. For example, we used additional quality assurance questions. Throughout the survey, we had multiple questions such as “For this particular question, please select the response option “moderately disagree”. Only subjects responding “moderately disagree” to these questions were allowed to continue with the survey. The others were redirected to the end of the survey and their responses were discarded.

We paid \$9 USD per complete. While relatively high, this price was justified given Empanelonline’s strict panel management and verification procedures (including a double opt-in process) alongside our various quality assurance mechanisms, which ensured high data quality.

In total, we collected data from 880 respondents. All respondents were located in the U.S. Thirty-eight percent of the respondents were female, the average age was 40 years (SD = 11.41), and the average organisational tenure was 8 years. While answering the survey questions, the respondents were asked to focus on the IT that they used the most frequently for workrelated purposes. The survey referred to this technology as “this IT”. For 50% of the respondents, “this IT” was function IT such as Microsoft Excel, Word, or PowerPoint that assists with the execution of discrete tasks. For 26% of them, it was network IT such as Microsoft Outlook that facilitates interactions with others. Finally, for 24% of them, it was enterprise IT such as SAP that supports entire business processes. The respondents had been using “this IT” for about seven years on average.

All measures were adapted from prior research (Appendix A details the full scales). Age was measured as a continuous variable of chronological age. Respondents’ perceived ability to be creative with IT was operationalised as creative IT self-eficacy (Rahrovani & Pinsonneault, 2014, 2015, 2020), consistent with the creativity model of ageing. The measures of trying to innovate with IT, job control, IT support, and perceived enjoyment were adopted from Ahuja and Thatcher (2005), Parker et al. (2006), Teo et al. (1999), and Van der Heijden (2004). As regards control variables, we used gender, organisational tenure, type of IT used at work (since the survey was administered to users of diferent types of organizational IT), experience with their respective IT, and conscientiousness as these variables have been shown to afect IT use and creative behaviour (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Amabile, 1988; Binnewies et al, 2008; Rahrovani & Pinsonneault, 2014, 2015, 2020; Venkatesh et al., 2012).

## 4.1.2. Study 1 analysis and results

A psychometric analysis confirmed that our survey instrument had good reliability and construct validity (see Appendix B). However, since common method bias can afect inferences of causality, we used both procedural and statistical remedies to control for it (Podsakof et al., 2003). We embedded relevant procedural remedies like the protection of respondent anonymity and the reduction of evaluation apprehension. To evaluate the significance of method variance in our data, the single factor test was performed through a factor analysis with Maximum Likelihood Extraction and Promax Rotation (Podsakof et al., 2003). In this approach, all items are forced to fit on a single factor representing method efects. Common method variance is considered significant if the model fits the data. In our data, a one-factor model showed substantial misfit $( \chi ^ { 2 } ~ [ 2 0 9 ] = 8 , 6 7 2 . 9 4 , ~ \mathfrak { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ Further, we compared this measurement model to the full model and found that the 1-factor model fit the data worse $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } ~ [ 7 8 ] = 8 2 2 5 . 7 3 , ~ \mathrm { p < 0 . 0 0 1 ) }$ . This indicated that common method variance was not found.

We proceeded with a formal test of our mediation hypotheses (H1a-H1c) and moderated mediation hypotheses (H2a-H2c) using conditional process analysis (Hayes, 2015, 2017). This type of analysis integrates moderation and mediation procedures. To do so, it relies on the principles of least squares regression, but it employs a bootstrapping procedure to estimate indirect efects and moderated indirect efects. Bootstrapping is widely used in statistical mediation analysis, and its performance has been extensively studied and shown to be superior to the Causal Steps Approach (Baron & Kenny, 1986) and the Sobel test (Hayes, 2017; Sobel, 1982; Zhao et al., 2010). It also combines high statistical power with good control over the Type I error rate (MacKinnon et al., 2004).

All the hypotheses were tested using PROCESS version 3 for conditional process analysis in SPSS (Hayes, 2017; Preacher et al., 2007). For Hypotheses 1a-1c, we employed PROCESS model number 4 with a 95% confidence interval and 1,000 bootstrap resamples in SPSS version 26. Hypotheses 2a-2c were tested using PROCESS model number 7 for assessing 1<sup>st</sup> stage moderated mediation. This model estimates the conditional indirect efects at diferent levels of the moderators. Consistent with our theorising that examined the separate efects of the three moderating variables, we estimated three separate models, one for each of the three moderators. In terms of procedure, conditional process analysis estimates the coeficients for the statistical model and calculates the index of moderated mediation (Hayes, 2017). This index assesses whether the moderated mediation efect is significant.

## 4.1.3. Study 1 discussion

Study 1 provides evidence that age reduces user innovation because it is accompanied by declines in creative IT self-eficacy. Study 1 also shows that this negative indirect efect depends on job control and user enjoyment, so that it is ofset by high levels of these moderators.

The study had several strengths. First, our relatively large sample of 880 users of organisational IT increased statistical conclusion validity. Specifically, it increased the reliability of our results by producing more accurate estimates. Indeed, there was less risk of error and, in particular, it was possible to limit the probability of type II errors. This means that we can have confidence in the conclusions drawn from our analysis. Second, our sample represented a variety of IT types, which we controlled for in our analysis. Therefore, our results are widely generalisable. Finally, we used established and validated measures for all constructs.

Study 1 also had some limitations. We did not find support for H2c, which posited that IT support should moderate the indirect efect of age through the perceived ability to be creative with IT on trying to innovate with IT. One plausible explanation is that IT support may not be any more relevant to older users than it is to younger ones. We know that both job control and perceived enjoyment are markedly more important for older persons than for their younger counterparts (Carstensen et al., 2003; Mettler et al., 2021; Schulz et al., 2015), but no research to date confirms that the same is true for aspects of organisational support such as IT support. For example, sending the signal to workers that an organization values new ideas about how to use an IT (through IT support) may not be any more important for older users than for younger ones in terms of counteracting perceptions of an inability to be creative with IT. The fact that IT support can create a climate of psychological safety may also be just as relevant to younger users as it is to older ones because younger users are often faced with greater risks as they develop their careers. Therefore, IT support may not be relevant to the relation between age and user innovation.

A second limitation of Study 1 is that the measure of age may have limited our ability to fully understand the role of age in user innovation because the measure was chronological. Age was measured as the number of years that a person has lived. On the one hand, this measure is consistent with much prior research in IS and in the reference disciplines, and it ascertains an objective measurement of age because it represents a physiological state. On the other hand, chronological age is only one aspect of age. Another important aspect is cognitive age (Ghasemaghaei et al., 2019), which refers to a psychological state – it refers to age as a state of mind. This means that it is a subjective measure, since it is based on how old individuals perceive themselves to be. Thus Study 2 should measure age subjectively to advance understanding of the link between age and user innovation.

Moreover, the measure of respondents’ perceived ability to be creative with IT that we used in Study 1 (creative IT self-eficacy) represents only one way of evaluating the extent to which users have a predisposition to being creative with IT. Another useful way of assessing the perceived ability of respondents to be creative with IT is personal innovativeness with IT (PIIT) (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998; Venkatesh et al., 2014). Much like creative IT self-eficacy, PIIT is linked to creativity with IT, and it is of great relevancy in the study of user innovation (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998; Dong & Yang, 2019; Liao et al., 2015; Scott & Walczak, 2009; Thatcher & Perrewe,). Thus Study 2 should use PIIT to evaluate the perceived ability of users to be creative with IT. This approach will shed additional light on the link between age and user innovation with IT.

## 4.2. Study 2

The objective of Study 2 was twofold: 1) to replicate the findings from Study 1 using a second dataset with diferent measures for age and for the perceived ability to be creative with IT in order to demonstrate that our findings are real and not measurement-specific, and 2) to extend the findings from Study 1 to a population of users who work with one specific technology. The second objective was especially important because it increased the ecological validity of our investigation.

## 4.2.1. Study 2 sample

For study 2 we collected data from knowledge workers who used electronic knowledge repositories (EKRs) to share their knowledge with co-workers. This sample population was appropriate for Study 2 because an increasing number of knowledge workers are older employees (Erlich & Bichard, 2008; Kogovsek & Kogovsek, 2013; Lord & Farrington, 2006). Moreover, negative stereotyping against older workers is a particularly critical problem for knowledge work because such work involves abilities like creativity that are often believed to be lacking in older workers (Lord & Farrington, 2006). Additionally, it is very important for organisations that older knowledge workers share their knowledge before they eventually retire so that their accumulated experience and expertise can be retained and does not “walk out the door” (Davenport & Prusak, 2000; Erlich & Bichard, 2008).

Analogous to study 1, we used the market research company Empanelonline for data collection. To satisfy sample frame requirements, three screening questions were developed. These questions asked the prospective respondents whether they had access to an EKR, whether contributing their knowledge to the EKR in their organisation helped co-workers they did not know, and whether they contributed their knowledge voluntarily to the EKR as opposed to being required to do so (see Appendix D.. Only prospective respondents who satisfied the sample frame requirements were able to participate in the study. Others, who did not satisfy the sample frame requirements, were screened out.

We also used an additional knowledge-based screening question to ensure that respondents were qualified to take the survey. The question assessed whether prospective respondents knew what the purpose of an EKR was. Specifically, we included the following quality assurance question: In your experience, what is the primary goal of electronic knowledge repositories? Only subjects responding “Sharing ideas and information, for example, creating documents and uploading them to Microsoft SharePoint” were allowed to continue with the survey. This question ensured that the respondents understood the context of the study. Thus, we directly targeted the desired sample frame.

As in study 1, we took additional steps to ensure data quality. This included trap questions that confirmed that the respondents were taking the survey seriously. For example, we included questions in the survey such as “For this particular question, please select the response option “moderately disagree”. Only subjects responding “moderately disagree” to these questions were allowed to continue with the survey. The others were redirected to the end of the survey and their responses were discarded.

We paid \$25 USD per complete. While relatively high, this price was justified given our restrictive sample requirements as well as Empanelonline’s strict panel management and verification procedures (including a double opt-in process) alongside our various quality assurance mechanisms.

In total, we collected data from 130 knowledge workers with access to EKRs. All respondents were located in the U.S. Sixty-nine percent of the respondents were female, the average age was 36 years (SD = 14.70), and the average organisational tenure was 7 years. The respondents were asked to report on the EKRs they used for knowledge sharing. Twenty respondents used Lotus Notes, 84 used Microsoft SharePoint, 16 used the Salesforce.com chatter tool, 57 used a company portal, and 19 used a company wiki. Some respondents used a combination of these systems such as Lotus Notes supplemented with the company portal or wiki.

## 4.2.2. Study 2 measures

All measures were adapted from prior research. Appendix A details the full scales. Following recent research (Ghasemaghaei et al., 2019; Tams et al., 2014), Study 2 measured age by assessing respondents’ cognitive age. Cognitive age is a psychological state; it refers to age as a state of mind. Accordingly, cognitive age denotes how old individuals perceive themselves to be.

For the mediator variable, we selected a wellestablished scale of IT creativity instead of the relatively new measure that was used in Study 1. This approach allowed us to triangulate the mediator variable across the two studies. Specifically, in study 2 we used personal innovativeness in IT (PIIT) as the mediator variable (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998). This measure is linked to IT creativity, and it is very relevant to the study of IT innovation (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998; Liao et al., 2015; Scott & Walczak, 2009; Thatcher & Perrewe, ; Venkatesh et al., 2014). As regards the dependent variable (trying to innovate with IT) and the moderator variables (job control and user enjoyment) we used the same measures as in study 1. We did not include IT support in Study 2 because there was no evidence of its relevance in study 1. As for the control variables, we dropped gender and tenure because these variables were not significantly related to the criterion variable in study 1. Instead, we used controls that were more relevant to knowledge sharing. Specifically, we controlled for the type of EKR that was used (e.g., Microsoft SharePoint or a company wiki), the frequency with which knowledge contributions were made, conscientiousness, and overload since these constructs have been shown to impact knowledge sharing and innovation (e.g., Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Bock et al., 2005; Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

## 4.2.4. Study 2 discussion

In study 2 we replicated our model using diferent measures and extended it using a more specific sample of IT users. Study 2 provided evidence that our mode holds even when a diferent set of measures is used (cognitive age instead of chronological age and PIIT instead of creative IT self-eficacy). Thus study 2 demonstrated that our model is not measurementspecific and that its relationships are robust.

In addition, study 2 showed that our model holds even for a particular population of users who work with one specific technology: knowledge workers using EKRs. Consequently, study 2 explained further the results from study 1 by extending them to the specific context of knowledge work. In doing so, study 2 contextualised the results from study 1, and it generated an in-depth understanding of the role of the IT artefact in the relationship between age and user innovation.

Study 2 had several strengths. For example, we targeted specifically the desired sample frame. Our panel company invited only knowledge workers to participate. We confirmed through additional screening questions and knowledge-based questions that all respondents had access to an EKR and suficient knowledge of it. We also ensured through quality assurance questions that the respondents were taking the survey seriously. As a result, we obtained high quality data. Second, we captured the type of EKR the respondents worked with, and we controlled for it in our analysis. This was important because diferent EKRs, such as Lotus Notes and Microsoft SharePoint, have diferent levels of complexity that might afect the extent to which they are used for knowledge sharing. Finally, we used well-established and validated measures for all constructs.

Study 2 also had some limitations. An important limitation was sample size. Given the relatively high cost associated with each respondent, we collected a smaller dataset consisting of 130 knowledge workers. This sample size could have limited our ability to detect the hypothesised efects. However, our sample size still gave us suficient statistical power to detect the hypothesized efects, and it was in line with seminal studies on knowledge sharing that used comparable sample sizes (e.g., Bock et al., 2005; Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Moreover, one of the objectives of study 2 was to reinforce and extend the findings from study 1. Thus study 2 was not a stand-alone study, but an integrative element of our two-study approach. As a result, its sample size was acceptable.

Moreover, common method variance could threaten the results. Yet we incorporated relevant procedural remedies in our research design as discussed earlier in this article. As a result, method bias did not appear to be an issue. This conclusion is consistent with the observation of numerous interaction efects in our study because method variance attenuates these efects and makes it more dificult to detect them (Conway & Lance, 2010). We also obtained consistent results across our two studies using diferent measures for age and IT creativity, a clear indication that our findings were not measurement-specific.

## 5. General discussion

The research presented here has several strengths, most notably the use of a relevant theory base for developing the research model. According to the creativity model of ageing, there is one overall factor – perceived ability to be creative with IT – that mediates age-related diferences in individual innovation (Binnewies et al., 2008; Czaja et al., 2019). This factor is a self-concept trait that can take diferent forms such as creative self-eficacy or personal innovativeness. In line with Bacharach (1989) and Gregor (2006), using the creativity model of aging for this investigation was extremely valuable because it helped provide a parsimonious account of age-related diferences in user innovation with IT.

To ensure rigour, we employed a two-study research design that was consistent with the theoretical frame and the extant literature (Binnewies et al., 2008; Tams et al., 2014). This design featured two complementary methods: a large-scale survey of users of various IT systems and a second survey targeting knowledge workers with access to EKRs. Thus, the research presented here achieved relatively high internal validity, external validity, and ecological validity. Internal validity was high because study 2 confirmed the causal relationships identified in study 1. In this way, study 2 reinforced the certainty and confidence in the nature of the age efects that were observed. External validity was relatively high because study 1 found the hypothesised efects to hold across various IT systems such as Functional IT (e.g., Microsoft Ofice), Network IT (e.g., email, videoconferencing), and Enterprise IT (e.g., SAP, Oracle ERP). Finally, ecological validity was high because study 2 contextualised our results, showing that they hold for a specific user population and a specific class of systems, that is, for knowledge workers using EKRs. This means that study 2 was consistent with the motivation for this research and that it captured much of the information system’s element that we theorised about.

Moreover, the use of diferent measures in studies 1 and 2 for the age of respondents and for their perceived ability to be creative with IT allowed us to undertake convergent validation, which is a central dimension of methodological triangulation (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). Since the diferent measures yielded consistent results, they reinforced the certainty and confidence in our findings even more.

As regards conceptual rigour, we conducted a careful analysis of the considerations for theory adaptation laid out by Truex et al. (2006). This analysis indicated that the creativity model of ageing was appropriate for this research. Moreover, in line with Luszcz et al. (1997) and Salthouse (1991), we hypothesised mediation explicitly by following the logic of distal and proximal individual diference predictors. This logic let us to conclude that age is distal to user innovation, whereas self-concept traits such as creative IT self-eficacy and personal innovativeness with IT are more proximal to it. Seldom used in IS research, this approach allowed us to hypothesize mediation directly instead of testing for it only post-hoc.

## 5.1. Implications for researchers

This article makes several contributions to the literature on IS usage. First, it responds to recent calls for more research on the efects of ageing on the post-adoptive use of IT (Abouzahra & Ghasemaghaei, 2022; Tams et al., 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Wagner et al., 2010). Because of its central role, age was treated as a substantive variable in this article. Second, although prior research has improved understanding of age-related impacts on IT adoption, it has not developed an in-depth explanation of these impacts by employing a relevant theory of aging. Using the creativity model of aging, this article creates an improved model for understanding the relation between age and user innovation. In line with Tams et al. (2014), we identify a user’s perceived ability to be creative with IT as a touch point of age. This touch point of age helps explain the relationship between age and user innovation more fully. We also find that job control and perceived enjoyment act as moderators. The negative indirect efect that age has on user innovation by way of the perceived ability to be creative with IT depends on these moderators. This indirect efect is weaker when these moderators are at high levels.

Overall, we contribute two central new insights to the literature on IS use (see Figure 3):

(1) We identify the mechanisms that account for the relation between age and user innovation. These are the self-concept traits: creative IT self-eficacy and personal innovativeness with IT. Thus we show that there is an indirect efect of age on user innovation by way of the perceived ability to be creative with IT.

(2) We identify two boundary conditions that explain when the indirect efect manifests itself. These are the two moderators: job control and perceived enjoyment. They are boundary conditions because they limit (or minimise) the efects of age on user innovation with IT.

These new insights build on past research and extend it in several ways. For example, Sipior et al. (2011) hypothesised that older workers would use IT less because of increased access barriers and reduced perceptions of ease of use and usefulness. However, they did not find support for these hypotheses. Yao and Murphy (2007) found that older adults are less accepting of remote electronic voting systems, although the precise mechanisms at play and the potential mitigating factors were not examined. Abouzahra and Ghasemaghaei (2022) found a relationship between the mental and physical capabilities of seniors and their willingness to use wearable devices. Niehaves and Plattfaut (2014) have developed a comprehensive model of internet use among older adults that incorporates various control beliefs, normative beliefs, and attitudes. The model developed in the present investigation contributes to this discussion because it focuses on the post-adoptive use of workplace IT and because it examines the relationship between age and IT adoption in greater detail using the concepts of mediation and moderation.

Third, the present investigation contributes to the literature on user innovation with IT. Specifically, our study responds to calls for more empirical work on the role of individual diferences in shaping innovativeness with IT. Ahuja and Thatcher (2005) suggest that individual diferences act as critical factors in determining the extent to which cognitions influence user innovation. We confirm this idea by showing that the age of IT users determines the extent to which they innovate with IT.

Fourth, study 2 contributes to the literature on knowledge management that has not yet examined user innovation in the context of EKRs. Seminal research on knowledge sharing has focused on explaining more basic use behaviours, such as usage intentions or the frequency of use of an EKR (e.g., Bock et al., 2005; Kankanhalli et al., 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Yet richer forms of usage such as trying to innovate with an EKR have greater potential to enhance users’ knowledge contributions (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Kappos & Rivard, 2008). Trying to innovate with an EKR means that users incorporate more types of knowledge into their contributions to the EKR or that they move from using basic features to richer applications of the tool. This means that study 2 contributes to the literature on knowledge sharing by directing attention to more advanced forms of EKR use, especially innovative use.

Finally, the present investigation contributes back to the reference literature by making valuable additions to the creativity model of ageing. It demonstrates that the indirect efect of age on individual innovation through the perceived ability to be creative depends on job control and perceived enjoyment. Whereas job control was validated earlier as a moderator of this indirect efect (Binnewies et al., 2008), perceived enjoyment is new to the literature.

## 5.2. Research implications for practice

Since the present investigation examines user innovation with IT, which is a long-standing phenomenon of interest in IS, it is practically significant. This conclusion holds especially true given that user innovation is linked to organisational productivity and performance. As clarified above in the introduction, many successful firms such as Microsoft, 3 M, and Nike encourage their employees to innovate with IT in order to realise the full potential of their existing IT infrastructure.

The driving force of our model, age, is particularly relevant to the real-world because the workforces in OECD countries are rapidly growing older (Beck, 2017; OECD, 2022). Our sample was comprised of US workers, but those in other OECD countries experience similar labour market changes with respect to age. Moreover, a contextual generalisation (Tsang & Williams, 2012) of the creativity model of age and innovation with IT is possible. The model was tested using US respondents, but it can be generalised to respondents from other OECD countries. Although creativity and innovative use can be interpreted diferently across countries (Agourram & Ingham, 2007; Hofstede, 1980; Morris & Leung, 2010), there is no reason to believe that the relationships between the study variables do not hold true in other contexts (Rosenzweig, 1994). Therefore, we assume that the relationship between age and IT creativity in other OECD countries is similar to the one that was discovered here. This conclusion is substantiated by the fact that our results were consistent with our theorising and that the latter was independent of specific OECD countries or cultures. In line with this conclusion, Klein and Bhagat (2016) showed that, despite diferences in consumers’ creativity, passion, and thinking style in India and the US, technological innovativeness was heavily influenced by young consumers in both contexts.

This research elucidates several factors that can mitigate the negative consequences of an ageing workforce for user innovation: creative IT self-eficacy, personal innovativeness with IT, job control, and user enjoyment. These factors represent four levers that practitioners can pull to control age-related efects on user innovation. Managers can build creative IT self-eficacy in older employees by providing them with slack resources such as access to additional IT features or with IT training that builds perceived competence for user innovation (Rahrovani & Pinsonneault, 2015). Moreover, they should increase older workers’ disposition to trust IT since this disposition has been shown to enhance individuals’ personal innovativeness with IT (McKnight et al., 2002, 2011). To this end, managers can implement IT systems that have outstanding help functions and are well-known for their reliability and comprehensive functionality (McKnight et al., 2011). These features instil trust in IT and, thus, can increase users’ personal innovativeness in IT (McKnight et al., 2002, 2011).

Moreover, managers can extend greater job control to older employees. Thanks to more control, older employees will feel that management trusts their ability to successfully complete their work tasks. This indication will run counter to the negative age stereotypes that older workers often hold against themselves. As a result, the negative efect of age on user innovation through creative IT self-eficacy or personal innovativeness with IT is weakened. Enhanced job control can take various forms, such as control over what work tasks to perform, how to perform the tasks, task objectives, work pace, and control over the technical environment (e.g., what technologies to use and how to use them). All these aspects of control will signal to older workers that managers believe in their ability to successfully complete their work tasks, and this mitigates their perceptions of incompetence and inability to be creative.

Moreover, extending more job control to older workers allows them to better manage competing job demands so that more cognitive resources remain available for user innovation. The cognitive ageing literature suggests that, on average, older workers experience declines in the speed of information processing (Salthouse, 2016), in working memory (Baddeley, 1992), and in selective attention (Darowski et al., 2008; Hasher & Zacks, 1988). Yet under conditions of high job control, the ability to better balance competing demands can counteract high mental workloads, and this could help especially older users maintain some cognitive resources for extra-role activities like user innovation with IT.

Like managers, systems designers learn what levers to pull to help an older workforce innovate with IT. They will learn how to design systems that older workers enjoy using. Following the notion of increasing selectivity with age (Carstensen et al., 2003), older users have the desire to increase enjoyment in their lives. The use of technology is no exception to this rule. Therefore, systems designers should leverage various aspects of systems design to make the use of technology more enjoyable for older workers. In keeping with our results, user involvement is one especially important aspect in systems design for older workers. Systems designers have to include small numbers of older users in the design and development process to learn what system characteristics they appreciate. Unfortunately, most organisational IT is developed without systematic regard for older employees (Fisk et al., 2009). All too often, systems are designed by younger developers who assume a similarity to the user. This assumption breaks down when systems are designed for older users, which means that getting them involved early in the design process is critical to obtain their feedback and to incorporate it. To maximise the benefits of involving older users early, the systems design process should use small iterations and employ rapid prototyping methods. This will help ensure that the concerns of older users are taken into account fully before a new system is deployed. As a result, older users will be more likely to enjoy using the system and, as our research has shown, this will mitigate the negative efect of age on user innovation.

Moreover, systems should employ keyword-based interface navigation so that older users can take full advantage of their crystallised intelligence, especially their knowledge of nuances in the meaning of words. This is important because 1) technologies trigger negative emotions when they are perceived as hindering performance, and 2) they trigger positive emotions when they are perceived as consistent with one’s own cognitive abilities (Jasperson et al., 2005). Given the greater vocabulary knowledge of older users and their reduced spatial abilities (Craik & Salthouse, 2011), they may experience more enjoyment when working with a keyword-based system compared to a hierarchy-based system. This is because keywordbased systems make ample use of vocabulary knowledge, a strength of older users, whereas hierarchybased systems require spatial abilities, a weakness of older users (Tams et al., 2014). This means that keyword-based systems design instead of the common folder-based design may lead to more enjoyment among older users and may, therefore, mitigate the negative efect of age on user innovation.

Finally, aesthetics will have to be considered since they are a major factor in triggering enjoyment visa-vis IT (Van der Heijden, 2004). Sensory aesthetics like screen colours and formal aesthetics such as screen layouts and screen designs can trigger positive emotions related to enjoyment (Van der Heijden, 2004). For example, cooler colors like blue and green have been found to be associated with greater enjoyment, whereas warmer colors such as red and yellow are generally associated with less enjoyment (Chebat & Morrin, 2007). This means that cooler colors may increase enjoyment and encourage older users to seek out stimulating experiences like developing new ideas about how to use an IT – even when they do not perceive enough IT-related creativity. Therefore, systems designers should use cooler colors like blue and green instead of warmer ones when designing systems that older workers are likely to use. This is an especially important design recommendation because it is counterintuitive to many designers, who often think that warmer colors are preferable.

## 5.3. Avenues for future research

Following Bacharach (1989) and other theorists who maintain that the primary goal of a theory is to answer the questions how, why, and when (i.e., under what conditions) a phenomenon occurs, the present investigation has examined the structure of the relation between age and innovation with IT using the concepts of mediation and moderation. To further improve understanding of this important relationship, future research can take two interrelated directions:

(1) developing a taxonomy of the mechanisms that explain the efect of age on user innovation;

(2) developing an understanding of how age afects the post-adoptive use of IT in general.

Developing a taxonomy of the mechanisms that explain the efect of age on user innovation: consistent with past research indicating that creativity is a key factor in explaining age diferences in innovation, we focused on self-concept traits related to the perceived ability to be creative with IT as mediators (Binnewies et al., 2008). Still, other age-related influences on user innovation might also exist. Future research should pay particular attention to computer anxiety and technostress. These factors are major inhibitors to user innovation because, in the presence of computer anxiety or technostress, individuals perceive that they lack the cognitive resources required to innovate successfully (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005). At the same time, older workers experience greater levels of computer anxiety and technostress than their younger counterparts (Tams et al., 2018). Since older workers experience more computer anxiety and technostress, and since computer anxiety and technostress inhibit in turn user innovation, there is reason to believe that computer anxiety and technostress may be other agerelated influences on user innovation with IT. However, since computer anxiety and technostress are themselves influenced by creative self-concept traits such as PIIT (Thatcher & Perrewe,), the mediators examined in this article are likely to remain the primary factors responsible for age diferences in user innovation.

Developing an understanding of how age afects the post-adoptive use of IT in general: this article focused specifically on user innovation with IT, to which creativity is essential. Hence the use of the creativity model of ageing in this article. However, user innovation is only one aspect of the post-adoptive use of IT. Another important aspect is extended feature usage, which represents the range of relevant system features that a user exploits to perform a task (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Future research should attempt to develop a universal model of age and post-adoptive IT use based on theories of cognitive aging. The goal would be to explain not only age diferences in trying to innovate with IT but also in extended feature usage or in the intention of users to explore new features. Theories of cognitive aging such as processing speed theory (Salthouse, 1996) or Cattell’s theory of fluid intelligence (Cattell, 1963; Horn & Cattell, 1967) would be useful in this endeavour because they can predict cognitive performance related to a variety of use behaviours (Tams et al., 2014). This means that a model of age and post-adoption based on a theory of cognitive aging has the potential to become a universal model of age and post-adoptive IT use. Future research should explore whether such a model can indeed predict diferent post-adoptive use behaviors, such as extended feature usage and trying to innovate with IT.

A universal model of age and post-adoptive IT use, able to predict diferent post-adoptive use behaviours, will be complementary to the one developed here. Our model is specific to user creativity and innovation and explains specific age-related variance in trying to innovate with IT. A diferent model based on broad cognitive abilities such as fluid intelligence can explain more general age-related variance. Together, both models will yield a nearly complete understanding of the challenges that the ageing workforce presents to managers who are struggling to improve user innovation with IT and post-adoptive IT use more generally.

## 6. Conclusion

This article has developed the creativity model of age and innovation with IT that explains why older users are less innovative with IT. It argues that older users are less innovative than younger ones as a result of negative age stereotyping, which leads to the erosion of older users’ perceived ability to be creative with IT. This means that the common stereotype that older workers are less innovative with IT holds true because it is a self-fulfiling prophecy, a false prediction that evokes corresponding beliefs and thereby causes the originally false prediction to become true. To alleviate this problem, our model identifies job control and user enjoyment as mitigating factors.

Ultimately, comprehensive theories that encompass all aspects of this phenomenon should be developed. However, at the present time, just developing an indepth explanation of the relation between age and user innovation with IT is a significant challenge. That being said, we believe that we have made an important step towards clarifying the role of age in user innovation with IT, and we hope that our research will lead to more work in this area to help older users like Bonnie, the 61-year-old knowledge worker in our opening vignette, to use information technology more efectively.

The results from this study will hold for future generations to the extent to which negative age stereotypes persist. Moreover, they will hold irrespective of the cohort of older users (e.g., Generation X or Generation Z). Even if younger generations grow up with current technologies, the nature of ICTs continues to evolve and change at a rapid rate (Benbasat & Zmud, 1999), and this means that older users mental models of how technology works will continue to be based on past technologies. Their mental models will continue to be dated and incongruent with later technological developments (Tams et al., 2014). This conclusion holds true because (1) ICTrelated training is usually received at a younger age, when technologies are very diferent from those present at an older age (OECD, 2011), and (2) learning about new technologies and new system features becomes more dificult as users grow older (Tams, 2021).

This argument has also been presented by the gerontology literature, which indicates that the idea that future generations of older people will use information technologies without problems is a myth that overlooks one essential point. This point is the progressive nature of technological development (Wandke et al., 2012). Technologies that are contemporary and novel today become obsolete within one or two decades. In particular, the development of devices such as computers, smartphones and tablets is fast-paced. Therefore, the knowledge required to use these technologies will continue to change, and the value of experience with formerly current technologies will decrease with age (Wandke et al., 2012).

In other words, while the idea is tempting that growing up in the digital age reduces the impact of age, the main assumption of this idea is that technology doesn’t change. However, this assumption is not valid (Benbasat & Zmud, 1999; Tams et al., 2014; Wandke et al., 2012). For example, current tablets, smartphones, applications, and application infrastructures are unlikely to remain relevant until the year 2060, when Generation Z will be older. New technologies and infrastructures will be in place by then.

In our analyses, we controlled for the amount of experience that the respondents had had with the technologies they used at work, and this means that the results hold irrespective of the diferent levels of experience that the various cohorts may have accumulated.

It is important to emphasise that our findings hold for older users in general, but not necessarily for all older users. No one can deny that there are older users who are using IT in innovative ways. Moreover, we do not argue that older workers are less creative. A recent meta-analysis of the relationship between age and innovation-related behaviour clearly refutes this idea (Ng & Feldman, 2013). Instead, we argue that older users feel less creative, that they perceive themselves as less creative as a result of negative age stereotyping. This perception leads them to innovate less with IT. Our results will help discourage negative age stereotyping in IS-enabled work environments in the long term. In the short term, they will facilitate the development of managerial intervention strategies and systems design changes aimed at increasing job control and perceived enjoyment for older users.

## Appendix B: Psychometric testing for Study 1

Evaluating the quality of our survey instrument included estimating the reliability as well as the convergent and discriminant validity of the construct measures. SPSS® 26.0 was used to calculate all statistics, which were obtained through a factor analysis with Maximum Likelihood Extraction and Promax rotation. Five factors were specified for extraction since we analyzed the items for five diferent constructs (age was an observed variable and was, thus, not included in this analysis). The internal consistency (reliability) of a block of items is represented by Cronbach’s coeficient alpha. Satisfactory values for this criterion exceed 0.70 (Nunnally, 1978). All alphas exceeded this threshold (see Table B1).

The convergent validity of a construct is considered satisfactory when its AVE is at least 0.50, and discriminant validity is considered adequate when the square root of the AVE is larger than the inter-construct correlations (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). All AVE values exceeded 0.50 (see Table B1), and the square root of each construct’s AVE was higher than the correlations between that construct and all others (see Table B2). These results indicated good construct validity.

Construct validity is further confirmed when all items load above 0.50 on their associated constructs and when the loadings within constructs are higher than those across constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Table B3 presents the loadings for this study. Visual inspection of these loadings and cross-loadings further confirms that all constructs had good convergent and discriminant validity. In fact, the cross-loadings were approaching zero. The cross-loadings are represented by the AVE value for non-associated items, which quantifies the amount of variance a construct measure captures from the items it is not associated with relative to the amount due to measurement error. This value was less than 0.01 for each construct (see Table B1), further confirming construct validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

## Appendix D. Screening and Quality Assurance Questions for Study 2

## Screening Questions

1) Question: In your employing organization, do you currently have access to an Electronic Knowledge Repository so that you can contribute your knowledge to it with the goal of helping your co-workers with your knowledge and expertise? Examples of knowledge contributions include creating documents, reports, articles, or videos and uploading them to Lotus Notes, Microsoft Sharepoint, Salesforce.com chatter, or to a company intranet or Wiki.

Answer: Options provided as answers to this question were “Yes,” “No,” and “Not Sure.” Only subjects responding “Yes” were invited to participate in the survey. This question ensured that the respondents fit the context of the study

2) Question: Does contributing knowledge to the Electronic Knowledge Repository in your organization help co-workers that you do not know?

Answer: Options provided as answers to this question were “Yes” and “No.” Only subjects responding “Yes” were invited to participate in the survey. This question ensured consistency of our study with prior research arguing that the most interesting aspect of knowledge sharing is that people help others they do not know (Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

3) Question: Are you required to contribute your knowledge to the Electronic Knowledge Repository in your organization, or are your knowledge contributions voluntary?

Answer: Options provided as answers to this question were “Required” and “Voluntary.” Only subjects responding “Voluntary” were invited to participate in the survey. This question ensured consistency of our study with prior research.

## Quality Assurance Questions

In addition to the screening questions, our survey included multiple quality assurance questions, which ensured that our respondents were knowledgeable in the subject area and were taking the survey seriously. For example, we included the following quality assurance question:

Question: In your experience, what is the primary goal of electronic knowledge repositories?

Answer: The response options included “Producing, storing, and manipulating text, for example, writing letters in Microsoft Word,” “Database management, for example, creating a customer database in Microsoft Access,” “Creation of presentations, for example, creating a sales presentation in Microsoft PowerPoint,” “Sharing ideas and information, for example, creating documents and uploading them to Microsoft SharePoint”, and “Creating spreadsheets, for example, creating a spreadsheet on car expenditures in Microsoft Excel.”

Only subjects responding “Sharing ideas and information, for example, creating documents and uploading them to Microsoft SharePoint” were allowed to continue with the survey. This question ensured that the respondents understood the context of the study.
