# Technology-driven mandatory customer participation: a new recovery strategy to promote customers’ online post-recovery satisfaction

Yu Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Xin (Robert) Luo <sup>b</sup>, Bingjia Shao<sup>c</sup> and Jose Benitez<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Modern Posts, Chongqing University of Posts and Telecommunications, Chongqing, China; <sup>b</sup>Anderson School of Management, University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, USA; <sup>c</sup>School of Economics and Business Administration, Chongqing University, China; <sup>d</sup>EDHEC Business School, France

## ABSTRACT

E-commerce businesses have been developing new ways of engaging customers in the online service recovery process to realise value co-creation. Such practices have been facilitated by the rapid development of information and communication technology. However, scant research has been conducted on these changes. To bridge this research gap, we explain how the application of technology guides and shapes customers’ responsibilities in online service recovery by constructing the concept of technology-driven mandatory customer participation (MCP), which is a new recovery strategy from the perspective of technology afordance. Moreover, we propose a comprehensive model to explain the influence mechanism of technology-driven MCP on value co-creation. Two sequential studies were then conducted. The first was an initial qualitative study based on interviews with e-commerce practitioners in China, in which three factors of technology-driven MCP (physical, mental, and emotional) and two categories of loss (utilitarian and symbolic) were identified. The second quantitative study explored and validated the relationships between technology-driven MCP, perceived cocreation, experienced burnout, and online post-recovery satisfaction from a loss-aversion perspective. The theory-driven empirical findings provide recommendations for e-commerce businesses to enhance online service management.

## 1. Introduction

Online service failure refers to the actual or perceived breakdown of online services provided by e-commerce businesses in terms of either outcome or process (Piercy & Archer-Brown, 2014; Sousa & Voss, 2009). Because online service failures reduce customer satisfaction and loyalty, proposing efective online recovery strategies as a valid response to these failures is crucial (Das et al., 2019; Ozuem et al., 2017; Tan et al., 2016). In 2019, China’s most prominent e-commerce consumer dispute mediation platform received 242,412 complaints from 980 mainstream e-commerce platforms, verifying that efective online service recovery from failure is a pressing concern in the e-commerce market.

Traditional service providers have introduced mandatory customer participation (MCP) as a new service recovery strategy for improving post-recovery satisfaction (Dong et al., 2008; Roggeveen et al., 2012). This is a practice whereby customers provide personal resources (i.e., physical, mental, and emotional) that are necessary to achieve a service outcome (Koc et al., 2017; Tsai et al., 2017), enabling customers to share responsibilities for service recovery (Dong et al., 2008). This practice has been referred to as a form of value co-creation realisation in traditional service recovery (Xu et al., 2014), which is also vigorously implemented in online service recovery. Unlike traditional service recovery, which requires the implementation of MCP based on face-to-face service interactions, MCP in online service recovery relies on various developments and applications of information technology (IT) artefacts in e-commerce platforms, such as “after-sales service” buttons and “smart customer service” avatars. By harnessing these IT artefacts that symbolise the authorisation of the enterprise, various responsibilities (e.g., recovery initiation and return and replacement negotiation) are transferred to customers, requiring them to provide the necessary resources to fulfil these responsibilities. However, contrary to the extensive theoretical research on MCP with traditional service recovery (e.g., Cheung & To, 2016; Dong et al., 2008; Park & Ha, 2016), the conceptualisation of such behaviours regarding resource investment through IT artefacts and whether and how these behaviours bring positive online service recovery outcomes has received little attention (Yu et al., 2021).

The lens of technology afordances suggests that IT artefacts can provide rich afordances for end-users to exhibit appropriate behaviours that are required by a specific scenario (Faraj & Azad, 2012), providing a suficient basis for explaining the mechanism of the previously mentioned problems. Several scholars have emphasised that everything related to IT artefacts is being labelled an afordance (Volkof & Strong, 2017, p. 235), which refers to what a user can potentially do using the technology to achieve an immediate outcome (Castillo et al., 2021). Accordingly, we infer that IT artefacts in online service recovery also provide afordances for customers to exhibit appropriate behaviours to meet their service recovery needs and enhance their acceptance of these behaviours. Herein, we conceptualise such behaviours as technology-driven MCP.

Because MCP is the concept of a highly contextual combination of diferent personal resources in varying degrees (Heidenreich et al., 2015), we employed a mixed-methods approach to define technologydriven MCP as a composite construct. This method was selected because it can fully describe what and how many resources are included in technologydriven MCP during online service recovery. Smith (2021) highlighted the need for studies that deepen the exploration of the formation and development of user information behaviour. Accordingly, in this study, we investigated whether IT artefacts’ advantages (in terms of convenience, eficiency, automation, and personalisation) can lead to positive participation outcomes by exploring the influence of technologydriven MCP on experienced burnout and online postrecovery satisfaction. Roggeveen et al. (2012) and Xu et al. (2014) stated that whether customers consider the invested resources a loss (conceptualised as perceived co-creation) is a determining factor that can afect the efectiveness of MCP’s value creation function. Since service failure itself means that customers have sufered a loss, the two categories of utilitarian and symbolic are usually included (Smith et al., 1999), which indicates that the resources invested in service recovery could be considered secondary losses. Thus, we embrace the loss-aversion theory to explain why customers consider technology-driven MCP in online service recovery as turning loss into profit rather than as secondary losses. This is achieved by exploring the mediating role of perceived co-creation in the relationships between technology-driven MCP and experienced burnout and between technology-driven MCP and online post-recovery satisfaction. As the diferent losses sufered by individuals can afect their perception of subsequent resource investment (Crisafulli & Singh, 2017), the moderate influence of loss type on these relationships is also explored.

## 2. Overall research design

A mixed-methods design that combined qualitative and quantitative methods was used to constitute and verify the research model and to achieve appropriate scientific research rigour and realism. There are two main reasons for using such an approach (Venkatesh et al., 2016). Owing to the highly situational, diverse, and multidimensional nature of MCP (Heidenreich et al., 2015), it is dificult to draw a unified empirical conclusion on the dimensions, content, and impact of MCP (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017). Therefore, it is necessary to explore the unique forms of MCP in specific service situations (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017). Technology-driven MCP is a new concept derived from MCP in a technology-enabled service environment. Hence, we utilised a qualitative analysis in Study 1 to deepen our understanding of the participation phenomenon and identify and characterise the construct propositions of technology-driven MCP in online service recovery according to the primarily used structure of MCP in service production (Koc et al., 2017; Tsai et al., 2017). We also gained insights into the influence of contextual factors (such as loss type) on participation outcomes. Based on the analysis results of Study 1 and existing theoretical foundations, in Study 2, we proposed a research model that was measured using construct development and validated and tested using structural equation modelling. Figure 1 shows the relationship between the two studies.

## 3. Study 1: exploring technology-driven MCP types and customers’ loss types

## 3.1. Literature review

## 3.1.1. MCP and its function as a recovery strategy in traditional service recovery

Customer participation refers to the degree to which customers participate in service production and delivery processes by contributing their time, knowledge, information, and other resources (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017). This is regarded as an important prerequisite for value co-creation (Tsai et al., 2017; Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2018). Depending on whether resources that involve customers are necessary to complete the service or whether these resources can be replaced by service personnel, customer participation is divided into mandatory, replaceable, and voluntary (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017). Therefore, existing studies pay more attention to the distinction between MCP and voluntary customer participation (VCP) and the different roles that these two types of participation play in service value co-creation (Tsai et al., 2017). In MCP, value is co-created through direct interactions between customers and enterprises (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017). By comparison, in the context of

VCP, value is co-created through direct or indirect interactions between customers and other existing or potential customers (Tsai et al., 2017). This indicates that MCP relies on the interaction of operational resources between customers and enterprises to complete the service (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017; Roggeveen et al., 2012). Correspondingly, the manifestation of value co-creation from MCP is reflected in both customers and enterprises (Chen & Raab, 2017; Tsai et al., 2017). The former emphasises improving customers positive evaluations of service outcomes and behavioural intentions (Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2018), while the latter emphasises the promotion of enterprise performance (e.g., employee and service innovations) (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017).

Based on the vital role of MCP in value co-creation, MCP has been regarded as a new recovery strategy in traditional service recovery (Dong et al., 2008) that can promote customer service quality (Dong et al., 2016), post-recovery satisfaction, repurchasing intentions (Roggeveen et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2014), positive wordof-mouth (Gohary et al., 2016; Vázquez-Casielles et al., 2017), experiences with service recovery (Xu et al., 2014), and intentions for future co-creation (Dong et al., 2016). Moreover, MCP positively afects customers’ cognition, which includes perceived justice (Cheung & To, 2016; Gohary et al., 2016; Park & Ha, 2016; Roggeveen et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2014), perceived control (Guo et al., 2016), and perceived value (Park & Ha, 2016). However, some researchers have proposed that MCP is a “double-edged sword”. They suggested that when customers view participation negatively (owing to a lack of role clarity or role conflicts and pressure), MCP cannot influence post-recovery satisfaction or even lead to a negative impact (Roggeveen et al., 2012). Thus, recent research (e.g., Dong & Sivakumar, 2017; Xu et al., 2014) has called for a strengthening of attention on the important mediating role of perceived co-creation between MCP and post-recovery satisfaction relationships and to mine the influencing variables of improving perceived cocreation and avoiding negative participation experiences (such as experienced burnout).

## 3.1.2. Technology-driven MCP in online service recovery: the technology afordance lens

Technology afordance originates from afordance theory (Faraj & Azad, 2012) and demonstrates the reciprocal relationship between actor intentions and technical features and that this type of relationship provides potential for specific actions (Piccoli, 2017). Technological afordance enables researchers not to simply regard technology as an artefact or tool (Doolin & Mcleod, 2012; Riemer & Johnston, 2014) and to consider the symbiotic relationship between the action to be taken in the context and the intrinsic features of the technology from two aspects. First, intrinsic features (which refer to the technology’s materiality) allow the same technology to be used by people in diferent ways (Majchrzak et al., 2013; Piccoli, 2017). Second, when people intend to achieve certain goals, they approach technologies based on their perceived afordances (i.e., their possible uses in supporting those goals) (Mettler et al., 2017). Hence, technology afordance is determined by the user who approaches the technology for a specific purpose, and the resulting efects depend upon users and specific contexts (Faraj & Azad, 2012; Te’Eni, 2016).

The intermediary of resource interaction in technology-enabled service environments changes from traditional human-to-human to human-machine encounters (Larivière et al., 2017). Therefore, the lens of technology afordance has been adopted as a valid tool to explain more efectively how the intervention of the technique reshapes the customer’s role in service processes. This favours understanding the imbrication between customer participation intentions and their use of technologies to co-create value with the enterprise and other customers (Lei et al., 2019). Current research has revealed that the afordances of embodied technology experiences in service environments facilitate customers being more likely to implement voluntary participation behaviours. These include making online comments (Piccoli, 2017), sharing service experiences on social media (Gunarathne et al., 2018; Majchrzak et al., 2013), and forming corresponding participation habits (Lei et al., 2020; Majchrzak et al., 2013), which can be constructed as “technology-driven VCP”. However, the efect of such afordances of embodied technology experiences on MCP has not yet been noticed.

The lens of technology afordance emphasises that the keynote for understanding afordance is not reviewing this term as a latent capability innate to the technology. Rather, it is perceived as a potentiality that only exists when leveraged within a specific domain and set of actions (Faraj & Azad, 2012). Thus, considering the importance of online service recovery in the whole service (Bijmolt et al., 2014; Crisafulli & Singh, 2017), we focus on the technology afordances within this context. As shown in Figures A1 and A2 (in Supplemental online material), several IT artefacts have been provided in online service recovery. Moreover, given that “everything related to IT artefacts is being labelled an afordance” (Volkof & Strong, 2017, p. 235), these IT artefacts must provide afordances for the customers. For example, the provision of frequently asked questions (FAQs) can help customers perceive the afordance, as they can find resolutions for the encountered service problem through scanning the FAQ. Similarly, service robot avatars or customer service personnel following the mouse position can help customers to receive the afordance, as they can quickly find the entrances by clicking the avatar. The module for “asking others” can also provide an afordance for the customers that they can get valid information about the causes, resolutions, and possible recovery strategies through reading the dialogues between other customers or asking questions to other customers who have purchased the same product. Another example is the five-star display of the store in “aftersales service”, which will help customers to have positive expectations about their service recovery quality and avoid any invalid debates with service providers or their employees. Finally, the “queue number” displayed within live-chat can make customers realise that they do not have to spend a long time waiting for the service staf. Given these examples, it is evident that these afordances from the IT artefacts lead to a change in and a shaping of customer participation behaviours. Although these participations may seem optional to the customer, with the large number of IT artefacts set up in various e-commerce platforms, online service providers are increasingly reliant on these IT artefacts to provide services rather than human resources. Therefore, customers non-participation can hinder or even prevent them from obtaining successful online service recovery. This is why technology-driven MCP is happening in online service recovery.

## 3.2. Study design

To identify the multidimensional construct of technology-driven MCP and explore the contextual factors that afect participation perceptions in online service recovery, in-depth interviews with 20 e-commerce practitioners were undertaken. Enterprise subjects were selected as interviewees in preference to end-user customers for two reasons. First, MCP is mostly an unconscious behaviour (Chen & Raab, 2017), and it is hard for the customers to elaborate how they have participated in the recovery process, which would afect data collection eficacy. Second, selecting e-commerce practitioners aforded a deeper understanding of the actual measures taken by online service providers to promote MCP through the application of IT artefacts and the contextual factors that influence the performance of these measures. The interview guide for the interviewers contained three sections: background information, the construct of technology-driven MCP in online service recovery and the contextual factors that influence participation outcomes. The second and third sections contained the following two open-ended questions: “From your experience, what must a customer do to ensure a successful online service recovery?” and “From your experience, what factors may influence their participation outcomes?”.

## 3.2.1. Participation

We conducted all interviews by telephone or WeChat Voice, with each one lasting between 45 and 60 minutes. Table A1 in Supplemental online material provides background information on the interviewees. All the participants were engaged in (or managing) work related to online customer service, including 4 customer service managers (or equivalent positions) and 16 from customer service departments. The interviewees levels of work experience ranged from 1.5 to 6.0 years (average of 3.3 years). Additionally, all interviewees indicated that they had received professional training in online customer service. The interviewees’ ages ranged from 20 to 45 years (average 27.3 years), and there were 7 males and 13 females.

## 3.2.2. Data analysis

Data analysis was performed in two phases: data collection and coding. Two authors who conducted the interviews recorded key phrases and interactions with participants, and one doctoral student familiar with qualitative methods in consumer behaviour research assisted in data collection and subsequent material coding. To improve the reliability and validity of the research responses, simple language was used in the interviews, while terms such as “mandatory customer participation” and “online service recovery” were explained at the outset. The coding process followed the principles and suggestions of interpretive research, which included preparation, pilot coding, and formal coding (Sarker et al., 2018, 2018), and was based on Strauss and Corbin’s (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) open coding technique. First, based on a literature review, we defined key themes through group discussions, including the main subdimension, the influencing factors of MCP, and open issues (such as the unique factors afecting MCP in online service recovery environments), and then developed a coding protocol. Second, we randomly selected data from four interviews to implement the pilot coding. In this step, all coding disagreements were raised in group discussions among all the authors, and a codebook containing the emerging themes was developed. Finally, in the formal coding step and according to the provisions of the coding protocol, two team members independently coded the transcribed responses line-by-line and compared the results with each other. If the concept matched some existing theme, the theme was determined as being a subcategory. Otherwise, a new theme was formed and added to the codebook. Any coding disagreements and ambiguities were again raised in the group discussions. Finally, the subcategories identified by the authors were grouped into a single category. Inter-coder reliability ranged from 0.81 to 0.93 using Krippendorf’s alpha (Hayes & Krippendorf, 2007), which verified interrater reliability. ## 3.3. Important findings

## 3.3.1. Types of technology-driven MCP in online service recovery

Although the content of MCP has changed under the afordance of technology application, it still contains three main kinds of personal resources and ensures that the construct of technology-driven MCP in online service recovery can be divided into physical, mental, and emotional. This finding aligns with previous conclusions in the field of service production (Koc et al., 2017).

Physical participation is “actual behaviour”, which refers to the act and magnitude of investment of a customer’s own tangible assets – efort or time – in their actions (Chen & Raab, 2017; Koc et al., 2017; Tsai et al., 2017). Some interviewees thought that although online services allow customers to use fragmented time to process transactions, customers still need to be online when dealing with service problems. In addition, customers need to take the time to master how to initiate service recovery and obtain intelligent customer service help through the information system. This type of participation inevitably consumes customers’ time and energy. Mental participation is considered “perceptual behaviour”, and refers to eforts made by the customer to study, accumulate, and share information and knowledge on e-commerce platforms, shopping forums, e-commerce businesses web pages, and other information platforms, which require cognitive resources (Cheung & To, 2016; Tsai et al., 2017). Compared with the other two types of participation, the distinctive characteristic of this type is that customers can utilise their own mental resources to obtain new knowledge and update their own knowledge capacity. Emotional participation is the “actual expression” of a customer’s positive attitude in online service recovery, which entails various behaviours. These include being patient and polite with the online service staf, not resisting the service robot, and forming positive expectations regarding recovery outcomes under the influence of word-ofmouth (Chen & Raab, 2017; Koc et al., 2017).

## 3.3.2. Efect of contextual factors on technology-driven MCP

According to the coding results, the interviewees proposed that the type of loss sufered by customers during a service failure is the primary factor that afects their motivation to participate. The loss type was classified into two categories: utilitarian and symbolic. Utilitarian loss refers to the loss of economic resources (e.g., money, goods, and time), while symbolic loss refers to the loss of psychological and social resources (e.g., status, respect, and sympathy). This division is similar to that used by Smith et al. (1999), who divided service failure types into outcome and process failures.

## 4. Study 2: Relationship between technologydriven MCP and recovery outcomes

## 4.1. Literature review

## 4.1.1. Online service failure

The e-commerce literature has indicated that online service failure can result in many negative consequences, including exit (ending any relationship with the company) and voice (complaints and negative word-of-mouth) (Bijmolt et al., 2014; Crisafulli & Singh, 2017). According to Smith et al. (1999), online service failure can be divided into two types. An outcome failure occurs when a service firm “does not fulfil the basic service need or perform the core service”. An example would be the website being down or not working properly, preventing the customer from engaging with the service. In comparison, a process failure occurs when “the service delivery is flawed or deficient in some way”. Examples of this would be the website being dificult to navigate or the online service staf treating customers rudely (Sousa & Voss, 2009). As outcome failures indicate that the service itself cannot be supplied (Crisafulli & Singh, 2017; Smith et al., 1999), outcome service failures can be considered utilitarian losses sufered by the customers. In contrast, process failure involves interpersonal quality and social/psychological factors (Smith et al., 1999), resulting in a loss of social resources (e.g., status or esteem), and process service failures can be construed as encountering symbolic loss.

## 4.1.2. Online service recovery

Online service recovery is the intervention procedure for online service failure (and any ensuing negative impact) and is defined as online measures taken by a service provider following a recent service failure for customers (Bijmolt et al., 2014). Online post-recovery satisfaction describes customers’ overall level of satisfaction with recovery measures and is the core indicator for evaluating online service recovery performance (Gohary et al., 2016; Lii et al., 2013). As shown in Table B1 in Supplemental online material, which summarises the relevant results of research into online service recovery, when exploring the influencing factors of online post-recovery satisfaction, scholars mainly depart from the research outcomes of traditional service recovery, which focus on the perspective of the service provider’s role in the recovery process (Gohary et al., 2016; Jung & Seock, 2017; Ozuem et al., 2017). Two theories are primarily used in the literature to explore the influencing mechanisms of recovery strategies: justice theory and attribution theory. Justice theory serves as the most important theoretical foundation for service recovery (Gohary et al., 2016). Moreover, it holds that recovery strategies, which include intangible strategies (e.g., the service staf’s politeness and positive responses) and tangible strategies (e.g., discounts and compensation), can influence customers’ perceived justice (Wu, 2013). In turn, this is composed of three dimensions (distributive, procedural, and interactional justice) that determine customers’ online post-recovery satisfaction (Jung & Seock, 2017; Kuo & Wu, 2012; Ozuem et al., 2017; Yeoh et al., 2014). Attribution theory explains why and how the three types of service failure attribution, which are controllability (controllable or uncontrollable), locus of causality (caused by customer or enterprise), and stability, can influence customers’ online post-recovery satisfaction (Weitzl et al., 2018).

Only a few studies have examined the role of the customer in online service recovery. Gohary et al. (2016) highlighted that customers’ attitudes towards participation positively moderate the relationship between perceived justice and online post-recovery satisfaction. Accordingly, perceived justice is positively related to customers’ future participation intentions. In addition, Van Vaerenbergh et al. (2018) verified the positive efect of MCP on recovery performance through a meta-analysis. However, these studies treated MCP as a binary concept, ignoring its multidimensional nature (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017). Moreover, they did not conduct a systematic analysis on the influence of MCP in the application of a technology-enabled service environment (conceptualised as technology-driven MCP herein) on online post-recovery satisfaction.

## 4.2. Hypothesis development

In this section, we hypothesise the relationships between technology-driven MCP, perceived cocreation, experienced burnout, and online postrecovery satisfaction. The hypotheses are based on the relevant empirical literature and the loss-aversion perspective, combined with the qualitative findings in Study 1.

## 4.2.1. Technology-driven MCP and online post-recovery satisfaction

MCP has been regarded as the main form of enterprise authorisation, meaning that customers adopt the role of amateur employees in the service process (Roggeveen et al., 2012). Deep integration of technology in the service environment has further accelerated these types of authorisations (Larivière et al., 2017), enabling customers to participate in the entire process of service recovery (Park & Ha, 2016). This will enhance customers’ perceived control and perceived recovery efort input by service providers, resulting in higher post-recovery satisfaction (Cheung & To, 2016; Guo et al., 2016). Additionally, authorisation provides more opportunities for customising the service, which can increase the likelihood that customer needs are met (Dong et al., 2008) and prompt self-reliant customer segments to innovate services and technology to work for their own individualised purposes (Larivière et al., 2017). Therefore, customers have become increasingly responsible for positively diferentiating between a recovery process and outcome in technology-enabled service environments (Larivière et al., 2017), and for diferentiating between the recovery outcome from standardised recovery solutions, ensuring customers’ recovery needs are met more efectively and their online post-recovery satisfaction is improved (Wu & Huang, 2015). Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1: Technology-driven MCP positively afects online post-recovery satisfaction.

## 4.2.2. Technology-driven MCP and experienced burnout

Burnout is a comprehensive psychological symptom that comprises mental, emotional, or physical fatigue and a metaphor of a person’s tired psychological state (Schaufeli & Salanova, 2014). It occurs when an individual resides in a stressful external environment, especially when they are faced with higher external needs and less disposable resources (Rawolle et al., 2016). The tendency to experience burnout and its characteristics are strongest when an individual’s personal resources are increasingly consumed, accompanied by a lower sense of competence and achievement (Bennett et al., 2017). Conversely, continuous acquisition of ability and a sense of achievement in resource consumption are important sources for breaking the sense of experiencing burnout (Markus et al., 2019).

Although MCP indicates the consumption of customer resources, making customers face a greater risk of experience burnout (Roggeveen et al., 2012). However, the application of technology has changed this phenomenon significantly. This is because online service environments integrated with technology expand the channels for customers and employees to exchange and share knowledge, information, skills, and other operating resources (Wu & Huang, 2015). This means that customers’ physical, mental, and emotional resource consumption can be returned over time (Bijmolt et al., 2014). This smooth and mutually beneficial way of resource exchange makes the customer realise that resource input has the utility value of optimising problem-solving procedures and improving the eficiency of problem solving. In addition, it can help in expanding the knowledge capacity of customers to make them realise the hedonic value of MCP, such as enhancing self-eficacy and selfevaluation (Park & Ha, 2016). Thus, technologydriven MCP is more likely to function as a path for customer self-learning and self-entertainment rather than acting as a source of burnout. We propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Technology-driven MCP negatively afects customers’ experienced burnout.

## 4.2.3. Technology-driven MCP and perceived co-creation

Loss aversion is an important concept in prospect theory. This concept proposes that the degree of pain experienced because of a loss is far greater than the degree of happiness derived from income of the same monetary value (Kahneman & Tversky, 2000). This results in individuals becoming more sensitive to loss rather than benefits, prompting them to avoid and reduce the sufering caused by a loss (Kahneman & Tversky, 2000). The impact of loss aversion on individual behaviour has been explained from two perspectives. The mainstream view focuses on potential loss scenarios, proposing that when individuals perceive potential loss, they are more likely to be influenced by loss aversion and to make irrational decisions, such as endowment efects, status quo bias, and risk decisions (Yechiam & Hochman, 2013). Another series of studies focused on losses that have already occurred, suggesting that when an individual has sufered losses, the existing loss experience and desire for loss aversion improves their overall attention to the current action, resulting in more rational decisions (Yechiam & Hochman, 2013, 2013). Our research setting belongs to the second category, as service failure inevitably leads to a loss of customers.

Our existing research provides the following analytical logic: To limit the loss caused by service failure, customers may pay more attention to the benefits (rather than the losses) of the resource investment in the recovery process under the influence of loss aversion, allowing them to positively evaluate their participation actions. Specifically, under the circumstances of loss, the loss can cause the customer to produce goal-oriented preferences (Rothman et al., 2006), prioritise information on the revenue of the current action (Yechiam & Hochman, 2013), reduce perceived risk of potential loss associated with the action, and expect a reduction in the pain by implementing actions to gain profits (Yechiam & Hochman, 2013, 2013). Therefore, when customers participate in online service recovery due to service failure, they are more inclined to view resource involvement from the goalorientation perspective. They focus on the benefits resulting from participation and reduce the perceived risk of personal resource loss caused by participation. Hence, they generate valuable evaluations of participation in online service recovery and form positive perceptions of co-creation. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3: Technology-driven MCP positively afects customers’ perceived co-creation.

## 4.2.4. Perceived co-creation and perceived experienced burnout

When customers view MCP negatively, they experience role pressures and role conflict (Roggeveen et al., 2012), thinking that their participation does not produce suficient value to ofset the psychological imbalance caused by resource loss (Park & Ha, 2016). This type of negative perception can result in emotional fatigue, lower self-eficacy perceptions, and a more cynical attitude towards interactions, causing burnout (Schaufeli & Salanova, 2014). Conversely, when customers view MCP positively, they regard participation as a responsibility they should undertake, consider it within their capabilities (Markus et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2014), perceive value from the participation process (Park & Ha, 2016), and gain a sense of achievement and confidence, leading to decreased levels of burnout (Schaufeli & Salanova, 2014). Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4: Customers’ perceived co-creation negatively afects their customers burnout.

## 4.2.5. Perceived co-creation and online post-recovery satisfaction

Roggeveen et al. (2012) defined perceived co-creation as a customer’s perception of participating in service recovery. Previous studies have shown that if customers have negative perceptions about customer participation, such as thinking that customer participation means that the company treats them as unpaid employees, they will perceive that they are not solving problems together with the service provider. Rather, they will consider themselves to be working in place of the service provider or its employees (Xu et al., 2014). This passive perception can lead to role conflict (Markus et al., 2019), eventually resulting in negative postrecovery satisfaction (Roggeveen et al., 2012). In the online service context, Gohary et al. (2016) also noted that customers’ post-recovery satisfaction is higher when they believe that participation is worthwhile and that they should conduct their duty. Thus, when customers invest personal resources in online service recovery, positively treating investment behaviours can reduce their role pressure (Markus et al., 2019). This makes them think that the investment is valuable (Roggeveen et al., 2012), improving their online postrecovery satisfaction (Gohary et al., 2016). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5: Customers’ perceived co-creation positively afects their online post-recovery satisfaction.

## 4.2.6. Experienced burnout and online post-recovery satisfaction

Researchers have determined the negative personal attitudes and behavioural efects of burnout from three perspectives. From the cognitive perspective, when customers are experiencing burnout, they are more easily bored, their personal perception of achievement is low, and their self-confidence and self-esteem are weak, leading to dissatisfaction (Bennett et al., 2017; Markus et al., 2019). From an emotional perspective, customers experiencing burnout are in a state of fatigue, exhaustion, or occupation, rendering it easier to trigger dissatisfaction with recovery (Schaufeli & Salanova, 2014). From a physical perspective, customers experiencing burnout exert more efort, easily feel both physical and mental fatigue, and are dissatisfied with recovery (Markus et al., 2019). Therefore, with an increased risk of burnout, the subsequent evaluation of recovery performance is more likely to be negative, and we propose the following hypothesis:

H6: Customers’ experienced burnout negatively afects their online post-recovery satisfaction.

## 4.2.7. The moderating role of loss type

Diferent types of loss due to service failure can prompt customers to form various recovery expectations (Smith et al., 1999). Symbolic loss mainly afects customers’ negative emotions. Hence, such losses would allow customers to adopt a more tolerant and evasive attitude, choosing to be silent and not complain directly to the service provider (Crisafulli & Singh, 2017). This ensures that symbolic loss does not have a direct influence on recovery expectancy (Smith et al., 1999). However, utilitarian loss is strongly related to core service failure. Hence, such losses would make customers pay great attention to problem-solving strategies and become eager to participate so that they could promptly reduce any pain caused (Roggeveen et al., 2012). In addition, this would propel customers towards forming high recovery expectancy (Smith et al., 1999). Essentially, when customers perceive their loss to be utilitarian rather than symbolic, higher recovery expectancy will make them focus on actions that favour compensating for the loss (Yechiam & Hochman, 2013), resulting in the influence of loss aversion on their evaluation of participation behaviour being enhanced and making them believe that their participation is meaningful and valuable. These positive perceptions will promote perceived co-creation and make customers more likely to create value through participation, reducing any sense of having experienced burnout (Markus et al., 2019) and promoting online post-recovery satisfaction (Roggeveen et al., 2012). Conversely, when customers encounter utilitarian losses, they are more likely to adopt a tolerant and evasive attitude, choosing to not complaint and express dissatisfaction to the service provider (Bijmolt et al., 2014; Crisafulli & Singh, 2017). Evidently, this can discourage customers from participating in service recovery, making it dificult for them to feel the benefits of participation. Hence, we propose the following hypotheses:

H7: Compared to symbolic loss, the influence of technology-driven MCP on perceived co-creation is more positive when the customer perceives utilitarian loss in service failure.

H8: Compared to symbolic loss, the influence of technology-driven MCP on experienced burnout is more negative when the customer perceives a utilitarian loss in service failure.

H9: Compared to symbolic loss, the influence of technology-driven MCP on online post-recovery satisfaction is more positive when the customer perceives a utilitarian loss in service failure.

In Study 2, the research model was quantitatively tested to validate the hypotheses. Since 2015, the outbound Wi-Fi rental market has experienced explosive growth in China, representing an important service in the online tourism sector. These failures have severely afected customers’ user experiences to such an extent that improving online post-recovery satisfaction has become the focus of service market management (Ctrip, 2016). Therefore, we selected the online service recovery of outbound Wi-Fi rentals as the research context of this study and conducted an online questionnaire-based survey to obtain data.

## 4.3.1. Questionnaire design and samples

We employed the following question proposed by Smith et al. (1999) to measure the type of loss that customers encountered: “What kind of loss did you encounter when you contacted the online support staf?” Accordingly, we defined symbolic and utilitarian losses as follows: symbolic loss is encountered from incidental services that do not afect the use of the Wi-Fi service for outbound tourists. Examples include expressions of poor attitudes by staf members, inconvenience of obtaining and returning equipment, and delays in the deposit refund process. Utilitarian loss refers to a loss encountered in the core service, such as problems afecting the expected normal functioning of the Wi-Fi service. These problems could include broken equipment, a poor network signal, long queuing times related to obtaining and returning the equipment, or equipment that cannot connect to the operator’s network at the destination.

In a technology-enabled service environment, the technology-driven MCP concept represents the comprehensive, multifaceted resource interactions between customers and the service provider in a specific service context, where customers freely choose the types of resources to combine and invest. Thus, technology-driven MCP is multidimensional and is conceptualised as a three-dimensional secondorder construct composed of the actual contributions of customers’ physical, mental, and emotional resources (physical, mental, and emotional participation). Moreover, these three first-order constructs are reflective variables. The measurements of these variables were conducted with reference to the MCP scale developed by Chen and Raab (2017) in the context of restaurant services. We modified some measurement terms according to the essence of the three types of participation demonstrated by Koc et al. (2017) and the results of Study 1. The other variables of our research model were reflective variables, as they have been verified as reflective constructs in several behavioural research studies in the service domain. Among these, perceived co-creation was measured using three items from Gohary et al. (2016), experienced burnout was measured using three items from Schaufeli and Salanova (2014), and online post-recovery satisfaction was measured using three items from Roggeveen et al. (2012). Three control variables were considered: gender, perceived justice, and product preference. Product preference was measured using the single item “What is your favourite method to obtain network services during outbound travel?”, and perceived justice was measured using three items from Park and Ha (2016). Table B3 in the Supplementary online material lists the measurement questions for all the reflective variables.

An English version of a questionnaire was prepared and translated into Chinese before being back-translated into English by two independent translators. Comparing the two versions helped ensure conceptual equivalence, and translation accuracy was audited by a bilingual researcher on the team through a backward-and-forward translation process. A preliminary test was conducted to validate the instrument, wherein 80 participants were invited to answer the pre-test questionnaire on the website https://www.wjx.cn/, which is a leading Chinese professional questionnaire platform. In accordance with Chen and Raab (2017), an additional question was designed for each measurement of technology-driven MCP to confirm that the measurements met MCP’s scope: “This thing is what I have to do in order to get satisfactory online service recovery results”. The t-test results indicated that all the mean values of this question for each measurement (5.838–6.325) were above the median (4.0), describing the extent of mandatory participation rather than voluntary participation and verifying content adequacy. Moreover, a few minor modifications were made to the wording and question sequence based on comments from the trial participants.

The formal survey involved sending the questionnaire via email to 1,083 Chinese tourists who had purchased a Wi-Fi rental service in the past year. The respondents were drawn from a customer database provided by two major travel agencies. The investigation was conducted in 2018 from June 1 to 13, and 487 responses were obtained. The following initial question was used to filter respondents: “During the latest consumer experience, have you ever sought an online service to resolve a service problem you encountered?” A total of 357 participants reported having pursued a recovery service online, and they answered the questionnaire based on their real experiences of online service recovery when encountering service failures involving Wi-Fi rental services. Table B2 in the Supplementary online material describes the samples.

## 4.3.2. Measurement model

We tested the proposed model empirically by using PLS path modelling, which is a variance-based

SEM technique (Benitez et al., 2020), with the statistical software package Advanced Analysis for Composites (ADANCO) 2.3.0 Professional. We determined that PLS was appropriate for this research, as our construction of technology-driven MCP is specified as composite. Moreover, PLS is particularly efective in providing consistent estimations for this type of model (Benitez et al., 2018, 2018, 2020). In addition, variance-based SEM techniques provide better results than covariance-based SEM techniques when estimating very complex models (i.e., those with multidimensional constructs) (e.g., Benitez et al., 2020; Riel et al., 2017). The PLS-SEM technique has also been widely used in the field of IS (Benitez et al., 2018, 2018, 2020).

Technology-driven MCP, which is a multidimensional construct, was estimated through a three-stage approach (Riel et al., 2017). In the first stage, we freely correlated all first-order constructs to obtain latent variable scores and consistent correlations of the constructs. In the second stage, latent variable scores were used as measures of the multidimensional construct to obtain consistent estimates for the structural model. In the third stage, the second-order construct was again included in the model. However, on this occasion, its correlations were corrected for attenuation by setting the reliability of a weighted composite $( \rho _ { \mathrm { S } } = 0 . 9 3 0 )$ to reveal the weight relationships between the first-order common factors and second-order composite. To estimate the level of significance of weights, loadings, and path coeficients, we ran the bootstrapping algorithm with 4999 subsamples.

Table B4-B6 (in Supplemental online material) shows the detailed results of all models from the three-stage approach. The values of AVE, ρA, ρc, and ɑ of all the variables exceeded the threshold for acceptable convergent validity. The highest HTMT value in the whole model was 0.713, and the highest VIF value of the composite model was 1.365. This means that suficient discriminant validity existed throughout the model (Benitez et al., 2020). Furthermore, neither model was rejected based on an alpha level of 0.05 since all discrepancies were below the 95%-quantile of bootstrap discrepancies (Benitez et al., 2020). These results suggest empirical support for this structure of composites at the first- and second-order levels, verifying that we could proceed with a structural model assessment (Riel et al., 2017).

## 5. Discussion, implications, and limitations

## 5.1. Discussion

This study adopted the lens of technology afordance to reveal how customers engage in a recovery process with IT artefacts to realise value co-creation under online service recovery, which is normally an unexpected, situated, and emergent context. Several important findings were revealed.

## 5.1.1. Types of technology-driven MCP

This study found that IT artefacts in online service recovery provide suficient afordances for customers to implement appropriate behaviour to meet their recovery needs. We also constructed the concept of technology-driven MCP to illustrate the internal change in customer participation behaviour under the influence of technological afordances. The proposed technology-driven MCP category in online service recovery was consistent with the MCP framework in service production adopted by Koc et al. (2017), which includes emotional, physical, and mental participation. However, in this study, we creatively proposed that a technology-driven MCP is a composite construct rather than a reflective construct. Existing studies have been controversial on the multidimensional and situational nature of MCP and the inconsistency of empirical research conclusions on the efect of MCP caused by the previously mentioned characteristics (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017). The construction of MCP as a composite concept can efectively solve this problem. This is because customers always combine resources that can be invested according to the situation’s needs, especially when facing various IT artefacts. Even though each customer can feel the technical afordance from the IT artefacts and implement the mandatory participation behaviour, owing to the diferent preferences of each customer for afordance, the degree of customers’ investment in emotional, mental, and physical resources difers. Finally, a technology-driven MCP with varying content at diferent levels is formed. This discovery is also significant for subsequent research on the technical afordances of IT artefacts. Although the view that IT artefacts can bring afordance has been widely recognised (Gunarathne et al., 2018; Piccoli, 2017), how afordance is related to customer behaviour and how to measure this correlation remains a problem that needs to be constantly discussed (Te’Eni, 2016). Our research links online service recovery, technical afordance, and MCP simultaneously, which constitutes a good attempt to resolve and explore the previously mentioned issues.

## 5.1.2. The impact of technology-driven MCP

This study further revealed that technology-driven MCP results in customers having higher perceived cocreation, resulting in lower experienced burnout and higher online post-recovery satisfaction. This finding verified that customers’ perceptions of co-creation are pivotal to ensuring the efectiveness of MCP (Roggeveen et al., 2012). Furthermore, it explains that whether the customers’ perceptions of cocreation based on technology-driven MCP are meaningful and valued depends on whether the customer maintains an internal resource balance in resource consumption (Yechiam & Hochman, 2013, 2013). Moreover, the research conclusion confirms that, in the online service recovery environment, this balance is easier to achieve owing to the assistance of technology. In the process of technology gradually and completely replacing manual services, customers’ awakening of participation consciousness is inspired by the technology afordance. This not only makes customers more likely to ignore the pain caused by losses in personal resources during participation (Lei et al., 2020) and regard these inputs as obligations rather than extra responsibilities (Dong & Sivakumar, 2017), it also makes them consider that their participation was within their power and could be valuable to achieve positive service outcomes. This finding proves that technology has obvious value in cultivating customers’ participation abilities and awareness and in encouraging customers to develop participation habits. Thus, under the influence of technological afordance, customers can form positive views of MCP and make MCP add value co-creation in online service recovery.

The three factors of technology-driven MCP have diferent contributions to value-creating outcomes. Among these, contributing emotional participation was the weakest, while mental participation was the strongest. This could be due to the diferent technical afordances of customers’ inputting to various resources. Technology makes it easy for customers to share knowledge with enterprises (making the investment of customers’ mental resources easy and pleasant) and makes online services realise the separation of production and consumption (Huang & Rust, 2017), reducing the necessity for customers’ physical existence and the importance of customer time and energy investment (Larivière et al., 2017). Thus, customers receive the strongest afordances from mental participation, and the contribution of mental participation to value co-creation is also more significant than physical participation. However, although online service providers can use technology to display necessary information (such as scores and service recovery procedures to manage customers’ expectations for online service recovery) and to enable them to implement emotional participation, online service failures cannot be changed. This results in customers wanting to adopt expressive coping strategies to release negative emotions, seek support, and express their need for help (Tsarenko & Strizhakova, 2013). This contradictory fact makes customers’ positive emotional resources the scarcest resources for them to be involved in online service recovery (Kuo & Wu, 2012). Hence, emotional participation has the minimum contribution.

## 5.1.3. Contextual factors that influence the impact of technology-driven MCP

In this study, we found that technology-driven MCP influences perceived co-creation more positively in utilitarian loss contexts. This is because the greater the loss that customers sufer as a result of service failure, the more likely they are to invest more resources (based on afordances) in cooperating with online service providers and sharing responsibility for service recovery (Roggeveen et al., 2012). Further, for customers to entertain loss-aversion preferences (Kahneman & Tversky, 2000), they tend to place more value on the resources that contribute to service recovery (Yechiam & Hochman, 2013, 2013), enhancing the belief that co-creation is meaningful and valuable (Park & Ha, 2016). However, contrary to our hypotheses, the moderate role of loss type on technology-driven MCP and online post-recovery satisfaction relationship is not significant. This could be caused by the technology-enabled service environment itself, as regardless of the type of service loss, it is the customer’s responsibility to participate in online service recovery. Hence, loss type has no significant influence on the relationship between technologydriven MCP and online post-recovery satisfaction.

## 5.2. Implications

## 5.2.1. Theoretical contributions to IS research

This study contributes to the IS and online service recovery literature in several important ways. First, we extended the application of technology afordance theory by adopting it as a lens to construct the concept of technology-driven MCP in online service recovery. We also answer the question of how applying technology plays a role in cultivating customers’ potential abilities and shaping customer behaviour. We further proposed that under the afordance of technology, customers have naturally become value co-creators in online service recovery through personal resource investment. In addition, technology-driven MCP should be considered a composite construct, as customers independently deploy the “participation” formula according to the characteristics of the service scene. In online service recovery, mental participation has the most significant contribution to the value creation of technology-driven MCP, while emotional participation has the weakest influence. This interesting finding provides support for further research to enhance our understanding of the role of technologydriven MCP combined with the characteristics of service scenarios.

Second, we contribute to research related to online service recovery by introducing the theoretically relevant and novel construct of technologydriven MCP as an important recovery strategy. We explored this strategy’s influence on experienced burnout and online post-recovery satisfaction in terms of customers’ perceived co-creation (a mediating efect) from a loss aversion perspective. Most studies have adopted the research parameters of traditional service recovery to investigate online service recovery. Moreover, they verified the applicability of traditional service recovery strategies in online service recovery settings. However, online service recovery is particular to context, space, time, and interaction, implying that it relies more on technology-driven customer roles. Therefore, this study fills the gap by exploring customer roles in online service recovery and further explains the influence of technology-driven MCP on recovery performance.

Third, this study contributes to a deeper comprehension of the ways firms can promote favourable customer experiences through participation processes by initiating a discussion on the moderating role of loss type in the relationship between technology-driven MCP and perceived co-creation. Although many studies have emphasised how customers view their contributions – the determining factor of the efectiveness of customer participation – scant research has focused on the analysis of the specific factors that can influence customers’ viewpoints. Our study fills this gap by considering loss type as a contextual factor. We discovered that customers perceive various types of loss under diferent service failure contexts. This sense of loss can afect recovery expectations, prompting them to form different return expectations on their own resource involvement, resulting in diferent perceptions of cocreation.

Fourth, this study utilised a mixed-methods approach to explore the relationship between technology-driven MCP and online post-recovery satisfaction. We composed the factors of MCP, meaning that their application scenarios could now be thoroughly understood, and the inconsistencies of existing empirical research conclusions could be explained. Extant studies have mainly exploited quantitative research methods to explore the degree of customer participation and its utility in specific service scenarios, overlooking the diversity and situational characteristics of customer participation. To achieve scientific realism and rigour, our study adopted a mixed-methods design by using a qualitative study to explore and epitomise the various composite factors of technology-driven MCP. The efectiveness of this approach was then explored and validated by the subsequent quantitative study by adopting the variance-based SEM technique. Accordingly, this research approach enhanced the validity and credibility of the conclusions, allowing service providers to make better managerial decisions.

## 5.2.2. Managerial implications

This study provides practitioners with useful information for online service recovery management. In summary, the results indicated that enterprises with a strong service marketing emphasis should invest strategic eforts in making customers view their participation behaviours positively. Enterprises can promote MCP through multidimensional online service system optimisation, such as innovative interface designs, including the provision of FAQ lists on service-consulting platforms. For example, Flying Pig (a large online travel agency in China) provides such a design for improving mental participation by motivating customers to take the initiative in the collection and exchange of information. Enterprises can also provide a concise and clear description of customers responsibilities in the service purchase agreement to promote physical participation and increase customers’ enthusiasm to initiate online service recovery.

Online service providers should encourage customers to express their demands rather than expect conformance with social norms that dictate a deliberate constraint of emotions after service failure. Emotional participation has the weakest contribution to value creation. Depression, feelings of loss, and other negative emotions represent the true mood state of customers who experience service failure. Accordingly, frontline service staf members should focus on helping customers ease their negative emotional state rather than hoping they can process it themselves. Additionally, although service robots can help solve the queuing problem, customers prefer to be served by real people after failure.

Enterprises should take the initiative of displaying care about the emotions of customers who have suffered utilitarian loss, as excessive emotional input from customers can reduce their positive perception of co-creation and satisfaction. However, firms should also make rational use of customers’ aversions to loss and stimulate their subjective initiative. This can be achieved by encouraging the input of physical and mental resources, as utilitarian loss prompts customers to harbour higher recovery expectancy.

## 5.3. Limitations and future avenues for IS research

This study contains some inevitable limitations. First, although the lens of technology afordances emphasised the application background of technology and the existing ability of end-users, some technology features (such as ease-of-use and the completeness of programme design) and the match of technology features and end-users’ existing abilities were not considered in this study. Future research could evaluate these potential factors, which could afect the afordances of customers from the application of IT artefacts in online service recovery. Second, the weights of physical, mental, and emotional participation when composing technology-driven MCP may be afected by cultural diferences. China and Western countries have divergent preferences for resource allocation. The latter tends to ascribe high value to a skilled labour service, while the value of labour based on the consumption of physical resources is comparatively lower in Eastern countries. Therefore, even if technical assistance reduces the contribution of physical participation, Western customers may still increase the weight of such participation. Future research could aim to verify the conclusions of this study to improve its eficacy and general applicability in diferent countries. Third, the study focused solely on the online service recovery scenario in Wi-Fi rental services and omitted situational factors of service failure, such as customers’ sense of urgency and the need for service recovery. These factors could afect customers’ technology-driven MCP willingness and further influence the value co-creation outcomes. Therefore, future research could also be extended to other business segments (such as online airline tickets and hotel bookings) and consider these failure factors to increase the universal applicability of the empirical results.

## 6. Conclusion

In an efort to investigate the emerging phenomenon of technology-driven MCP and to advance the e-commerce literature on service recovery, this study is an early efort to reveal how the application of technology guides and shapes customers’ responsibilities towards online service recovery. This was achieved by introducing the concept of technology-driven MCP, which is a new recovery strategy constructed from the perspective of technology afordance. We propose a comprehensive model to elucidate the influencing mechanisms of technology-driven MCP on value co-creation. To achieve scientific rigour and relevance, we employed a mixed-methods approach and discovered that using technology-driven MCP leads to value co-creation, a decrease in customers’ experienced burnout, and a promotion of online post-recovery satisfaction through the mediator role of perceived cocreation. Loss types encountered in service failure also moderate the previously mentioned relationships. This study lays the groundwork for future research to further probe how technologies can be strategised to improve service recovery and organisational performance.
