# A field experiment on ISP training designs for enhancing employee information security compliance

Ilja Nastjuk $^{a}$ , Florian Rampold $^{a}$ , Simon Trang $^{b}$ and Jose Benitez $^{c}$

$^{a}$ Chair of Information Security and Compliance, Georg-August-Universität Göttingen, Göttingen, Germany; $^{b}$ Chair of Information Systems and Environmental Sustainability, Faculty of Business and Economics, University of Paderborn, Paderborn, Germany; $^{c}$ Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, Ambassador Crawford College of Business and Entrepreneurship, Kent State University, Kent, OH, USA

## ABSTRACT

Information security policy (ISP) training plays an important role in enhancing organisational resilience against cyber threats by providing employees with the necessary knowledge and skills to effectively identify, prevent, and respond to security breaches. This research aims to explore how the use of deterrence arguments and threat arguments can enhance the effectiveness of ISP training. We theorise how ISP training affects employees' ISP compliance behaviour by arguing for a transfer of training lens to study the effectiveness of ISP training. The results of our field experiment with triangulated data suggest that the effect of argumentative-enhanced ISP training is twofold. First, employees who participated in enhanced training sessions with deterrence and threat arguments demonstrated superior training outputs after the training, which, in turn, translated into a sustained training outcome three weeks after the training. Second, we also find evidence that threat arguments can reinforce the application of training outputs in the maintenance stage of learned behaviours. With this applied research study, we contribute to the research and practice by providing empirical evidence of the effectiveness of ISP training designs.

## 1. Introduction

The projected annual cost of cybercrime worldwide caused by malware, phishing, social engineering, and other attacks is set to reach \$10.5 trillion by 2025 (Esentire, 2022). In such an environment, a well-defined and communicated information security policy (ISP) is central to sustainable security. ISPs define rules and guidelines for handling information within organisational boundaries (Baskerville & Siponen, 2002). They form the foundational framework for the organisation's information security posture and are typically established at the strategic management level. Despite ISPs being central for fostering security awareness and compliance within organisations (Cram et al., 2017), employee awareness remains a significant concern globally. For instance, a study by Kaspersky Lab and B2B International found that only 12% of surveyed employees are fully aware of their organisations' ISP (Kaspersky, 2018). This is alarming, considering that 74% of data breaches are attributable to human actions or errors (Verizon, 2023). Generally, complying with ISPs, such as password or data encryption policies, can be challenging for employees, as it not only requires additional effort and impedes work but also involves changing learned work habits (D'Arcy & Lowry, 2019; Vance et al., 2012; Yazdanmehr et al., 2022).

To overcome these challenges, organisations heavily invest in security education, training, and awareness (SETA) programmes, designed to equip employees with the skills to identify and respond to security threats effectively (Whitman & Mattord, 2018). Numerous studies demonstrate the significance of SETA interventions, showing their positive impact on ISP compliance (D'Arcy et al., 2009; Lowry et al., 2015; Talib & Dhillon, 2015; Wong et al., 2022). The scope of SETA programmes ranges from awareness and micro-interventions aimed at increasing a user's situational security awareness through awareness posters, intranet notes, or emails to more resource-intensive educational programmes designed to build security competencies (Hu et al., 2022; Whitman & Mattord, 2018). This research focuses on educational programmes, which are further referred to as “ISP training”. ISP training is the central means for communicating ISPs, as it can provide a comprehensive learning environment, equipping users with the knowledge and skills needed to change non-secure routines into secure behaviours (ENISA, 2007; ISO/IEC, 2022). However, only a minority of practitioners view their training programmes as highly effective in achieving lasting compliance (Alshaikh et al., 2018; Caldwell, 2016, Hsu et al., 2022; Richardson &

Director, 2008). For instance, Sikolia et al. (2023) found that despite short-term effectiveness, security training interventions frequently suffer from a decline in knowledge retention shortly after training, raising doubts about its effectiveness.

Recent research emphasises that the absence of motivational elements is a significant factor contributing to the low effectiveness of training (Alshaikh et al., 2018; Silic & Lowry, 2020). Interestingly, best practices in information security management, such as ISO 27,002, advocate that effective ISP training should cover not only the “what” (declarative knowledge about policies) and “how” (procedural knowledge about security procedures) but also focus on motivational aspects, such as the “why” (relevance of compliance) (ISO/IEC, 2022). Understanding the “why” behind compliance fosters deeper commitment to security practices and enhances awareness of associated risks and consequences. Despite these recommendations, there is a lack of specific guidance on effectively incorporating the “why” of compliance into ISP trainings. While most studies have focused on the effectiveness of important mechanisms for awareness and microtraining intervention (e.g., Dincelli & Chengalur-Smith, 2020; Schuetz et al., 2020; Silic & Lowry, 2020), effective ISP training design – the cornerstone in each SETA programme to impart knowledge – has only received little attention (Hu et al., 2022).

Against this background, the goal of this research is to provide specific guidance on how to design ISP training programmes that effectively communicate the “why” of compliance behaviour. The “why” has been extensively studied in the awareness and micro-training literature, focusing on its communication through fear appeals that employ two primary argumentative enhancements: deterrence and threats (Boss et al., 2015; D’Arcy & Herath, 2011; Herath & Rao, 2009; Jensen et al., 2021; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Schuetz et al., 2020; Vance et al., 2022; Wall & Buche, 2017). $^{1}$ Deterrence arguments emphasise the consequences of noncompliant behaviour, including formal and informal sanctions, such as disciplinary warnings, fines, demotions, and even dismissals (Herath & Rao, 2009). Research suggests that stricter and more consistently enforced sanctions increase adherence to policy regulations (D’Arcy & Herath, 2011; Peace et al., 2003). Threat arguments highlight the severity and likelihood of harm associated with security threats (Herath & Rao, 2009; Menard et al., 2017; Putri & Hovav, 2014). A lack of awareness about security threats can negatively affect the entire organisation, often leading to organisational norms exerting social pressure on employees to comply with information security practices (Ifinedo, 2012; Mou et al., 2022). Employees’ awareness of potential threats affecting operations, data security, or organisational reputation shapes their perceptions of threat severity and likelihood (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015).

We transfer the concepts of argumentative enhancements using deterrence and threat arguments from the awareness and microtraining literature to the context of ISP training. While extant research on computer abuse and ISP policy violations provides evidence that appraisals of sanctions and threats shape security behaviours (D'Arcy & Herath, 2011; Herath & Rao, 2009; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015), we are not aware of research that provides conceptual and empirical insights on this promising enhancement in the context of ISP training. Building upon this gap, we posit the following research question: How do deterrence and threat arguments influence ISP training effectiveness?

We employ the transfer of training model as a lens to answer this research question (Baldwin & Ford, 1988; Blume et al., 2010; Ford & Weissbein, 1997). We argue that threat and deterrence arguments can influence the effectiveness of training by helping to increase employee adherence to compliance guidelines. Moreover, a situational effect can unfold, whereby the stimulus set initially increases attention during the training, resulting in a better and more effective perception of the training overall. We conducted a field experiment at four companies to assess how argumentative enhancements affect ISP training outcomes and compliance behaviours. We integrate findings from ISP research on deterrence and threat arguments (D'Arcy & Herath, 2011; Herath & Rao, 2009; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015) and demonstrate how these mechanisms – which found mixed support in broader ISP compliance studies (Wall & Buche, 2017) – can be applied to the specific context of ISP training. Our study addresses the need for practical and longitudinal research in this area, aiming to enhance the validity and applicability of findings to real-world settings (Lowry et al., 2017; Silic & Lowry, 2020; Willison et al., 2018). By quantifying treatment effect sizes, our research establishes a baseline for practical relevance, improving the usefulness of outcomes in real-world scenarios (Dhillon et al., 2021; Siponen & Baskerville, 2018).

## 2. Literature review

ISPs and SETA programmes are key organisational instruments for achieving information security (D'Arcy et al., 2009; Straub & Welke, 1998). ISPs comprise a set of rules and regulations related to the processing and use of information within an organisation's boundaries of authority (Baskerville & Siponen, 2002), while SETA programmes are designed not only to communicate ISPs but also to empower compliance behaviour (D'Arcy et al., 2009; Peltier, 2005). We conceptualise SETA practices into two distinct intervention classes: awareness and microtraining and ISP training (Table 1). We will then review three streams of literature on SETA effectiveness to inform our research.

## 2.1. Conceptualising SETA interventions

SETA interventions conceptually differ in their objectives, content, scopes, and techniques used (Whitman & Mattord, 2018).

Awareness and microtraining interventions are integral to SETA programmes (Boss et al., 2015; Eminagaoğlu et al., 2009; A. C. Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Menard et al., 2017; Wall & Warkentin, 2019; Warkentin et al., 2016). Their primary objective is to “keep the idea of information security in users’ minds and to stimulate users to care about security” (Whitman & Mattord, 2018, p. 213), fostering a strong organisational security commitment (Siponen, 2000). Delivered through various channels such as intranet messages, emails, videos, posters, newsletters, short briefings, flyers, and reminder notices (Guttman & Roback, 1995; Hansche, 2001; Rantos et al., 2012), these interventions are resource-efficient and easy to implement, operating as one-way communications (Hansche, 2001). However, scholars have noted the risk that recipients may ignore the intended message, which might hamper long-term effectiveness (Albrechtsen, 2007; Guttman & Roback, 1995).

Training conveys multiple types of knowledge: declarative, procedural, and conditional/interpretative. Declarative knowledge focuses on facts, procedural involves applying those facts, while conditional guides when and why to apply declarative and procedural knowledge (Alexander et al., 1991; Gibson, 2008; McCormick, 2004).

Awareness and microtraining interventions primarily focus on conveying declarative knowledge, such as identifying security threats and appropriate responses (Kesh & Ratnasingam, 2007). They may also touch on procedural knowledge, teaching specific security procedures (Guttman & Roback, 1995; Whitman & Mattord, 2018). However, procedural knowledge varies in context and lacks precise definition (Smith, 1994). We therefore take a pragmatic stance on defining procedural knowledge (Alavi & Leidner, 2001), viewing it as theoretical knowledge about practical skills, such as instructions on security procedures. The motivation to comply with ISPs in these interventions typically relies on persuasive fear appeal messages, detailing the harm and probability of threats, alongside recommended countermeasures (Herath & Rao, 2009; A. C. Johnston & Warkentin, 2010). The “what” and “why” in ISP training interventions differ from those in awareness and microtraining because they reinforce an understanding of the purpose, effectiveness, and operating principles of security concepts (Peltier, 2005). Thus, establishing a connection between ISPs and recipients' work tasks and security experiences is central to ISP training. This can only be achieved through a high level of involvement by the recipient of the intervention, as illustrated in Karjalainen and Siponen's (2011, p. 531) example of good password practice: “[...] the training would start with a discussion of the relevance of passwords as protection mechanisms in the employees' work situation. The trainer would clarify the assets each employee protects using passwords and the threats and implications if someone cracks the employees' passwords. The training would further demonstrate what password cracking means and how it happens using examples of the employees' passwords”. This example also demonstrates how ISP training interventions allow the incorporation of other knowledge types: causal (understanding why security practice works), conditional (understanding when security practices apply), relational (understanding interactions between security practices), and pragmatic (understanding best practices and experiences) (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Kesh & Ratnasingam, 2007). Procedural knowledge (how) is not confined to theoretical instructions about practical skills but can also encompass hands-on, practical guidance. Conditional knowledge, being context-dependent, poses significant challenges for trainees to acquire (Klotz et al., 2015; Shavelson, 2008). Comprehensive training programmes like onsite training may outperform microtraining in imparting this knowledge, as trainers can emphasise situational nuances and strategies to counter diverse security threats. Text- or video-based training might fall short in generating comparable outcomes due to the limitations of one-way communication channels. ISP training interventions stand out for their flexibility, allowing dynamic adaptation to recipients' evolving needs, which is essential for high effectiveness (Rantos et al., 2012). This includes, for example, adjusting the intervention's scope and content based on recipients' responses during training.

The SETA variable is either set as a direct antecedent of compliance behaviour (Albrechtsen & Hovden, 2010; D'Arcy & Hovav, 2007, 2009) or set in a nomological network with mediating factors based on deterrence, motivation, or other psychological or organisational theories (Chen et al., 2015; D'Arcy & Hovav, 2009; D'Arcy et al., 2009; Hovav & Putri, 2016; Lowry et al., 2015; Putri & Hovav, 2014; Talib & Dhillon, 2015). The basic assumption is that SETA programmes are unidimensional, meaning that regardless of their style, format, and content, they have the same effect on behavioural outcomes. One exemplary item used in quantitative empirical research included the following statement: "My organization provides training to help employees improve their awareness of computer and information security issues" (D'Arcy et al., 2009, p. 97). Overall, this research stream highlights the significant role of SETA programmes in shaping employees' ISP compliance behaviour. However, it does not offer insights into the quality or design of the specific SETA programmes participants encounter in their organisational context.

The second stream of research delves deeper into the interventions' design. This stream of research can be further divided into two substreams. The first substream looks into the training style, format, and communication of security-related content as antecedents of training outcomes in microtraining and awareness interventions. Instead, the second substream investigates how ISP training should be designed to foster training outcomes. A few studies in this first substream examine training style as an influencing factor of SETA programmes' effectiveness (Dincelli & Chengalur-Smith, 2020; Jensen et al., 2017; Nguyen et al., 2023; Silic & Lowry, 2020). In these studies, the general assumption is that more engaging SETA programmes that foster experiential training outcomes can contribute to the improvement of instrumental outcomes. Jensen et al. (2017), for example, compared the effect of rule-based phishing training vs. mindfulness training in a field experiment and found that incorporating mindfulness tasks when handling phishing attacks leads to a decreased probability of a response. These results are reinforced by a field experiment by Nguyen et al. (2023), which showed that mindfulness training predicts low phishing susceptibility more than rule-based training and over-learning. Dincelli and Chengalur-Smith (2020) compared an engaging SETA artefact based on storytelling to a traditional text-based intervention focusing on online privacy and found that it increases experimental outcomes such as satisfaction and memorability while simultaneously decreasing online self-disclosure. Hull et al. (2023), applied a similar approach by comparing mindfulness anti-phishing training with narrative training in a digital experiment. The research study indicates that interactive and narrative storytelling can lead to enhanced instrumental training outcomes (Hull et al., 2023).

A broader number of research studies in the first substream of the second research stream draw on different theoretical angles from the persuasion literature (e.g., coping theory, protection motivation theory, and elaboration likelihood model) to examine how SETA interventions can be designed to increase intervention effectiveness in terms of behavioural outcomes (e.g., compliance intentions). In the microtraining and awareness literature, research commonly applies message customisation by using fear appeals as a persuasive argument for employees to comply with organisations' ISPs (Jensen et al., 2021; A. C. Johnston & Warkentin, 2010).

A fear appeal is typically conveyed through a persuasive message outlining the likelihood and severity of a threat (threat appraisal). It also incorporates strategies that illustrate how to cope with the threat (coping appraisal) (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; R. W. Rogers, 1975; Wall & Buche, 2017).

In fear appeal theories, employees weigh threat appraisals against coping appraisals (Jensen et al., 2021). According to protection motivation theory (PMT), a threat appraisal is defined by an individual's perception of threat severity and threat susceptibility (sometimes referred to as threat vulnerability) (Mou et al., 2022).

While threat severity refers to the personal perception of the harm a specific security threat may cause, threat susceptibility is defined as the extent to which individuals believe that security threats will negatively impact them or their organisation if they occur (Wall & Buche, 2017). When the threat appraisal is evaluated by the recipient, the coping appraisal process begins. Coping appraisals provide one with measures to defend against a particular threat (Wall & Buche, 2017). The actual response of the recipient is then dependent on individual perceptions of self-efficacy, response efficacy, and response costs (Floyd et al., 2000; Schuetz et al., 2020).

By using fear appeals in awareness and microtraining, researchers address the “why” in SETA interventions by eliciting fear, as they represent persuasive arguments to follow security-related policies by employees. The objective of the fear appeal is to convince the recipient that the threat is severe and likely to occur while simultaneously providing individuals with sufficient knowledge of how to cope with a security threat (Jensen et al., 2021; A. C. Johnston & Warkentin, 2010). The manipulation of threat severity and threat susceptibility thus enhances the understanding of employee’s recognition of the importance of protecting organisational assets and data (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Wall & Buche, 2017). Researchers have both measured the effect of fear appeals on behavioural intentions (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015, 2019; Mwagwabi et al., 2018) and actual behaviour (Boss et al., 2015).

For example, A. C. Johnston and Warkentin (2010) found in their laboratory experiment that fear appeal emails can influence behavioural intentions to comply with security procedures but also depend on perceptions of response efficacy, self-efficacy, threat severity, and social environment influences. In recent years several reasons have been identified in IS persuasion literature hampering the effectiveness of fear appeals. First, fear appeals occasionally fail to elicit fear (Warkentin et al., 2016) or are only effective in the short term (Mwagwabi et al., 2018). Second, employees might perceive security threats as distant or abstract since they might not directly affect them personally (e.g., Jensen et al., 2021; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Moody et al., 2018; Schuetz et al., 2020). Researchers addressed this issue by extending traditional fear appeals to incorporate drivers of personal relevance such as sanctioning rhetoric (deterrence arguments) (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015), enhanced perceived argument quality (Wall & Warkentin, 2019), reduced message abstractness (Schuetz et al., 2020) or rhetorical framing (A. Johnston et al., 2023; A. C. Johnston et al., 2019). A. C. Johnston et al. (2015) explored adding constructs from general deterrence theory (GDT), such as perceived sanction certainty and perceived sanction severity, to traditional fear appeals enhancing the perceived personal relevance of the message employees are directly affected by consequences in the form of punishment for noncompliant behaviour (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015). Perceived sanction certainty can be defined as the probability of being punished when not complying with an ISP. Instead, perceived sanction severity is associated with the perceived expected degree of punishment (D'Arcy et al., 2009; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Tittle, 1980). Additionally, informal sanctions conceptualised as disapproval of an ISP violation by the recipients' social environment, such as "letting down a colleague", effectively increase the intention to comply with ISPs (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015).

In the second substream (ISP training literature), past research has provided guidelines on design recommendations for ISP training. In an action research study, Straub and Welke (1998) found evidence that training effectiveness depends on incorporating deterrence measures into the training process, a major goal of which is to “convince potential abusers that the company is serious about securing its systems” (Straub & Welke, 1998, p. 445). Puhakainen and Siponen’s (2010) action research study dismantles the learning process of security training. They proposed an information security training course based on a learning theory and a behavioural change theory, and the results suggested that learners’ systematic cognitive processing of information should be activated within the training sessions, that the learning tasks should be personally relevant, and that the training should be oriented towards the learners' knowledge (Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010). Karjalainen et al. (2013) revealed the importance of cultural dimensions in training design, as different cultures prefer different learning and communication methods. In a quasi-experimental field design, Siponen et al. (2020) found that instructor-led onsite training incorporating the principles of cognitive dissonance theory reduces the usage of neutralisation techniques of end users when choosing and creating passwords in an organisational context.

In summary, the educational ISP literature acknowledges the relevance of learning in the design of ISP training, but the studies are primarily qualitative and conceptual, with little quantitative empirical evidence of the effectiveness of different ISP training intervention designs. Our review of both research streams indicates that the current literature lacks an understanding of the effectiveness of different design options for ISP training. The first research stream identified in this study assumes that SETA programmes are unidimensional in nature. Mediation and outcome variables are investigated to be affected in quantitative studies when a SETA programme in an organisation is in place. Instead, the second research stream considers the design of the SETA programme an important feature, resulting in different levels of security-related outcomes. Most SETA studies in this stream of research focus on the design and implementation of awareness and microtraining interventions. None of the quantitative studies, except for Siponen et al. (2020), examine the effectiveness of an ISP training intervention.

This is particularly important because ISP training conceptually differs from awareness and microtraining in its objective, main attributes, knowledge type, level of involvement, communication, and impact timeframe (see Table 1). Many research studies in the microtraining and awareness literature have shown that the usage of fear appeals can be beneficial if the recipient perceives the message as personally relevant (e.g., Jensen et al., 2021; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Schuetz et al., 2020; Wall & Warkentin, 2019).

However, studies in this research stream are limited to micro- and awareness training(e.g., A. C. Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015). Appendix A shows a summary of previous studies addressing the effect of the communication of the “why” through fear appeals on behavioural outcome measures in the micro-training and awareness literature. A study qualified for our analysis when the effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were obtainable from the dataset and when the outcome measure was behavioural (e.g., protection motivation, compliance intention, and compliance behaviour).

The effect sizes in most studies suggest that fear appeals can be an important driver of security-related behavioural outcomes. However, the results also show that the communication of the “why” in microtraining produced different effects, sometimes with high (e.g., A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Mwagwabi et al., 2018) or medium effect sizes (e.g., A. C. Johnston et al., 2023, 2019; Schuetz et al., 2020) and occasionally with low (e.g., Boss et al., 2015; Jansen & van Schaik, 2019) effect sizes. This inconsistency in findings is sometimes attributed to the varying usage of fear appeals in personal and organisational contexts (Schuetz et al., 2020). In this regard, Schuetz et al. (2020) found that against expectations fear appeals lead to higher levels of fear and protection motivation in organisational contexts and that the message abstractness of a fear appeal could also result in the ambiguous results.

However, all studies included in Appendix A have in common that they use fear appeals as part of a text- or video-based intervention in which the persuasive message is presented in a one-way communication channel outside of a full training regimen. Although some studies have investigated the long-term effects of threat or deterrence arguments in fear appeals (e.g., Mwagwabi et al., 2018), the training format is conveyed through online messages rather than in a physical environment.

In ISP training, the communication of the message itself differs from that of microtraining and awareness studies (see Table 1 for detailed information). This in turn relates to the “why” transferred through the intervention which applies for several reasons. First, in instructor-led training, for example, a facilitator is responsible for initiating the process of the learner’s attitudinal change (Knowles et al., 2015; C. Rogers, 1969).

Instead of presenting a security-related message in a one-way communication channel, trainers can stress the importance of specific content (the “what” and the “why”) in an interactive communication style. This also allows for immediate feedback on participants’ questions and can thus motivate learners to reflect on the learning content (Knowles et al., 2015; Steinberg, 1991).

Second, being in a physical training room can lead to fewer distractions than in non-physical environments (e.g., videos, posters, and emails) and steer the attention of participants towards the training session (Bai et al., 2020; Cigognini et al., 2015; Ramlogan et al., 2014; Williams et al., 2012; Zureick et al., 2018). This situational effect can improve learners' retention of important content and its relevance. Hence, we suspect that the commonly applied constructs of fear appeals incorporate threat and deterrence arguments to transfer well to instructor-led ISP training.

Third, most quantitative empirical findings regarding the link between SETA interventions and compliance behaviour are based purely on self-reported data. Except for two studies, most quantitative studies based their results on cross-sectional and correlational analyses. An experimental design with time-lagged and secondary data would increase the rigorousness of the findings and contribute to the communication of the “why” through fear appeals in ISP training.

## 3. Research model

Our research model is guided by the transfer of training literature and the dominant nomological network of the transfer process (Baldwin & Ford, 1988; Ford & Weissbein, 1997). The transfer of training is defined as the extent to which trainees effectively apply the knowledge, skills, and attitudes acquired in a training programme to a real-world job (Baldwin & Ford, 1988). One of the central questions in this stream of literature is how to configure training inputs that lead to optimal transfer in real-world settings (Baldwin & Ford, 1988). The transfer process comprises three stages: training inputs, outputs, and transfer conditions reinforcing sustained training outcomes. The training inputs include the training design, the characteristics of the trainee, and the work environment. Training outputs refer to the new knowledge gained during training and the retention of that knowledge after completing the training. The transfer conditions describe the generalisation of learned training procedures to the work context and the maintenance of the trained behaviour over time. The model of Baldwin and Ford (1988) includes three premises. First the transfer process implies that training material must be learned (i.e., training output) before it can be applied to the job (i.e., transfer conditions). Second, the trainee's learning success (i.e., training output) depends on the training session's content and personal preconditions for learning and reinforcing environmental work conditions (i.e., training inputs). Third, training inputs and the work environment also directly influence transfer conditions. Procedures and skills learned during training are more likely to be applied at work if personal and environmental conditions reinforce the behaviour. The transfer of training literature contends that training coupled with the organisational transfer climate, for example, in terms of process and reward systems, allows for superior transfers (Baldwin & Ford, 1988; Blume et al., 2010) (see Appendix B for a detailed review).

We apply the transfer process to ISP training concerning declarative and procedural ISP knowledge and ISP compliance behaviour. In line with the transfer process, we posit that a transfer gap between trained and maintained security behaviour can arise. If the training design does not reinforce the desired behaviour, well-learned skills may not be maintained on the job. Arguments encompassing threat appraisals and sanctioning rhetoric have been found in studies to be effective in promoting desirable security behaviours (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Mwagwabi et al., 2018; Wall & Buche, 2017). Moreover, the constructs of PMT and GDT have been widely applied to IS security research (Cram et al., 2019; Mou et al., 2022), especially in fear appeal messages (see, e.g., Boss et al., 2015; Jensen et al., 2021; A. C. Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015). We argue that both threat arguments (threat severity and threat susceptibility) and deterrence arguments (sanction certainty and sanction severity) can be transferred to the ISP training context. More specifically, we see deterrence and threat arguments as methods of communicating to trainees the relevance of ISP compliance behaviour. The effect of both argumentative enhancements is twofold: First, if the training emphasises relevance to the employees, they will be more motivated to undertake the cognitive processing of declarative and procedural knowledge. Second, awareness of its relevance also motivates compliance behaviour at the generalisation and maintenance stages. The research model is depicted in Figure 1.

Little is known about how the manipulation of the “why” through fear appeal elements (deterrence and threat arguments) influences training outcomes (e.g., ISP knowledge) over time. Only three studies in our analysis focused on long-term effects in microtraining and awareness studies (see Appendix A).

## 4. Hypotheses derivation

4.1. The influence of deterrence arguments on ISP training output and sustained ISP training outcomes

A standard measure for ensuring that employees adhere to ISP regulations is the implementation of deterrence. It is argued that the more severe the sanctions are and the more likely they are to be applied, the more likely employees are to follow the regulations (D'Arcy & Herath, 2011; Peace et al., 2003). In line with this mechanism, it is recommended that policies be designed to implement deterring sanctions and measures to detect deviant behaviour. We apply this deterrence perspective to training design and argue that deterrence arguments can be powerful tools in ISP training.

The effect of persuasive messages on attitude changes and adaptive behaviours based on deterrence finds broad support across diverse fields, such as earthquake preparation, health behaviour, and criminal behaviour (Gleicher & Petty, 1990; Peters et al., 2013). In this paper, deterrence arguments refer to deliberately placed messages in the communication process that stress personal relevance due to the threat of personal sanctions. Personal relevance is elicited by messages stressing that formal or informal sanctions are expected if employees deviate from policy regulations. The messages include both the severity of the sanctions and the certainty with which they will be applied.

In line with the transfer of training literature (Ford & Weissbein, 1997), the effect of deterrence arguments on ISP training is twofold. First, highlighting personal relevance increases an employee's motivation to cognitively process training procedures instead of ignoring them (Schuetz et al., 2020). Second, when trained procedures need to be applied in a real workplace setting, fear arousal relates to procedural knowledge in two ways. Knowledge about negative consequences increases situational awareness of training procedures in daily working routines, which is a necessary condition to start thinking about ISP compliance. Moreover, employees consider potential consequences when deciding for or against deviant behaviour and are, therefore, more likely to follow the prescribed procedures (D'Arcy et al., 2009). Accordingly, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1a (H1a): Deterrence arguments increase the effect of ISP training on ISP training output in terms of ISP knowledge

Hypothesis 1b (H1b): Deterrence arguments increase the effect of ISP training on ISP training output in terms of ISP behaviour intention.

We also argue that ISP training enriched with deterrence arguments can lead to learners retaining important content and its relevance better due to increased perceived personal relevance (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015). We therefore firstly expect the training material to be remembered by employees over a longer period of time enhancing the building process of declarative and procedural knowledge. Accordingly, we propose:

Hypothesis 2a (H2a): Deterrence arguments increase the indirect effect of ISP training over ISP training output on sustained ISP training outcomes.

In line with A. C. Johnston et al. (2015) the training enhanced with deterrence arguments can increase the perceived personal relevance of the training content in the long-term. This leads to higher motivation to apply the learned training material in the job context. Followingly, we hypothesise:

Hypothesis 2b (H2b): Deterrence arguments increase the direct effect of ISP training on sustained ISP training outcomes.

## 4.2. The influence of threat arguments on ISP training output and sustained ISP training outcomes

Security threats can involve a variety of risks, including social engineering attacks, phishing, malware, ransomware, distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks, zero-day exploits, and man-in-the-middle attacks (Aslan et al., 2023). These threats can impact the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data, information, and information systems (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015). Moreover, they can have implications for the daily work and functioning of the team. If one fails to follow security regulations, such threats will likely cause severe damage. The nature of deterrence arguments is different from threat arguments. While the former refers to the consequences imposed on the trainee by the organisation, the latter sees the trainee as part of the organisation being exposed to external threats (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015).

According to PMT, two conditions must be met for individuals to view the recommended behaviours as effective. First, the threat must be perceived as severe. At the same time, the individual must feel that the occurrence of the threat will endanger them (threat appraisals). Secondly, they must see the recommended behaviours as effective and believe that they are capable of carrying them out (coping appraisals) (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; R. W. Rogers, 1975; Wall & Buche, 2017).

While some studies in the persuasion literature have shown that threat arguments significantly impact training outcomes (especially short-term compliance intentions), others have found no effect (see Appendix A). These studies commonly used fear appeals as microtraining to convey these arguments to the recipient in a text- or video-based format. As described in the previous sections, the communication of such a message is one way, meaning that the recipient cannot interact with an instructor who further underlines the severe consequences and impact that a specific security threat can cause to the organisation. We posit that in ISP training, the communication of threat arguments can enhance the perceived importance of the training, as the setting allows for feedback loops and might also lead to high levels of the perceived relevance of the problem for the organisation. This situational effect then enhances the short-term outcomes directly measured after the training was conducted. Thus, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 3a (H3a): Threat arguments increase the effect of ISP training on ISP training output in terms of ISP knowledge (H3a)

Hypothesis 3b (H3b): Threat arguments increase the effect of ISP training on ISP training output in terms of ISP behaviour intentions (H3b).

Moreover, we see threat arguments as enhanced training features that impact the conditions of transfer (maintenance and generalisation of learned behaviours). First, the highlighting of the organisation's vulnerability and the severity of security threats stimulates the absorption of training content in the physical training session. By emphasising real-world consequences of non-compliance, cognitive involvement and processing can be increased. This in turn leads to improved maintenance of the training material, resulting in sustained ISP training outcomes through an indirect effect (Baldwin & Ford, 1988). Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 4a (H4a): Threat arguments increase the indirect effect of ISP training over ISP training outputs on sustained ISP training outcomes.

Another premise of transfer of training is that learned behaviours have to be generalised to the job environment of employees (Baldwin & Ford, 1988). We argue that fear appeal messages presented during training sessions can motivate trainees to learn the procedures, align their expected behaviour with the regulations, and reinforce desired security behaviour in working life. This in turn leads to an increased generalisation stage of the transfer process. Therefore, we posit the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4b (H4b): Threat arguments increase the direct effect of ISP training on sustained ISP training outcomes.

## 5. Research design

We follow a between-subjects design with two treatments and one control group as part of revised ISP training across four German companies (referred to as Company A, B, C, and D). Each treatment consisted of training sessions imparting declarative and procedural ISP knowledge. Two sessions were enhanced with deterrence and threat arguments, while the third served as a baseline group with no additional arguments. Data from companies A, B, and C were collected using a questionnaire directly after training, and the measurement of actual behaviour was determined using a fake phishing attack. The data collected at Company D involved an additional questionnaire before the training session and three weeks after the training.

## 5.1. Ex-ante power analysis

Based on the comparable transfer of training literature (Facteau et al., 1995; Tracey et al., 1995), effect sizes for the differences between the training sessions were estimated to be medium (f = 0.250). The power analyses assumed an $\alpha$ -error probability of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.90. A power analysis in G\*Power revealed that the lower threshold for the sample size of a multigroup analysis with three groups is 172 participants (Faul et al., 2009). As our analysis also includes a partial least squares (PLS) analysis, we calculated a second minimum required sample size to estimate the structural model, assuming the same $\alpha$ -error and statistical power. The exponential smoothing and gamma function methods reveal a minimum sample size of 151 (Kock & Hadaya, 2018). The square root method suggests a minimum sample size of 169. Accordingly, we consider 169 an appropriate lower bound for our target sample size.

## 5.2. Study context

Training sessions were conducted between 2017 and 2019 and were central to the annual SETA measures for all four companies. Company A, a prominent energy-trading firm established in the late 1980s, employs approximately 550 individuals across five European countries. Company B, a software company with about 250 employees, operates across Europe. Company C is a human resource (HR) consulting company with approximately 100 employees. The training participants mainly consisted of business consultants, IT engineers, and administrative staff at the headquarters. Company D, a medium-sized automotive supplier, has about 800 employees. The training covered the same core content such as password and email security and clean-desk behaviour and lasted between 45 and 90 minutes.

## 5.3. Design of data collection

Figure 2 depicts the data collection design. We conducted three distinct training sessions, building upon existing material focused on declarative and procedural information security knowledge. While Group Con's training material only builds on declarative and procedural security knowledge, Group Det's training material received additional deterrence and Group Thr's material received additional threat arguments. Trainees were randomly assigned by HR departments to these groups, with varying sizes ranging from 10 to 35 participants. Training effectiveness was measured at different intervals. Companies A, B, and C had trainees complete questionnaires after training, capturing demographics, control variables, course feedback, and expected security behaviour. Company D's trainees completed questionnaires before training, immediately after, and three weeks later. $^{2}$ Two weeks after the final training session, information security behaviour regarding the email policy was assessed. This was done by sending a spear-phishing email to all trainees and recording the frequency of link clicks. Participants were instructed not to discuss the email with colleagues to prevent influence and maintain the integrity of the click results.

## 5.4. Design of ISP training sessions and manipulations

We collaborated with company information security management to create training materials focusing on policy regulations and procedural knowledge. The training material included argumentative enhancement manipulations to emphasise the relevance of ISP compliance behaviour. Relating to deterrence, our manipulations included elements outlining sanctions for noncompliant behaviour, such as fines, disciplinary actions, peer disapproval, and references to law violations. Relating to threats, our manipulations involved arguments highlighting the company's vulnerability to security threats should employees fail to behave according to security regulations. These included threats enabled by weak passwords, true stories of company document theft, and a growing number of phishing attacks. Additionally, we contextualised the arguments within employees' work environments, emphasising potential consequences like losing project files or facing work disruptions due to security breaches. $^{3}$ An excerpt of these enhancements at Company A is included in Appendix C for reference.

## 5.5. Measurement of variables

Research variables were assessed via questionnaires and a simulated spear-phishing campaign, measuring personal characteristics, ISP knowledge, and intended ISP compliance. Intended ISP compliance behaviour was measured as situational and generic ISP compliance intentions. Nine experts, including six in information security and three in IS research, reviewed the design and scales. Adjustments were made to training materials based on their feedback, resulting in effective training, realistic scenarios, and valid scales regarding content validity.

Intended ISP behaviour and ISP knowledge. We utilised a two-item scale from Bulgurcu et al. (2010) to measure “intention to comply with the ISP”. Additionally, we evaluated specific ISP behaviours through hypothetical scenarios related to three policies covered in the training. These scenarios included instances like screen locking (a line manager asks an employee not to lock their screen, claiming it helps colleagues work faster), passing on login credentials (a colleague requests login credentials from an employee on a business trip, citing time-saving for the company), and requesting credentials (the CIO sends an internal email requesting credentials to be entered into an external link). We opted for one-item scales to measure responses to each scenario, adapted from Siponen and Vance (2010). Two items from Bulgurcu et al. (2010) were modified to measure knowledge and responsibilities regarding ISP procedures and guidelines. The questionnaire items can be found in Appendix E.

Actual behaviour. A spear-phishing campaign measured actual behaviour by sending a fake email to all training participants, purportedly from the CIO, asking them to click a link. The number of participants who clicked the link was recorded per group. To ensure anonymity, individual questionnaire responses were not linked to actual behaviour. Instead, data was collected at the group level.

We first provide descriptive statistics as well as information on the manipulation checks. Then, we perform three analyses that assess 1) short-term effects of ISP training using threat or deterrence arguments on training outcomes; 2) short-term and long-term impacts of both argumentative enhancements on training output and sustained training outcomes with respect to general ISP compliance intentions; and 3) total effect of ISP training with threat or deterrence arguments on actual behaviour.

## 6.1. Descriptive statistics, randomization test, and manipulation checks

The demographic characteristics of the samples of all four companies are depicted in Appendix F. Overall, the profile of the total sample suggests that the participants are educated and experienced. The total sample size of Companies A, B, and C (i.e., 198 observations) exceeds the minimum sample size for adequate statistical power of Analyses 1 and 3 (minimum of 172 observations). The same is true for the sample size of Company D (i.e., 193 observations) and Analysis 2 (minimum of 169 observations).

To do so, we checked whether the treatment groups differed in terms of demographic characteristics. A series of chi-squared tests for independence revealed no significant differences (see Appendix G), which suggests that our randomisation procedure worked as expected. Details on the manipulation checks can be found in Appendix H. Overall, the results of the manipulation checks suggest that the trainees correctly interpreted the treatments regarding deterrence and threat arguments.

## 6.2. Analysis 1: examining the influence of ISP training design on ISP training output

The first analysis aims to examine whether an enhanced ISP training design in terms of deterrence or threat arguments leads to improved ISP training output. In this analysis, we operationalised training output regarding ISP knowledge, situational ISP compliance intention, and general ISP compliance intention. The first analysis relies on the data sets of Companies A, B, and C, in which ISP training outputs were only measured after the training.

## 6.2.1. Assessment of the measurement model

We first assessed the quality of our measurement model. The items' mean values and distribution properties are typical of their respective scales (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2013; Moody et al., 2018). We assessed convergent and discriminant validity for the latent constructs. To do so, we ran a confirmatory factor analysis that included all three dependent variables. The results display an acceptable model fit ( $\chi^{2}/df = 2.313$ , CFI = 0.969, RMSEA = 0.059). All latent variables display a sufficient level of reliability (composite reliability > 0.7; Bagozzi & Yi, 1988), convergent validity (average variance extracted > 0.5; Bagozzi & Yi, 1988), and discriminant validity (heterotrait – monotrait ratio < 0.85; Henseler et al., 2014). We concluded that our latent variables exhibited good measurement properties and that the data were suitable for further analysis. We computed the mean scores for all three dependent variables for subsequent analysis. Information on descriptive statistics, the validity and reliability of the scale as well as interconstruct correlations can be found in Appendix I.

## 6.3. Analysis 2: Examining the influence of ISP training design on sustained ISP training outcome

We then examined the long-term effects of ISP training design on ISP behaviour change maintenance using the data collected at Company D. In contrast to the datasets from Companies A, B, and C, the dataset includes measures for ISP compliance behaviour before and immediately after the training and three weeks later. Thus, it enabled us to find evidence for the long-term effects of enhanced ISP training. The measurement of long-term training outcomes is in line with the transfer of training framework. Here, we measured the same variables as in Analysis 1. The main objective of this procedure was to approximate how well participants were able to transfer the learned behaviour (learned three weeks before Analysis 2) to their personal work context.

We decided to use a structural equation modelling (SEM) approach consistent with other experimental IS and management research studies (Fombelle et al., 2016; Trenz et al., 2020). SEM provides two advantages for our setting. First, Bagozzi et al. (1991) argued that in experimental research designs with latent variables, SEM is preferable to other methods because it can account for measurement errors in constructs. Second, our research model suggests that training design indirectly affects ISP training outcomes. As SEM estimates all relationships simultaneously, indirect effects are estimated based on the same latent composite and can, thus, be directly interpreted. We decided to apply a PLS estimator because it offers advantages in terms of less restrictive sample size and distribution assumptions (Bagozzi et al., 1991). It has broad application in experimental IS and management research (Fombelle et al., 2016). Moreover, the PLS estimator fits our primary target: predicting training effectiveness rather than theory testing. We dummy-coded the experimentally manipulated training design for our structural model into two variables.

## 6.3.1. Assessment of the measurement model

Before estimating the structural model, we also inspected the measurement model of Company D's dataset for indicator reliability, composite reliability, convergent, discriminant validity, and measurement invariance. The resulting measurement model reveals that all items load significantly on their respective constructs above the threshold of 0.70 (Hulland, 1999). Furthermore, all composite reliabilities lie above the acceptable limit of 0.70 (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994), and each AVE also exceeds the lower bound of 0.50 (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988). The results are presented in Appendix J. We then assessed discriminant validity using the heterotrait – monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT) indicator (Henseler et al., 2014). The highest pairwise HTMT value was 0.717, which was below the threshold of 0.85. Additionally, the 95% quantiles of the bootstrapped HTMT values are below 1. The results suggest that discriminant validity can be confirmed. We checked for metric measurement invariance, as we used repeated measures of general information compliance intention and interpreted the relationships between them. Using the same factor structure, we tested whether the factor loadings differed significantly across all three time points. By bootstrapping our model, we received distributions for the factor loadings at each time point. The pairwise t-test reveals no significant difference $p > 0.05$ . Overall, we concluded that our measurement model has good measurement properties and that we can proceed with our analysis given the empirical design (i.e., measurement of the independent and dependent variable using the same instrument), there is a potential bias through common-method variance. Although the longitudinal (i.e., temporally separated) nature of our research design per se limits the potential for common-method variance (Hulland et al., 2018), we employed a priori methods to minimise common-method variance arising from social desirability (i.e., explaining to respondents that their answers are essential for research, that only their personal opinion matters, and that all data are collected anonymously; Podsakoff et al., 2003). Accordingly, we argue that common-method bias is not a strong concern for our analysis.

## 6.3.2. Estimation of the structural model

To examine ISP training design's long-term effects, we used a autoregressive structural equation model. General ISP compliance intentions were measured before training (t0), immediately after training (t2), and three weeks later (t3). Moreover, we controlled for the initial behaviour at t1 of general ISP compliance intention at t3. This autoregressive structure allows us to investigate intra-individual change. We added dummy variables for the two enhanced ISP training designs (t1). The inclusion of experimentally manipulated predictors allows us to estimate the effects of enhanced ISP training designs with deterrence arguments and threat arguments (in comparison with the baseline training) on ISP training output and ISP behaviour maintenance and alleviate concerns of collinearity (Kock & Lynn, 2012). We chose this analytical strategy over other methods for two reasons. On the one hand, autoregressive modelling is an established approach (Benitez et al., 2018; Courtois et al., 2014; Qu et al., 2022) that allows us to investigate the trajectory of the variable of interest over time. On the other hand, in contrast to other methods, such as latent change models (Tao et al., 2022), it allows us to keep the model and parameter parsimony (given sample size restrictions). Appendix K provides an overview of the path model estimation. Table 4 details the model estimation and differentiates between the direct effects and total effects of our research variables.

In light of the research model, we discuss three observations in the following. First, the results further support the effect of ISP training enhancements on ISP training output. We find a significant direct effect on general ISP compliance (t2) for both training with deterrence arguments $p < 0.01$ and training with threat arguments $p < 0.01$ , yielding further support for H1b and H3b. We also compared the effect sizes (Cohen's d) for both treatments to the baseline control group. In this context, we found small effect sizes between the deterrence group $d = 0.197$ and the threat group $d = 0.173$ in comparison to the control group.

Second, the results emphasise the importance of taking a differential perspective on the effect of training enhancement on direct ISP training output and sustained ISP training outcomes. Overall, we find a positive and significant total effect on general ISP behaviour (t3) for training with deterrence arguments $p < 0.05$ and training with threat arguments $p < 0.01$ . For the deterrence group, the total effect comprises a significant indirect effect via training output (H2a, p < 0.05) and an insignificant direct effect (H2b). For the threat group, we find both a significant indirect (H4a, p < 0.05) and direct effect (H4b, p < 0.05). We also examined the effect sizes for the long-term measure of general intention to comply with ISP. While the data showed no effect between the deterrence and control groups $d = 0.066$ , a small effect was present between the threat and control groups $d = 0.281$ .

Finally, the results mirror the autoregressive structure of general ISP compliance behaviour intention in the training context. The behaviour intention in t0 significantly predicts behaviour intention in t2 (p < 0.01), and behaviour intention in t2 significantly predicts behaviour intention in t3 (p < 0.01). At the same time, we also observe a substantive and significant effect of behaviour intention in t0 on behaviour intention in t3 (p < 0.01). The significant direct effect of the initially reported behavioural intentions before training on compliance intention in t3 suggests that the change induced by training did not fully translate into a new ISP behaviour. It appears that ISP training changed the participants' intention to comply, but after some time, this effect diminished, and the participants returned to their old behaviour.

We found strong evidence for the impact of deterrence and threat arguments on ISP training output. Our results indicate that trainees in the deterrence group and threat group retain significantly higher levels of ISP knowledge and reported higher intentions for both situational and general ISP compliance behaviours compared to those in the control group. These findings suggest that the inclusion of threat and deterrence arguments in ISP training results in positive training outputs, indicating important short-term effects. The calculated effect sizes also reinforce this finding in the results section. In analysis 2, we identified comparable short-term effects on overall ISP compliance intentions for both training interventions enhanced with deterrence and threat arguments.

In terms of sustainable ISP training outcomes, our second and third analyses overall provide ample evidence that addressing the “why” through threat and deterrence arguments is an important mechanism for ensuring information security. However, when examining the distinct impacts of deterrence and threat arguments, the picture becomes more nuanced. First, our data reveals no significant direct long-term effect (measured after a three-week period of time), of training enhanced with deterrence arguments on general ISP compliance intention. This could indicate that the persuasiveness of deterrence in evoking fear of sanctions wears off over time. Moreover, the stand-alone effect of deterrence arguments does not transfer into long-term behaviour change. On the contrary, the indirect effect through the mediation of general ISP compliance intention, directly measured after the training session, had a significant impact on general ISP compliance intention in the long term. Our findings indicate that deterrence arguments in training significantly influence intentions to comply with ISP before translating into sustained behaviour. Thus, the training’s initial impact on general ISP compliance intention is important for long-term sustained effects.

In analysis 3, we observed a lasting impact of training that incorporated deterrence arguments, resulting in a notable reduction in susceptibility to fake phishing emails. Although we could not confirm the direct effect of security training with deterrence arguments on long-term compliance intentions, this result could be attributable to the indirect mediated effect via short-term compliance intentions. One additional reasonable explanation for this could lie in the specific context (spear-phishing intervention) in which actual security behaviour has been measured. The specific context of the required behavioural outcome could vary from measuring general ISP compliance intentions (Aurigemma & Mattson, 2019).

The effect of training enriched with threat arguments had a significant direct and indirect impact on general ISP compliance intention in the long term (measured three weeks after the training session took place). However, in analysis 3 we found no significant effect of training with threat arguments on actual security behaviour. Although we observed a positive impact on general ISP compliance intentions (both in the short and long term), the results provide the first evidence that threat arguments in ISP training lack triggering a behavioural change in real-world situations.

## 7.1. Theoretical contributions

Our review of SETA interventions highlights that ISP training is a centrepiece in SETA programmes for imparting theoretical and practical knowledge and even building the foundation for the effectiveness of other interventions, such as awareness and micro-training campaigns. At the same time, we observe that prior empirical research has focused on awareness and microtraining. However, further examinations are required to translate and re-examine these findings due to the different nature of ISP training and its specific role in SETA programmes (e.g., outcomes, embeddedness, and goals). Given the limited theory-based empirical evidence on ISP training, our findings provide four specific theoretical contributions.

First, we provide contextualised empirical insights for argumentative enhancements to ISP training in a real SETA context and provide a baseline for intervention effectiveness. Research on persuasion and fear appeals through deterrence and threat arguments has a rich tradition in the awareness and microtraining literature (Boss et al., 2015; Hu et al., 2022; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015; Wall & Buche, 2017). This study is the first to uncover how deterrence and threat arguments influence training effectiveness at the ISP training output and sustained outcome level. By showing that deterrence and threat arguments increase training effectiveness, we complement the primary qualitative and conceptual ISP training literature (Karjalainen & Siponen, 2011; Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010). We provide the first evidence of the magnitude of the intervention effect sizes regarding long-term behavioural consequences and actual security behaviour. By uncovering these potentials, we also underline the practical importance of studying threat and deterrence arguments and their distinct mechanisms in the broader SETA programme context (Mou et al., 2022). For example, we are among the first to find a time-lagged effect of deterrence theory on ISP policy behaviour in a field experiment. In doing so, we contribute to the discussion of the merits of deterrence theory in ISP studies and provide compelling evidence that deterrence mechanisms can work in specific organisational settings (Willison et al., 2018). We also complement the current fear appeal literature; our results suggest that deterrence arguments have a stronger impact on training outcomes than threat arguments. With our empirical evidence highlighting the potential magnitudes of deterrence and threat arguments, we hope to inspire further research in this area.

Second, we introduce a new angle to the ISP training literature on training effectiveness. Drawing on the transfer of training literature, we differentiate between ISP training output (i.e., the immediate effect of the training intervention) and sustained ISP training outcome (i.e., the sustained time-shifted effect of the training intervention). This differential perspective is important, as the effect of ISP design can be twofold. On the one hand, an enhanced ISP training design can increase the change in training output, and thus the change in sustained training outcomes. It appears that addressing the “why” motivates participants to learn. On the other hand, the results of threat arguments suggest that training enhancements can also directly improve sustained outcomes. When trained procedures are implemented, a “why” can motivate them to act. This dual perspective enhances the classical perspective that focused either on learning (Albrechtsen & Hovden, 2010; Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010) or on behaviour (A. C. Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; A. C. Johnston et al., 2015) and uncovers the true magnitude and importance of ISP training enhancements in terms of deterrence and threat arguments. Moreover, the differentiation between training output and outcome reveals a non-sustained ISP training effect. It appears that, directly after the training, the participants overoptimistically report a willingness to comply with ISP. However, the training effect diminishes as time passes, and the participants return to old behaviours. While low persistence of educational programmes has already been found in other educational programmes (Kizilcec et al., 2020), our results suggest that this is also a substantial concern for ISP training. Such an effect has important implications for other evaluation research on security interventions. Studies that solely rely on measures of training output should be cautious to refer directly to this output as a surrogate for training outcome, as it is likely to be overoptimistic. While we provide the first evidence for the necessity of differentiating between training output and sustained training outcome in the context of argumentative enhancements, we believe that the transfer of training perspective provides further opportunities to integrate SETA interventions, such as human-centred security interventions, and ISP compliance behaviour in the workplace.

Third, with our research, we respond to a call for more applied research in information security research. Siponen and Baskerville (2018) argued that it is crucial to show that basic research on fundamental explanations for a specific phenomenon can advance to the intervention level and, ultimately, reach the stage of practical application. This is specifically important because practitioners commonly note a gap between cybersecurity spending and effective protection as a result of the implementation of one-size-fits-all strategies that fail to adequately address the unique security needs of individual organisations (McKinsey, 2019). In response to this challenge, recent research has emphasised the need to adopt more holistic, tailored approaches to cybersecurity that go beyond technical measures and encompass social and organisational factors to improve an organisation's overall security posture (Dhillon et al., 2021).

Finally, this paper contributes to the methodological discussion in ISP compliance behaviour research and studies with high external validity. Senior scholars in the field of ISP behaviour criticise the fact that ISP studies often rely on intentional or hypothetical behaviour measures based on cross-sectional, purely self-reported data (Lowry et al., 2017; Siponen & Baskerville, 2018; Willison et al., 2018). This study has three features that, in combination, significantly complement existing studies on deterrence and threat. It is grounded in a practical non-hypothetical setting (i.e., actual training procedures in companies), relies on longitudinal data (measurements before the training, after the training, and three weeks after the training), and uses data triangulation (experimentally manipulated training, questionnaire data, and phishing campaigns). In doing so, our research provides further rigorous support for the effectiveness of persuasion through threat and deterrence in SETA interventions. In light of security awareness and microtraining studies conducted in a field setting (A. C. Johnston et al., 2015), this study stands apart in terms of external validity for at least two reasons. First, we implemented our experimental research design within the companies' real SETA programmes. Our comprehensive ISP training represented a significant share of the organisation's SETA programme. Despite the fact that the trainees were aware that the training was accompanied by researchers, it was likely that they perceived it as authentic. Second, in this setting, with the actual look, feel, and procedure of ISP training, we collected longitudinal data on training output and sustained training outcome. This increases external temporal validity, as we show that an effect observed at one point in time generalises across other occasions (Wang et al., 2017).

## 7.2. Managerial contributions

This research also provides important contributions to practice. First, it supports the ISO 27,002:2022 recommendation for SETA programmes (ISO/IEC, 2022), emphasising the need to address the “what”, “how”, and “why” in information security training. For example, when using off-the-shelf training, it is important to clarify how information security impact individuals, offering a cost-efficient improvement over other procedural and technical measures. Second, we found that deterrence arguments exert a greater impact than threat arguments in the short term, specifically on compliance intentions and ISP knowledge when compared to the control group. This may be due to the ineffectiveness of threat arguments, especially severity and vulnerability, when organisational commitment is low (Jensen et al., 2021; Schuetz et al., 2020). Thus, it underscores the necessity for managers to actively strengthen organisational commitment to improve the effectiveness of threat-based ISP training designs. Our results also underscore that the manipulation of the “why” in ISP training translates to sustained training outcomes. Deterrence arguments have short-term impact, while threat arguments better motivate employees to integrate learned content into daily routines. Our fake phishing campaign results support this, showing lower susceptibility in the deterrence-based group (17.6%) compared to the threat-based (25.2%) and control group (33.9%). Thus, combining deterrence and threat arguments can effectively convey the “why” of ISO 27,002 in workplace settings. Finally, our data suggest that single security training sessions are not silver bullets. While enhanced ISP training effectively changes intended behaviour, altering actual compliance behaviour, especially unconscious security habits, proves more challenging. Therefore, we stress the need to avoid isolated ISP training designs. Instead, managers should opt for holistic SETA programmes, viewing ISP training as a tool to nudge security behaviour while addressing workplace environment changes.

## 8. Limitations and future research

Our study is not free from limitations and offers several opportunities for future research. First, our findings are specific to a sample of German employees in companies prioritising information security. Contextual factors, such as prior training designs or cultural factors, may affect the mechanisms of ISP compliance behaviour and training success (Karjalainen et al., 2013; Trang & Brendel, 2019). Second, another limitation is the presence of confounding factors at the group level. Our experimental manipulation occurred during training sessions, a common approach for ISP training. Due to privacy concerns, individual data could not be linked to specific sessions. Despite efforts to maintain consistency in group-level variables like time and facilities, unobserved factors may have influenced results. Variations in group sizes across sessions, for instance, could have affected participant engagement and introduced potential biases.

By identifying these limitations, this study also creates several opportunities for further research. One promising area is exploring differences in the effects of argumentative enhancements. While this study laid groundwork for understanding the transfer process in ISP training, examining the effectiveness of various (combined) training enhancements could provide valuable insights for designing effective programmes. Moreover, the transfer of training perspective suggests avenues for integrating existing ISP compliance behaviour research into the training context. For instance, future studies could integrate research on ISP and personality (A. C. Johnston et al., 2016; Warkentin et al., 2012) into the transfer of training process. Furthermore, while threat and deterrence arguments were employed to facilitate learning content transfer, not tailoring these arguments individually may have influenced training outcomes (Johnston et al., 2023). Future research could explore the impact of personalised messaging in onsite training, providing insights into the optimal balance between message customisation and improved training outcomes. Finally, our results indicate that the effects of our training intervention on improved security behaviour may not be lasting. In line with recent calls for longitudinal investigations (Cram et al., 2024), we thus hope to inspire future research to explicitly model and examine decay in security training intervention effects over time (e.g., using latent growth models).

## 9. Conclusion

This research explored how deterrence and threat arguments can enhance ISP training effectiveness. While prior research has shown their impact on information security behaviour, there is a gap in applying them to ISP training. We argue that this is a shortcoming since microtraining and awareness interventions conceptually differ from ISP training (see Table 1). Hence, we hypothesised that communicating the “why” through these arguments could yield different outcomes in ISP training effectiveness.

Our field experiment results, supported by triangulated data, indicate that employees exposed to enhanced training with deterrence and threat arguments exhibited improved training outputs immediately and sustained these outcomes three weeks post-training. Moreover, we observed that these arguments reinforced the application of training outputs during the maintenance stage of learned behaviours. Through this applied research, we contribute empirical evidence to the effectiveness of ISP training designs, aligning with the call for treatment effect rates based on real company data (Siponen & Baskerville, 2018).

## Notes

1. Argumentative enhancements are persuasive messages integrated into SETA programs, aiming to enforce

compliance behaviour by instilling fear, such as detailing the potential consequences of security threats and the personal ramifications of non-compliant behaviour (Wall & Buche, 2017; Witte, 1992).

2. At Company D, we had the opportunity to collect data over a timeframe of several weeks, enabling us to examine the long-term effects of ISP training design on ISP behaviour change maintenance. At Companies A, B, and C, we encountered company-specific data collection constraints, which limited our ability to measure ISP training outcomes solely after the training.

3. While research suggests that employing individualised message rhetoric, including the use of personally relevant language, can significantly impact security behaviour (A. C. Johnston et al., 2023, 2019), we have chosen not to use personally relevant language in our threat and deterrence arguments. This decision is grounded in the fact that onsite trainings typically involve diverse groups with individuals holding varying roles and responsibilities, making the creation of individualised messages challenging. Our messages are designed to resonate personally with employees. For instance, the deterrence messages integrated into the ISP training emphasise the potential consequences of sanctions for individual employees, making the content personally relevant. Furthermore, by heightening awareness about potential threats that could disrupt operations at the employee's, departmental, or organisational level, our aim is to emphasise the importance of security practices in the workplace. This approach not only underscores the relevance of security but also transforms the abstract concept of security threats into personally relevant considerations for employees.

4. We utilised a random allocation process for rooms, training times, and trainers across different groups. This randomisation was designed to account for unobserved factors at the trainer, time, and location levels, reducing biases related to participant fatigue/alertness, trainer motivation, and variations in training facilities. To establish a standardised and comparable experimental environment as much as possible, we maintained consistency in the duration of training sessions. By aligning them within a similar range, we aimed to control for variations that could potentially introduce bias into our results.
