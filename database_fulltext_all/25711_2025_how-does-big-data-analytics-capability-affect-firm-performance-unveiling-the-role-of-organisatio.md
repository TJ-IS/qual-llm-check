# How does big data analytics capability afect firm performance? Unveiling the role of organisational resilience and environmental dynamism

Jiabao Lin<sup>a</sup>, Shaowu Wu<sup>a</sup> and Xin (Robert) Luo <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>College of Economics and Management, South China Agricultural University, Guangzhou, China; <sup>b</sup>Anderson School of Management, University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, New Mexico, USA

## ABSTRACT

In a dynamic environment, organisations are increasingly compelled to utilise big data analytics (BDA) to develop organisational resilience, which will help them weather disruptions and gain competitiveness. By investigating 213 Chinese firms across a diverse array of industries, this study seeks to uncover the mechanisms and boundary conditions through which BDA capability afects firm performance. Drawing on the organisational capabilities-based theory and information technology-enabled organisational capabilities perspective, we both theoretically and empirically verify that two types of organisational resilience (i.e., proactive resilience and reactive resilience) mediate the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance. Our findings reveal that environmental dynamism positively moderates the impact of BDA capability on reactive resilience and strengthens the positive mediation efect of reactive resilience on the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance. This study contributes to the information systems literature by theoretically and empirically clarifying the pivotal roles of proactive resilience and reactive resilience as mediating mechanisms and by describing the contingent efect of environmental dynamism on the relationship between BDA capability and performance. The results provide new insights into how managers deploy BDA to achieve superior performance in a dynamic environment.

ARTICLE HISTORY Received 9 January 2023 Accepted 25 June 2024

KEYWORDS Big data analytics capability; environmental dynamism; organisational resilience; firm performance

## 1. Introduction

In today’s business environments that are characterised by pervasive uncertainty, securing a competitive advantage is pivotal for the survival of firms (Zhou et al., 2023). Many companies are ramping up the deployment of their big data analytics (BDA) initiatives with the aim of developing critical insights that can ultimately confer distinct competitive advantages (S. Gupta et al., 2020; Wamba et al., 2017). BDA, as an idiosyncratic technology, empowers firms to analyse extensive volumes of unstructured data from multiple sources (Choi & Park, 2022). It plays a crucial role in facilitating firms to transform their businesses and outperform competitors, and it enables organisations to make informed, strategic decisions based on data-driven insights (L. Chen et al., 2022). BDA also aids in developing risk management strategies, allowing firms to effectively mitigate threats and ensuring a stable performance environment (L. Li et al., 2022; S. Lin et al., 2022; Mikalef, Boura, et al., 2020). It fuels innovation by providing insights into market demands and emerging trends (Mikalef, Boura, et al., 2020), thereby availing companies to develop new products and services that meet customer needs and preferences (Mikalef & Krogstie, 2020).

Despite the growing investment in BDA, effectively managing and sustaining the positive outcomes of its implementation poses significant challenges for firms. The success rates of digitalisation have not surpassed 26% in some digitally savvy industries, whereas the success rates in less tech-savvy industries are even lower, ranging from 4% to 11% (L. Li et al., 2023). This underscores that firms have yet to grasp the potential of BDA, emphasising the imperative for academics and practitioners to delve into how BDA can be effectively deployed to enhance firm performance.

Notwithstanding the recognised importance of BDA capability in enhancing competitive advantage (Behl et al., 2022; Mikalef, Krogstie, et al., 2020), our current understanding of its relationship with firm performance remains starkly limited. First, existing research has largely ignored the mediating role of organisational resilience in delineating intermediate variables within this relationship. Although some mediating factors, such as market orientation (Song et al., 2023), dynamic capabilities (Wamba et al., 2017), and business models (Olabode et al., 2022), have been discussed in prior studies, the unique mechanism from an organisational resilience perspective has been surprisingly neglected. Organisational resilience, denoting the ability of a firm to prepare for, respond to, and recover from disruptions in its supply chains (Y. Jiang et al., 2023), emerges as a critical mediator in this context. A resilient organisation is well positioned to navigate the complexities of BDA, leading to a more substantial and positive impact on firm performance (J. Lin et al., 2023). By contrast, a less resilient organisation may struggle to capitalise on its BDA investments when confronted with unforeseen challenges, potentially compromising its performance (Cohen et al., 2022). Therefore, understanding the unique role of organisational resilience is imperative when exploring the effect of BDA capability on firm performance.

Second, while it is acknowledged that the external environment can affect BDA capability to support firm performance (Mikalef et al., 2021), there is a paucity of research incorporating environmental dynamism into the examination of how BDA capability affects firm performance. Environmental dynamism, encapsulating the volatility and unpredictability of the external environment of a firm (Schilke, 2014), is a paramount factor that warrants attention. Notably, overlooking the role of environmental dynamism vis-à-vis the effect of BDA capability on firm performance may lead to an insufficient theoretical understanding of the BDA capability – firm performance relationship. In light of the gaps in the existing literature, this study seeks to address the following research questions:

RQ1: How does organizational resilience mediate the impact of big data analytics capability on firm performance?

## RQ2: To what extent and how does big data analytics capability affect firm performance under different levels of environmental dynamism?

Motivated thus, we draw on the organisational capabilities-based theory and information technology (IT)-enabled organisational capabilities perspective to develop a research model to examine the mediating role of organisational resilience and the moderating role of environmental dynamism in the association between BDA capability and firm performance. This study makes three important contributions to information systems (IS) research. First, it uncovers the intrinsic mechanism by which BDA capability acts on firm performance, and specifies the mediating paths of proactive resilience and reactive resilience, thus finding a new explanation for the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance. Second, it establishes that environmental dynamism positively moderates the impact of BDA capability on reactive resilience and strengthens the positive mediation effect of reactive resilience on the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance, thereby providing a nuanced understanding of the boundary conditions under which BDA capability exerts its influence on firm performance. Third, while previous studies have drawn on resource-based theory and dynamic capability theory to explain the effects of BDA capability on firm performance (Akter et al., 2016; Corte Real et al., 2019), the present research extends this epistemological discourse by incorporating the organisational capabilities-based theory and information technology-enabled organisational capabilities perspective. Specifically, we scrutinise organisational resilience in terms of proactive resilience and reactive resilience as distinct forms of organisational capabilities. We further outline the possible mediation mechanisms in the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance under different levels of environmental dynamism. In doing so, this study provides fresh insights into how firms strategically deploy their BDA capabilities to develop organisational resilience in complex and dynamic environments.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Organizational capabilities-based theory and Information Technology (IT)-enabled organizational capabilities perspective

The organisational capabilities-based theory posits that firms with organisational capabilities are able to perform a coordinated set of tasks using organisational resources to achieve particular outcomes (Helfat & Peteraf, 2003). For example, firms can cultivate operational flexibility (Yu, Zhao, et al., 2021), agility (L. Li et al., 2020), innovation ambidexterity (Bozic & Dimovski, 2019), and market-directed cap ability (Suoniemi et al., 2020) to achieve superior performance in a dynamic environment (Z. Wang et al., 2016). Firms with strong organisational capabilities will be able to achieve competitive advantage through the deployment of resources in a distinctive and inimitable manner (Castillo et al., 2021). Importantly, the IT-enabled organisational capabilities perspective, which is derived from the founda tional organisational capabilities-based theory, emerges as an indispensable perspective within the ambit of IS research (Benitez Amado & Walczuch, 2012). IT capability is generally defined as the ability of a firm to mobilise and deploy IT-based resources in combination with other organisational resources and capabilities (Bharadwaj, 2000). As the strategic significance of IT in augmenting and cultivating organisational capabilities intensifies, the perspective of investigating how IT drives organisational capabilities has ascended as an important pillar within the domain of IT-business value research (Mikalef, Boura, et al., 2020). Such a perspective argues that IT capability influences firm performance through the development of organisational capabilities (Castillo et al., 2021; Tallon et al., 2019). In this study, the IT-enabled organisational capabilities perspective is suitable for understanding how BDA capability affects firm performance. First, BDA capability is a specific IT capability that boasts superior functions, such as augmented data processing, predictive analytics, refined decision-making, and the extraction of strategic insights, surpassing the confines of traditional IT capabilities (L. Chen et al., 2022; Mikalef, Krogstie, et al., 2020). Second, organisational resilience represents the ability of a firm to cope with disruptive events in order to achieve sustainable development, which is considered a critical organisational capability (Ambulkar et al., 2015). Firms exhibiting strong organisational resilience endeavour to sustain routine operations while adeptly addressing and recuperating from disruptions. Previous literature has indicated that resilient firms tend to achieve superior firm performance (J. Lin & Fan, 2024). Finally, by developing mature BDA capabilities, organisations will be able to monitor the status of each internal process, business units’ performance, processes, assets, and bottlenecks in real time (Rialti et al., 2019; Rossmann et al., 2018). Organisations can precisely forecast and discern potential disruptions by proffering incisive insights, and they can dynamically adjust business processes and structures according to the changing environment (Mikalef, Krogstie, et al., 2020; E. Wang et al., 2013), thereby furnishing substantial support for organisational stability and recuperation amid adversities. In this sense, BDA capability can enable the development of organisational resilience and further foster firm performance.

## 2.2. Big data analytics capability

A plethora of conceptualisations of BDA capability exist, and most literature on the definition of BDA capability is based on the perspective of resourcebased view theory. For example, Akter et al. (2016) suggested that BDA capability includes BDA management, technology, and talent capability. Similarly, Wamba et al. (2017) proposed that BDA capability is composed of management, technical, and talent abilities. Mikalef, Krogstie, et al. (2020) indicated that BDA capability is composed of tangible resources, human skills, and intangible resources, reflecting the ability of a firm to effectively deploy technology and talent to capture, store, and analyse data towards the generation of insights. L. Chen et al. (2022) proposed that BDA capability reflects the BDA human skills of a firm in integrating and deploying big data-specific resources. Overall, these studies mainly regard BDA capability to be composed of relevant BDA resources, which encompass tangible resources, human skills, and intangible resources. In this research, BDA capability is defined as the ability of a firm to assemble, integrate, and deploy its big data-specific resources; its dimensions include big data technical skills, big data managerial skills, and a data-driven decision culture (M. Gupta & George, 2016; Y. Jiang et al., 2023). Big data technical skills refer to the know-how required to use new forms of technology to extract intelligence from big data (M. Gupta & George, 2016; Y. Jiang et al., 2023). Big data managerial skills reflect the analytics acumen of the big data managers of firms (M. Gupta & George, 2016; Y. Jiang et al., 2023). A data-driven decision culture is defined as the extent to which organisational members (including top-level executives, middle managers, and lower-level employ ees) make decisions based on the insights extracted from the data (M. Gupta & George, 2016). To develop BDA capabilities, firms require technical skills that are used to utilise advanced analytical tools and extract useful insights from big data (Ghasemaghaei, 2019; S. Gupta et al., 2020), as well as managerial skills to accurately understand customers’ demands and business needs. Importantly, a data-driven decision culture has been an indispensable factor in the success of the big data projects of firms (Ross et al., 2013). As environments have become more dynamic and businesses have become more data driven, big data have been critical assets; big data technical skills, managerial skills, and a data-driven decision culture are the core strategic capital for developing BDA capability (L. Chen et al., 2022; Y. Jiang et al., 2023). Extensive literature shows that BDA capability is a critical antecedent of organisational capabilities and an important enabler of firm performance (S. Lin et al., 2022; Shamim et al., 2020). Firms with strong BDA capabilities can analyse data quickly and obtain useful insights, thus leading to competitive advantages (Mikalef, Krogstie, et al., 2020).

## 2.3. Organizational resilience

Scholars understand organisational resilience in different ways. Some have conceptualised it as a unidimensional construct, in which it is typically defined as the capability of a firm to be alert to, adapt to, and quickly respond to changes brought about by a supply chain disruption (Ambulkar et al., 2015; L. Li et al., 2022). Because of the dynamic and multidimensional nature of organisational resilience, it has developed as a multidimensional construct. Ge et al. (2023) categorised organisational resilience into resistance and recovery capability and defined organisational resilience as the capability of a firm to withstand, adapt to, and recover from disruptions in order to meet customer demand, achieve the performance target, and maintain operations in a vulnerable environment. Grego et al. (2024) divided organisational resilience into the adaptive dimension and the absorptive dimension and defined it as the capability of a firm to not only absorb disruptive shocks but also develop specific responses and engage in transformative actions that will enable it to capitalise on such events. S. Jiang et al. (2023) also divided organisational resilience into the resistance and recovery dimensions and defined it as the ability of an organisation to cushion itself against damage and recover rapidly from a disruption. Taken together, the commonality across definitions and dimensions of organisational resilience in mainstream literature is the focus on its proactive aspect, which emphasises the capability of maintaining business continuity during disruptions, and on its reactive aspect, which emphasises the capability of responding and recuperating during the recovery period (S. Jiang et al., 2023). In this study, we follow the line of research that examines organisational resilience to disruptions at the firm level and focus on the proactive and reactive aspects of organisational resilience. We define organisational resilience as the ability of a firm to prepare for, respond to, and recover from disruptions in its supply chains (Y. Jiang et al., 2023). Proactive resilience refers to the capability of an organisation to maintain its functions and operations against supply chain disruptions (Brandon Jones et al., 2014; Y. Jiang et al., 2023), while reactive resilience reflects its capability to respond quickly to and recover from supply chain disruptions (Ambulkar et al., 2015; Y. Jiang et al., 2023). Proactive resilience emphasises continued operations despite disruption, while reactive resilience highlights urgent responses and recovery from disruptions. Adequate proactive resilience and reactive resilience are necessary for constructing organisational resilience, given the importance of these two aspects of organisational resilience.

Previous literature has examined the role of organisational resilience in improving performance. J. Lin and Fan (2024) demonstrated that supply chain integration facilitates the improvement of sustainable performance through the mediating role of organisational resilience. X. Gu et al. (2023) revealed that organisational resilience is an important mediator of the relationship between digital transformation and firm performance. Martin Rojas et al. (2023) highlighted the mediating role of organisational resilience in the linkage between social media use and firm performance. Although previous studies have suggested that organisational resilience can positively influence firm performance (X. Gu et al., 2023; J. Lin & Fan, 2024; Martin Rojas et al., 2023), little research has examined its mediating role in the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance. To understand how BDA capability affects firm performance, we primarily focus on the proactive and reactive aspects of organisational resilience as important mediating mechanisms.

## 2.4. Environmental dynamism

Environmental dynamism refers to the volatility and unpredictability of the external environment of a firm (Schilke, 2014). It is an external factor that can force firms to leverage their BDA capabilities to guide their actions, which may enhance their critical organisa tional capabilities (Afuah, 2001; Mikalef, Krogstie, et al., 2020). Prior literature has unveiled the role of environmental dynamism in the relationship between digital technologies or IT capabilities and performance outcomes. For example, environmental dynamism was found to positively moderate the impact of BDA use on business growth (D. Q. Chen et al., 2015), cost performance, and operational performance (Wamba et al., 2020). Sharma et al. (2023) established that environmental dynamism positively moderates the relationship between blockchain adoption and firm performance. Sullivan and Fosso Wamba (2024) found a significant moderating effect of environmental dynamism on the relationship between artificial intelligence (AI)-powered capability and firm performance from the perspective of adaptive responses to market changes. Some scholars have also highlighted the suppressing role of environmental dynamism. For example, J. Lin and Mao (2023) found that an uncertain environment negatively moderates the effect of digital transformation on sustainable innovation performance. D. Q. Chen et al. (2015) reported that the impact of BDA use on asset productivity is negatively moderated by environmental dynamism. Overall, there are competing views on the role of environmental dynamism in performance outcomes. Moreover, although scholars have considered the contingent roles of external factors in the BDA capability – firm performance relationship (Mikalef et al., 2021), little research has incorporated environmental dynamism into the examination of how BDA capability affects firm performance; whether environmental dynamism enhances or damages the process of value creation generated by BDA capability also remains ambiguous.

## 2.5. Firm performance

Firm performance is an important indicator of the business value of BDA capability (S. Gupta et al., 2020). Organisational decision-making necessitates the handling of voluminous data. Within the increasingly complex commercial milieu, there is an imperative for organisations to collect and process massive business and market information to obtain in-depth insights into customer demand, which provides clearer prognostications for future corporate development. Existing literature has identified the positive association between BDA capability and decisionmaking performance. For example, Shamim et al. (2019) have revealed the facilitating role of big data management in improving big data decision-making capability and quality. L. Chen et al. (2022) empirically demonstrated that BDA capability is positively related to strategic decision decision-making in a dynamic environment. Big data analytics capabilities furnish profound insights derived from the interplay of internal resource conditions and external environmental changes, thereby facilitating commendable decisionmaking performance (Ghasemaghaei, 2019). For example, Procter & Gamble harnessed real-time big data analytics to predict demand fluctuations across different regions and product categories more accurately, subsequently adjusting their production planning and inventory management to ensure a rapid supply of categories experiencing surges in demand (D. Q. Chen et al., 2021), such as cleaning and hygiene products. Furthermore, BDA capability helps identify market trends, consumer needs, and potential opportunities to guide innovative activities and develop new products and services, thereby improving innovation performance (Ghasemaghaei & Calic, 2020; Upadhyay & Kumar, 2020). By integrating BDA into business, firms will be able to achieve improvements in productivity and revenue growth relative to their competitors, thereby enhancing economic performance (Akter et al., 2016). In this sense, using BDA facilitates economic performance (Wamba et al., 2017), decisionmaking performance (L. Chen et al., 2022; Ghasemaghaei et al., 2018), and innovation performance (Ghasemaghaei & Calic, 2020; Upadhyay & Kumar, 2020).

In dynamic environments, organisational resilience emerges as a pivotal organisational capability, underpinning decision-making performance, economic performance, and innovation performance. First, it is reported that firms with robust organisational resilience outperform peers in economic performance (Accenture, 2023). Second, organisational resilience enables the perception of risks and the seizing of opportunities with timely operational adjustments (Augier & Teece, 2009; Liang & Li, 2024). Resilient organisations possess the acumen to accurately identify and appraise external environmental capabilities, coupled with a potent learning and adaptation mechanism (Ambulkar et al., 2015). Unencumbered by specific behaviours or reliance solely on past experience, this guides managers towards judicious decision-making. Robust and flexible inventory management and production control abilities ensure the efficacious execution of strategic decisions. For example, BYD, the world’s leading manufacturer of new energy vehicles, boasts formidable industrial chain cost control capabilities, allowing its products to align with market product structures and to precisely target the mid-to-high-end vehicle sector, which undergirds the robust sales of its offerings. Moreover, BYD represents the sole automaker in China with a comprehensive industry chain and pioneering technologies globally, showcasing formidable organisational resilience. Such advantage ensured that BYD, following its announcement to cease the production of fuel vehicles, could supplant Tesla as the world’s best-selling new energy brand (Forbes, 2023). Third, organisational resilience denotes the aptitude for adept resource management and the accurate identification and appraisal of external environments (Ambulkar et al., 2015). This provides a reliable foundation for firms to analyse market trends and operational statuses, thereby strengthening effective decisions and innovation (Kumar et al., 2020). For example, BYD’s stable operations have permitted the continuous augmentation of R&D investment and the expansion of external resources, achieving a high degree of vertical integration and integrated innovation (Forbes, 2023).

Therefore, we have operationalised firm performance as a second-order formative construct consisting of these three dimensions in this study. We use economic performance, decision-making performance, and innovation performance to capture firm performance in this study (Ghasemaghaei et al., 2018; L. Li, 2022; Upadhyay & Kumar, 2020). Economic performance refers to the profitability, return on investment, and return on sales of a firm (L. Li, 2022). Decision-making performance refers to users evaluations of decision quality and efficiency in the decision-making processes of firms (Ghasemaghaei et al., 2018). Innovation performance refers to the ability of a firm to produce patents and new knowledge in terms of intellectual property (Upadhyay & Kumar, 2020).

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

## 3.1. The mediating efect of proactive resilience

Proactive resilience is considered an important organisational capability that focuses on ensuring normal operations in the face of unexpected disruptions (Brandon Jones et al., 2014). Confronted with disruptive events, an organisation faces increased information processing needs, particularly the collection and analysis of information about the disruption (Bode et al., 2011). This requires the firm to improve operational transparency and visibility, as well as adopt the velocity to respond to the disruptive event, which is critical for shaping proactive resilience to the disruption (Brandon Jones et al., 2014). Big data analytics capability helps improve business transparency, visibility, and velocity to deal with disruptions, which should increase the proactive resilience of firms to disruptions. First, big data technical skills enable firms to effectively collect, process, and analyse large amounts of data from their business operations, thus improving business transparency and visibility (L. Li et al., 2022). For example, such skills help firms monitor and determine the optimal inventory levels to maintain the appropriate amounts of safety stock (Munir et al., 2024). Furthermore, consumer demand can be predicted to reduce the risk of overproduction or insufficient inventory (S. Gupta et al., 2020). These technical skills are the technical basis for the rapid identification of potential disruptions. Second, proac tive resilience requires organisational routines to con figure resources to maintain operational robustness Big data managerial skills help organisations quickly and effectively gain insights from large amounts of data to guide the effective allocation of resources, thus increasing the velocity and efficiency of coping with disruptions (L. Chen et al., 2022; S. Gupta et al., 2022). These managerial skills ensure the integration of insights obtained from data analytics into strategic planning and business operations, allowing organisa tions to enact mitigation measures prior to the occur rence of disruptions and thus ensuring the alignment of performance with objectives (Bag et al., 2022; Behl et al., 2022). Third, a data-driven decision culture profoundly influences the construction of proactive resilience by ingraining evidence-based decision mak ing into the corporate ethos (Dubey et al., 2022; S. Gupta et al., 2022). It can promote digital solutions that facilitate the storage and sharing of information and enable the exchange of information among differ ent departments, leading to more efficiently synchro nised responses to disruptions (Singh et al., 2023). In this sense, a data-driven decision culture enhances the disruption management of an organisation from both strategic and operational perspectives, which leads to the development of proactive strategies for risk miti gation and the establishment of robust contingency plans (Naseer et al., 2021). For example, Wens is a leading national agricultural firm in China that has utilised its BDA capability to bolster its proactive resilience before the outbreak of the African swine fever epidemic in 2019. Specifically, by integrating BDA into its production operations, Wens was able to anticipate and monitor risks for pig farming in real time. While many of its competitors suffered severe losses, Wens withstood the pressure of soaring pork prices while minimising losses and ensuring business continuity (Wens, 2022). Thus, we propose the follow ing hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1a (H1a): Big data analytics capability has a positive effect on proactive resilience.

Proactive resilience enables firms to minimise the impact of disruption on business continuity during the disruption stage. First, proactive resilience contributes to the improvement of innovation performance. Proactive resilience requires firms to constantly scan the external environment and internal business and to continuously improve operations (El Baz & Ruel, 2021). To maintain operational robustness, organisations need to better monitor their internal businesses and anticipate market trends and the tech nologies needed, further enabling them to efficiently allocate research and development resources (X. Gu et al., 2023). This preparation is not only about maintaining operational continuity, as it can also stimulate creativity and lead to the development of new products, services, or processes (Do et al., 2022). Second, proac tive resilience enables firms to design flexible strategies and business continuity plans that mitigate the eco nomic impact of disruptions (H. Li et al., 2022). By ensuring that operations are maintained during adverse events, proactive resilience supports operational efficiency by minimising the costs associated with downtime; it also sustains revenue streams and market shares (Essuman et al., 2020; Martin Rojas et al., 2023). Third, proactive resilience enhances decisionmaking performance. Resilient organisations tend to have better situational awareness and a greater capacity for foresight, which allows them to identify potential disruptions and respond more quickly (Ozanne et al., 2022). This capability results in higher-quality decisions, as decisions can be evaluated with more comprehensive risk assessments and scenario planning. For example, Hema Fresh is a new retail supermarket owned by the Chinese e-commerce giant Alibaba Group. To sustain business continuity during the COVID-19 pandemic, it quickly launched new services in response to the outbreak, such as contactless delivery and online shopping, thereby enhancing the innovation output of the firm. By enhancing its internal communication mechanisms, forecasting market trends, and conducting forecasting analyses and real-time monitoring of commodity inventories, Hema Fresh ensured the rapid and accurate flow of information to quickly make more accurate business decisions and solve problems efficiently. As a result, despite the various challenges it faced during the pandemic, its online order volume surged by 220% compared with that during the same period last year, achieving superior performance (Hema, 2020). Thus, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 1b (H1b): Proactive resilience has a positive effect on firm performance.

## 3.2. The mediating efect of reactive resilience

Reactive resilience is an indispensable organisational capability for firms to make resource reconfigurations and thus recover from disruptions in the recovery stage; however, developing reactive resilience is often challenging (Ambulkar et al., 2015). Big data analytics capability improves the adaptability and reconfiguration of resources in the recovery stage, which is essential for firms to quickly adapt to and recover from disruptions. First, big data technical skills enable firms to swiftly integrate and process big data from diverse sources (M. Gupta & George, 2016). They facilitate rapid information assimilation to identify the extent and impact of disruptions. As firms need more real-time and granular information to respond promptly to disruptive shocks, big data technical skills become more essential (Ivanov & Dolgui, 2021; Y. Jiang et al., 2023). Second, big data managerial skills facilitate more informed decisions and ascertain recovery action priorities with greater acuity during crises, thus enhancing the efficiency of resource allocation and ensuring more effective recovery (M. Gu et al., 2021; Munir et al., 2024) Additionally, they help firms understand how disrup tions affect the entire organisation, leading to better cross-functional collaboration (Behl et al., 2022; D. Q. Chen et al., 2021; Feng et al., 2024). Finally, a data-driven decision culture facilitates the superior conversion of the technical value of big data into organisational functionality at both the operational and strategic levels (Vidgen et al., 2017). Data-driven decisions encourage the perpetual integration of data feedback into business operations, inherently refining the reactive strategies of an organisation (Dubey et al., 2022; S. Gupta et al., 2022). Specifically, a data driven decision culture is fundamentally adaptive. It assimilates lessons from each disruption by analysing data to ascertain what occurred and its underlying causes, thereby enhancing the response of the orga nisation to future disruptions (Yu, Wong, et al., 2021). For example, BYD Company is a leading new technology enterprise in the automotive sector, boasting three major industrial clusters: IT, automo biles, and new energy. Amid the pandemic-induced downturn in the demand for automobiles, BYD harnessed BDA for the real-time assessment of market demand, supply chain dynamics, and government directives. This move enabled an agile realignment of BYD’s production infrastructure, transitioning from the manufacture of electric vehicles to the pro duction of face masks and sanitisers and thus swiftly adapting to market demand shifts (BYD, 2021). Leveraging machine learning and AI technologies the company also optimised its production processes to enhance efficiency and reduce reliance on manual labour, thereby ensuring business continuity. Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis2a (H2a): Big data analytics capability has a positive effect on reactive resilience.

Reactive resilience focuses on the capacity of an organisation to respond to and recover from disruptions in the recovery stage. First, innovation is a critical aspect of firm performance. By responding to disruptions, firms may identify gaps in the market or in their capabilities that can lead to the generation of innovative ideas and intellectual property. To adapt to disruptions, organisations turn the lessons they learned in previous disruptions into opportunities to improve existing products or processes, and this continuous learning and adaptation are key to innovation (Ghasemaghaei & Calic, 2020; Khan et al., 2023). In this sense, firms that are used to reacting quickly are often able to develop new products or services more quickly in response to market changes. Furthermore, when firms recover from disruption, they often gain a deeper understanding of their vulnerability and the robustness of their systems and processes (Do et al., 2022). During the recovery process, organisations need to restructure resource allocation effectively and adjust their business processes to restore normal operations. Importantly, the reallocation of resources often leads to business transformation, enabling innovation within the organisation in terms of products or processes (Huber, 1991). Second, reactive resilience is crucial for minimising the economic impact of disruptions. A firm with strong reactive resilience can reduce the duration of downtime and accelerate the recovery process, thereby limiting the loss of revenue and additional costs incurred during recovery and protecting its market share and customer base (X. Li et al., 2023). Third, in the recovery stage, firms may also identify gaps in the market or in their capabilities vis-a-vis those of their competitors, which can lead to more efficient decision making towards future development. Firms with strong reactive resilience value their learning experiences from disruptions (Ambulkar et al., 2015), which will help them constantly adjust and optimise their decision-making models to prevent potential disruptions. Additionally, reactive resilience ensures that businesses maintain additional redundancy and alternative suppliers in critical systems and processes (Y. Jiang et al., 2023). In the decision-making process, this can act as a security buffer, giving companies more response options in the face of unforeseen events. Based on this theoretical analysis, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2b (H2b): Reactive resilience has a positive effect on firm performance.

## 3.3. The moderating efect of environmental dynamism

Proactive resilience focuses on the robustness of operations during disruptions. In this way, strong proactive resilience is needed when addressing the adverse impact of disruptions in a highly dynamic context. In an environment in which firms face everchanging market conditions and unpredictable external events, the role of BDA capability in improving proactive resilience becomes important. First, a highly dynamic environment necessitates that businesses effectively analyse both their internal operations and environmental factors to forecast and prepare for potential instabilities (L. Chen et al., 2022; Dubey et al., 2019). Big data analytics capabilities enable organisations to effectively monitor their business operations and analyse external changes. By engaging in continuous data collection and analysis, firms can identify potential threats and opportunities within the external environment (Mikalef, Krogstie, et al., 2020). These activities can enhance the sensing capability, thereby promoting the ability to maintain operations amid volatility. Apart from monitoring potential disruptions, BDA capability optimises resource allocation (Srinivasan & Swink, 2017). To maintain a robust system, firms often require change at the structural or component level to maintain functionality in a dynamic environment (Brandon Jones et al., 2014; Kitano, 2004). Big data analytics capability supports firms in obtaining valuable insights from both interna organisational data and external sources. This allows for a more precise analysis of resource utilisation efficiency, external resource availability, and operational weaknesses, which are crucial for firms to efficiently refine their business operations during disruptive phases (Hosseini et al., 2019; Spieske & Birkel, 2021). In low-dynamic environments, external changes are slower and more predictable (Schilke, 2014). Firms face fewer disruptions to their operational robustness compared to situations characterised by a high level of environmental dynamism; they also usually have valuable experiences to rely on to maintain normal operations with ease in relatively familiar environmental conditions (Zhou et al., 2023). Consequently, firms are not required to utilise BDA capability as frequently or necessarily to prepare for and manage disruptions in order to maintain normal operations. Accordingly, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 3a (H3a): Environmental dynamism positively moderates the effect of big data analytics capability on proactive resilience.

A highly dynamic environment necessitates a quick response and recovery from disruptions, which amplifies the impact of BDA capability on reactive resilience. Specifically, at high levels of environmental dynamism, BDA capability helps accelerate the process of information processing, enabling organisations to quickly implement effective countermeasures (D. Q. Chen et al., 2015; Schniederjans et al., 2020). Additionally, a high level of environmental dynamism significantly affects the responses of firms to environmental threats and leads to ambiguities in mobilising resources to generate capacity (El Baz & Ruel, 2024; El Baz et al., 2023). Big data analytics capability enhances resource reallocation efficiency by generating realtime data analysis and insights (Srinivasan & Swink,

2017), such as inventory management and supply chain optimisation based on instant data analytics. Furthermore, environmental dynamism requires firms to respond early and rapidly to market changes (Sullivan & Fosso Wamba, 2024). Big data analytics capability facilitates the rapid collection and analysis of vast amounts of data on market and technological trends (Olabode et al., 2022), thereby providing support for business adjustments. At a low level of environmental dynamism, the external environment is relatively stable and predictable (Schilke, 2014). The pressure to adapt to and recover from disruptions is reduced, and firms can respond to relatively familiar environmental conditions with ease (Zhou et al., 2023). Usually, firms can utilise previously successful responses to manage disruptions, and the dependency on BDA capability as a means of cultivating reactive resilience becomes less critical. Previous literature has indicated that low-level environmental dynamism typically favours the consistent exploitation of existing resources (Leonard Barton, 1992; Schilke, 2014). Consequently, in this condition, the strategic impetus for utilising BDA capability may pivot from developing reactive resilience towards exploiting existing resources to optimise operational efficiency and yield sustained competitive advantages over a prolonged period. Based on this theoretical analysis, we propose the following:

The proposed research model is shown in Figure 1.

Hypothesis 3b (H3b): Environmental dynamism positively moderates the effect of big data analytics capability on reactive resilience.

## 4.1. Scale development

The measurement items in this study were adopted from established scales and measured on a seven-point Likert scale. All items were translated into Chinese and refined through back translation (Zhou et al., 2023). We administered the pre-test questionnaire to the senior business and IT executives of 20 firms to further refine the items and assess logical consistency, ease of understanding, and item sequence. We believe that this process mitigated measurement errors and ensured the accuracy, comprehensibility, and validity of our questionnaire. First, BDA capability was measured following Y. Jiang et al. (2023). We considered the proactive and reactive dimensions of organisational resilience, and the measures we used also followed those applied by Y. Jiang et al. (2023). Second, we considered three types of firm performance. Decision-making performance was adapted from Ghasemaghaei et al. (2018), innovation performance was adapted from Upadhyay and Kumar (2020), and economic performance was adapted from L. Li (2022). Additionally, given that environmental dynamism is a key construct in our research, we obtained objective data from archival data to measure this variable. Specifically, we measured environmental dynamism by calculating the volatility in subindustry sales and operating income associated with the industry. Volatility in subindustry sales and operating income are common indicators in the literature (Soh & Setia, 2022; Xue et al., 2010). To compute these indexes of environmental dynamism, we regressed sales and operating income for a period of five years (i.e., 2018–2022) prior to the survey on a variable representing the period and then divided the standard errors of the regression by the mean level of the dependent variable (Schilke, 2014). The period covering the past five years included the prepandemic era, the COVID-19 outbreak phase, and the subsequen post-peak interval, characterised by a gradual return to nonpandemic conditions, which is ideal for measuring environmental dynamism. We operationalised six basic firm demographic variables as control variables: firm size, firm age, industry type, firm ownership, IT department size, and BDA experience (J. Lin et al, 2024: Mikalef. Krogstie, et al, 2020) We also controlled for IT investment, top management support, and innovation orientation, as firms that largely invest in IT, highly support BDA initiatives, or maintain an innovation orientation may achieve superior performance outcomes (Benitez et al., 2018; D. ## 4.2. Sample and data collection

We conducted a firm-level survey of Chinese firms in a variety of industries. First, digital technology has emerged as a critical engine for the stable growth and transformation of China’s economy. In 2022, the scale of China’s digital economy reached 50.2 trillion yuan, enabling the country to maintain its position as the second largest in the world by volume (Cyberspace Administration of China, 2023). In this sense, China is a country equipped with sufficient digital capacity in terms of preparedness, knowledge, and technologies. Second, China’s data output amounted to 8.1 zettabytes, accounting for 10.5% of the global data production and ranking second globally (Cyberspace Administration of China, 2023). Numerous firms have also progressively harnessed BDA technologies to integrate with their businesses, advancing their business transformation processes profoundly. In 2022, the scale of China’s big data industry expanded to 1.57 trillion yuan (Cyberspace Administration of China, 2023), which provides a suitable context for examining the role of BDA capability in performance gains. Third, over the past five years, several disruptive events (e.g., the COVID-19 pandemic) have profoundly affected China’s firms, which tend to face varying degrees of disruptive impacts; this context offers variability and validity to investigate the potential moderating effect of environmental dynamism. Fourth, the varying degrees of organisational resilience exhibited by Chinese firms, which are critical sources of their competitive advantages as they confront environmental dynamism, provide a suitable context for testing the research model. Therefore, we chose Chinese firms as the research sample to meet our research purpose.

We cooperated with a survey company in China that has been commonly used by scholars and has a private database comprising more than 30,000 Chinese firms. To mitigate potential common method bias and improve the accuracy and overall quality of the survey data, we used a matched-pair survey method. Two structured questionnaires were developed. The first targeted senior IT executives, while the other targeted senior business executives. Senior IT executives provided information on BDA capability, IT department size, IT investment, top management support, and BDA experience. Senior business executives provided information on organisational resilience, firm performance, innovation orientation, and other firm characteristics. In the first round, the survey company randomly contacted 1,200 firms from its private database to inquire about their use of BDA. Those firms that were not utilising BDA were removed, and the criteria led to a sample selection of 658 target firms. In the second round, the survey company delivered the business version and the IT version of the questionnaires to one senior business executive and one senior IT executive of each firm separately, followed by two rounds of follow-up reminders, each separated by a two-week interval. Of the 658 firms contacted, 254 paired questionnaires were collected. After filtering out invalid questionnaires, such as those a) with missing or invalid items and b) with identical answers to all items, we obtained 213 valid paired responses used for empirical analysis.

Before testing the effects of the proposed research model, the G\*Power tool was used with $\mathrm { f } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 5$ and power = 0.80 to identify the sample size requirements (Faul et al., 2007); the total sample required was 128. The results indicated that our sample size of 213 was adequate for testing the research model. A test of the differences in the means of firm characteristics (ownership, firm size, firm age, and industry type) between the first 25% and the last 25% of responses to determine nonresponse bias revealed no statistical difference. Therefore, nonresponse bias was not a serious concern. Table 1 summarises the characteristics of the sample. Firms with more than 250 employees accounted for 56.34% of the total sample, whereas privately owned companies accounted for 78.87%. In terms of industry type, technology firms accounted for the largest proportion (30.05%), followed by manufacturing firms (28.58%). Firms older than 10 years accounted for 69.48% of the sample, and firms with more than 2 years of BDA experience accounted for 72.77%.

## 4.3. Common method bias test

As the data were mostly collected from self-report questionnaires, our research may be affected by common method bias. To address this concern, we adopted several measures. First, we provided a detailed introduction at the beginning of the questionnaire to explain the concepts of BDA capability, organisational resilience, and firm performance, which helped the respondents give more accurate answers. Second, the identity of the respondents was anonymous, and they were informed that there were no right or wrong answers to the items on the questionnaire. Third, the items were arranged randomly. Fourth, to improve the accuracy and overall quality of the survey data, we collected data from two sets of informants (i.e., senior IT executives and senior business executives) from each firm; they are considered important respondents because they have a comprehensive understanding of the BDA initiatives and strategic business initiatives of their firms, respectively. Finally, to test whether common method bias affected our results, we used the marker variable method (Malhotra et al., 2006). We chose an unrelated variable (number of hours watching TV per week), which was theoretically unrelated to the other dependent variables, and then examined the correlation coefficient between the marker variable and the other dependent variables. The results showed that the average correlation coefficient was less than 0.1, and the significance of the path coefficient did not change before and after the addition of the marker variable (Table 2). Therefore, common method bias did not have a significant impact on the validity of our study.

## 5. Empirical analyses and results

## 5.1. Assessment of the measurement model

The construct specification is defined by the interplay between indicators and their associated constructs (Braojos et al., 2019). Proper construct specification needs to align with the theoretical understanding of the concept being measured to accurately capture the relationships among constructs. Correct construct specification is important for the validity and reliability of our findings, while misspecification of construct can lead to incorrect inferences about the relationships between constructs and ultimately misguide both theory and practice. Reflective and formative constructs are two types of constructs. Specifically, reflective constructs assume that indicators are manifestations of the underlying construct, expecting them to co-vary and be highly correlated. In contrast, formative constructs view indicators as components that define or cause the construct, which do not necessarily correlate with one another. In this study, proactive and reactive resilience are measured as first-order reflective constructs. Importantly, higher-order constructs can either be manifested or constructed from their subdimensions (Becker et al., 2012). Adhering to the four decision rules for determining formative constructs (M. Gupta & George, 2016), BDA capability was measured as a second-order formative construct, determined by three first-order reflective constructs: big data managerial skills, big data technical skills, and data-driven decision culture. Firm performance was also measured as a second-order formative construct composed of three first-order reflective constructs: economic performance, innovation performance, and decision-making performance. First, BDA capability cannot be manifested by its sub-dimensions; rather, each dimension of BDA capability collectively delineates its characteristics and contributes to the formation of the construct (Edwards, 2001). Second, each sub-dimension represents a distinct and irreplaceable facet of the construct. Third, the antecedents for each sub-dimension of BDA capability are considerably diverse. Fourth, within a formative framework, indicators are not obliged to covary, nor are correlations among indicators typically expected (Ghasemaghaei et al., 2018). For example, a change in one subdimension of BDA capability (e.g., big data technical skills) does not necessitate a corresponding change in another sub-dimension (e.g., data-driven decision culture or big data managerial skills). The same guidelines were followed for firm performance. Thus, following the guidelines for determining formative measurement, BDA capability and firm performance are both appropriately identified as second-order formative constructs.

Partial least squares structural equation modelling was appropriate for this research for the following reasons. First, it facilitates understanding complex research models that incorporate many constructs, indicators, and relationships, such as the mediating effect of organisational resilience and the moderating effect of environmental dynamism (Braojos et al., 2024). Second, it allows the estimation of first-order or higher-order formative constructs, with BDA capability and firm performance both being second-order formative constructs in our research (Hair et al., 2019). Third, it enables model estimation in the presence of both reflective and formative constructs

(Mikalef et al., 2023). To assess construct reliability and validity, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis by using SmartPLS (Sullivan et al., 2023). Regarding the first-order constructs, we assessed their convergent validity, reliability, and discriminan validity. First, we examined convergent validity. We found that the value of one of the indicator loadings (DDDC3) of the data-driven decision culture construct was below the threshold of 0.70, so we dropped DDDC3. All other indicator loadings were between 0.701 and 0.873, which were above the threshold of 0.70, each with a p value less than 0.001, suggesting good convergent validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Furthermore, all AVE values were between 0.529 and 0.700, which were all above the threshold of 0.50 (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Second, we examined the composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha of the constructs. The values of Cronbach’s alpha ranged from 0.703 to 0.897, and those of composite reliability ranged from 0.818 to 0.921, which were all above the threshold of 0.70 (Lance et al., 2006). Additionally, as shown in Table 3, the square roots of the AVE of each construct were much larger than their correlations with other constructs, suggesting good discriminan validity (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio and cross-loadings were also employed, and the results presented in Table B1 and Table B2 indicated good discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015). To further ensure the validity of the second-order constructs (i.e., BDA capability and firm performance), we tested their outer mode weights and variance inflation factors (VIFs). The findings showed that the weights of big data managerial skills, big data technical skills, and a data-driven decision culture were 0.421\*\*\*, 0.412\*\*\*, and 0.341\*\*\*, respectively. Each weight had a p value less than 0.001, indicating its importance in forming BDA capability (Gefen & Straub, 2005). The VIF values of big data managerial skills, big data technical skills, and a datadriven decision culture were 1.873, 2.199, and 1.236, respectively, which were below the threshold of 3.3 suggested by Petter et al. (2007), indicating tha multicollinearity was not a concern for these constructs. In addition, the weights of economic performance, innovation performance, and decision-making performance were 0.365\*\*\*, 0.444\*\*\*, and 0.348\*\*\*, respectively. Each weight had a p value less than 0.001, indicating the importance of all these constructs in determining firm performance (Gefen & Straub, 2005). Finally, the VIF values of economic performance, innovation performance, and decisionmaking performance were 1.956, 1.917, and 1.848, respectively, which were below the threshold of 3.3, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a concern for these constructs.

## 5.4. Test of endogeneity

To examine the potential endogeneity of the proposed model, we conducted two important analyses. First, we used a three-stage least squares regression analysis to correct for endogeneity in this model. If there was endogeneity in the model, the results in Table 4 would be affected by omitted variables or measurement errors, and the results of the three-stage least squares regression analysis would be inconsistent with those in Table 4. Following the steps suggested by Poppo et al. (2016), in the first stage, we regressed BDA capability (BDAC) against the control variables and environmental dynamism to obtain $\mathrm { B D A C _ { r e s i d u a l } = B D A C - B D A C _ { p r e d i c t e d } } .$ . In the second stage, we regressed the control variable, $\mathrm { B D A C } _ { \mathrm { r e s i d u a l } }$ , on reactive resilience and proactive resilience separately. In the third stage, to test the moderating effect of environmental dynamism, we interacted $\mathrm { B D A C } _ { \mathrm { r e s i d u a l } }$ with environmental dynamism to obtain the interacted items. We then regressedBDAC<sub>residual</sub> with its own interacted items plus the respective control variables on reactive resilience and proactive resilience.

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Reactive resilience} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} B D A C _ {\text {residual}} + \beta_ {2} E D \\ \qquad + \beta_ {3} (B D A C _ {\text {residual}} \times E D) \\ \qquad + \beta_ {\text {controls}} \text {Controls} + e. \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Proactive resilience} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} B D A C _ {\text {residual}} + \beta_ {2} E D \\ \qquad + \beta_ {3} (B D A C _ {\text {residual}} \times E D) \\ \qquad + \beta_ {\text {controls}} \text {Controls} + e. \end{array}
$$

## 5.5. Test of robustness

To examine the robustness of the proposed model, we re-examined it in two ways. First, we randomly selected 176 out of 213 total samples and then reran the model. As shown in Table 11, the main results were consistent with those of the original data analysis. Second, our model hypothesised that BDA capability is an important antecedent of organisational resilience. To prevent reverse causality, we assumed that reactive resilience and proactive resilience positively affected BDA capability, and then reran the model. The results indicated that both reactive resilience $( \beta < 0 . 0 0 1 , p > 0 . 9 0 0 )$ and proactive resilience $( \beta$ < 0.001, p > 0.900) had no significant effects on BDA capability. The testing results for robustness indicated the robustness of the research.

In contrast, the unique and inimitable characteristics, the combination of managerial behaviours, employee behaviours, and organisational structures (e.g., a datadriven decision culture) form a robust framework for building organisational capabilities such as organisational agility and resilience to cope with risks (Y. Jiang et al., 2023; Salmela et al., 2022).

In the digital era, the greatest challenge organisations face is the acquisition of the human skills to transform data into actionable insights (Barney, 1991; Mikalef et al., 2018). Scholars have underscored the paramount importance of human capital in cultivating BDA capabilities, asserting that the managerial and technical skills, coupled with organisational structures (e.g., a data-driven decision culture), are distinctive and challenging to imitate, maximising the value derived from data (Y. Jiang et al., 2023). Consequently, we argue that in the digital age, big data managerial skills, big data technical skills, and a data-driven decision culture are critical components in fostering organisational resilience. First, big data technical skills pertain primarily to employee behaviour, which is a crucial condition for improving organisational resilience. Such skills enable the discernment of insights from data and facilitate the scientific integration and coordination of internal and external organisational resources to respond to rapidly changing environments (Mikalef, Krogstie, et al., 2020; Muller et al., 2018). Second, big data managerial skills pertain to managerial behaviours, influencing organisation-level decisions and actions. Insights derived from big data managerial skills are deeply embedded in the organisational context and guide managers in refreshing the organisation’s value propositions (L. Chen et al., 2022). Literature on IT-enabled organisational resilience has consistently highlighted the importance of strategic foresight among managers and the capability of establishing an organisation that maintains its resilience in the face of disruptive events (Y. Jiang et al., 2023). Third, data-driven decision culture is regarded as an important part of organisational structure (Salmela et al., 2022). It facilitates both managerial and employee behaviours and plays a decisive role in shaping the organisation’s BDA capabilities (Y. Jiang et al., 2023). In volatile environments, organisations often need to leverage data-driven structural changes in organisational structure and management systems to enhance resistance to and recovery from disruptive events, which is instrumental for organisational resilience (Dubey et al., 2022), which in turn propels the enhancement of organisational performance (J. Lin & Fan, 2024). Hence, our research contributes novel insights to the literature on IT-enabled organisational resilience, expanding the understanding of how to deploy BDA-related resources to underpin the proactive and reactive dimensions of organisational resilience.

Second, we both theoretically and empirically proved that BDA capability positively influenced proactive resilience $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 8 , \ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and reactive resilience $( \beta = 0 . 3 4 6 , \ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This finding extends the work of L. Li et al. (2022), who empirically proved that the depth of digital technology deployment enhances firm resilience by uncovering that BDA capability is a critical antecedent of proactive resilience and reactive resilience. Our findings also extend those of prior studies on IT-enabled organisational resilience (Martin Rojas et al., 2023) by shedding new light on how BDA initiatives can be leveraged to construct both proactive and reactive resilience. Additionally, our empirical findings also showed that both reactive resilience $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 6 , \mathrm { C I } = [ 0 . 0 5 2 , 0 . 2 3 9 ] )$ and proactive resilience $( \beta = 0 . 1 1 0 , \mathrm { C I } = [ 0 . 0 3 7 , 0 . 2 2 9 ] )$ mediated the process by which BDA capability affected firm performance, thereby supporting H1 and H2. Our findings extend those of previous studies (Do et al., 2022; X. Gu et al., 2023; J. Lin & Fan, 2024) by investigating the impacts of the two types of organisational resilience on firm performance, thereby enriching the nuanced understanding of the role of organisational resilience. Notably, the values of $R ^ { 2 }$ changed from 0.564 to 0.603 in Models 10 and 11 (Table 5), explaining the substantial variation in firm performance attributable to the two different aspects of organisational resilience. Overall, our findings demonstrate that proactive resilience and reactive resilience are complementary and critical to firm performance. Firms should combine these two aspects of organisational resilience to reap superior firm performance.

Third, we found that environmental dynamism positively moderated the relationship between BDA capability and reactive resilience $( \beta = 0 . 1 5 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ thereby supporting H3b. Our findings extend those of previous studies (Yu et al., 2019) by considering environmental dynamism as a boundary condition for the effect of BDA capability on reactive resilience. We also found that environmental dynamism strengthened the positive mediation effect of reactive resilience on the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance. This further deepens the understanding of the role of environmental dynamism in facilitating the business value of BDA.

Unexpectedly, a nonsignificant moderating effect of environmental dynamism on the relationship between BDA capability and proactive resilience was identified $( \beta = 0 . 0 3 2 , n s )$ . There is a plausible reason for this result. Proactive resilience mainly focuses on maintaining normal operations and resisting disruptions. However, when a disruption involves uncertainty, predicting its form or consequences is difficult (Ge et al., 2023; Ivanov, 2021). Despite the role of BDA capability in enhancing proactive resilience, disruptions, such as city lockdowns and labour unavailability, inevitably disrupt the operational robustness of firms; this requires firms to highlight the reactive aspect of organisational resilience – that is, to be flexible and agile in quickly adjusting their operating models and strategies for survival (Alicke et al., 2021). The value of BDA capability in enabling quick, informed responses and recovery is amplified when the environment becomes less predictable (L. Chen et al., 2022).

Fourth, previous literature examining firm performance impacted by BDA often focuses on single dimensions of firm performance. For example, scholars have investigated the effect of BDA capability on economic performance (Wamba et al., 2017; Yasmin et al., 2020). L. Chen et al. (2022) empirically demonstrated the direct effect of BDA capability on strategic decision-making, while Upadhyay and Kumar (2020)

revealed the facilitating role of BDA capability in innovation performance. Importantly, our study extends this body of work by theoretically and empirically revealing the mechanisms and boundary conditions through which BDA capability influences comprehensive firm performance, including economic performance, decision-making performance, and innovation performance. This broader epistemological perspective further extends the literature on BDA-enabled performance.

Finally, our investigation into how BDA capabilities bolster firm performance within the Chinese context presents a unique and compelling case. China not only stands at the forefront of the global digital economy in terms of scale (China Academy of Information and Communications Technology, 2022) but also plays a pivotal role in ensuring the stability of global supply chains (L. Li et al., 2023). First, China possesses the world’s largest digital market, leads globally in data resources, and ranks at the forefront of digital technologies in terms of innovation capacity, such as big data analytics (China Academy of Information and Communications Technology, 2022). China holds the second position worldwide concerning the total scale of data centre computational power (Cyberspace Administration of China, 2023). Therefore, our findings are extendable to other emerging or developed economies with similar digital economy markets. Second, our research context is anchored on China, a nation with a unique institutional environment where guanxi is a long-standing and influential cultural orientation. This context also displays salient idiosyncrasies, which may limit the generalisability of our findings. In essence, understanding BDA capabilities in such a unique environment provides valuable insights that can inform practices in other regions with similar cultural and economic landscapes.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions

First, this study identifies the underlying mechanisms through which BDA capability affects firm performance. Existing literature has explored the vital role of BDA capability in enhancing competitive advantage (Behl et al., 2022; Mikalef, Krogstie, et al., 2020) through market orientation (Song et al., 2023), dynamic capabilities (Wamba et al., 2017), and business models (Olabode et al., 2022). However, despite the critical role of organisational resilience in navigating the complexities of implementing and utilising BDA, leading to a substantial and positive impact on firm performance (J. Lin et al., 2023), few studies have examined how BDA capability influences firm performance through the unique perspective of organisational resilience. To address this research gap, we investigated the mediating effect of organisational resilience. The findings indicated that proactive resilience and reactive resilience mediated the impact of BDA capability on firm performance. Notably, this research both theoretically and empirically elaborates on the effects of BDA capability on firm performance and complements the existing literature by enriching the understanding of the important mediating roles of proactive resilience and reactive resilience.

Second, environmental dynamism emerges as an external factor that can force firms to leverage their BDA capabilities to improve firm performance (D. Q. Chen et al., 2015; L. Chen et al., 2022). Despite its acknowledged importance, scant research has delved into the moderating role of environmental dynamism in this relationship. Our study fills this gap by identifying the moderating role of environmental dynamism in leveraging BDA capability to enhance firm performance through reactive resilience. Specifically, we demonstrate that environmental dynamism positively moderated the impact of BDA capability on reactive resilience, thus strengthening the positive mediation effect of reactive resilience on the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance. The results elucidated that the impact of BDA capability on firm performance was contingent upon the level of environmental dynamism, thereby highlighting the role of environmental dynamism as a boundary condition. In essence, our study offers a nuanced understanding of the boundary conditions (i.e., environmental dynamism) under which BDA capability exerts its influence on firm performance.

Third, while previous studies have predominantly drawn on resource-based theory and dynamic capability theory to explain the effects of BDA capability on firm performance (Akter et al., 2016; Corte Real et al., 2019), the present study leverages the organisational capabilities-based theory and IT-enabled organisational capabilities perspective to illustrate how BDA capability affects firm performance. Our research examined organisational capabilities through the lens of organisational resilience and outlined the possible mediation mechanisms and boundary conditions by which BDA capability affected firm performance. Specifically, BDA capability as a specific IT capability can facilitate the capability of a firm to prepare for, respond to, and recover from disruptions in its supply chain, thus obtaining a competitive advantage. The indirect effect of BDA capability on firm performance is contingent on the moderating role of environmental dynamism. This study identified the boundary conditions that can help in better understanding how the BDA capability – firm performance relationship functions in a particular context, thereby enriching the scope of IT-enabled organisational capabilities perspective.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

First, organisations are encouraged to allocate big dataspecific resources to the enhancement of pertinent resilience competencies. This endeavour encompasses an array of initiatives, including the recruitment of person nel proficient in sophisticated data analysis, the provision of vocational training aimed at augmenting the data stewardship skills of the incumbent workforce, and the integration of cutting-edge technologies and frameworks that buttress data analytic processes (M. Gupta & George, 2016). Firms need to instigate adaptable strategic planning mechanisms to resist disruptions and enhance business robustness. They should employ BDA to forecast impending perils and prospects and to formulate corresponding contingency protocols.

Second, firms need to highlight the role of BDA initiatives in responding to and recovering from disruptions. For long-term survival, integrating big data initiatives into business is suggested to better understand internal operations and the market and to improve the efficiency and quality of decision making (L. Li et al., 2022). Moreover, IT managers are advised to institute systems that facilitate the real-time analysis and surveillance of data, thereby empowering them to mount swift responses in the advent of unforeseen occurrences (Laguir et al., 2022). This capability to process information forthwith is the cornerstone of reactive resilience, empowering firms to expedite the recalibration of their strategies and curtail the time required for recovery. Organisations are also encouraged to cultivate an organisational culture that enables data-driven decision making to quickly adapt to disruption; this can be done by encouraging innovative thinking and problem solving among employees to improve adaptability and recovery (Munir et al., 2024).

Third, firms should integrate real-time insights derived from BDA into refined risk management strategies and business continuity plans. This integration enables an agile adjustment of strategic orientations to ensure that operational and service standards are upheld amid the diverse types and magnitudes of disruptions (L. Chen et al., 2022). In essence, the discrepancies in the moderating effect of environmental dynamism in the role of BDA capability in supporting the proactive and reactive aspects of resilience are meaningful, underscoring the particular importance of reactive resilience (Ge et al., 2023). Firms should recognise the nuanced roles of BDA capability in building organisational resilience, and balance their foci to reap the benefits of BDA in a dynamic and uncertain business landscape.

## 6.4. Possible avenues for future is research

This research is not without its inevitable limitations, which merit consideration for future research. First, this study was undertaken in China, so our research findings may not be fully applicable to other countries or cultures. Future research should conduct a crosscultural comparative analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of our model across diverse cultural settings. Second, while our study identified proactive and reactive resilience as key mediators in the relationship between BDA capability and firm performance, we did not account for other organisational capabilities that could influence this relationship. Future research could further explore the mediating role of other organisational capabilities, such as organisational agility. Third, we considered environmental dynamism an external factor and examined its contingent role without taking into account other potential moderating factors. Future research could broaden the epistemological scope by investigating additional moderators from diverse perspectives to unveil a more comprehensive understanding of the contextual dynamics influencing the relationships studied. In addressing these limitations, future research can enhance the robustness and applicability of our findings, ultimately contributing to a more nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between BDA capability, organisational resilience, and firm performance across diverse contexts.
