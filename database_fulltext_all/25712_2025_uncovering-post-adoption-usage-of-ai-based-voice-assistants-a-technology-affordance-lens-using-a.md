# Uncovering post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants: a technology afordance lens using a mixed-methods approach

Zhen Shao <sup>a</sup>, Jing Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Lin Zhang<sup>b</sup> and Jose Benitez <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China; <sup>b</sup>School of Management, Northwestern Polytechnical University, China; <sup>c</sup>Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, Ambassador Crawford College of Business and Entrepreneurship, Kent State University, Kent, OH, USA

## ABSTRACT

Despite the growing proliferation of AI-based voice assistants in our daily lives, how diferent functions of AI-based voice assistants afect users’ post-adoption usage behaviours remains an under-investigated research question. This study explores the nature and causation of postadoption usage behaviours (efective utilisation after initial adoption or difusion) within the context of AI-based voice assistants. Using a sequential mixed-methods research design, we first identify the post-adoption usage behaviours of AI-based voice assistants as a multidimensional concept comprised of routine use and extended use, then develop a contextualised model by revealing technology-specific antecedents, cognitive beliefs, and boundary conditions. By integrating results from the quantitative study and qualitative study, we find that three technology afordances (i.e., anthropomorphism afordance, interactivity afordance, and personalisation afordance) are salient antecedents of two cognitive beliefs, which further afect users’ routine use and extended use of AI-based voice assistants. Additionally, we uncover use frequency as a boundary condition and obtain a complementary view of post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants. The empirical research findings can extend the post-adoption IS usage literature and provide practical implications for crafting user-centred functionalities to facilitate efective human-AI interactions.

## 1. Introduction

With the burgeoning of advanced artificial intelligence (AI) technology, AI-based voice assistants have increasingly penetrated various aspects of public welfare, including healthcare, the workplace, entertainment, and business commerce (Han & Yang, 2018). Miscellaneous AI-based voice assistants are embedded in mobile devices, such as Amazon’s Alexa, Apple’s Siri, and Xiaomi’s Xiaoai. AI-based voice assistants primarily aim to streamline users’ daily tasks by enabling them to execute various commands via a voice interface, including initiating phone calls or text messages, setting alarms and reminders, controlling home devices, and obtaining answers to inquiries (Han & Yang, 2018; Q. Hu et al., 2021). According to a recent survey by the Statista Research Department,<sup>1</sup> global investment in AI-based voice assistants is expected to reach a staggering eight billion by 2024 (i.e., a number roughly equals to the world population). Despite their tremendous potential, AI-based voice assistants are still in the early stage of development, primarily due to the challenge of lower level of usage over time (Malodia et al., 2022; McCaffrey et al., 2018). That is, users tend to utilise a restricted set of functions (i.e., lower level of usage), leaving many functions remain unexplored (McCaffrey et al., 2018; See et al., 2019). Additionally, mere acceptance and basic usage are insufficient to unlock the full potential of digital technologies (S. S. Kim & Son, 2009), and sustained post-adoption usage is vital for realising their enduring value (Maier et al., 2021; Moussawi et al., 2023). Particularly, considering the importance of user engagement and extensive interactions in both social and commercial settings (Elshan et al., 2023), a comprehensive utilisation of AI-based voice assistants in daily routines would help users to realise business value through avenues like voice advertising, voice e-commerce, and ecosystem device sales (Jasperson et al., 2005; See et al., 2019). This argument is also echoed by practitioner publications, in which a paramount concern is proposed to stimulate users to actively engage with and utilise AIbased voice assistants in their daily use (McCaffrey et al., 2018). However, despite prior literature having offered valuable insights into users’ post-adoption behaviours towards AI-based voice assistants (e.g., McLean and Osei-Frimpong (2019); Son et al. (2023)); see Appendix A), a majority of studies focused on the extensive continuance behaviours (Q. Hu et al., 2021; Moussawi et al.,

2023), while limited evidence exists for why users extend beyond basic functions towards more advanced functions of AI-based voice assistants in the post-adoption stage. Consequently, it is urgent and crucial to uncover what are the salient technology functions of AI-based voice assistants and how do they affect users’ post-adoption usage behaviours. In light of the above research gap, our study proposes a primary research question:

RQ: How do the technology functions influence users’ post-adoption usage towards AI-based voice assistants?

Users’ decision to utilise a system can be determined by the result of a relational exchange between the user and the technological artefacts (Moussawi & Benbunan-Fich, 2021). In particular, the extant literature generally treats the use of AIbased information technology (IT) as a black box and fails to develop a more nuanced understanding of how technology functions facilitate postadoption usage of AI-based voice assistants (Nambisan et al., 2019). Unlike traditional digital technologies, AI is not merely static assemblies of interacting modules and features that carry out specific computing tasks (Baygi et al., 2021; Goto, 2023); rather, it serves as an active agent in interacting with users and supporting individual tasks (Borner et al., 2023). Whereas our understanding regarding users’ perceptions towards and interactions with the technological functions of AI-based voice assistants is still limited, especially in the post-adoption stage (Moussawi et al., 2023). As such, it becomes imperative to systematically identify and conceptualise technical characteristics innate to AI-based voice assistants at the postadoption usage stage (Bailey et al., 2022).

Additionally, the technical characteristics attributed to AI-based voice assistants not only cognitively stimulate users’ positive attitude beliefs based on their efficiency and effectiveness, but also trigger their concerns about the potential risks associated with their daily usage (Knote et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2023). Moreover, the coexistence of opposing attitudinal beliefs towards the emerging technology plays a critical role in affecting users’ post-adoption usage behavioural decisions (Karahanna et al., 1999). A substantial body of research demonstrates that AIbased voice assistants often fail to achieve long-term success and sustainability because a majority of users hold various apprehensions and biases about postadoption usage of AI-based voice assistants, such as privacy and ethical concerns (Ha et al., 2021; Vimalkumar et al., 2021). However, to our knowledge, prior literature mainly focuses on positive or negative cognitive evaluations of AI-based voice assistants (Buil-Gil et al., 2023; Moussawi et al., 2021), while few studies pay attention to an equilibrium of cognitive beliefs (Xie et al., 2023). Considering that AIbased voice assistants usher in a pressing issue of both the bright and dark sides of emerging technology, it is essential to include both positive and negative cognitive beliefs, allowing a comparison of their separate and combined effects on post-adoption usage behaviours.

To sum up, a mixed-methods study is conducted to achieve the above objectives. Venkatesh et al. (2016) advocate the application of such a design method, especially when previous research on a topic is either fragmented or missing. With a sequential mixedmethods design (Shao, Zhang, et al., 2022; Venkatesh et al., 2016), we first employ an exploratory qualitative investigation to elicit holistic insights into key factors and develop a comprehensive research model from a user’s point of view. We then test the proposed research model using a quantitative investigation through a longitudinal online survey. The empirical findings are expected to offer rich insights regarding how to improve users’ routine and extended use of voice assistants in the emerging human-AI interaction realm.

The remainder of our study is organised as follows. First, we propose an overarching framework to study post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants. Second, we introduce the methodology design and the qualitative study. We then develop a theoretical model and propose the hypotheses, followed by the quantitative study with empirical analysis results. We conclude with theoretical implications and practical implications.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical framework

## 2.1. AI-based voice assistants

AI-based voice assistants are software agents designed to intelligently assist users with a variety of tasks through natural language interaction (Moussawi & Benbunan-Fich, 2021). AI-based voice assistants are distinguished from traditional applications by their unique human-like design, personalised and contextual information, and prompt service delivery (Elshan et al., 2023; Knote et al., 2019; Moussawi & Benbunan-Fich, 2021). Particularly, this study focuses on the AIbased voice assistants utilised to support tasks in private contexts to enhance users’ well-being and comfort (Ammari et al., 2019; Elshan et al., 2023; Han & Yang, 2018).

Prior studies have investigated the adoption and acceptance of AI-based voice assistants from different theoretical perspectives, such as the lens of technology acceptance (Fernandes & Oliveira, 2021; Moriuchi,

2019; Moussawi et al., 2021), the lens of psychological determinants (Hernandez-Ortega & Ferreira, 2021; Mishra & Shukla, 2020; Pelau et al., 2021), and the lens of technical feature design (Elshan et al., 2023; J. Zhao & Rau, 2020). For instance, Fernandes and Oliveira (2021) explored the underlying motivations that facilitate users to adopt voice assistants based on the service robot acceptance model. Wald et al. (2023) integrated the technology acceptance model and uses and gratifications theory to investigate the parents motivations to use virtual assistants with their children in the family home.

With the proliferation of AI-based voice assistants, the emerging focal research aims to explain why and how users are motivated to continue using AI-based voice assistants in the post-adoption stage (Calahorra-Candao & Hoyos, 2024; Han & Yang, 2018; Moussawi et al., 2023). Researchers have found that users are mainly motivated to continue using voice assistants by effective technology performance, such as utilitarian benefits (McLean & Osei-Frimpong, 2019), perceived usefulness (Moussawi et al., 2023), ease of use (Mari et al., 2024), and task attraction (Han & Yang, 2018), perceived intelligence (Moussawi et al., 2023; Yoganathan & Osburg, 2024), autonomy (Q. Hu et al., 2021); or stimulated by social and emotional needs, such as social benefits (McLean & Osei-Frimpong, 2019), interpersonal attraction (Han & Yang, 2018), and friendliness (Calahorra-Candao & Hoyos, 2024).

Additionally, most scholars have generally focused on identifying the enablers of AI-based voice assistants’ adoption and understanding how they are interrelated with each other, and to what extent they affect users’ adoption and continuance intentions (Fernandes & Oliveira, 2021; Q. Hu et al., 2021; Mishra & Shukla, 2020). For example, Q. Hu et al. (2021) argued that trust in conversational AI is the basic enabler to support users’ willingness towards such digital agents. Moreover, a wider debate on this topic has acknowledged the potential challenges and risks in identifying and developing use cases for AIbased voice assistants. For instance, Vimalkumar et al. (2021) highlighted users’ concerns about information access to untrusted third parties and raised the challenges related to vulnerabilities and privacy in the context of AI-based voice assistants. Considering that focusing on the benefits or risks of AI-based voice assistants alone is often insufficient to depict a holistic evaluation towards the functional capabilities of AI-based voice assistants, this study leverages the technology affordance framework to understand the impacts of technology functions and oppositional beliefs on postadoption behaviours in the context of AI-based voice assistants.

## 2.2. The post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants

Post-adoption behaviours represents users’ technology usage behaviours after adopting and implementing information technology (Saga & Zmud, 1994). Recently, an increasing body of research has paid attention to the antecedents and outcomes of postadoption usage of AI-based voice assistants (Q. Hu et al., 2021; Moussawi et al., 2023; Son et al., 2023). A majority of the research centres on users’ continued use (Q. Hu et al., 2021; Moussawi et al., 2023) and satisfaction (Choi & Drumwright, 2021; Han & Yang, 2018) in the post-adoption stage. For instance, Moussawi et al. (2023) investigated the determinants of users’ continued use from the hedonic and utilitarian perspectives. Choi and Drumwright (2021) conducted a mixed-methods approach to explore the effects of individual motivations on their postadoption behaviours towards AI voice assistants based on the use and gratifications theory. Nonetheless, there still exists evidence indicating the underutilisation of the potential of AI-based voice assistants, i.e., users tend to employ quite narrow function breadths, engage in lower level of function utilisation, and rarely extend to utilise available advanced functions (McCaffrey et al., 2018). Specifically, most users leverage AI-based voice assistants to achieve basic tasks like setting reminders or playing music, while only a few go beyond simple prompts in more extensive ways, such as personalised travel assistance, integration with third-party services, or voice-based shopping and e-commerce.

Prior research has recognised the value and appropriateness of distinguishing user post-adoption behaviours based on their engagement with a system’s basic routine functions versus more complex and advanced functions (Mudau et al., 2024). In the IS domain, various studies have been conducted to capture the emerging nature of post-adoption system use, such as exploitive and explorative use (Shao, Li, et al., 2022), routine and innovative use (Li et al., 2013; Maier et al., 2021; Roberts et al., 2016), continued and extended use (See et al., 2019). Despite these measures having depicted various post-adoption IS utilisation, they are limited in reflecting the repeated routine use versus advanced function use in the context of AI-based voice assistants.<sup>2</sup> On a feature-centric view, post-adoption usage behaviours can be specified as users’ feature adoption decisions, feature use behaviours, and feature extension behaviours after an information technology has been applied (Jasperson et al., 2005). To our best knowledge, there is limited literature that distinguishes between the utilisation of basic functions (i.e., routine use) and advanced functions (i.e., extended use) in users’ post-adoption usage of AIbased voice assistants (Mudau et al., 2024). Routine use and extended use are distinguishable by the sophistication and extension of the functions employed (Hsieh & Wang, 2007). Specifically, routine use is the core stage of post-adoption, representing a standardised way to use basic functions in support of daily tasks (Li et al., 2013), while extended use is a widely used post-adoption behaviour that enables users to leverage advanced technology functions to their fullest potential (Hsieh et al., 2011). Together, the two behaviours offer a fairly holistic representation of post-adoption usage by capturing the extension variation of the utilised technology functions of AIbased voice assistants. Therefore, we extend this nascent stream of literature by disentangling postadoption behaviours into routine use and extended use and investigating their formation mechanisms from the technology affordance lens.

## 2.3. An overarching framework to study post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants

Structuration theories claim that interactions between human agents and advanced technology functions affect the formation of cognitive beliefs, thus leading to human behaviours at different levels of individual, organisation, and society (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Schmitz et al., 2016; Shao et al., 2023; Shao, Li, et al., 2022). On the basis of structuration theories, Markus and Silver (2008) introduced the relational concept of affordance to delineate the deep structures interwoven between users and technology features.

The concept of technology affordance originates from the affordance lens in the realism of ecological psychology, which differs from prior theories, such as the technology acceptance model and information system success model, in three important ways. First, technology affordance highlights the interaction between technology functions and users and describes how action possibility is conveyed or made visible to users (Norman, 1988; Y. Zhao et al., 2013). Second, it acknowledges an individual’s perceptions of technology functions after each behavioural action and follows a process sequence of technology affordances – beliefs formation – behavioural outcomes (Dong & Wang, 2018). Third, it considers the role of individual usage patterns; that is, each user or user group interprets and perceives a target technology feature differently (Grgecic et al., 2015). In this sense, using technology affordance as a theoretical lens allows us to comprehensively understand users’ cognitive beliefs and post-adoption usage behaviours from a humantechnology interaction view. Therefore, we take the technology affordance perspective to uncover users post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants and propose an overarching framework, as described in Figure 1. We explicate the three interrelated constructs in detail in the following sections.

## 2.3.1. Technology afordance

Technology affordance is defined as the goal-oriented action potentials permitted by technical objects to a specific user group (Markus & Silver, 2008). There exist inconsistent research findings with regard to the technology functions of AI-based voice assistants. Several scholars argue that technology is static and downplay the influence of AI-based voice assistants technology functions on users’ cognitive beliefs and behavioural change, e.g., focusing on perceived usefulness as the antecedent (Gursoy et al., 2019; Moussawi et al., 2023). Whereas other academic researchers posit that individuals’ perceptions of technology functions evolve over time (Dizon, 2021; Knote et al., 2021; X. Lin et al., 2022). Recently, IS scholars have addressed the above-mentioned contradictions by calling for employing technology affordance theory to uncover technology functions that interact with individuals’ goal-oriented action possibilities (Cheong, 2020; Gu et al., 2020; Lunberry & Liebenau, 2021). Therefore, we extend the technology affordance lens to the context of post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants by examining the significant antecedents afforded by specific technology functions.

## 2.3.2. Beliefs

Previous literature suggests that specific affordances determined by technology functions indirectly influence IT usage behaviours through the formation of beliefs (Grgecic et al., 2015). As such, research on AIbased voice assistants entails an in-depth understanding of the role of human-technology interactions in dynamically shaping users’ psychological beliefs. Noteworthily, Cheng et al. (2022) argued that the emerging AI for personal biometric information collection and analysis has dual belief mechanisms, involving the bright and dark sides of individuals’ cognitive appraisals. The beliefs, therefore, can be either positive or negative to human behaviours. Consequently, it is essential to capture users’ belief formation as a tradeoff process in the context of AI-based voice assistants and investigate the influences of specific technology affordance on the potential benefits and constraints.

## 2.3.3. Usage patterns

The goal-oriented use of technology functions in relation to a specific environment (that is, affordances) may influence cognitive beliefs and behaviours to varying degrees, depending on usage patterns (Majchrzak & Markus, 2012; Shao et al., 2021). Users with different use experiences would achieve a distinct comprehension of a system’s functions. Previous research indicates that usage patterns, reflecting individuals’ ways of using AI-based voice assistants, are significant for comprehending how users perceive different technologies and generating underlying psychophysiological beliefs andIS usage behaviours (Q. Hu et al., 2021; Park et al., 2021; Sano et al., 2016). Particularly, use frequency is recognised as a salient characteristic of usage patterns, which plays a vital role in shaping users’ attitudes and behaviours (Park et al., 2021; Vaast et al., 2017; Venkatesh et al., 2012). For instance, Molinillo et al. (2021) highlighted use frequency as a salient boundary condition of positive attitudes towards social commerce websites. However, it remains unclear regarding how different affordances affect the dual cognitive-processing mechanisms (positive and negative) with different use frequency propensities, especially in the phenomenon of AI-based voice assistants. As such, there is a need to incorporate use frequency as a contingency factor to understand how users perceive technology functions of AI-based voice assistants differently and how these affordances shape users’ cognitive beliefs and facilitate their post-adoption usage behaviours.

Overall, we have three major objectives: (1) identify the salient affordances enabled by technology functions of AI-based voice assistants that affect users behaviours in the post-adoption usage phase; (2) reveal the role of cognitive beliefs in the AI-based voice assistant context from both positive and negative sides; and (3) uncover use frequency as a boundary condition of assumptions and constraints. To achieve these objectives, we use a combination of qualitative and quantitative studies, as discussed in the next section.

## 3. Research methodology: the mixed-methods design

We adopted a mixed-methods approach to comprehensively understand the contextual details and relationships of technology affordances, beliefs, and behaviours towards AI-based voice assistants, which provides stronger inferences than a single method (X. Zhang & Venkatesh, 2017). Specifically, we conducted a sequential mix-methods approach that leveraged the findings from the qualitative study to inform the development of the research model for the subsequent quantitative study (see Figure 2). Given the limited research on identifying key functional affordances that facilitate oppositional cognitive beliefs and postadoption usage behaviours in the emerging context of AI-based voice assistants, a sequential mix-methods approach is appropriate with a qualitative study for constructs validation, followed by a quantitative study for model validation (X. Zhang & Venkatesh, 2017). As such, following Venkatesh et al. (2013)’s procedure, we first employed a qualitative study by using semiinterviews with experienced AI-based voice assistants users to uncover the technology affordances and cognitive beliefs that determine users’ post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants. We then conducted a quantitative study by using a longitudinal survey to examine the impact of the identified technology affordances on cognitive beliefs and post-adoption usage behaviours of AI-based voice assistants.

## 3.1. Phase 1: the qualitative study

## 3.1.1. Data collection

Interviewers were recruited from the user pool of a popular MTurk-like website in China, called Credamo, focusing exclusively on those who are experienced in using AI-based voice assistants in private contexts and knowledgeable about their functions. Specifically, we carried out an online semistructured interview in China in December 2021 through video calls on Tencent Meeting. We briefly introduced the interview background and provided each participant with an interview guide and openended questions in Chinese to uncover their perceptions of AI-based voice assistants (Wunderlich et al., 2019). The interviews lasted about 15 to 30 minutes for each informant, which were then audio recorded and transcribed into text documents for case analysis. We stopped the interview when data reached saturation and limited new content appeared (Cheng et al., 2022). Totally 30 samples participated in the interviewing process. The detailed interview protocol is presented in Appendix B1.

## 3.1.2. Data analysis

We employed an inductive method to extract insights from the qualitative data to identify contextualised affordances and cognitive beliefs towards postadoption usage of AI-based voice assistants (Glaser, 1992; McCaffrey et al., 2018). Following the grounded theory methodology, we analysed the interview data through a three-stage iterative coding process in the NVivo software. First, we identified the key concepts and summarised interviewees’ responses into an abstract category. Three researchers independently and carefully reviewed the case evidence and completed the open coding. Second, we constantly compared and classified the abstract category of responses with common characteristics into a general theme. Third, we integrated and centralised the general themes into higher-level categories based on the selected coding (Shao et al., 2016; Shao, Zhang, et al., 2022; Strauss & Corbin, 1990). To ensure the rigour and consistency of scientific coding, we held a group meeting to discuss the coding results in the final phase (Shao, Zhang, et al., 2022). Table 1 illustrates the exemplary case evidence, the identified functionalities, and the aggregate affordances in the three-stage coding. Meanwhile, Appendix B2 and B3 present the emergent themes and selected quotations.

## 3.1.3. Qualitative analysis findings

3.1.3.1. Afordances of AI-based voice assistants. By carefully distinguishing between use cases based on the functions and assigning them to the affordances, we summarised the three most frequently mentioned affordances from the case evidence, namely anthropomorphism affordance, interactivity affordance, and personalisation affordance, as noted in Table 1. First, anthropomorphic design is important for AI-based voice assistants to afford users a human-like experience via natural language and mimics, and align minds by providing interesting, creative, and humorous experiences to users (Knote et al., 2021). The qualitative analysis shows that users perceive the anthropomorphic capability of AI-based voice assistants through the human-like conversational cognition and humour conversational style of the assistants. Specifically, conversational cognition affordance refers to the extent of AI-based voice assistants’ cognitive intelligence in conversations. AI-based voice assistants can mimic human conversations through natural lan guage processing by presenting human-like understanding, response logic, and even emotions (P. Hu et al., 2021). According to use cases, we observed that such conversational cognition capability enables users to feel they have a natural human-like conversation with a real human being, particularly when users engage in casual conversations or seek comfort from the assistants, e.g., some users want companionship or develop emotional connections with their voice assistants. One of our interviewees mentioned the conversational cognition of AI-based voice assistants and stated: “It’s like there’s a human being answering the questions, and that’s what appeals to me the most about my voice assistants.” (R16).

Additionally, as a salient human-like characteristic of AI-based voice assistants, humour affords a more enjoyable and natural conversation between the voice assistants and users (Knote et al., 2021; Moussawi & Benbunan-Fich, 2021). Conversational humour affordance refers to the extent of humour in AI-based voice assistants’ responses. According to our interview evidence, AI-based voice assistants can entertain users with jokes, stories, and trivia, and add an element of fun and entertainment to conversations. Such a humour response style can satisfy users’ entertainment goals by helping users relax, unwind, and alleviate stress, especially during leisure time or before bedtime. For example, Siri often responds to a question with a witty remark or a sympathetic message to create a more human-like humour experience, which was also mentioned by our interviewee: “After coming back from the library, I was comfortable in my chair and just wanted to rest. I usually voice-summon my phone’s intelligent assistant YOYO and let her tell me jokes and play fun videos.” (R19).

Second, the main functions of AI-based voice assistants are voice interactivity, by engaging in two-way communications with users and facilitating tasks via responding to questions, commands, and prompts in real-time (Elshan et al., 2023; J. Kim et al., 2022), which fulfil the accessibility and convenience goals of users. Based on interactivity theory and the qualitative analysis results, users usually perceive such interactivity affordance through the control, responsiveness and communication capability of AI-based voice assistants, enabling them to communicate and interact with AI-based voice assistants as they have expected, which is consistent with theoretical literature (Fan et al., 2017). Specifically, control affordance refers to users’ perceived control over the manipulation manner towards the AI-based voice assistants, representing their perceptions of the voice assistants’ capacity to perform appropriate actions. For instance, one interviewee mentioned “I can freely control my voice assistants directly through the voice commands and assignments to complete the to-do settings”. (R25). Communication affordance represents the extent of two-way communication engagement of AI-based voice assistants. Through the voice communication function, users can synchronously communicate with the voice assistants anytime and anywhere, which is also mentioned by one interviewee: “When I speak to my smart assistants, she can provide vocal feedback to my commands, and I can continue the conversation by giving another command”. (R29). Responsiveness affordance refers to the extent of response speed of AIbased voice assistants to users’ actions, indicating users’ perceptions of the assistant’s ability to act promptly. As one interviewee said, “Sometimes I will ask for proper nouns verbally through my assistants, and it can give me quick answers efficiently.” (R17).

The third affordance that emerged from the coding evidence is personalisation, reflecting AI-based voice assistants’ ability to afford personalised services to fulfil individuals’ requirements and preferences (X. Lin et al., 2022), which can be applied in voice marketing (Rhee & Choi, 2020). AI-based voice assistants would leverage user data and preferences to offer personalised recommendations, similar to how a human anticipates someone’s needs based on past interactions (Rhee & Choi, 2020). For example, if a user frequently asks for recipes, the voice assistants might proactively suggest new cooking skills or ingredients based on their interests. One of our interviewees mentioned “There are so many takeout options, and I sometimes don’t have time to sift through. So I ask my assistants to recommend some options”. (R9). Another interviewee also stated, “My intelligent assistants can provide real-time step count information at different moments of the day so that I can easily master my workout pace.” (R1).

3.1.3.2. Oppositional cognitive beliefs towards AI-based voice assistants afordances. We then summarised the salient cognitive beliefs that affect users post-adoption usage towards AI-based voice assistants. As noted in Appendix B2 and B3, trust and privacy concerns are identified as the most salient positive and negative cognitive beliefs that account for users’ post-adoption usage towards AI-based voice assistants. On the one hand, AI-based voice assistants not only can timely and effectively communicate with users like real humans but also can provide customised services conforming to users’ needs (Benbya et al., 2021; Hernandez-Ortega & Ferreira, 2021; K. Liu & Tao, 2022). This is beneficial for promoting users’ trust in the AI-based voice assistants, as mentioned in one interview:

“Smart assistants serve as a life assistant who can remind me to complete tasks on time and accomplish specific tasks, and a chatting friend who goes with me to chat and helps me pass the time when I’m bored. This greatly adds to the convenience of my life, and I fully trust it.” (R9).

On the other hand, AI-based voice assistants’ ubiquitous and autonomous nature enables them to learn user habits and make recommendations, which requires the formation of solutions from a vast array of data sources collected from users. This may expose user identifications to the service providers and cause issues of privacy concerns, as echoed by one interview: “I am concerned with the privacy disclosure issue of the intelligent assistants. I am worried that the smart assistants would have too much power over my phone and access all its locations.” (R10). The research findings identified from the case evidence suggest that users are more likely to experience a trade-off process of trustprivacy concerns to balance benefits and risks when using AI-based voice assistants. This argument is echoed by R7:

“Despite intelligent assistants may have privacy problems, it is a trustworthy and selective software in general, which can bring a lot of benefits to me. I would like to keep utilising, even trying some new functions of the intelligent assistants on my phone.”

## 3.1.3.3. Post-adoption usage behavioural outcomes.

We further summarised users’ post-adoption usage behaviours from the case evidence. Aligned with an extensive social survey report (McCaffrey et al., 2018), we observed that a majority of participants got used to applying AI-based voice assistants to carry out basic tasks. For example, one interviewee stated: “I usually use intelligent assistants to solve tedious or repetitive operations in daily life, such as setting alarm clocks and checking the weather.” (R11). Similarly, R3 also echoed that: “The smart assistants have been integrated into my daily life. I usually ask my smart assistants to set the alarm for the next day, or create a to-do list for today or the next day, which has totally changed the previous manual way and greatly contributed to the convenience of my life”. Aligned with prior discussions on the postadoption behaviours of AI-based voice assistants, the abovementioned usage behaviours can be epitomised as routine use as proposed by previous literature (Li et al., 2013), which represents individuals’ regular and standardised usage of AI-based voice assistants to support their daily lives. As expected, there exists a small number of participants who utilise more advanced activities than normal usage to support their daily lives, such as completing transactions and linking connections with other smart devices. For instance, interviewee R24 mentioned: “I have grown to rely on smart assistants via habitual daily use and want to progressively explore their new functions. I have heard that it could record parking spots through voice commands. If I get the chance, I would like to give it a shot.” The aforementioned usage behaviours can be summarised as extended use (Hsieh & Wang, 2007), which describes individuals’ extension usage of the function scope of AI-based voice assistants to support their daily lives. Drawing upon both support from theoretical literature and case evidence, we divided post-adoption usage behaviours of AI-based voice assistants into two dimensions of routine use and extended use.

3.1.3.4. Contingency factor. Interestingly, we found from the case evidence that interviewees with different use frequency show different preferences towards the three identified technology affordances. For instance, interviewees with higher use frequency showed more interest in the technology feature of personalisation, as stated by R9, “In my opinion, my intelligent assistant’s tailored recommendation feature is one of the most alluring functions. The recommended content will change with my habits as I use it more frequently.” While interviewees with lower use frequency were more attracted by the technology feature of anthropomorphism. For example, when talking about the functionalities of AI-based voice assistants, R18 said: “The communication style of the smart assistants is really appealing to me. When I first contacted intelligent assistance, I was shocked by its humanized expression. It is quite charming, hilarious, and conversing, which makes me happy”. To further uncover the potential effects of use frequency on users’ affordance perceptions, we reconducted the iterative process and divided interviewees into high-frequency and low-frequency groups<sup>3</sup> based on the extant literature and practical report (McCaffrey et al., 2018; Rodgers et al., 2005; Stouthuysen et al., 2018). As noted in Appendix B4, we found that low-frequency users showed more preference to anthropomorphism affordances, while high-frequency users preferred personalisation affordances more, and interactivity affordances were averagely used in both groups.

## 3.2. Building and hypothesizing the proposed research model

Drawing upon the theoretical lens of technology affordance and case evidence from the qualitative study, we develop a nomological theoretical model to examine the influence mechanisms of anthropomorphism, personalisation, and interactivity affordances on users trust, privacy concerns, and post-adoption usage towards AI-based voice assistants, as illustrated in Figure 3. We integrate the personalisation-privacy paradox theory with the technology affordance lens to better understand users’ positive and negative belief formation process in the post-adoption usage stage. We also incorporate use frequency as a moderator to examine the contingency effects of three technology affordances on user trust and privacy concerns. We add gender, age, education, and AI-based voice assistant type as control variables to rule out other potential influences on the endogenous variables.

## 3.2.1. Technology afordances of AI-based voice assistants, user trust, and privacy concerns

Drawing upon the technology affordance lens, affordances related to anthropomorphic cues could boost users’ perceptions of trust beliefs in AI-based voice assistants (Lankton et al., 2015; Qiu & Benbasat, 2008). On the one hand, the presence of effective understanding ability in AI-based voice assistants would reduce the blunt feeling of AI agents, just like confronting a real person, and stimulate users to exhibit trust beliefs (Y. Kim & Sundar, 2012). The statement is echoed by the qualitative investigation. For example, interviewee (R15) mentioned: “When I’m interacting with the voice assistants, I feel like talking with a real person, and that’s what attracts me the most and enhances my sense of trust in the voice assistants.” On the other hand, the presence of humour indicates the expressive, self-assured, and extrovert personalities of AI-based voice assistants, which raises their likeability and interpersonal appeal and improves credibility assessment during the conversation (Niculescu et al., 2013). The statement is echoed by interviewee R7, who mentioned, “I like the funny interacting way between the smart assistants and me. For instance, if I ask her what she likes to eat, she will not just respond ‘Food’, but instead respond ‘Don’t mention food when losing weight’. This type of conversation appeals to me since it seems more real and gives the impression that it is a competent intelligent assistant which is more credible than a computer program.” Thus, we propose that:

H1. Anthropomorphism affordance of AI-based voice assistants is positively associated with user trust.

Interactivity affordance refers to users’ perceptions of reciprocal and synchronous communications and interactions with AI-based voice assistants (Y. Liu, 2003; Shao et al., 2020), which is a multi-dimensional construct comprised of three dimensions: control, responsiveness, and communication (Fan et al., 2017). In the context of AI-based voice assistants, control affordance represents that users can exert control over the manipulation manner towards the AIbased voice assistants. Responsiveness affordance refers to the extent to which AI-based voice assistants can provide an expeditious response to users’ actions. Communication affordance represents the extent to which AI-based voice assistants can facilitate several rounds of two-way synchronous communications.

Interactivity is identified as a critical technology feature in improving users’ credibility perceptions in the digital technology use context (Chattaraman et al., 2019; Y. Kim & Sundar, 2012). In particular, active control gives users the power of initiative, which would boost their confidence and comfort in taking action (Fan et al., 2017); prompt feedback involves users in the interactions, which can promote users trust in the voice assistants (Fan et al., 2017); synchronic communications facilitate naturally interpersonallike interactions with AI-based voice assistants, which is beneficial for facilitating a higher degree of trust beliefs (Y. Kim & Sundar, 2012). The qualitative investigation also supports the argument, as interviewee R2 mentioned: “From my personal experience, I trust the voice assistants since they can complete the tasks quickly and accurately as the way I assigned, and give me clear and prompt responses in most cases. Moreover, it will provide me feedback after receiving and completing the task, such as ‘the alarm clock for 6:50 tomorrow morning has been set for you’ as an answer to my indication” The above analysis leads to the following hypothesis:

H2. Interactivity affordance of AI-based voice assistants is positively associated with user trust.

Personalisation affordance refers to the extent that AIbased voice assistants can understand users’ needs and provide customised services based on their preferences (Kalyanaraman & Sundar, 2006; X. Lin et al., 2022; Rhee & Choi, 2020). AI-based voice assistants regard each user as a unique entity, and their ubiquitous nature allows personal information to be collected, digitised, and analysed; thus providing personalised service through embedded AI algorithms. Users are inclined to trust voice assistants if they can provide tailored and customised services based upon users requirements (Ameen et al., 2022; Waytz et al., 2014). In the qualitative study, interviewee R4 corroborated the same view: “I believe that the intelligent assistants can complete the assigned tasks very well, because it can track my usage record and master my preferences, and can perform the assigned task well based on powerful automated techniques.”

Moreover, the personalisation-privacy paradox indicates that personalisation would also trigger users’ privacy concerns (Awad & Krishnan, 2006). When AI-based voice assistants provide tailored services to satisfy their personalised needs, users may also exert privacy concerns resulting from collecting, storing, processing, and using personal information (Ameen et al., 2022). This viewpoint is echoed by R28, who mentioned: “I think the use of the voice assistants would collect my preferences, resulting in a privacy leak”. Thus, we put forward the following hypotheses:

H3. Personalisation affordance of AI-based voice assistants is positively associated with user trust.

H4. Personalisation affordance of AI-based voice assistants is positively associated with privacy concerns.

## 3.2.2. User trust, privacy concerns, and user post-adoption usage

Originating from social psychology, trust is conceptualised as a trustor’s psychological state to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of a trustee’s behaviour (Gefen, 2000, 2002; Mayer et al., 1995). Trust in IT artefacts has aroused academic attention and has been examined in various contexts, such as trust in the website (Flavián et al., 2006), trust in the online store (Y. Kim & Sundar, 2012), and trust in the mobile application (Gong et al., 2020; Patil et al., 2020). Particularly, the growing research has begun to focus on trust in AI-based voice assistants (Bawack et al., 2021; Fernandes & Oliveira, 2021), and emphasises that trust is the premise of users’ leverage of benefits offered by artificial intelligence (Gillath et al., 2021). For instance, Delgosha and Hajiheydari (2021) indicated that trust plays a vital role in affecting users’ post-adoption behaviours towards digital assistants on e-commerce websites. X. Wang et al. (2023) found that employees’ trust in the chatbot is critical to their innovative usage of chatbots in work practices.

Users are inclined to keep using and exploring the technology if they have formed trust beliefs in it. Trust is the expectation of the outcome from technology usage and the judgement regarding whether the attributes of this technology perform favourably as expected in a risky circumstance (Mcknight et al., 2011; Tams et al., 2018). Considering that postadoption usage presents a risky situation characterised by the significant potential for time loss, mistakes, and errors (Tams et al., 2018), we assume that users’ trust perceptions enable them to overcome risk perceptions and form confidence in AI-based voice assistants, which is beneficial for stimulating users’ post-adoption behaviours, i.e., routine use and extended use of AI-based voice assistants. Hence, trust in technology is vital in predicting post-adoption usage behaviours (Tams et al., 2018). The case evidence also supports the argument, as interviewee R3 stated that: “I would like to continue using the intelligent assistants and even explore more new functions, since I believe in its capability to complete the tasks I assigned and increase the convenience in my daily life”. Thus, we proposed that:

H5a. User trust is positively related to the routine use of AI-based voice assistants.

H5b. User trust is positively related to the extended use of AI-based voice assistants.

Privacy concerns refer to users’ concerns about the possible loss and unauthorised use of their personal information when they interact with AI-based technologies (Ameen et al., 2022). Previous research emphasises that a high level of privacy concerns would impede users’ usage of AI technologies (Bawack et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2022; J. Lin et al., 2021). For example, Cheng et al. (2022) explored the impacts of users’ perceptions of identity theft, financial costs, and unauthorised secondary use of AI technologies on their participation behaviours in the ridesharing platform. J. Lin et al. (2021) investigated the barrier effects of privacy risks on users’ behavioural intentions towards the COVIDSafe app, which collects real-time data from users’ locations.

In the context of AI-based voice assistants, users who perform high-level privacy concerns show a low level of trust in data access and usage (Ameen et al., 2022). The advancement of technologies embedded in AI-based voice assistants raises users’ concerns about personal information leaks, unintentional access, and continuous tracking (Ha et al., 2021). Particularly, privacy concerns have become a major barrier to user trust and their usage of AI-based applications (Ameen et al., 2022; K. Liu & Tao, 2022; Vimalkumar et al., 2021). In the qualitative investigation, several interviewees emphasised that they have privacy concerns when using AI-based voice assistants, which decreases their trust beliefs. For example, interviewee R10 mentioned: “The issue of privacy leakage will affect my trust in the AI-based voice assistants, and I am worried that it has too much authority, and can manipulate my phone arbitrarily and access various locations on the phone.” Thus, we propose that:

H6. Privacy concerns are negatively related to user trust in AI-based voice assistants.

Meanwhile, users’ fear of personal information breaches may decrease their routine usage behaviours and limit their exploration of new functionalities of AI-based voice assistants (Jang & Sung, 2021; Malodia et al., 2022). As interviewee R29 declared: “When I use Siri, I often search for and play the music I like. If I subscribe to music or video services through Siri, my personal service subscription information may be sent to Apple. So I will hesitate or even refuse to submit personal information in the daily use process since I worry that my personal account and password will be known by a third party.” The statement is also echoed by interviewee R6, who said: “If I try some new functions that require more personal information from me, I would be concerned about my privacy and information leakage issues, and this mindset would limit my use of smart assistants to some extent.” Thus, we propose that:

H7a. Privacy concerns are negatively related to the routine use of AI-based voice assistants.

H7b. Privacy concerns are negatively related to the extended use of AI-based voice assistants.

## 3.2.3. Routine use and extended use

Based upon IS use literature (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Saga & Zmud, 1994), routine use would trigger the potential for extended use. When individuals utilise IS in a way that goes beyond routine and standardised use, they achieve a higher level of usage that may allow them to exploit the fullest potential of IS (Ahmed et al., 2022; Cooper & Zmud, 1990). In the context of AI-based voice assistants, the higher the degree of users’ routine use of AI-based voice assistants, the more likely they will explore new functions. As interviewee R9 mentioned “I frequently use the smart assistants on my mobile phone at home and school, which has really supported my life. I’ve also discovered several other handy functions over time, such as ordering food delivery.” Thus, we propose that:

H8. Routine use is positively related to the extended use of AI-based voice assistants.

## 3.2.4. The moderating efect of use frequency

Use frequency refers to the number of times in using AIbased voice assistants during a specific period (Haque et al., 2020). Considering that use frequency is shaped by iterative interactions in the IS usage context, users’ perceptions of technology affordances may be variant upon the extent of familiarity and interactions with IS (Fernandes & Oliveira, 2021; Fu et al., 2020; Q. Hu et al., 2021). Contextualised to the case of AI-based voice assistants and situating the deductions from the qualitative analysis, we argue that the impacts of two technology affordance perceptions, namely anthropomorphism, and personalisation, on cognitive beliefs may vary across user frequency levels.

Anthropomorphism. The influence of anthropomorphism affordance on user trust may decrease as use frequency increases. The positive feelings derived from anthropomorphism affordance may diminish over time as its newness wears off (Uysal et al., 2022; Wells et al., 2010). For example, when users initially contact an AI-based voice assistant with a high level of anthropomorphism, they are more likely to form trust beliefs in terms of its interpersonal appeal and credibility assessment. However, the positive effects of anthropomorphism on trust beliefs are likely to be short-lived because users may not feel its novelty over time and generate strong desires for task success and efficiency with the increase in use frequency (Nowak & Rauh, 2008). Aligned with the qualitative investigation, interviewee R7 emphasised that: “The assistants originally aroused my interest since I was inquisitive about its responses to my inquiries. The conversation was quite natural-sounding, which provided me the confidence to assign the assistants some chores. While along the time, I have higher expectations for it and care more about functionalities, such as smoother conversation, quicker response, and personalized services” The above analysis leads to the following hypothesis:

H9a. Use frequency negatively moderates the relationship between anthropomorphism affordance and trust, such that anthropomorphism affordance exhibits a stronger influence on trust when use frequency is lower.

Personalisation. Personalised affordance requires a large amount of users’ information, history choices, and actions to accurately predict their preferences, which are learned incrementally based on the accumulated use experience. On the one hand, the personalisation feature of AI-based voice assistants affords high-frequency users more recommendations and choices to efficiently complete tasks. Therefore, highfrequency users may benefit more from personalisation than low-frequency users do and become more trustful in voice assistants. For example, in the qualitative investigation, interviewee R8 emphasised that: “I depend more on the voice assistants with the increase of use frequency, since the smart assistants would better understand my requirements and habits, and complete the tasks more quickly and accurately.” Thus, we propose that:

H9b. Use frequency positively moderates the relationship between personalisation affordance and trust, such that personalisation affordance exhibits a stronger influence on trust when use frequency is higher.

On the other hand, with the increased interactions between users and voice assistants, recommendations on users’ preferences and personal information would become more accurate and private, which may aggravate users’ privacy concerns about personal information leaks and abuse. As such, the personalisation feature of AI-based voice assistants may afford high-use frequency users more privacy concerns than low-use frequency ones. For instance, when talking about privacy issues, interviewee R3 argued that: “Actually, AIbased voice assistants store more information as I use it more frequently. I feel that my privacy and security cannot be guaranteed since intelligent assistants can record all kinds of private information such as my voice and personal preferences and sync them to different devices.” Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H9c. Use frequency positively moderates the relationship between personalisation affordance and privacy concerns, such that personalisation affordance exhibits a stronger influence on privacy concerns when use frequency is higher.

## 3.3. Phase 2: the quantitative study

## 3.3.1. Data collection

To guarantee a sufficient pervasiveness of the sample, we entrusted a well-known professional third-party online survey platform called Sojump in China (www.wjx.cn), to collected data from AI-based voice assistants (e.g., Apple Siri, Xiaomi Xiaoai, or Huawei Xiaoyi) users who utilised the technology to support their daily life (L. Zhang et al., 2022). Sojump has been widely employed by IS researchers to find a representative population in China, particularly when specific participant characteristics are required (Y. Huang et al., 2020; L. Wang et al., 2022). In particular, Sojump could allocate survey respondents randomly and trace participants by their account (registered with personal contact information) on the platform, which is sufficient for a longitudinal study (Lowry et al., 2016).

As shown in Figure 4, we collected data through a longitudinal online survey following a two-stage approach. In the first stage, we conducted an online survey to measure the constructs of technology affordances, trust, privacy concerns, and use frequency. A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data and was randomly distributed by Sojump to their eligible panel participants (i.e., actual users of AIbased voice assistants) in November of 2022. The questionnaire has three portions. In the first section, we briefly stated the privacy policy of the study, to assure the confidentiality of the participants. We then asked the participants to recall their past usage experience and envision themselves utilising the AI-based voice assistants within a habitual private context. In the second section, we collected data for the core constructs (i.e., technology affordances, trust, privacy concerns, and use frequency) in our research model. In the third section, we asked demographic questions about gender, age, and education. At the end of the questionnaire, participants who successfully finished the survey were compensated with red envelopes based on the monetary incentive mechanism of Sojump. Particularly, to encourage them to persistently participate in the longitudinal study, we also informed them that they have the opportunity to receive three times of rewards if they could keep participating in the second stage of data collection.

A week after stage 1 (Venkatesh et al., 2011), we contacted the previous participants who joined the first stage of the survey to measure post-adoption usage behaviours (i.e., routine use and extended use) (see Figure 4). First, we asked participants to complete questionnaires about their routine use behaviours. Second, we conducted four rounds of weekly surveys<sup>4</sup> within a specific time frame of a month, which allowed us to capture a range of extended usage activities being performed based on routine usage (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018; Hsieh & Wang, 2007). We asked participants to record their emerging use cases and return those records through the Sojump platform on a weekly basis. At the end of stage 2, we provided the promised monetary rewards to the participants who successfully finished all four rounds of investigation.

We sent out 1000 questionnaires and received 847 feedbacks in the first data collection stage. We filtered the questionnaires according to the following rules: 1) dropping samples who have no experience with AI-based voice assistants; 2) deleting samples with straight liners; 3) deleting samples that fail to answer the trap question (e.g., “Please answer ‘Agree’ to this question”). We finally got 719 valid samples for data analysis. In the second data collection stage, we sent messages to the 719 respondents through the personal contact information on the survey platform to invite them to the second round of the investigation. Participants who did not complete the questionnaire on routine use and who failed to record extended use behaviours during the one-month time frame were excluded from the dataset. Finally, we received 433 usable responses in the two-stage data collection process.

We illustrated the demographics of the 433 participants in Table 2. The sample is evenly distributed between men and women, and Huawei’s Xiaoyi and Apple’s Siri are the two most popularly used AI-based voice assistants. In terms of use frequency, 41.3% of individuals use AI-based voice assistants less than once or once a week, while 58.7% of individuals use AI-based voice assistants more than once a week.

## 3.3.2. Instrument design

We referred to the extant literature to design the instrument, and all items of the exogenous constructs and routine use were measured by a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “1=strongly disagree” to “5= strongly agree”. Table 3 describes the definitions and references of the constructs, and detailed items are presented in Appendix C.

3.3.2.1. Afordances, cognitive beliefs, and use frequency. According to the quantitative analysis results, anthropomorphism affordance was comprised of two dimensions of conversational cognition affordance and conversational humour affordance. Previous studies mostly focused on the single dimension of cognition affordance, while ignoring the multifaceted and multidimensional feature of anthropomorphic design (Li & Sung, 2021). Recent studies on AI-based voice assistants have particularly underscored the significance of humour and cognitive intelligence (i.e., voice assistants can think, learn and communicate with users in an understandable manner) as two pivotal elements in shaping users’ perceptions of anthropomorphism (J. Kim & Im, 2023; Moussawi & Benbunan-Fich, 2021; Xie et al., 2023). Thus, we incorporated conversational humour and conversational cognition as two salient dimensions of anthropomorphism in the context of AI-based voice assistants. Additionally, in line with the established criteria for discerning formative and reflective constructs (Jarvis et al., 2003), we conceptualised anthropomorphism affordance as a reflective second-order construct for two reasons. First, conversational cognition affordance and conversational humour affordance alone can create anthropomorphism affordance and people do not need to experience the presence of all the dimensions to perceive the anthropomorphism affordance, pointing to their reflective specification (D’Arcy et al., 2014). Second, the correlation between the two dimensions of conversational cognition affordance and conversational humour affordance is high (around 0.5), suggesting reflective specification is more appropriate since all first-order factors measure the same phenomenon. Thus, we operationalise anthropomorphism affordance as a - reflective second-order construct in the context of AIbased voice assistants. To measure the two dimensions of anthropomorphism affordance, we adapted scales from Moussawi and Benbunan-Fich (2021) and Tay et al. (2016). Similarly, based on the criteria indicated by the scale of interactivity (Fan et al., 2017), we modelled interactivity as a reflective second-order construct, with first-order reflective items measuring control affordance, responsiveness affordance, and two-way communication affordance. We also verified this appropriately by confirming the criteria for reflective specification (D’Arcy et al., 2014). Personalisation affordance is operationalised as a reflective first-order construct based upon Kalyanaraman and Sundar (2006)’s and Rhee and Choi (2020)’s studies. The scales for trust and privacy concerns were adapted from Gefen (2002) and Vimalkumar et al. (2021), respectively. Use frequency was measured by a single item, with the anchors of a 5-point scale ranging from “less than once a week” to “several times a day”.

3.3.2.2. Routine use and extended use. We adopted scales from M. Huang et al. (2018) and Li et al. (2013) to measure routine use. We recorded each participant’s regular daily usage behaviours by asking three self-reported items, with the anchors of a 5-point scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” and one open-ended question: “What functions did you use most frequently in the past?”.<sup>5</sup>

According to Hsieh et al. (2011), extended use is defined as the exploration of new functionalities to support goal-oriented tasks after routine usage. As such, extended use should be measured against a specific time frame after routine use. Following the previous literature (Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Schwarz, 2003), we operationalised extended use as a monthly usage behaviour and coded it as a binary variable (0: without extended use; 1: with extended use). Specifically, we asked participants to record their usage of AI-based voice assistants by asking the following question each week: “Over the past week, did you use new functions beyond the functions frequently used before? If yes, what new functions did you use?”.<sup>6</sup> We recorded the emerged new functions in four rounds (i.e., once a week) across a one-month time frame to measure their extended use. If new functions were identified compared with the routine use, we labelled the participant’s extended use behaviour as “1”, otherwise it is marked as “0”. For example, one user mentioned that she usually used the assistants to play music, ask about the weather and time, set a countdown, and so on for routine use; while in the one-month period, the user mentioned no new use cases compared with routine use. Thus, we marked the user’s extended use as 0. Meanwhile, another user listed that his/her routine use covers searching apps and contacting and noting down to-do lists through the AI-based voice assistants; while in the one-month records, the user mentioned a new use case of ordering a food delivery through the assistants; thus, we marked the user’s extended use as 1. The questionnaire was administered in Chinese. Since the original scales for the constructs were written in English, we performed a back-translation process to maintain the accuracy of the Chinese version scales. Specifically, a senior IS professor translated the measures into Chinese firstly, and then two IS doctoral students back-translated the Chinese items into English. We compared and carefully checked the back-translated and original versions of the scales to ensure consistency (Brislin, 1970). We conducted a pilot study to examine the reliability and validity of the measurements before the final data collection. We distributed the original questionnaire to 50 college students who had used AI-based voice assistants for a period of time. Statistical analysis suggests good reliability of our scale. We made minor revisions on three items of interactivity affordance and two items of personalisation affordance, according to the feedback from the respondents, to better reflect the measured constructs.

## 3.3.3. Common method bias analysis

Given that data were self-reported by the respondents in the survey, common method bias (CMB) may be a potential threat (L. Zhang et al., 2021). We adopted two commonly used methods to assess the CMB issue: the Harman single-factor test (Podsakoff et al., 2003) and the marker variable method (Rönkkö & Ylitalo, 2011). First, we performed the Harman single-factor test by including all items of the major constructs. Statistical results indicated that the first factor explained 36.16% variance, which is below the 50% criteria. Second, we employed fashion consciousness as a marker variable that is theoretically unrelated to the constructs in our research model and compared the baseline model with the model including the marker variable (Rönkkö & Ylitalo, 2011). The results indicated that the marker variable had no significant effect on the endogenous variables, and there existed no significant differences regarding the path coefficients between the two models (see Appendix D). Overall, the results suggested that CMB is not a serious concern in our study.

Factor Analysis (CFA) approach and then estimated the structural model using the bootstrapping method (5000 repetitions, 95% bias-corrected CIs).

## 4. Discussion and implications

## 4.1. Discussion of findings

Following a mixed-methods approach with two sequential studies, we investigate the salient technology functions and cognitive beliefs that affect postadoption usage of AI-based voice assistants. To address the research question, we perform a qualitative study using interviews and propose three context-specific technology feature-enabled affordances and two cognitive beliefs. Based upon technology affordance theory and the evidence from our qualitative study, we develop a research model and conduct a longitudinal quantitative investigation to examine the hypothesised research model. The remainder of this section discusses the comprehensive result findings from the combination of qualitative and quantitative studies (also called meta-inferences, see Appendix F), and additional explanations (Venkatesh et al., 2013).

Overall, the results indicate that three technology affordances, namely anthropomorphism affordance, interactivity affordance, and personalisation affordance, play significant roles in influencing users’ trust, while interactivity affordance is identified as the most important factor. Moreover, aligned with the personalisation-privacy paradox, personalisation affordance is positively associated with privacy concerns. Additionally, our study highlights the significant effect of user trust on post-adoption usage behaviours, which is consistent with previous studies (e.g., Hasan et al. (2021)). Notably, our result suggests that trust is a key determinant of extended use in addition to routine use, thereby extending previous works $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ Malodia et al. (2022); Calahorra-Candao and Hoyos (2024)) and enriching our understanding of trust in facilitating the effective utilisation of AIbased voice assistants.

Although our qualitative data suggests the important role of privacy concerns as a negative cognitive belief in affecting post-adoption usage behaviours, the quantitative data fails to support the specific hypothesis. This finding echoes the empirical evidence in the extant smart technology studies (Cheng et al., 2022; K. Liu & Tao, 2022). One plausible explanation is that users pay more attention to the benefits rather than potential privacy risks when utilising AI-based voice assistants (Cheng et al., 2022; Wunderlich et al., 2019). To make sense of these results, we revisit the qualitative data and find that some interviewees indicate that they indeed have privacy concerns when using AIbased voice assistants, but they would weigh against the benefits and costs based on the trade-off process and the effects of privacy violation seem less important than the potential benefits that AI assistants can offer.

Furthermore, the research findings indicate that use frequency has significant moderating effects between technology affordances and cognitive beliefs, and the findings are consistent across both qualitative and quantitative studies. Specifically, we find that anthropomorphism affordance exhibits a stronger influence on trust for users with lower frequency, while personalisation affordance has stronger influences on trust and privacy concerns for users with higher frequency. Therefore, compared with higher frequency users, lower frequency users may be more predisposed to form trust beliefs when anthropomorphism affordance perceptions increase. In contrast, when novices transit into veterans through repeated interactions with AI-based voice assistants, they are more likely to generate trust beliefs and privacy concerns when personalisation affordance perception increases. As such, our study provides a comprehensive understanding of users’ behaviours in the context of AIbased voice assistants by identifying use frequency as a contingency factor that aligns with users’ preferences towards specific technology functions.

## 4.2. Theoretical implications

Our study makes four main contributions to the existing body of knowledge on AI-based voice assistants. First, we revisit and reconceptualise IS usage behaviours in the post-adoption stage and move beyond their present dominant use to the novel phenomenon of AI-based voice assistants. Although IS use is one of the most critical metrics for gauging the effectiveness of AI-based voice assistants’ implementation, prior studies mostly shed light on users’ initial adoption of AI-based voice assistants (Fernandes & Oliveira, 2021; Moussawi et al., 2021; Song et al., 2022), rather than the spectrum of post-adoption usage. To our best knowledge, this is one of the first studies focusing on post-adoption usage when AI-based voice assistants are integrated into users’ daily tasks, enabling them to deeply and comprehensively use the functions of AIbased voice assistants to increase convenience and productivity. Specifically, we propose two salient but distinct post-adoption usage behaviours, i.e., routine use and extended use, that coexist in the post-adoption phase of AI-based voice assistants. Furthermore, we find that routine use positively affects extended use, suggesting that users’ regular utilisation of AI-based voice assistants is beneficial to fostering their explorations of new technology functions. Therefore, our research extends previous literature on post-adoption usage by identifying the juxtaposition of routine use and extended use in leveraging AI-based voice assistants, and uncovering the causal chain of these two distinct usage behaviours. Moreover, by examining users’ post-adoption behaviours under the functioncentric paradigm over time, our research can provide a comprehensive understanding of why different users evolve in different levels of function use. Additionally, our study can offer insights into the broader field of human-machine interactions, such as chatbots, robots and AI avatars, all of which are experiencing rapid expansion of functions and features with the development of AI technology.

Second, the research findings provide a comprehensive understanding of technology affordance and enrich the affordance literature in the emerging AI-based voice assistants context. Although prior literature has broadly discussed the technology affordances of AI-based voice assistants (Bawack, 2021; Knote et al., 2021; Mols et al., 2022), few studies have performed quantitative analysis to validate the impacts of technology affordances on post-adoption usage behaviours. We conceptualise three salient technology affordances of AI-based voice assistants, namely anthropomorphism, interactivity, and personalisation affordances, and uncover their influence mechanisms on post-adoption usage behaviours. Particularly, this study contributes theoretically as well as methodologically in defining and measuring anthropomorphism affordance and interactivity affordance as reflective second-order constructs by capturing the multidimensionality of these constructs. We thus offer a more comprehensive explanation of technology affordances in the specific context of AI-based voice assistants. Taken together, this study fills in a gap within the IS literature by revealing the technology affordances of AI-based voice assistants and empirically examining their significant effects on post-adoption behaviours under the technology affordance framework in the context of AI-based voice assistants.

Third, this study enriches the IS literature by contextualising and extending technology affordance theory, personalisation-privacy paradox, and trust theory to the human-AI interaction domain. By responding to the recent call for explaining cognitive beliefs (positive versus negative) of AI technologies (e.g., Cheng et al. (2022)), we identify two ambivalent constructs, i.e., trust versus privacy concerns, to reflect the overall cog nitive assessments of AI-based voice assistants. Furthermore, we theorise that the presence of trust and privacy concerns are crucial to explicate how and why different technology affordances successfully or unsuccessfully engage users in AI-based voice assistants at the stage of post-adoption usage. We find that anthropomorphism and interactivity affordance significantly enhances users’ trust perceptions; while personalisation affordance facilitates users’ trust perceptions directly on the one hand, and diminishes users’ trust beliefs indirectly by intriguing their privacy concerns on the other hand. Moreover, aligned with previous literature, trust plays a critical role in promoting users’ postadoption usage behaviours towards AI-based voice assistants (Bawack et al., 2021; Chattaraman et al., 2019; Fernandes & Oliveira, 2021). While trust alone is inadequate to explain the complexity of user beliefs towards various technology affordances. Our work signifies the vital role of trust and privacy concerns as two cognitive beliefs, shedding light on how the two contrasting forces simultaneously, yet differentially, shape the nature of post-adoption behaviours in the context of AI-based voice assistants (Moussawi et al., 2021; Prakash & Das, 2021; Vimalkumar et al., 2021). In this sense, our study contributes to the literature by unco vering the duality of cognitive beliefs towards technol ogy affordances (i.e., user trust and privacy concerns) and revealing the nomological network relationship between technology affordances, cognitive beliefs, and subsequent behavioural outcomes in the domain of AIbased voice assistants.

Fourth, we extend the existing affordance theory by incorporating the unique boundary condition of use frequency in the emerging context of AI-based voice assistants, thereby untangling the unexpected relationships in previous literature. Given that users’ perceptions of technology functions change incrementally, use frequency may play a key role in shaping technology affordance perceptions (Vaast et al., 2017). Notably, our study reveals the moderating effect of use frequency between the three technology affordances (i.e., anthropomorphism, interactivity, and personalisation affordances) and cognitive beliefs in the emerging context of AI-based voice assistants. We find significant differences regarding the trust formation mechanisms across users with different use frequency. Particularly, users of higher frequency depend more upon the personalisation services when building trust towards the AI-based voice assistants, while users of lower frequency pay more attention to the humanlike conversation features when forming trust beliefs. Overall, the research findings contribute to the current discourse on how technology affordances shape users cognitive beliefs by considering the contingency influence of use frequency in the context of AI-based voice assistants.

## 4.3. Limitations and direction for future research

This study has limitations that could be addressed in future research directions. First, this study identifies three salient dimensions of technology affordances of AI-based voice assistants, future research can incorporate other salient technology characteristics (e.g., autonomy, emotional connection and so on) that may facilitate users’ effective usage behaviours in different contexts of AI-based applications. Second, future studies could also incorporate other contingency factors (e.g., task type, use context, individual differences, and conversation type) to examine their moderating effects, which could potentially impact the conjectured associations. Third, this study primarily focuses on the post-adoption usage of AI-based voice assistants, while future studies can empirically compare the difference between the adoption and postadoption usage behaviours to obtain a more comprehensive understanding. Last but not least, we conduct an online survey to investigate the effects of functional affordances on user attitudes and behaviours. Future studies can employ an experiment approach and control the usage context (i.e., the specific type of AIbased voice assistants, and the time and place to use it) to further validate the results of this study.

## 4.4. Practical implications

In regard to practical contributions, our study provides managerial guidelines and insights that are of interest to AI-based voice assistant developers. Specifically, our study offers insights into adopting an effective AI-based voice assistant design principle and developing user-centred products. Developers should pay attention to the essential roles of anthropomorphism, interactivity, and personalisation affordances of AI-based voice assistants in facilitating trust formation and post-adoption usage behaviours. First, since the anthropomorphism affordance plays a salient role in affecting users’ trust beliefs, devel opers can design a more fluent and humorous conversation to facilitate users’ communications with AI-based voice assistants. Second, given interactivity affordance is considered the most important trigger of user trust, developers need to create a more userfriendly interactive mode, design a more agile sensor to enhance the response speed and promote the interaction between users and the AI-based voice assistants. Third, personalisation affordance plays a dual role in facilitating user trust and leading to privacy concerns. As such, developers should provide tailored services to users based on their preferences, and improve the interpretability and transparency of the personalisation function to reduce privacy concerns.

Moreover, our findings indicate that AI-based voice assistant developers can provide different strategies to trigger user trust and reduce their privacy concerns based on use frequency. Considering the moderating effect of use frequency, AI-based voice assistant developers can add human-like image features, improve the extent of human-like conversations and enhance the interaction functions to appeal to the low-frequency users’ behavioural intentions towards AI-based voice assistants. With the increase in use frequency, developers should pay more attention to the AI-based voice assistants’ competence by increasing the personalisation services. Despite the benefits of personalisation in enhancing users’ trust beliefs, the potential concern of privacy information leak and abuse would also diminish its effect on users’ post-adoption usage behaviours to a certain degree, especially for higher frequency users. Thus, developers should improve personalisation services and establish strict information security rules simultaneously.

## 4.5. Conclusion

Despite the considerable potential for utilisation offered by AI-based voice assistants, their postadoption usage remains limited, necessitating a deeper comprehension of this phenomenon. Thus, following a sequential mixed-methods approach, we conduct a qualitative study by interviewing 30 users to identify the salient factors and uncover their nomological relationships under the technology affordance framework; we then employ a quantitative study using two-stage longitudinal survey data from 433 samples to empirically test the impact of technology affordances on users’ post-adoption usage behaviours, revealing the intermediate cognitive beliefs and the boundary conditions (i.e., use frequency) that affect this relationship. We find that three technology affordances – anthropomorphism, interactivity, and personalisation – significantly facilitate users’ post-adoption usage behaviours by enhancing trust beliefs, whereas personalisation affordance triggers privacy concerns that inhibit users’ post-adoption usage behaviours by diminishing trust evaluations. Use frequency positively moderates the impact of personalisation affordance on user trust and privacy concerns, while suppressing the effect of anthropomorphism affordance on user trust. The results also reveal that postadoption usage of AI-based voice assistants is composed of routine use and extended use, with routine use acting as a basis for extended use. Our theoretical framework and empirical findings can establish a groundwork for future research on AI-based voice assistant implementation and usage from a technology affordance perspective.
