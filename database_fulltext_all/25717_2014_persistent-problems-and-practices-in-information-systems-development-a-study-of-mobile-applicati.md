# Persistent problems and practices in information systems development: a study of mobile applications development and distribution

Birgitta Bergvall-Kåreborn\* & Debra Howcroft\*<sup>,†</sup>

\*Social Informatics, Luleå University of Technology, Luleå, Sweden, email: Birgitta.Bergvall-Kareborn@ltu.se, and <sup>†</sup>Manchester Business School, The University of Manchester, Manchester, UK, email: Debra.howcroft@mbs.ac.uk

Abstract. The widespread uptake of mobile technologies has witnessed a re-structuring of the mobile market with major shifts in the predominance of particular <sup>fi</sup>rms and the emergence of new business models. These sociotechnical trends are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the ways that they are in<sup>fl</sup>uencing and shaping the working lives of software professionals. Building on prior research investigating the persistent problems and practices of systems development, this paper examines mobile applications development and distribution. A qualitative study of 60 developers based in Sweden, the UK and the USA was analysed around the interrelated problems of diversity, knowledge and structure. The analysis revealed how platform-based development in an evolving mobile market represents signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes at the business environment level. These changes ripple through and accentuate ongoing trends and developments, intensifying the persistent problems and challenges facing software developers.

Keywords: Apple, Google, systems developers, crowdsourcing, platform, ecosystem

## INTRODUCTION

In 2007, the Information Systems Journal published a paper that highlighted persistent problems and practices within information systems development (ISD), calling for more research to help better understand contemporary practices (Kautz et al., 2007). Since 2007, mobile applications development and distribution (MADD) has emerged as an area of mounting importance with rapid rates of adoption of mobile computing (Sørensen, 2011; Tilson et al., 2012). The combination of product innovation and device convergence, along with digita content and services, has been complemented by the rising importance of proprietary platforms and ecosystems (Cusumano, 2010b). The increasing success of MADD platforms has been documented in both the popular press and academic literature, suggesting that platforms are emerging as a dominant model for software development (Tiwana et al., 2010).

The launch of the iPhone in 2007 catalysed a shift in the architecture of the mobile phone industry (Kenney & Pon. 2011). based on opening up the market for mobile applications to third party developers, along with the provision of internet access on a hand-held device. Apple’s success within a short period was quickly followed by Google who launched a comparable dig ital platform available across multiple devices. As new entrants, Apple and Google’s arrival into the mobile market contributed towards the emergence of a new business model centred on platform-based ecosystems that function like markets and are designed to coordinate supply and demand (Tilson et al., 2010). This form of open innovation is seen to be particularly advantageous when technology has not yet achieved stabilisation or saturation, because it enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to take advantage of a far broader spectrum of knowledge and creativity. However, this is far from straightforward, and maintaining platform control is perceived as critical to business success (Cusumano, 2010b).

Despite the increasing signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of platforms, there is limited research on the developers’ perspective of the problems and practices associated with this environment. Research tends to focus on de<sup>fi</sup>ning and categorising the platform phenomena (Bosch, 2009; Tiwana et al., 2010; Boudreau, 2012), identifying and comparing business models (Boudreau & Lakhani, 2009; Kenney & Pon, 2011) or investigating how best to manage these platforms (Roberson & Ulrich, 1998; Cusumano, 2010b). This should not be surprising given the tendency to focus on the apparent newness and unique features of emerging innovations, disregarding fundamental concerns and challenges (Kautz et al., 2007). Therefore, in the context of MADD and in response to the call (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013) for further research on third-party developers working on platforms, this paper poses the following research question: what are the persistent problems and practices for external developers participating on platform ecosystems, such as MADD?

The paper proceeds with a discussion of platforms and external developers, focussing on Apple and Google, given their prominent position as market leaders (Distimo, 2012). This follows with an explication of the research methods before presenting the qualitative analysis from the <sup>fi</sup>eldwork carried out among 60 mobile application developers. The data analysis is based on Kautz et al.’s (2007) framework and is focussed on diversity, knowledge and structure. These themes are further divided into distinct contextual levels that provide a layered understanding of how trends in the business environment ripple through. The analysis revealed how platform-based development represents considerable changes at the business environment level. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this is then discussed in terms of power asymmetries between the platform owners and the developers themselves, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the appeal of MADD in the context of the challenges of working in the information technology (IT) labour market.

## PLATFORMS AND EXTERNAL DEVELOPERS

Central to the argument presented by Kautz et al. (2007) is the ways in which the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has undergone numerous trends and developments, many of which were initially perceived to be transformatory. One recent trend is that of platforms that utilise third-party developers to provide digital content (Yoo et al., 2010) and whose participation has risen so signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly that their supply of apps has been described as ‘business as normal’ (Boudreau, 2012). While platforms can be found in various markets, they share a common architecture based on reuse of core components to achieve economies of scale and create a wide variety of complementary components (Baldwin & Woodard, 2009). This modular architecture symbolises an ideal type of generativity through the ‘overall capability to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied and uncoordinated audiences’ (Zittrain, 2006). With emerging digital innovations, there is a tendency to focus on the more technical aspects, as is the case with research on platforms. Others take a more sociotechnical perspective viewing both platforms and ecosystems as a form of ‘web of computing’ (Kling & Scacchi, 1982), which extend the focus from hardware, software and services to include actors, connections between actors, the transactions along these connections and processes of use (Robertson & Ulrich, 1998; Bosch, 2009; Gawer & Cusumano, 2002; Kenney & Pon, 2011).

## Open and closed platforms, collaborative communities and competitive markets

Increasing attention is being paid to understanding the dynamics of digital platforms, given the interdependence between actors and uncertainty surrounding the future trajectory of the mobile industry (Kenney & Pon, 2011). This is often centred around the concept of open innovation (Chesborough, 2006) and whether an open or closed model is preferable (see, e.g. Boudreau & Lakhani, 2009) as it determines the levels of control of technological development and revenue streams and the nature of the relationship with the customer. The interconnections between primary and complementary <sup>fi</sup>rms can enable the former to establish a leadership position as a seemingly limitless supply of digital content strengthens their market share. The opening up of platforms to large numbers of developers can stimulate network effects, whereby value increases geometrically as additional products and services attract more users (Cusumano, 2010a). Therefore, a key theme in platform research is how best to draw in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant numbers of apps developers to extend the installed base of the platform, generate network effects and further expand the market. Although generativity is possible within the boundary of a single <sup>fi</sup>rm, the chances of succeeding are limited because of economic, structural and institutional constraints As shown by Boudreau (2012) in his analysis of personal digital assistant (PDA) developers, enhancing the numbers of developers does not simply increase the innovation level but qualita tively transforms the nature and sources of innovation, thereby enhancing value.

With these debates as the backdrop, platform business models have been categorised into three distinct types: integrator, product and two-sided (Boudreau & Lakhani, 2009). Based on this, Apple and Google can be seen as integrators, whereby the platform is located between external developers and customers. Given the potential rewards associated with tapping into external expertise, a question remains as to whether the business model should operate as a collaborative community or competitive market (Boudreau & Lakhani, 2009). According to this distinction, Apple is placed within the competitive markets category, while Google operates as a collaborative community. While the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of third-party development became visible through open source software, some <sup>fi</sup>rms are moving from a collaborative model towards the competitive market or private investment model (or a combination) (von Hippel & von Krogh, 2003), with the free and open source software foundation representing an ideal type of the former and with the Apple platform as the archetype of the latter. While divergences exist, nevertheless, it has been argued that the architecture of many platforms is fundamentally similar (Baldwin & Woodard, 2009): The platform owner exercises control by constructing the context for the development process and taking charge of customer transactions.

## Apple and Google: value capture and creation

Platform taxonomies and debates concerning models of innovation are useful on one level, but a broader understanding can be gained from recognising that the challenge for platform owners goes beyond value creation and extends to capturing value as well (Pisano & Teece, 2007: 278). Given the competitive landscape of the mobile industry has not yet achieved stabilisation, <sup>fi</sup>rms such as Apple and Google, who were established market leaders in the IT industry at the point of entering the smartphone market, are continuing to shape this emerging value chain con<sup>fi</sup>guration and maximise their capture of the value created (Kenney & Pon, 2011). There are numerous reasons why <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt an ecosystem (Bosch, 2009), and having some appreciation of how Apple and Google create and capture value in this emerging arena is crucial to our understanding of the environment that encompasses developer’s practices and experiences.

The Apple business model is based on the interconnection of hardware and software, embedding high-end commodities into a centralised ecosystem that links artefacts with an electronic marketplace offering digital content and applications. Such is the success of this combination of products, platform, digital content and services that the <sup>fi</sup>nancial pro<sup>fi</sup>le of Apple has been compared to ‘a phoenix rising from the ashes’ (Haslam et al., 2013: 64). For over three decades, Apple has used end-to-end systems design to create and capture value, and the iPhone re<sup>fl</sup>ects such an approach (West & Mace, 2010). Rather than simply trading devices as products, Apple leveraged its integrated systems capability to solidify value creation as it entered the more contested market of mobile telephony. The strength of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s systems integration is crucial to its ability to pro<sup>fi</sup>t from innovation when there are high levels of modularization and external sourcing, both of which require integration (Pisano & Teece, 2007). Building on an existing knowledge base of hardware, software and systems design, while using the iPod ecosystem, Apple followed a platform tipping strategy across markets from digital media player to smartphones (Cusumano & Gawer, 2002). They created a platform suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently attractive to draw in substantial numbers of app providers, whilst maintaining control and extracting a rent of 30%, which in January 2013 had generated revenues of around \$3bn for Apple (Apple, 2013).

Google’s primary business activity is that of a service provider with revenues generated from search advertising that is delivered from the cloud. Google monetizes its leadership position by capturing value from ensuring targeted advertising is available on as many mobile devices as possible (Cusumano, 2010b). In recognition of the potential uptake of mobile services, Google acquired an operating system (Android) and set up an industry consortium of around 50 technology and mobile <sup>fi</sup>rms (Grotnes, 2009). This business association enabled weaker handset makers who lacked software capabilities to enhance their market proposition by adopting the royalty-free Android. Android is not a direct revenue-generator but a means for device manufac turers to design smartphones quickly and cheaply while extending the reach of Android. Given that Google champion open systems, the SDK and APIs are available to anyone who wishes to make use of them.

Google provide Android as a low-cost operating system because value capture is generated via search-led advertising, hence, the desire to encourage multiple handset providers and apps developers. The recent collaboration between Facebook and Android indicates the importance of strengthening multiple entrants. To encourage apps developers to join the platform, Google have opted to build market share and take advantage of network effects rather than extract rents from developers. Similarly, Google offers little support and exerts little control over the apps community with minimal control of review and distribution. From Google’s perspective, their market presence is also about ensuring that their market share will inhibit competitors from switching their default search engine (Kenney & Pon, 2011). In this respect, Android serves as a block on Apple and their ability to absorb the entire mobile internet space, using the platform as an auxiliary to a different value capture strategy than has been traditionally understood in the platform literature.

While Google’s mobile strategy differs substantially from that of Apple, fundamental to both <sup>fi</sup>rms is their ability to maximise value (Cusumano, 2010b). With many <sup>fi</sup>rms a strong ‘appropriability regime’, with high levels of protection afforded by intellectual property is often assumed to offer the best position (Pisano & Teece, 2007). Yet paradoxically, innovators sometimes bene<sup>fi</sup>t from weakening the intellectual property environment and opening up the architecture of the industry, pushing digital innovation into the public domain. The complexity of these strategic decisions cannot be captured in a simple dichotomy of either open vs. closed or collaborative communities vs. competitive markets, because these disparate approaches re<sup>fl</sup>ect how operating system competitors create and capture value and depend on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics of their business model. At different points of the value chain, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts may be gained from open, closed or a combination of approaches. Apple has a strong appropriability regime, and they consistently look to strengthen it as they collect rents from device sales and apps. By contrast, a weak appropriability regime using open source software, such as Android can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial as Google has strong upstream assets (the monetization of advertising), and so, the locus of value capture shifts upwards. An added bonus is that weak appropriability harms Google’s competitors (such as Apple) because it attracts developers to a competing platform. Understanding how these dynamics operate is crucial to our appreciation of what shapes the practices of platform developers.

## Platform-based software development: democratising innovation?

There is limited research on platform-based software development, yet the transition within the software industry from a product-based approach to ecosystems is affecting many organisational functions, particularly software engineering (Bosch, 2009). Yoo et al. (2010: 733) note how clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned roles traditionally associated with system development have be come blurred, replaced by ‘heterogeneous <sup>fi</sup>rms pursuing con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals, participating in multiple design hierarchies, and intertwining a range of innovation trajectories’. Platforms leverage the expertise of a diverse developer community in anticipation that their insights will creatively develop new capabilities unforeseen in the platform’s original design (Tiwana et al., 2010). Therefore, when constructing ecosystems, the focus of the platform owner shifts from in-house development to providing adequate resources to encourage developers to participate. Ecosystems represent a decentralised approach to systems development with coordination being achieved through the architecture rather than developmental processes (Bosch, 2009). As increasing numbers of developers join the platform, the network periphery continually extends, amplifying the challenge of managing, coordinating and connecting resources (Yoo et al., 2010). Yet, this heterogeneity is coupled with strong integration that knits together the various elements and connections on the platform. Platform boundary resources are the main junction points that interface between the platform owner and external parties: How these platforms are governed concerns the navigation of decision-making authority between the platform owner and external developers (Tiwana et al., 2010). Achieving an equilibrium that maintains platform control while encouraging third-party developers to supply apps has been described as a ‘delicate tension (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013), which is pivotal to issues of power and autonomy (Baldwin & Woodard, 2009) and may involve resistance from developers (Eaton et al., 2013).

In opening up the platform, Apple and Google have outsourced mobile applications development to a global base of external developers, thereby minimising their own risk, while developers create applications that may or may not be successful. This sourcing strategy can be seen as a form of ‘crowdsourcing’ (Brabham, 2008), which occurs when internet technology is used to leverage the crowd to contribute labour that could alternatively be performed by employees or contractors. The term was coined in 2006 (Howe, 2006) and has since matured and evolved in numerous directions, which accounts for the confusing message regarding what precisely constitutes crowdsourcing (Brabham, 2013). Consequently, crowdsourcing can take many forms, including competitions, posting challenges and recruiting labour for one-off tasks (Poetz & Schreier, 2012). The latter closely relates to Apple and Google who leverage an open call over the internet to members of their platform to supply digital content. This can be viewed as an extension to outsourcing in that rather than simply sourcing expertise from low-cost locations, companies use distributed labour networks to source labour power via the internet, harnessing expertise on an unprecedented scale (Tiwana et al., 2010). Ordinarily, crowdsourcing labour is not free, although the costs are substantially less than those associated with more traditional forms of employment (Kleeman et al., 2008), yet in the case of Apple and Google, no direct remuneration is provided for the development and supply of mobile applications.

The increased uptake of crowdsourcing seemingly provides support for the claim that the uniqueness of digital technology has democratised innovation and enabled many to participate (von Hippel, 2005; Yoo et al., 2010). This sentiment is echoed in research on boundary resources, which may ‘empower the community of third-party developers and their possibility to extend the platform with applications' (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson. 2013: 5). Similarly platform-based development is depicted as ‘inclusive’ based on low-cost, facilitated development, reduced coordination costs, which enable experimentation using reuse and ease of access and entry (Boudreau, 2012). With these claims in mind, we utilise qualitative techniques to examine the practices of external developers participating on platforms.

## RESEARCH APPROACH

## Research design

In January 2009, we undertook a study of developers’ experiences of the under-explored area of MADD. While qualitative research methods were used, the epistemological assumptions are more broadly interpretivist (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991) as we attempt to understand phenomena through the meanings that people assign to them as a result of human sense-making activities The study was exploratory and was carried out among developers based in Sweden, the UK and the USA, because these geographies have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant levels of maturity in terms of handsets and the mobile application marketplace. We adopted an organic sampling practice that was strategic (Mason, 2006) in that we aimed to encapsulate a relevant and meaningful range of development contexts, experiences and perspectives that would enable us to develop key understandings and explanations about this area. The focus was on appreciating the various perspectives of those creating the artefact rather than the evolution of the artefact through its developmental stages. We aimed to include developers involved in a range of different activities, with varying levels of experience and with a variety of contracts and employment statuses. We did not aim to capture experiences of top ranked companies and developers because they represent such a limited number of the total community and would likely skew the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. We opted not to study one organisation, and in this respect, the research is neither organisationally nor spatially bounded. While not intending to imply that the participants are representational, of either the general population of developers or the MADD community, the sample displays characteristics that are similar to the wider population of software development

## Data collection

A pilot study involved two face-to-face focus groups, each consisting of six Android/iPhone developers. This provided a vantage point from which to launch the broader study, feeding into the development of a semi-structured interview guide. The recruitment process primarily involved contact through various social media channels. The IT industry is notorious for the under-representation of women (Adam et al., 2006), and so, effort was placed into recruiting developers from the social network ‘Girl Geeks’. We experimented with a mixture of face-to-face, co-located interviews, synchronous Skype interviews and asynchronous online discussion forums (depending on preferences and geographical location; Table 1). Each interview lasted between 1 and 2 h; all were taped and transcribed verbatim. Interviewers worked in pairs, often exchanging the lead role as topics alternated. This generated a lively pace and provided opportunities for a shift in focus, also allowing deeper probing of salient points. The early interviews contained open-ended questions that covered a range of topics including current working practices, reasons for developing for mobile devices, attraction to particular platforms and the development and distribution process. As the research progressed, the later interviews tended to focus on more speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues, such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial matters, social networks, future plans and work–life balance.

Every participant answered each question and often commented on each other’s answers, thereby generating debate in a similar way to a focus group. Overall, 60 developers participated in the study: 55 men and <sup>fi</sup>ve women. Participants were guaranteed anonymity, but many were happy to be personally identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in publications (by <sup>fi</sup>rm or app name) as this was seen to raise awareness of their work. The developers represented a fairly homogenous grouping with extensive coding and platform knowledge. Their publishing experience spanned from one to more than 40 applications, with around half having published between 10 and 40. Most of the developers opted for single platform development to focus their expertise, although some developed across multiple platforms. A minority of developers worked on outsourced applications, whereby a third-party commissioned work, but most ‘owned’ the product they were developing. There were differences in the levels of success and the amount of revenue generated. The developers represented a diversity of employment types including permanent employees, contract workers and entrepreneurs, as well as a couple of people who developed applications as a sideline. Our interviewees often experienced multiple types of employment simultaneously, such as formal employment and freelancing in their leisure time or sub-contracting for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c project. Almost 60% were self-employed, either working as contractors or managing their own start-up. Only a small proportion of participants were formally employed as MADD developers with many developing apps outside the boundaries of the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Complementary to the interviews and forums was the continuous collection of secondary data in the form of press releases, consultancy reports and newspaper articles. This secondary data help to place developers’ experiences in the evolving context of the mobile business environment while considering the perspectives of other stakeholders.

## Data analysis

Data analysis was carried out in parallel with data collection and was facilitated by the qualitative analysis tool, NVivo. The analysis was based on discussion among the two researchers with an ongoing iterative process of re<sup>fl</sup>ection to help identify concepts, themes and issues (Miles & Huberman, 1994). In the initial coding, virtually, every passage of the transcripts was associated with one or more codes of recurring topics. A set of higher-order categories was then developed to aggregate the codes into broader themes. For example, the higher-order code ‘motivations’, consisted of contributions from <sup>fi</sup>rst-order codes such as ‘autonomy’, ‘technical challenge’, ‘generating income’ and ‘recognition’. The initial <sup>fi</sup>ndings and themes were also shared with a sample of participants from the study, and their helpful comments con<sup>fi</sup>rmed and veri<sup>fi</sup>ed these themes, yielding additional insights. The quotes have been selected from the ful range of sites, but the names of participants and their workplaces are not cited.

## RESE A RC H FI NDI NGS A N D AN A LY SIS

The analysis is based on the framework provided by Kautz et al. (2007) in their study of persistent problems and practices in ISD. It is structured around three themes (coping with diversity, knowledge and structure) that enable identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and conceptualisation of problems and practices as they emerge. The themes are further sub-divided into contextual levels (business environment, company, project, team and individual) drawing on the seminal work of Curtis et al. (1988), which facilitates interpretation within the context of external in<sup>fl</sup>uences. The layered model enables consideration of how problems at one level ripple through and how the effects can be interdependent. However, the contextual levels are intended to help understand the problems of designing large software systems, but in the context of MADD, whereby developers may be part of a small start-up company or operate as lone self-emploved. the strict differentiation may be conflated. Therefore. reflective of the environment and to aid clarity, three levels will take prominence in the analysis: (1) business environment, (2) company (including project and team) and (3) individual.

## Coping with diversity

Coping with diversity is an ongoing challenge due to the speed of technological innovation, increased diversity in user needs, application domains, system size and complexity and expansion of use contexts and stakeholder groups. At the business environment level, although trends suggest mass customisation of consumer demands (Bosch, 2009; Boudreau & Lakhani, 2009), currently, there is limited diversity with MADD platforms. The mobile market is dominated by big tech <sup>fi</sup>rms such as Apple and Google, with only a handful of competitors given the barriers to entry. The combination of brand reputation and market reach is working as a clear separator, with platforms proceeding beyond a central tipping point becoming dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to dislodge (Gawner, 2009). From the developers’ perspective, diversity at the business environment level is also minimised as the platform enables the reuse of core components to achieve economies of scale (Baldwin & Woodard, 2009).

Although the number of popular platforms is limited, at the company level, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to remain aware of the evolving commercial and technological landscape. As one developer commented: ‘It is important to look at the bigger picture, consider other platforms and respond to changes in the business environment. This space is evolving and changing rapidly’ (33). In selecting the platform, aligning with a strong commercial player is seen as advantageous given they provide a gateway to a large market of users that are accessible for monetization (Bosch, 2009). The lack of diversity of MADD platforms means reasonably low multi-homing costs, i.e. additional costs developers could incur by associating with several platforms (Tiwana et al., 2010). Regarding platform selection, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings point to pragmatism, because having specialised, the switching costs can be considerable:

The most expensive platform for us is going to be the iPhone. We have four to <sup>fi</sup>ve years of Java development behind us and all the source code needs to be transferred to objective C – a totally different language. (7)

Coping with diversity involves an appreciation of how individual platforms link with numerous de vices. While Apple has closely coupled hardware and software, Android is available across multiple devices. Developing on numerous platforms may enhance market share, yet this adds further complexity:

Screen real estate, processing power and data abilities vary across each mobile device. A solution that works and looks good on an Android phone may not port well to another device. The effort to port these apps to new languages and devices could be as much effort as developing a new application. (41)

The persistent problem of pricing diversity is one of the main problems for external developers and often results in uncertain revenue streams. A prominent example concerns paid and free applications, because the latter is often adopted to entice potential customers. Deciding at which point in the lifecycle to switch from free to paid applications is complex as free ‘light’ versions tend to have a positive impact on downloads. While developers are acutely aware of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and drawbacks of various combinations as well as the market-driven pricing levels, nevertheless, we found limited evidence to suggest a correlation of the investment of time and resources with future revenue streams. While there is nothing new in the challenge of accurately estimating costs and resources (Curtis et al., 1988) in the context of MADD, the challenges are heightened given the uncertain market. As one developer noted: ‘the price has a relatively limited span despite the quality of the app and therefore the development costs need to be adapted to this’ (13). Revenue generation pressures are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in <sup>fi</sup>gures, which suggest developers receive an average of 17.5 cents per app download, which includes all in-app purchases and revenues (Distimo, 2012).

For individual developers, contract work on short-term projects may increase diversity. Given the emergent nature of the industry, projects are subject to improvisation, often coming together organically and bringing together differing skill sets, such as graphic design, computer vision techniques and audio technicians. As Curtis et al. (1988) note, many software projects evolve to encompass a widening coalition of skills, because most personnel are knowledgeable in only a couple of areas. In their study, the widening coalition developed within the organisation, whereas with MADD, this often takes the form of sub-contractors, who are temporarily enrolled to cope with diverse projects.

Coping with diversity extends to effectively managing a user base because how apps are received can in<sup>fl</sup>uence sales and reputation. Part of the success of mobile apps can be attributed to their reach into the everyday world of citizens, and so, the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of managing user expectations were noted, particularly regarding paid applications:

If you price it directly without a free version people will actually complain saying ‘I didn’ expect it to be this bad, I expected it to be much better since I paid you one dollar’. (10)

In order to encourage consumer loyalty, one developer maintained a mailing list of more than 10 000 users, informing them of product updates and new releases, while other developers maintain user contact via blogs. A common theme was responding positively to users: ‘I think the only way is to listen to the feedback and really try to deliver exactly what the user wants. You have to keep the updates coming which have noticeable new features’ (32).

To summarise, the persistent problem of coping with diversity permeates different contextua levels, and the amount of variation is wide-ranging. In terms of platforms, there is limited diversity, but for developers who are entering an emerging market with uneven and unpredictable dynamics, the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of developing new products and forecasting product reception means there is little stability regarding revenue and pricing models. Developers respond by cultivating awareness of market and product developments and by managing their user base via wider social media. Although platform choice is limited, it is usually based on what best suits their needs in terms of market coverage, the review process and distribution channel and <sup>fi</sup>ts with their existing skill set.

## Coping with knowledge

Related to diversity are the demands for different kinds of knowledge. At the business environment level, the mobile marketplace requires a knowledge base of developers whose expertise continu ously provides new products and services. Developers were well aware of how their capabilities bene<sup>fi</sup>t platform owners, particularly when a 30% rent is extracted by Apple:

I <sup>fi</sup>nd it very unfair that 30% of my application bill goes to a company that had nothing to do with it. Apple gets the money from the actual hardware and even the software they created. When you develop apps this makes the phone better, so Apple shouldn’t then be making money out of developers as well. (14)

On the company level, the rapid dominance of Apple and Google with similar structures im poses simpler knowledge demands as compared with coping with numerous infrastructures. Yet working within an emerging market is clouded with uncertainty, and this is exacerbated by Apple and Google’s limited communication of their future strategic direction. Apple is renowned for being a closed system, and this was frequently remarked upon: ‘Apple is quite secretive about everything so people don’t usually even speak about the development outside Apple forums’ (60). By contrast, Google aims to symbolise openness, yet many developers were cynical about this:

Google promises that they will release a new version of the operating system and they don’t. They never respond to bug requests. They never respond to anything….When Apple releases new versions of an operating system or <sup>fi</sup>xes a bug they never really tell us what is really going on and what is being <sup>fi</sup>xed. Sometimes they actually <sup>fi</sup>x and change things and break our apps. So that is the real problem – transparency and consistency. (56)

This lack of transparency and communication was remarked upon in numerous quarters as it became dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for <sup>fi</sup>rms to develop their knowledge base and plan for the future:

From our perspective as a company if we spend £10,000 on training someone and then spend another £10,000 paying them to produce the application and then not know when the phone that hosts the app will change, that is a hell of a risk. (60)

At the individual level, coping with knowledge demands is a persistent problem since contin uous competence development is needed. While many participants were technical graduates, working in a sector increasingly dominated by small and medium-sized enterprises (Christopherson, 2004) means that employees are rarely given support for further training and so undertake development either informally or through self-tuition within online forums, with one developer commenting: ‘It’s me and Google’ (12). This points to developers’ awareness that knowledge of new technologies enhances their attractiveness in the job market, yet keeping updated provides additional pressure.

Building informal networks also becomes increasingly important for occupational advice and support. Developers generate work based on recognition from professional communities, and future work become reliant on ‘who you know’, as well as how well-informed they are about past achievements and reputation. Participating in networks is a frequent occurrence, and during our <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, we were struck by the crucial role of networks in the construction of careers. These forums enable developers to cope with unstable labour markets and act as a vehicle for work distribution. Informal networks have long played a role in knowledge development, but previously, these operated within organisational boundaries among IT professionals working in a secure environment (Curtis et al., 1988). Given the increasing predominance of start-ups and small and medium enterprises, these networks operate across organisational boundaries, and knowledge development extends to having an understanding of the labour market. The importance of social networks for recruiting competence regionally also points to a mixed relation of collaboration and competition. Given the same <sup>fi</sup>rms can compete on one project and coexist or collaborate on others (Yoo et al., 2010) is a further example of these transient relations.

To summarise, the persistent problem of coping with knowledge is interrelated to changing career structures in the IT sector, which have helped fuel signi<sup>fi</sup>cant levels of participation in platform-based app development. The shift in labour market structures from large <sup>fi</sup>rms that provide training and support to smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms means that many developers bear responsibility for their own competency and career development. Developers try to counteract the downside of unstable labour markets by becoming involved in social networks to enhance their understanding of the job market, the imminent projects and the skills that are required. This enables them to ‘market’ themselves in anticipation of securing future projects

## Coping with structure

As Kautz et al. (2007) note, structure is traditionally associated with formal project organisation and methodological discipline. However, they recognise that wider social structures shape and structure the process of development in practice. At the business environment level, many platforms build on reinforcing mechanisms culminating in a ‘winner takes all’ position, regardless of technological superiority (Schilling, 2009). Regarding apps, services such as search tools, recommendation systems, online communities and social networks have the ability to support both super star and niche buying behaviour (Brynjolfsson et al., 2010). At present, we are witnessing a clear tendency towards the super star side (Cusumano, 2010b) and clustering with seven applications accounting for 10% of revenue in 2012 (Distimo, 2012). The notion that ‘success breeds further success’ was remarked upon:

The App Store’s top 100 ranking is based on downloads, and once you’re in the top list, you’l stay. If you’re more visible, you’ll get more downloads (46)

Coping with structure concerns issues of control. The Apple and Google platforms shape and steer both the development and the distribution environment. Although developers have intellec tual property rights, Apple and Google concede limited decision rights, which raises interesting questions about decentralisation and autonomy, termed the ‘paradox of control’ (Tilson et al., 2010). This manifests as process control (methods and procedures such as SDKs and APIs) and output control, which governs whether developers’ products will be made available for wide release. Particularly problematic for iPhone developers is output control in the form of the application review process prior to wider distribution. This was succinctly described by one devel oper as ‘a majorly crappy experience’, a comment re<sup>fl</sup>ecting widely shared sentiments. Despite the provision of extensive guidelines, one developer was irritated by the lack of consistency:

Apple does not really have a standard process for application submission. Each time I submit, am paranoid that I might have left in a bad keyword, or used an icon that they don’t like, or have some other small, inane issue that will keep my app from the store for another two weeks. (17)

The <sup>fl</sup>ip side to this is that Google accept all submitted applications, but for some developers, this lack of quality control was viewed negatively: ‘They should have a self-decontamination system so that things that are good get high up the list and things that are bad get further down. As it is now, it feels like a platform ruled by anarchy’ (6). This balance between control and freedom can be compared with those of Atari and Nintendo in the 1980s, whereby Nintendo entered the market with a set of strict policies regulating third-party developers that ultimately led to their market success (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009). These divergent strategies point to the persistent problem of control and different ways of coping with it.

Ostensibly, the platform market functions as a <sup>fl</sup>at structure, but on closer inspection, we note that subtle distinctions are in operation. For example, developers operate in an environment with variable success rates. For the developers in this study, the number of downloads for their most successful application ranged from over 1 000 000 to just over 10 000, but there were examples of zero downloads. Similarly, the amount of revenue generated varied widely. This underlines the highly unpredictable nature of platform-based development where apps suffer from quick rates of obsolescence. The pricing structure is determined by developers, but the market ensures this is subjected to normative controls. For example,

There are clear price boundaries linked to customer expectations. One is between free and 99 cents. For games, this is between three and <sup>fi</sup>ve dollars. Three dollars can be an OK game but <sup>fi</sup>ve dollars needs to be really good. (20)

There are also challenges working within a marketplace, which consists of more than 775 000 apps competing for attention (Apple, 2013): ‘There are so many apps now that’s it hard to be noticed and <sup>fi</sup>nd an idea that hasn’t already been done’ (41). Consequently, the market is characterised by competitive price cutting:

The top 50 list is just <sup>fl</sup>ooded with 99 cent applications. Some individual developers say that those prices are too low to get any money, real money. But that’s not really Apple’s problem – it is the developers’ problem. (48)

These practices structure the market and challenge some of the claims about democratising innovation, certainly from the perspective of long-term sustainability. In the context of the IT sector, at the company level, there is increasing bifurcation and feelings of comparative powerlessness vis-a-vis large <sup>fi</sup>rms is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the following remark:

If some big boy decides to jump in and build the app themselves – such as Google who rolled out Scoreboards, Finance, My Tracks, Listen, and a slew of others, they effectively kill my apps because of the Google brand. I have to think twice if my app will be in danger of com peting with other big companies. (41)

Historically, when working on large software systems, technological obsolescence sometimes occurred because the system took a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of time to complete (Curtis et al., 1988); with MADD, the problem concerns intense competition. Entering new markets can be risky, and companies respond to these unstable structures in various ways. An owner of a start-up described how he speculated with his personal savings: ‘Initially I invested a fairly large chunk of my own money in getting the company going and I probably invested the money in the right places, but it didn’t necessarily take off’ (56). In order to keep a<sup>fl</sup>oat, of<sup>fl</sup>oad some of the risk or scale up the business, examples where provided of <sup>fi</sup>rms seeking venture capital investment. The counterbalancing of up-front investments in anticipation of future rewards is evident in examples where developers sacri<sup>fi</sup>ced their level of pay for the prospect of future returns. The dif<sup>fi</sup>culties facing small <sup>fi</sup>rms became clear as one developer explained that ultimately he hoped that his <sup>fi</sup>rm would be purchased by a larger out<sup>fi</sup>t:

We sort of started out with that very clear vision that we want to build something that either has suf<sup>fi</sup>cient underlying intellectual property or a product or customer stream that makes us interesting to acquire. (50)

Systems methodologies can also impose structure within companies, which permeates the different levels. The analysis highlighted a repertoire of development practices that constitute a fairly holistic process of application creation, development and distribution. Kautz et al. (2007) noted how Web development required an increasingly varied skill set, and our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are compa rable, recruiting specialisations as and when required. Likewise, development practices are often ad hoc and sequential as formalised approaches are inappropriate for dynamic environments such as MADD. Developers improvised according to the contingencies of their environment, and in this respect, development can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as ‘amethodical’ (Truex et al., 2000).

At the individual level, the majority of participants were developing applications by themselves or in a small group. Team-based development tended to be co-located rather than distributed, and communication was predominantly face-to-face rather than virtual. This form of working required versatility on the part of many of the developers who were involved in the totality of the process. The development environment is one of the rapid applications, and time to market is crucial, because ‘<sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage’ can be pivotal to success:

I ended up being 3 or 4 days behind another guy who wrote a similar podcast application. He got a massive amount of downloads compared to me since it was the <sup>fi</sup>rst application with this functionality. (8)

Our empirical research pointed towards uncertain career structures for many individuals, as con<sup>fi</sup>rmed elsewhere in studies of IT workers (SIM, 2006). This means that maximising return on investment is crucial to survival:

The primary concern for me is whether or not the time spent developing the app is worth it in potential revenue. I quit my job to start my own business and so I do not have the liberty of spending all my time developing apps that will not make money. (22)

To summarise, the persistent problems of coping with structure are that crucially Apple and Google de<sup>fi</sup>ne the parameters of MADD as they frame the terms of development and distribution. Their dominant market share means that options are limited for developers wanting to pur sue alternatives. Control is skewed in favour of platform owners: For Apple, this manifests as a ‘walled garden’, whereas for Google, it is based on openness. The value created by the developers is captured by the platform owners in the form of enhancing the demand and use of digita products and services. In response, developers attempt to work their way around restrictions and creatively develop strategies to maximise success.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

The aim of this paper has been to examine MADD, as a contemporary illustration of ISD. This is summarised in Table 2, which encapsulates how persistent problems and practices span across different contextual levels.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings point to commonalities surrounding the persistent problems and practices of developers as highlighted in the study of Web development (Kautz et al., 2007), which suggests that MADD represents a process of ‘accentuated evolution’ rather than presenting changes of a revolutionary nature. Our analysis shows that while challenges may mutate into different forms, their persistence continues at the company level and for individua developers. However, developments within the business environment signify a step-change: The combination of the crowdsourcing business model and the different ways in which value is created and captured by platform owners may be classed as a ‘disruptive technology (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003). This refers to ‘an architectural innovation originating in the information technology base that has subsequent and pervasive and radical impacts on development processes and their outcomes’ (ibid: 557), which goes beyond IT innovations and includes complementary organisational innovations. In this respect, it avoids dichotomous generalisations and points to how some things change, while some remain the same (ibid: 579). This seems applicable to MADD, which builds on the foundations of the internet, modular architecture and service innovation. When combined, these elements create a context of disruptive technology with new platforms shaping the production and distribution process.

Leveraging platform technology for the global sourcing of digital content centrally positions platform owners within a satellite of distributed suppliers. Although platformbased development is perceived as decentralised ( Bosch, 2009), the relationship is unidirectional with glaring power asymmetries as developers have to contend with no/lim ited in<sup>fl</sup>uence over platform owners and their future strategic direction. In this context, decentralisation does not necessarily equate to devolved autonomy; rather, it manifests as a highly centralised core that orchestrates a huge network of peripheral and largely unconnected individuals and small <sup>fi</sup>rms. This contrasts with the literature that suggests that MADD is inclusive (Boudreau, 2012), may empower third-party developers (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013) and has the potential to democratise innovation (von Hippel, 2005; Yoo et al., 2010).

Given the history of participatory design within the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, it is largely assumed that greater inclusion is more democratic. With mobile app development, while the opening up of a previously closed market has extended the network of participants and undoubtedly provided opportunities for some, this is not comparable to democracy in the sense of creating a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently inclusive deliberative process of opinion formation and will formation (Habermas, 2008: 103). Although MADD appears super<sup>fi</sup>cially to represent greater levels of inclusion, this needs to be assessed in the context of how large capitalist enterprises, such as Apple and Google, create and capture value. As the number of participants extends, the more competitive the environment becomes for them, while higher levels of external participation create additional value for platform owners. Rather than frame the process as one that empowers third-party developers, an alternative reading might suggest that opening up boundaries to external parties has consolidated the position of already powerful platform leaders, which is key to their ongoing success.

We recognise that framing MADD as an instantiation of crowdsourcing is contentious, and indeed, crowdsourcing itself is subject to competing de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and interpretations (Brabham, 2013). Although not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally addressed during data collection, given that MADD is operationalised by the use of internet technology as a means to source tasks that would traditionally have been carried out by employees, use of the term crowdsourcing seems appropriate in this context. It is indicative of how the phenomenon of crowdsourcing, which was initially experimental and innovative, has evolved over time and been appropriated as a means of extracting value for a number of industries. In relation to systems development, crowdsourcing follows the demand for the production of lower-cost content as developers assume responsibility for the transformation of resources into innovative products. In this regard, crowdsourcing is not that dissimilar to open source software, which was initially perceived as community-based volunteering and later transformed to become a more mainstream and commercially viable form (Fitzgerald, 2006). For this reason, dichotomising platforms into categorical types, such as open and closed or collaborative community and competitive market (Boudreau & Lakhani, 2009), over simpli<sup>fi</sup>es the evolving complexity of platform developments.

Without doubt, the MADD operations of Apple and Google have provided considerable benefits for some developers. but are these sufficient to explain why external contributions have been sustained on such a scale? An alternative account could point to changing labour markets, whereby the attraction of platform-based development is symptomatic of the challenges of working in the IT industry (Bergvall-Kåreborn & Howcroft, 2013). As Lazonick (2009) points out, the ‘New Economy Business model’ has altered how people are employed and represents a mode of employment characterised by volatile stock markets, unequal incomes, unstable employment, the offshoring of high-skill jobs and insecurity. When faced with a shifting labour market, perhaps developers are more inclined to consider submitting their skills and knowledge to crowdsourcing platforms, when previously they may have been employed on a permanent contract with employment rights and protection. For many creative workers who may be struggling to make a living, the arrival of crowdsourcing offers a chance at entrepreneurship while being described as a continuation of the ‘race to the bottom (Brabham, 2013).

Returning to the persistent problems facing software developers, the changing labour market remains an ongoing challenge. If platform development is emerging as a dominant model (Tiwana et al., 2010), offering no speci<sup>fi</sup>c contract or guarantee of remuneration, then future research that explores IT work as an increasingly precarious and unstable career, far removed from long-term, organizationally based careers, is worthy of investigation. Studies of a longitu dinal nature that follow the life histories or career narratives of individual developers could enable more detailed understanding of the evolution of IT careers in the context of changing markets. This could feed into the development of appropriate educational and training programmes, so that IT workers are better positioned to react to the increasingly diverse demands that are placed upon them.
