# Advancing the assessment of automated deception detection systems: Incorporating base rate and cost into system evaluation

Douglas P. Twitchell | Christie M. Fuller

College of Business and Economics, Boise State University, Boise, Idaho, USA

Douglas P. Twitchell, College of Business and Economics, Boise State University, Boise, ID 83725.

## Abstract

In the last two decades, there has been an increased interest in automated deception detection systems (ADDs) for use in screening, although little attention has been paid to the usefulness of these systems. ADDs use various means, both invasive and non‐invasive, to ascertain individual intent to deceive or engage in malicious behaviour. Many papers introducing ADDs use signal detection theory to compare a technique's ability to detect malicious intent with other techniques, but in doing so, they do not include contextual information such as base rate and cost. In this paper, we aim to improve future research by showing how the inclusion of contextual information provides a more realistic picture of the research. Through both theoretical arguments and a real‐data example, we show that especially for those contexts where malicious intent is infrequent (ie, with low base rates of deception) that not factoring in the base rate overestimates the accuracy and therefore usefulness. We conclude with recommendations for how future research should provide a fuller picture of the accuracy and usefulness of ADDs.

## KEYWORDS

automated deception detection systems, base rate, Bayes' theorem, signal detection theory, usefulness

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

In the last two decades, research in security, analytics and at the intersection of the two has increased, Much of this research focuses on detection. For example, researchers have studied malware detection (Kolter & Maloof, 2004; Souri & Hosseini, 2018), network intrusion detection (Shah, Qian, Kumar, Ali, & Alvi, 2017; Zhu, Premkumar, Zhang, & Chu, 2007), suspicious debit card transactions (Saghehei & Memariani, 2015), insider threat detection (Legg, Buckley, Goldsmith, & Creese, 2017), and phishing detection (Thabtah & Abdelhamid, 2016) using analytics methods. Many of these are low base rate problems, or problems in which the class of interest makes up only a small portion of the overall sample. Among this research, there has been a great deal of interest in automated deception detection systems (ADDs) for use in screening for malicious intent, perhaps influenced by the National Research Council's (NRC) thorough analysis of the polygraph (Fienberg et al., 2003) and three highly cited journal article papers that were published around the same time (DePaulo et al., 2003; Zhou, Burgoon, Nunamaker, & Twitchell, 2004; Zhou, Twitchell Qin, Burgoon, & Nunamaker, 2004) and has continued for more than a decade (eg, Nunamaker, Burgoon, & Giboney, 2016). These systems use behaviour in the form of written language (Zhou, Burgoon, et al., 2004), spoken language (Throckmorton, Mayew, Venkatachalam, & Collins, 2015), body movement (Meservy et al., 2005), eye movement (Cook et al., 2012), and other cues (Warmelink et al., 2011) to determine the veracity of subject statements. The veracity of these statements is then used to screen people for malicious intent in contexts such as airport screening (Burgoon et al., 2009), financial fraud (Throckmorton et al., 2015), and online reviews (Zhang, Zhou, Kehoe, & Kilic, 2016). Over the last 15 years, this body of research has established that ADDs have wide application possibilities. In many cases, especially in the information systems field, the aim of this type of research is to provide ADDs that can be useful in some context. To move this body of research forward, we review how these studies have assessed the potential of thei systems and offer suggestions for how this assessment might be expanded upon in future works

The usefulness of an ADD, as used in this paper, is “the degree to which the ADD can be used advantageously ir the context for which it is proposed.” A useful ADD is not only accurate in that it identifies deception when it is present; it is also accurate in its context in that it identifies deception even when deception is rarely present and does not overwhelm its users with false alarms. A useful ADD also balances costs and benefits. Deployed in the field, usefu ADDs improve the performance of their users by making the job of detection deception easier or more accurate. This usefulness is different than the perceived usefulness described in TAM (Davis, 1989), which focuses on the subjectiv perception of the user. Here, we focus instead on objectively measured benefit as defined by expected utility theory That is, if the benefits of the ADD's correct classifications exceed its costs when the ADD is used in its intended con text, we consider it useful.

Part of our attempt to move ADD research forward by incorporating usefulness includes a probing look at past applied ADD research. Although we point out issues in prior ADD research, especially in its proposed application, we want to emphasize that we believe that the effects this research explores are likely real. Many of the features an combinations of features that ADDs use have been shown to consistently distinguish between truth and deception However, in most cases, ADD research in an applied context has not addressed the proposed ADD's usefulness. In some contexts, this can be easily corrected to provide a more realistic view of the ADD. In other contexts, the abilit of these ADDs to distinguish truth from deception may not be enough to justify their use. In very low base rate situations, the accuracy needed for these tools to have practical application would require order of magnitude improve ments from that reported in the currently available literature. While much of this research has been exploratory and proofs of concept, expanding the measures included when results are reported can give insight into the degree of refinement required to move from proof of concept to a system that can be implemented in the desired context Including base rates and costs early in research, even with rough estimates, will provide researchers and end users with a view of the additional efforts required to make an ADD useful.

In this paper, we aim to set the stage for future studies by following the groundwork laid out by the NRC (Fienberg et al., 2003) to recommend improvements in the assessment of performance and applicability of method used in ADD research, primarily in low base rate contexts. We begin in Section 2 by reviewing signal detection theory (SDT) with an emphasis on how base rates affect accuracy measures. We then review a selection of papers from recent research in ADDs, specifically those papers that apply an ADD in a context with a low base rate. In subsequent sections, we take a closer look at a few of these and demonstrate different metrics that can contribute to the assessment of usefulness in low base rate contexts. We then develop one of them, fraud in crowdfunding into a more thor ough example. Finally, we conclude with a set of recommendations for future ADD research. These recommendations can also be applied to the wider set of security analytics research and other IS analytics studies

## 2 | BACKGROUND

In 2003, the NRC published an evaluation of the use of the polygraph for detection deception (Fienberg et al., 2003). They did their analysis in the context of screening potential employees for government employment in positions wit access to classified information. Through a thorough evaluation of the polygraph literature and the use of SDT, the ultimately concluded that because of the very low base rate of deceivers among potential employees and the hig cost of investigating the large number of false positives, the use of the polygraph was not warranted for screening Since then, research on more automated, less intrusive ADDs, which we review below, has become prevalent. Like the NRC, we discuss deception detection under uncertainty and review research on ADDs with applications in specific contexts. However, rather than focus on one context, we will consider many of the low base rate contexts t which ADDs have been applied.

## 2.1 | Decision‐making under uncertainty

From the point of view of the ADD, automated deception detection is a problem of decision‐making under uncertainty. In other words, the ADD does not have enough information to provide perfect decisions: There will be error. Despite the claims to the contrary, no current detection method has been proven to perfectly distinguish an uttered truth from falsehood (Farwell, Richardson, & Richardson, 2013). Therefore, we need to deal with the inevitable error resulting from making uncertain decisions. ADD researchers have turned mainly to SDT (Biros, George, & Zmud, 2002; Burgoon et al., 2004, 2009; Elaad, 2009; Ho, Hancock, Booth, & Liu, 2016; Meijer, Selle, Elber, & Ben‐Shakhar, 2014; Twyman, Lowry, Burgoon, & Nunamaker, 2014) to help explain the trade‐offs inherent in uncertain decisionmaking. SDT mainly concerns itself with two aspects of decision‐making under uncertainty for comparing different detection mechanisms, the accuracy of the classifier and the decision threshold. The other two aspects, the base rat and cost/benefit, while important for determining the usefulness of a mechanism in a particular context, are not considered central to SDT's focus on classifier comparison (Swets & Pickett, 1982). In the following sections, we discuss all four of these aspects of decision‐making under uncertainty.

## 2.2 | Signal detection theory

SDT (Green & Swets. 1966) is applicable when determining the relative accuracy of systems intended to find some signal among noise in uncertain data, which are commonly presented as classification problems. Although it is possi ble to extend SDT beyond two classes (Fawcett, 2006), for the purposes of this paper, we focus on the case of classifiers with only two possible outcomes. While the SDT measures are widely used. we present a review of the fundamentals here. In SDT, the signal (deception) and the noise (everything else) are measured on some criterion such as, in a simple case, the number of words uttered or, in a more complex case, a linear combination of deception cues The range of this criterion is modelled as two normal distributions with one normal distribution representing noise and the other representing noise plus signal. A threshold placed at some point on the criterion divides one class from another. On each side of the threshold are areas of each distribution that are correctly and incorrectly classified. Th counts corresponding to each of these areas are captured in the confusion matrix, as shown in Table 1. The cells contain the number (or proportion) of cases that are true positives (deceptive cases correctly classified as deceptive) false negatives (deceptive cases incorrectly classified as truthful), false positives (truthful cases incorrectly classifie as deceptive), and true negatives (truthful cases correctly classified as truthful).

The counts or proportions in the confusion matrix are used to calculate accuracy measures including sensitivit (or recall), specificity, false‐positive rate, positive predictive value (PPV; or precision), negative predictive value (NPV) the F measure, and overall accuracy. Each of these measures, which are listed and described in Table 2, provides dif ferent ways to look at the performance of a classifier. A full treatment of each can be found in Powers (2011) and Japkowicz and Shah (2011, chapter 2).

Armistead (2012) notes that when sensitivity is reported in deception detection (specifically using linguistic methods) without correcting for the base rate, the accuracy of the methods is likely exaggerated. Further, although helpful in gauging the accuracy of a classifier, these measures are only valid for one decision threshold. For example a classifier that uses logistic regression will provide an output of a number on a continuous scale between one and zero for each case. For cases to be classified a decision threshold must be set. Only after this threshold is set wil there be a confusion matrix and values for these measures can be calculated

Trade‐offs between false‐positive and false‐negative errors over all possible thresholds can be visually modelled using a ROC curve, which graphs the false‐positive rate against the true‐positive rate for all thresholds. A classifier no better than random chance would create a diagonal line from the origin of the ROC graph to the upper‐right corner. The further from this line the ROC curve is, the more accurate the classifier is, and the more area is between this curve and the diagonal. That area, the area under the ROC curve or AUC, is a comprehensive measure that captures the accuracy under all possible decision thresholds.

The AUC, the ROC curve, and the original accuracy measures in SDT, however, are all designed to ignore the base rate. Why? Because SDT's primary purpose is not to determine the usefulness of a detection mechanism in a particular context but to compare the individuals and mechanisms doing the detection by factoring out the contextua information embedded in the base rate (Swets & Pickett, 1982). The AUC removes contextual information by sum marizing the accuracy of the classifier across all thresholds. In its original use with radar, SDT's purpose was not to determine if people‐operated radar was good at finding aircraft but to determine which of those radar operators was best compared with the others. SDT, therefore, is appropriate for comparing the relative merits of detection mechanisms within a context but not for determining the absolute usefulness of a mechanism. For that, we neeod to include contextual, or prior, information.

## 2.3 | Base rates, Bayes' theorem, and SDT

Bayesian analysis extends the concepts in SDT to include prior information about the probability of the signal being detected. SDT discusses the use of prior probabilities but treats them as given constants, emphasizing the likelihood ratio, the ratio of the true‐positive rate to the false‐positive rate, instead as a measure of comparative—rather than absolute—accuracy. Bayesian analysis, on the other hand, explicitly includes prior information. Bayesian analysis is based on Bayes' theorem expressed (using deception [D], truth [T], classified as deception [+]) in the following equation:

$$
P (D | +) = \frac {P (+ | D) P (D)}{P (+ | D) P (D) + P (+ | T) P (T)}.\tag{1}
$$

The classic example of the use of Bayes' theorem is the detection of cancer using a diagnostic test with a sensitivity of 0.99 and a specificity of 0.99 when that cancer has a population prevalence (or base rate) of 0.01. A positive test on a patient results in the nonintuitive posterior probability of 0.5 of the patient having the disease (Casscells, Schoenberger, & Graboys, 1978). This method is directly applicable to deception detection with the deception detection mechanism replacing the cancer test and the cancer prevalence, its base rate, replaced with the base rate of the form of deception being detected.

The base rate, or prior in Bayesian terms, captures information about the context in which the detection mech anism is used. When detecting deception, the base rate is the prevalence of deception within the context the detection is occurring. Therefore, the base rate in criminal investigations is the proportion of suspected criminals that li during investigations. When detecting deceptive reviews, the prevalence of deceptive reviews is the base rate. In a financial context, the rate may be the proportion of executives that lie during quarterly earnings conference calls.

The posterior probability of Baves' theorem, or probability of a subiect being deceptive given that a classifier saic it was and the left-hand side of Equation 1. is eguivalent to the precision or PPV defined by SDT as shown in Table 2 Precision, along with NPV, accuracy, and E1 are all affected by the base rate, Precision and its counterpart NPV are often the most interesting when detecting deception, because they tell a system user the probability of a subjec being deceptive given that they were labelled so by the system in the case of precision, or in the case of NPV, the probability of a subject labelled truthful being so. In a sense, these measures tell the user how much confidence they can have in the predictions of the system. If, according to Equation 1, the base rate, or P(D), is artificially set at 0.5 when the real base rate is lower, these measures will report higher values than they would if set at the base rate, giving an overly optimistic view of the ADD's use in the context. An artificial base rate of 0.5 in the cancer example above would imply a posterior probability of cancer of 0.99 given a positive test when the real posterior probability is 0.5.

A particularly powerful measure for understanding how the base rate affects the deception detection task is the false‐positive index. It is the number of false positives a classifier outputs for every true positive. When using the polygraph specifically, previous research has shown that base rate is the biggest driver of the false‐positive index (Fienberg et al., 2003). For example, in what the NRC terms specific incident tests, such as for analysing the guilt or innocence of suspects, the base rate of guilty suspects might be 50%. In contrast, when screening a population for major security risks, only 1 in 1000 may be a legitimate concern. For a test that is 80% sensitive and with an AUC of 0.9, there would be 0.21 false positives for every true positive when conducting specific incident tests, whereas there would be 208 false positives for every true positive when screening for security risks (Fienberg et al., 2003), suggesting that such a test would be much more appropriate for use in specific incident tests than in use for screening. As we show in the next section, most researchers ignore the exponential effects of the base rate on their accuracy.

## 2.4 | Automated deception detection systems

We reviewed the literature in deception detection since the NRC's study was published in 2003 to the present to identify journal papers that proposed or applied ADDs in low base rate contexts. For this review, we searched Web of Science using the search terms “lie detection,” “deception detection,” “credibility assessment,” and deception. This review only included papers that used automated methods of deception detection to classify subjects as truthful or deceptive. We excluded papers that limited the analysis to within subject designs. Studies with human raters unaided by technology to determine whether subjects were truthful or deceptive are beyond the scope of our stud (Akehurst, Manton, & Quandte, 2011; Nahari, 2016). Further, while we identified a few papers that applied deception detection tools and techniques to the problem of financial fraud, we did not attempt to identify all studies that classified companies as fraudulent or not, only those that applied ADDs to that task. As our focus is deception detection, rather than just the study of deception, we also include only those studies that approach this topic as a classification problem and exclude those papers that only test for differences between truthful and deceptive subjects using tech niques such as ANOVA. While few papers directly address the base rate of the sample, we have identified severa studies (see Table 3) that we can infer belong in the category of low base rate deception detection research based on the context provided by the authors.

Researchers have explored ADDs that incorporate many different techniques in low base rate contexts. Tools using linguistic analysis appear most frequently in our review (Fuller et al., 2009; Hernández‐Castañeda et al., 2017; Larcker & Zakolyukina, 2012; Siering et al., 2016; Throckmorton et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2016). That there are six studies that used linguistic analytics illustrates how far this research has come in the last 15 years. In 2004 Zhou et al. (Zhou, Twitchell, et al., 2004) demonstrated the potential of using data and text mining tools to detect deception using student subjects and the well‐known desert survival problem. Since then, authors have studied addi tional tools, software features, and perhaps most importantly, real-world samples. Among those additional tools are ocular‐motor measures of deception, which were also the focus of multiple studies (Cook et al., 2012; Kircher & Raskin, 2016; Twyman et al., 2014). Brain fingerprinting (Farwell et al., 2013), vocal cognitive‐dissonance measures (Hobson et al., 2012), the polygraph (Honts & Amato, 2007), temperature signals (Tsiamyrtzis et al., 2007), and ther mal skin imaging studies have also been conducted. Recently, one paper combined multiple non‐verbal indicators and one paraverbal indicator, vocal‐pitch, into an ADD system, an approach that may lead to more accurate and usefu systems (Twyman et al., 2015). While the specific tools and techniques of these ADDs vary, the same performance measures can be applied across these studies.

As noted in a previous section, an ADD's usefulness depends on its accuracy, decision threshold. base rate, anc costs. The papers reviewed here demonstrate a wide variety in the reporting of performance measures. While som papers report simply overall accuracy (Cook et al., 2012; Hernández‐Castañeda et al., 2017; Honts & Amato, 2007), others only report ROC and/or AUC (Hobson et al., 2012; Throckmorton et al., 2015; Twyman et al., 2015). Severa report multiple traditional SDT measures such as accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and/or false positive and negative (Farwell et al., 2013; Fuller et al., 2009; Twyman et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2016). Only a couple of paper report both more traditional SDT measures and ROC (Larcker & Zakolyukina, 2012; Warmelink et al., 2011).

While accuracy (and its associated measures such as sensitivity and specificity) is regularly addressed, decision threshold and base rate are addressed less freguently, although both impact the confusion matrix and therefore the accuracy and other performance measures, as shown in Table 2. Three papers addressing fraud in quarterly earnings conference calls give base rates ranging from 1.6% to 14.1% (Hobson et al., 2012; Larcker & Zakolyukina, 2012; Throckmorton et al., 2015). The base rate of deception is only provided in a few other studies. We identified publications on crowdfunding platforms (Siering et al., 2016) and Yelp reviews (Zhang et al., 2016) that provided the base rate. Siering et al. (2016) study fraud in crowdfunding platforms and state that the prevalence of suspended projects (which may be fraudulent) is 0.74%. In both studies, the analysis is performed on a balanced (or approximately balanced in Zhang et al., 2016) sample using 10‐fold cross validation without an interpretation of the results in relation to this very low base rate. Fuller et al. (2009) say that the sample they collected was about 75% truthful and 25% deceptive and mention that this is believed to reflect the underlying population. However, the analysis does not eval uate the performance in relation to this base rate. Warmelink et al. (2011) briefly allude to the base rate, mentioning that most airport passengers are likely to be truthful. The remaining papers in Table 3 did not estimate the base rat of their samples.

Like base rate, threshold is an important component of usefulness. Many researchers likely use the default threshold of their chosen technique, commonly 0.5 in classification algorithms, such as logistic regression and neura networks, and simply do not mention using this default threshold. Additionally, while it might make sense to adiust the threshold in a polygraph study to account for truth/deception bias of an individual examiner, such a choice is perhaps less relevant when using automated techniques as adjusting the threshold leads to concerns such as overfitting. A few of the papers in Table 3 incorporated the threshold into their analysis. Honts and Amato (2007) successively tested different cut‐off values for their participants' scores and classified outcomes using the optimal cut‐off that they determined. Throckmorton et al. (2015) demonstrate the change in the number of false positives across the range of probability of detection from 0.10 to 0.90. In their sample of 1572 calls, there would be 1047 false positives at a probability of detection of 0.90, with only 97 false positives with a probability of detection of 0.10. Larcker anc Zakolyukina (2012) show cut‐off values at the 50th, 70th, and 90th percentiles and demonstrate the dramatic impact that varying these cut‐off values has on true‐positive rate, false‐positive rate, precision, and overall accuracy. For example, at the 50th percentile for one of their samples, the false‐positive rate is 48.74% and the accuracy is 52.19%. At the 90th percentile, the corresponding false‐positive rate is 9.45% with an accuracy of 80.22%. Twyman et al. (2014) mention that an optimal cut‐off value could be determined based on a variety of values.

With some exceptions (Farwell et al., 2013; Hobson et al., 2012; Larcker & Zakolyukina, 2012; Throckmorton et al., 2015; Tsiamyrtzis et al., 2007), particularly those related to financial fraud (see Table 3), the majority of studie use balanced samples, or samples that are nearly balanced. Interestingly, Throckmorton et al. (2015) mention using ROC as the measure of their technique due to the imbalance found in their data. Balancing is a common and well justified technigue in building classification models. If a sample includes 99% truthful subiects and 1% deceptive sub jects, classifications can be 99% accurate simply by classifying all subjects as truthful. While this model has high overall accuracy, it is not useful as the model does not provide any information that helps us identify the class of most interest, the deceivers. While it can be necessary to balance the data to overcome this issue, the results should be adapted to the base rate of the sample studied for researchers and practitioners to assess the likelihood of practica application of the technique in a given population. The literature certainly has an appropriate place for proofs of concept, and one paper specifically mentions that they are not trying to present a deployable system (Twyman et al., 2015). While these previous studies have provided an excellent foundation of knowledge for future ADDs, as this literature matures, researchers should demonstrate that there can be an acceptable trade‐off between true positive and false positives.

We also note that there is a surprising lack of research in low base rate deception. Given the potential nationa security and financial implications of successful deception, we encourage more researchers to conduct research in these contexts. At the time of publication (2003), the NRC noted only seven of 194 polygraph studies that were related to the low base rate context of screening current or potential employees. As the deception literature was reviewed to identify low base rate classification studies, we found that most studies since 2003 were of mock crimes which is not generally a low base rate context. While most of the public are not criminals, of those that classifiers are applied to, the majority have reason to deceive. While there has not been a great deal of research in low base rate deception, it is notable that most of those studies identified in Table 3 have taken on the difficult task of obtaining and analysing real-world data

## 3 | DETERMINING USEFULNESS

In the introduction, we defined usefulness as the extent to which the ADD system can be used advantageously in a context. In this section, we show several ways to measure usefulness; each of which can be used independently or together. First, basic Bayesian analysis simply applies Bayes' theorem (Equation 1) using the deception base rate of the context being studied. Basic Bavesian analysis answers the question most users of a system want answered Given that the system says deception is present, what is the probability that it actually is? In a useful system, thi probability will be high enough to justify its use. Second, information gain provides a measure of how much additional information the ADD provides above the base rate. An ADD with information gain close to zero is one that does little better than guessing in proportion to the base rate and is not very useful. We then add in expected utility, which, along with the base rate, allows the researcher to determine an optimal point on a ROC curve. The further this point is from the lower‐left and upper‐right corners of the ROC curve, the more useful the system. We apply each measure to ADD studies from several of the papers in Section 2.4 and show that while some systems have acceptable useful ness, others will reguire further refinement

## 3.1 | Basic Bayesian analysis

The most basic form of Bayesian analysis we can use to determine usefulness is simply applying a base rate to the likelihood of detecting deception. For example, Siering et al. Unlike online crowdfunding, many contexts do not have readily available base rates. In such situations, research has often evaluated classification across all possible base rates using base rate independent measures, such as sensi tivity, or measures that incorporate all possible base rates such as AUC. Use of these measurements allow for good comparison among classifiers but assume no knowledge about the base rate. In most contexts where ADDs ar applied, we may not know the exact rate, but we often have some information about its boundaries.

For example, Burgoon et al. (2009) summarized various ADDs and proposed applying them to transportation screening. The paper does not discuss the base rate, which in this case is the prevalence of travellers with some sort of malicious intent. The exact rate is unknown, but, because we can easily observe that there are many travellers (3.696 billion per year worldwide; World Bank, 2018) and very few commercial aviation‐related crimes of the type aviation security is interested in, we can say that the rate is very low. One estimate of average airline hijackings sinc 1960 is 19 per year (Roser, Nagdy, & Ritchie, 2018). The rate is higher if we include crimes like drug or human traf ficking that use aviation but are not directed at aviation like terrorism or hijackings. So, if the rate were 0.0001, for example, the worldwide number of intentional, human‐caused aviation incidences per year would be 360, 960 (989 per day), which is reasonable if we include both aviation‐targeting and aviation‐using crimes. Of course, the rate of aviation‐related crimes is very likely lower than the rate of deception, but it should be similar to the rate of decep tion aviation security is interested in. Presumably, aviation security is not interested in minor lies such as passengers innocuously lying about their destination

The point of the thought exercise above is although we do not know what the true base rate of malicious decep tion in aviation security is, we do know what it is not. It is not 0.1, 0.01, or even 0.0001 since each of those rates produces an absurd number of aviation incidences. Therefore, we can bound our belief about the base rate with probability bounds (Walley, 1991) with an upper bound of 0.0001 or 989 incidences per day. We can then perform our analysis of usefulness based on this still‐high upper bound. Any ADD that is not useful at this base rate will not b useful at the true lower base rate in this context.

## 3.2 | Information gain

Honts and Schweinle (2009) proposed using the concept of information gain (Wells & Olson, 2002) to evaluate the value added by the polygraph. Using Bayes' theorem, they define information gain as the absolute value of the dif ference between the prior probability and the posterior probability, which logically requires the knowledge of th base rate. As the base rate can be unknown, they demonstrate the calculation of information gain across a range of base rates and generate an information gain curve. While basic Bayesian analysis is the probability that deception is occurring given that the ADD says it is, information gain is how much of that probability is due to the use of the ADD. In other words, it provides a measure of an ADD's usefulness above chance. Plotting the information gai across all possible base rates creates a curve showing at what base rates the ADD is most useful. ## 3.3 | Expected utility

The evaluation of ADDs for low base rate contexts can improve if we consider cost. In the low base rate contexts we have considered, the costs of false negatives outweigh the cost of false positives. In the context of screening emplovees for employment in national security. for example, mistakenly denving employment to an individual is costly but not as costly as allowing a spy access to sensitive information. This is the case for most contexts since the types of deception that systems are built to detect are usually costly deception. The stakes are less dire in fraudulent crowdfunding and financial fraud, but the costs are still lopsided. For example, in financial fraud, a false negative may result in investor losses of millions of dollars. If done discreetly, a false positive only results in unnecessary time and resources spent on further investigation. As shown by the right‐hand side of Equation 2 (see below), the highe the costs of false negatives, the numerator in the utility term, the more a low base rate is offset

To include cost, we use expected utility theory, which simply multiplies the cost (or benefit) of an outcome by it probability for all possible outcomes (Briggs, 2016). Using the SDT framework above, the expected utility of a deception detection is the benefit of detecting deception when it exists (TP) and not detecting it when it does not (TN) plu: the (negative) cost of not detecting it when it exists (FN) and detecting it when it does not (FP). We can compare th expected utility of having a particular deception detection system with the expected utility of a trivial system that simply chooses truth or deception depending on their prevalence. If the expected utility with the system is higher, we have a system with net benefit, and, according to expected utility theory, we should consider the system usefu and implement it. Unfortunately, it is difficult to obtain estimates of these costs. It may be more tractable to assume benefits of zero (because truthfulness is in most contexts considered the normal state of affairs) and estimate th ratio of the cost of a false negative to the cost of a false positive

With this ratio and an estimate for the base rate, we can use the utility information plus the base rate to choose an optimal threshold for a classifier. To find the threshold with the maximum expected utility, we use the following well‐established equation for finding the slope of a ROC curve at a particular point (Green & Swets, 1966; Schonhoff & Giordano, 2006):

$$
\frac {T P R}{F P R} = \frac {1 - b a s e r a t e}{b a s e r a t e} \cdot \frac {U _ {T N} + U _ {F P}}{U _ {T P} + U _ {F N}},\tag{2}
$$

where $U _ { T P }$ is the utility, ie, cost (negative) or benefit (positive), of a true positive; $U _ { T N }$ is the utility of a true negative; $U _ { F P }$ is the utility of a false positive; and $U _ { F N }$ is the utility of a false negative. The threshold that creates the left side equal to the right side is the threshold with maximum utility and produces the confusion matrix with maximum utilit for the classifier.

We can compare this maximized classifier with a trivial classifier to determine its usefulness. A trivial classifie that represents not having a deception detection system is one that simply chooses the most prevalent class.

Siering et al. (2016) performed an economic analysis of their crowdfunding ADD. In it, they assumed that the costs of false positives are equal to the costs of false negatives and that both are equal to the amount pledged. This is possible, but it is also likely that false negatives are more costly than false positives. A false positive will at least result in wasted investigation effort and at worst result in the suspension of genuine campaign. A suspended campaign will be noticed immediately by those wrongly suspended and, in most cases, result in reinstatement. A fals negative, by its very nature, is not noticed and results in financial losses to the funders and reputational loss to the platform. We believe the annoyance to the platform and the campaign of a false positive is overshadowed by the real financial loss to funders of the false negative

Whether this is true and the difference in costs between false positives and false negatives are questions that can only be answered empirically. When, however, that is not possible or is not yet available, we can make rough estimates. Given our theory above, we can say that the costs likely are not equal, but the false negatives are als not likely more than 100 times that of the false positives. So, to gauge the usefulness of the ADD in this context we can assume false negatives will cost somewhere between equal and 100 times as much as false positives. In Figure 5. the points labelled U1 represent egual costs with an unbalanced test set while the points labelled U10 and U100 represent false negatives costing 10 times and 100 times more than false positives, respectively

This analysis, although rough, helps us know what kind of accuracy would be needed to justify the use of an AD] to detect fraudulent crowdfunding campaigns. The very low base rate of fraudulent campaigns is a difficult hurdle to overcome. Only when the cost of a false negative are 100 times that of a false positive does the utility of the actua base rate classifier approach the one tested on unrealistic, balanced data.

## 5 | RECOMMENDATIONS

Our literature review and analysis lead us to two sets of recommendations. The first set is related to improved and expanded reporting of results of ADDs. The second set of recommendations provides more general suggestions for ADD researchers. Based on our analysis, our primary recommendation related to assessing the performance of ADDs is this: “when applying ADDs to a specific context, contextual information including the base rate and costs should be used to establish usefulness." We break this recommendation down into the following actionable recommendations for researchers, reviewers, and editors. While this study has relied on ADD research to illustrate our recommendations for improved analysis, these recommendations also can be applied more broadly to research in security and ana Iytics, such as intrusion detection, malware detection, and insider threat detection, Our recommendations can also be used by IS researchers studying any low base rate classification problem, not simply those in the areas of deceptio and security. For example, intrusion, malware, and insider threat detection are a major low base rate problems in information security. Finding sales leads and targeting ads can be low base rate problems in eCommerce. Detecting failing IT projects and cultural problems can be low base rate problems in IT organization.

## 5.1 | All deception detection research should include a confusion matrix for their sample(s) and a known or estimated base rate of the population of interest

While the goal of a single study may not be to compare a tool to other tools or contexts, research is a cumulative endeavour, and it will be helpful to both reviewers and future researchers to include this information. Including the confusion matrix and the base rate will allow others to calculate accuracy measures beyond those the authors choose to report. Both Ho et al. (2016) and Seiring et al. (2016) provide the raw confusion matrix and base rates The threshold value used with classification methods with continuous outputs (e.g., neural networks) should also be noted. Though threshold is not the primary focus of the current study, previous research has shown how the choice of threshold can have a dramatic impact on results (Larcker & Zakolyukina, 2012). Researchers should be cau tious in using thresholds other than the default, as a more ‘friendly’ threshold may reduce false positives, but this wil come at the expense of finding deceivers in low base rate scenarios

## 5.2 | Research in which the base rate is experimentally set at 0.5 or the focus is on comparing classification algorithms should use ROC analysis

Since each point on a ROC curve represents a particular base rate/cost combination, ROC analyses and ROC curves like those shown in Figures 3, 4, and 5 allow readers to gauge the usefulness of ADDs across all possible contexts. They also allow for comparing ADDs. For example, a recent paper experimentally compared several detectio methods in which the base rate of deceivers was set at 0.5 (Ho et al., 2016). The authors reported confusion matrices for two different thresholds to show how the change affected overall accuracy. A ROC curve would have shown how all changes in threshold affect not only the overall accuracy but also the trade‐offs between false positives and false negatives. Brown and Mues (2012) is a good example of using ROC analysis to compare classification algorithms in a specific context where they use the area under the ROC curve (AUC) as a test statistic for comparing the accurac the classifiers' performance at predicting loan defaults given credit scoring data at several artificial base rates.

## 5.3 | In experimental or field research that applies a deception detection technology to a specific context, the base rate of that context should be estimated and applied to base‐rate dependent measures

This recommendation is the crux of this paper. We have shown using several examples that, especially when it is very low, the base rate has a large impact on the usefulness of a system. The analysis of fraudulent crowdfunding included in this paper is a good example of including the base rate. Analyses should incorporate base the context's base rate into base rate dependent measures as well as report base rate independent measures such as the ROC curve. Th medical field has grappled with this problem for decades (most famously with breast cancer screening; Saslow et al., 2007) and provides good examples of how to incorporate base rates and cost. Chapman and Sonnenberg (2000) provide a good overview and has chapters on prevalence (the base rate) and costs.

## 5.4 | When the base rate or costs are unknown, estimate probability bounds and use the most conservative bound

Often the base rate is not known or is imprecisely estimated. In these cases, researchers should create upper and lower bounds around plausible base rates and use the most conservative of the two bounds as the base rate estimate Most often, when estimating base rates, the conservative estimate will be the upper bound, the highest plausible base rate. For example, in the context of screening all emplovees in a national security service using an ADD. it is absurd to consider half of them to be spies. The NRC suggests that the base rate is far less than 1%. The ADD should evaluated at this maximum base rate of 1% (Fienberg et al., 2003), with the caveat that this base rate is likely higher, but not lower than the true base rate. Bounding the base rate in this manner is highly context dependent Researchers should consider the relevant literature and consult with subject matter experts when building thes bounds. A good example is Flander, Dixon, McBride, and Burgman's (2012) use of probability bounds analysis to elicit from experts the acceptable amounts of water treatment chemicals

## 5.5 | Researchers should estimate information gain when base rates are unknowable

In some cases, a base rate cannot be estimated because it will be determined in the future. Wells, Yang, and Smalarz (2015), for example, argue that when evaluating criminal eyewitness statements the base rate is unknowable in advance and highly variable because it is set by the criminal justice system on a case‐by‐case basis. They use information gain graphs like Figure 2 to compare different eyewitness identification processes across all possible base rates, In similar situations with an ADD, information gain should be used, Information gain graphs explicitly illustrate the effects of base rates where in ROC must be derived from the slope of the curve. Using information gain rathe than a ROC curve more directly illustrates the effect of the base rate on the effectiveness of the ADD. This may be more understandable to practitioners who, in contexts like intrusion detection, are most interested in the value the ADD adds in detecting intrusions rather than the relative performance of different ADDs offered by ROC analysis.

## 5.6 | Researchers should estimate costs when possible

Expected utility, the mathematical representation of usefulness, includes both the base rate and costs. Often in deception detection contexts like airport screening, the costs of not detecting deception outweigh the costs of falsely detecting deception. In addition, these are often same contexts with low base rates. As shown in Equation 2, the cost ratio counteracts the base rate ratio meaning that high false‐negatives costs counteract low base rates and pull the optimal ROC point from the corners of ROC curve to the middle where usefulness is more apparent. For example although Siering et al. (2016) did not incorporate the base rate of fraudulent crowdfunding campaigns into their analysis, they did estimate the costs of identifying and misidentifying campaigns to show the economic benefit of their ADD. Just as with base rates, when costs cannot be estimated, a range of plausible costs should be used. As recommended above, when costs are difficult to estimate using probability bounds analysis or similar system for capturing uncertainty is recommended When all possible cost ratios are plausible then the entire ROC curve should be reported.

## 5.7 | More low base rate samples are then needed to get a true picture of the usefulness of ADDs, and additional real‐world studies can give the most accurate picture of the usefulness of ADDs

Due to the relationship between the context and the accuracy, we also recommend that, whenever possible researchers use a sample from the same context for which the ADD is being developed. We applaud the researchers identified inTable 3 that used real‐world, low base rate data, as most studies continue to rely on a mock crime or specific incident contexts. For example, three of the papers in our literature review (Hobson et al., 2012; Larcker & Zakolyukina 2012; Throckmorton et al., 2015) focused on financial fraud. Two of them gave base rate estimates of 0.016 to 0.141 (Larcker & Zakolyukina, 2012) and 0.268 (Throckmorton et al., 2015). Together, these base rates for financial fraud wil give future researchers and practitioners a basis for evaluating potential ADDs for detecting financial fraud

## 6 | FUTURE RESEARCH

We believe that the recommendations above will improve ADD research, but more improvements to ADD research methods are also needed. The following are suggestions for improving ADD research methods.

There is still room for additional theoretical development and determination of context‐specific conditions that impact accuracy. Since 2003, a great deal of work has been done to outline the cues that underlie the process of deception. A meta‐analysis has even been conducted to determine which cues can be detected by computers (Hauch, Blandón‐Gitlin, Masip, & Sporer, 2015). It was suggested by the NRC then (Fienberg et al., 2003) and still believed now that accuracy may be highly variable across situations. Theoretical development and determination of context‐specific conditions can help clarify this aspect of ADD research.

Another unexplored area for research is the secondary effects of ADDs such as the deterring effect to deceivers when they know an ADD is in use. Deterrence in this form has been shown effective in information security (Strau & Welke, 1998), but ADD researchers have not shown whether it is a factor in the effectiveness of their system (Fienberg et al., 2003). These kinds of secondary effects may result in confessions, increasing ADD accuracy, or may deter the action, reducing the base rate and therefore the accuracy but increasing the overall utility of the ADD. It is possible that in some contexts, the deterring secondary effect of an ADD could be larger than the primary detecting effect

Researchers should also look at ways to influence the base rate before the ADDs are applied. For example, currently the U.S. Transportation Safety Administration allows travellers to sign up for a trusted‐traveller program called PreCheck. This program requires the traveller to provide security‐relevant information and submit to background checks. In return, the traveller can use a quicker, less intrusive screening option. Presumably, the base rate of deceivers in the PreCheck screening option will be lower than that of the non‐PreCheck. Future research could show how similar interventions could lead to higher base rates resulting in higher ADD accuracy.

Finally, future research could use recent developments in Bayesian statistics (Gelman et al., 2013) to better incorporate uncertainty for more accurate estimates of base rates and costs. Modern Bayesian statistical methods involves having a full probability model that incorporates many sources of uncertainty. One of those sources of uncertainty is the prior probability, which is the base rate we discuss throughout the paper is the Bayesian prior and in Bayesian statistics is represented by a probability distribution. Our recommendation of using probability bounds is essentially recommending the use of a uniform probability distribution. We leave it to future research, though, to build bette models of the uncertainty of the prior.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

The goal of ADDs is to provide useful systems to users to detect deception. While the extant research in ADDs has provided an excellent foundation for future studies, this early research has largely reported basic accuracy measures

Moving forward, we encourage researchers to include base rate or cost information to measure usefulness throug Bayesian analysis, information gain, or determining ROC points. Without measuring and reporting usefulness, ADDs research will miss its ultimate mark of fielding systems that users can advantageously employ in their contexts. Our recommendations are a beginning point for moving ADD research towards this goal. Future research questions could include the following: Which representations of classifier performance are best for ADDs? How can researchers bet ter estimate base rates? How can researchers better estimate costs? When base rates and costs are uncertain, how can researchers represent that uncertainty without removing base rates and costs entirely from the analysis? And, finally, future research should focus on those contexts in which the base rate and costs allow for the greatest usefulness.
