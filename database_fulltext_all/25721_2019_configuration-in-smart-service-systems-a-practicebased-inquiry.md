## Abstract

In this paper, we develop a new perspective on configuration processes inherent to smart service systems that brings human actors to the theoretical foreground, is informed by practice theory, and is grounded in an intervention study of smart locating systems used in home care of persons with dementia. Maintaining control of the location of persons with dementia is a major source of caregiver burden, and assistive technologies such as smart locating systems hold the promise of enhancing quality of life and reducing caregiver burden. Drawing from research on smart service systems, organizational configuration research, and practice theory, we articulate a conceptual model of configuring in practice to explain how practice elements and the capabilities of smart technologies become mutually configured. Our analysis of study participants' experiences with the locating systems identified three distinctive configurations, characterized as enhanced smartness, degraded smartness, and fragile smartness, which developed through configuring processes. The configuring in practice model we develop here helps explain why unexpected and anticipated configurations develop and thus the conditions that facilitate or inhibit value cocreation in smart service systems.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Technological advances such as smart cars, smart household devices, and smart assistive technologies are heralde as transformational for society, business, and individual human experience. The “smart technologies” underlying these advances (eg, artificial intelligence, deep learning algorithms, and Internet of things [IoT] sensing devices) are expected to become ubiguitous. beneficial elements of human practices (Maglio. 2014: Medina-Boria. 2015). Smart technologies can sense, collect, and process data in order to learn, adapt to environmental circumstances, and mak partially independent decisions (Allmendinger & Lombreglia, 2005; Maglio & Lim, 2016; Wünderlich, Wangenheim, & Bitner, 2012). These properties then enable them to configure and reconfigure services over time (Beverungen, Matzner, & Janiesch, 2017; Lim Kim, Kim, Heo, et al., 2018; Lim, Kim, Kim, Kim, & Maglio, 2018) so that service become more person centred and individualized (Medina-Borja, 2015; National Science Foundation, 2014).

A corpus of research addressing smart service systems is developing across disciplines such as information systems (Beverungen, Matzner, & Janiesch, 2017; Beverungen, Müller, Matzner, Mendling, & vom Brocke, 2019 Geirbo, 2017), service science (Barile & Polese, 2010; Maglio & Lim, 2016; Wuenderlich et al., 2015), and engineering (Gretzel, Werthner, Koo, & Lamsfus, 2015; Massink, Harrison, & Latella, 2010). Researchers hav advanced this topic by articulating what types of smart services can be offered on the basis of smart technologies (Gavrilova & Kokoulina, 2015), by advising how to innovate in smart services (Geum, Jeon, & Lee, 2016; Lim, Kim, Kim, Kim, & Maglio, 2018; Lim, Maglio, Kim, Kim, & Kim, 2016), and by applying a smart service systems lens to particular domains (Carrubbo, Bruni, Cavacece, & Moretta Tartaglione, 2015; Gretzel et al., 2015) However, a common critique is that this research pays scant attention to how humans and smart technologies will actually interact and with what consequences for humans and for smart service systems (Breidbach, Antons, & Salge, 2016; Maglio, 2015; Maglio, Kwan, & Spohrer, 2015). In contrast to the close attention paid to smart technologies, references to “people” (Lim et al., 2016; Maglio & Lim, 2016), “value co-creation” (Lim & Maglio, 2018), or “human-centeredness” (Maglio et al., 2015) maintain analytical distance from day-to-day human activities. Smart technologies and services are forecast to enter in human experiences in highly personal, even intimate ways, so that theoretical and empirical insights from a human-centred perspective are needed to guid innovation towards anticipated improvements in individual well-being (see also Breidbach et al., 2016; Maglio et al., 2015).

In this paper, we bring humans and their practices to the theoretical foreground to investigate configuration i smart service systems. Humans and technologies are key elements in configurations that form service system (Maglio, 2014, p. i; Spohrer, Maglio, Bailey, & Gruhl, 2007). Smart technologies are expected to enable these systems to configure or reconfigure elements as they learn from and adjust to change and become “smarter” (Demirkan et al., 2015; Lim & Maglio, 2018; J. Spohrer, Siddike, & Kohda, 2017). Smart technologies thus play dual roles as elements of configurations and, at the same time, as agents that (re)configure elements (Barile & Polese, 2010; Calza et al. 2015; Polese, Tommasetti, Vesci, Carrubbo, & Troisi, 2016; Spohrer et al., 2017). We suggest humans also have thes dual roles, but how humans figure into configuration has received little analytical attention. Because smart service systems are likely to “resist traditional optimization and automation” (Maglio, 2015, p. ii), a greater appreciation of how humans as well as smart technologies interact and participate in configuration will be needed

Against this background, we pose the question “How do humans and smart technologies interact in configuration processes and with what consequences?” Drawing on practice theory (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011 Orlikowski, 2000), we investigated this question in an empirical study in which smart locating systems were introduced into dementia home care, a setting where family caregivers (CGs) care for persons with dementia (PwDs). The study was conducted as an interdisciplinary project that combined an interventional study from medicine (Brodaty, Green, & Koschera, 2003; Wallerstein & Duran, 2010) with a theory-building study. The intervention study provided a unique opportunity to introduce smart technologies into dementia home care and to evaluate their efficacy to improve quality of life as well as to inform theory building about configuration

Smart technologies such as Global Positioning System (GPS)-enabled locating systems hold much promise to benefit PwDs and their CGs. However, the quality of life for PwDs relies on existing practices, and deviations can cause stress, anxiety, and fear that exacerbate cognitive functions (see, for example, Calkins, 2018). During the intervention, 20 dyads (CG and PwD) were provided with smart locating systems to use at home. Partici pants were interviewed three times over a 2-month period. We adopted an inductive, theory-building approac (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013; Yin, 2013) using established procedures for qualitative data analysi (Breidbach & Maglio, 2016) to analyse the intervention study findings. Drawing from literatures on smart service systems, organizational configuration, and practice theory, our analysis revealed how enhanced smartness degraded smartness, and fragile smartness configurations unfolded through the interactions of the smart locating system with elements of the dyads' location control practice (Oborn, Barrett, & Davidson, 2011; Reckwitz 2002). From this analysis, we develop a model of configuring in practice and propositions to guide future research.

In these ways, we contribute a new perspective on configuration processes inherent to smart service systems. Grounded in practice theory (Oborn et al., 2011; Orlikowski, 2000; Reckwitz, 2002), this perspective brings humans to the theoretical foreground in a conceptual model of configuring in practice. This model addresses how practice elements and the capabilities of smart technologies are mutually configured in practice through reciprocal reinforcin interactions (Barrett, Oborn, Orlikowski, & Yates, 2012; Barrett, Oborn, & Orlikowski, 2016). By balancing assump tions about the central roles of smart technologies in configuration (National Science Foundation, 2014; Demirka et al., 2015; Spohrer et al., 2017) with a fuller consideration of human elements and practices, we are better able to explain how unexpected and anticipated configurations develop and thus the conditions that facilitate or inhibit valu cocreation.

## 2 | THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS

A common assumption in literature on smart service systems is that human and nonhuman actors form configurations that cocreate value (Beverungen et al., 2019; Lim & Maglio, 2018; Wünderlich et al., 2012). Smart technologies ar viewed as central components, which play dual roles in configuration: Smart technologies are elements of a configu ration and also are agents that configure or (re)configure the elements in the configuration (Beverungen, Matzner, & Janiesch, 2017; Lim, Kim, Kim, Heo, et al., 2018; Lim, Kim, Kim, Kim, & Maglio, 2018). How humans participate in and influence configuring processes is less clear in the literature. Table 1 outlines three perspectives in smart service sys tems research that illustrate these points.

Evolutionary perspectives typically present a macro view of service systems as people, technologies, organiza tions, and information. With the addition of smart technologies, the assumption is that systems can evolve towards becoming “smarter” in response to their environment in a manner analogous to biological evolution (Demirkan Spohrer, & Badinelli, 2016; Spohrer et al., 2017). Humans are accounted for as one of several “elements” that ente into configurations, albeit by abstracting humans into highly general terms such as “people” or “organizations” (Demirkan et al., 2015, 2016; Spohrer et al., 2017). The roles of humans in configuring processes, such as dynamic adaptations based on data sensing and computation, are not ruled out, but how humans actually participate in config uration vis-à-vis smart technologies is generally not articulated.

The viable systems perspective draws from systems theory a macrolevel view of smart service systems as dynamic configurations of resources (elements) capable of self-reconfiguration in order to meet system goals and assure survival in the service ecosystem (Barile & Polese, 2010; Gretzel et al., 2015; Kennedy & Keskin, 2016). Smar service systems then “have to adapt in a manner analogous to Darwin's theory of the adaptive capacity of organism for survival” (Polese et al. 2016, p. 29) in order to continuously ensure fit with their environments (Gretzel et al 2015; Keskin & Kennedy, 2015; Polese et al., 2016). Smart technologies dynamically configure elements to achieve system goals (Barile & Polese, 2010). Interactions of humans such as customers and employees with smart technologies can reveal whether human practices and smart technology are compatible (consonance) and operate in harmony (resonance); smart technologies reconfigure system elements as needed to achieve and maintain these system properties (Barile & Polese, 2010; Polese et al., 2016).

Beverungen et al. (2019) offer a more microlevel perspective by situating the smart products featured in smart service systems into human activities. Acting as boundary objects (Bergman, Lyytinen, & Mark, 2007; Carlile, 2002; Star & Griesemer, 1989), smart products integrate the resources of individual or collective actors (customer and service provider) to allow value propositions to be realized. Interactions with smart technologies as objects span “front stage” (consumer) and “back stage” (provider) activities, so that “the remote monitoring, optimization, and control of smart products enable the day-to-day activities of service consumers and service providers to be networked based on continuous interactions and a continuous exchange of data” (Beverungen et al., 2019, p. 12). In these ways, smart products (re)configure human and technical elements by integrating resources (Becker et al., 2013 Leigh Star, 2010).

Each of these perspectives assumes that (re)configuration is intrinsic to smart service systems and that smart technologies are by and large responsible for configuration. However, what configuration actually entails, how con figuring processes unfold, and how humans interact with smart technologies in these processes have not been fully explicated. The boundary object perspective begins to conceptualize this process by zooming in from macroleve views of the system as a whole to conceptualize interactions between humans and smart technologies in terms of resource integration. To advance theorizing about configuration further, we draw on key concepts from the organiza tional configuration literature

## 2.1 | Conceptualizing

The notion of configuration has received wide attention in organization theory literature (for reviews, see, eg, Dess Newport, & Rasheed. 1993: Misangyi et al., 2017: Short, Payne, & Ketchen, 2008), Similar to ideas about smart ser vice systems, organizations have been viewed as complex systems, in which different elements are configured to carry out the organizational mission (Fiss, 2011; Misangyi et al., 2017; Siggelkow, 2001). Unlike the focus on value cocreation that characterizes much of the smart services literature (Spohrer & Banavar, 2015; Spohrer et al., 2017) organizational theorists have considered configuration in relation to a wider range of outcomes, such as fit to th environment (Forte, Hoffman, Lamont, & Brockmann, 2000; Kikulis, Slack, & Hinings, 1995; Siggelkow, 2002), misfit between configuration elements (Siggelkow, 2001, 2002), decay (Tyrrall & Parker, 2005), configuration change in response to environmental change (Siggelkow, 2001, 2002), and resistance to change (Fox-Wolfgramm, Boal, & Hunt 1998). In sharp contrast to the smart service systems literature, organizational theorists generally assume that managers drive configuration processes, while employees enable or resist change (Dyck, 1997); technologies generall have no explicit role in configuration theory (except in some cases as an element of a configuration)

Although this research addresses configuration at the organizational level, its conceptual foundations are applica ble to microlevel analyses as well (Mever. Tsui, & Hinings. 1993). For instance, configurations are addressed holisti: cally, and interactions among elements are assumed to be nonlinear as well as contextually determined (Meyer et al., 1993). The work of Siggelkow (2001, 2002) on how organizational configurations arise and change highlights concepts useful for our purposes. Similar to the smart service systems literature, Siggelkow characterizes an organiza tional system as composed of various elements and their interactions. He notes, however, that the concept o configuration is narrower: “The term configuration usually implies that the core elements are reinforcing, such that the overall system is in a state of coherence or consistency” (Siggelkow, 2002, p. 128). This is consistent with the notions of consonance and resonance in the viable system's perspective

From this general definition, configurations can then be described on the basis of their elements and interactions between them. Unlike attempts in the smart service literature to specify components (Lim et al., 2016; Lim & Maglio 2018), organizational researchers specify analytically useful elements on the basis of theory, or they derive elements through empirical study (Siggelkow, 2002). Core elements are those elements in a configuration that interact wit many other elements and that have a strong impact on future developments (Siggelkow, 2002, p. 127). Elaboratin elements primarily act to reinforce existing core elements: “two elements are said to interact if the value of one ele ment depends on the presence of the other element. In particular two elements are said to reinforce each other if the value of each element is increased by the presence of the other element” (Siggelkow, 2002, p. 127). Because interactions bind elements together in configurations, identifying interactions among elements is critical to under standing how configurations develop or change. Siggelkow (2002) focuses on reinforcing interactions that contribut to cohesion of a configuration but allows that some interactions may be inconsistent or disruptive (misfit).

These concepts (core vs elaborating elements, reinforcing interactions vs misfit) provide a conceptual vocabulary absent from the smart service systems literature. However, a limitation of using this vocabulary in organizational configuration studies is the underlying ontology in which configurations are considered as static states and organizations gravitate towards them (Dyck, 1997; Tyrrall & Parker, 2005). This view echoes the evolutionary perspective that smart systems evolve towards being “smarter,” although here the primary actors are managers rather than technologies. Against this background, to develop dynamic and process-oriented understanding of phenomena, Bakken and Hernes (2006) argue that phenomena typically approached as nouns (entities, states) should be conceived in terms of verbs (processes). That is, theorizing around configuring as an ongoing process as well as configuration as a state or an endpoint may lead to new insights about how humans and smart technologies participate in configuration. To do so, we turn finally to practice theory.

## 2.2 | Conceptualizing

The term practice theory applies to a rich literature in sociology and organization theory that foregrounds what actors actually do (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Orlikowski, 1996). Individuals enact practices in their day-to-day activities, but practices are not simply an individual's activities or habits (Barnes, 2001). Instead, Reckwitz (2002, p. 249) defines practices as “a routinized type of behavior which consists of several elements, interconnected to one other: forms of bodily activities, forms of mental activities, ‘things’ and their use, a background knowledge in the form of understand ing, know-how, states of emotion and motivational knowledge.” Research that focuses on these concrete “doings” i organization science (Danner-Schröder & Geiger, 2016; Nicolini, 2009; Smets, Jarzabkowski, Burke, & Spee, 2015), information systems (Barrett & Walsham, 1999; Leonardi, 2013; Orlikowski, 2000), and service science (Echeverri & Skalen, 2011; McColl-Kennedy, Vargo, Dagger, Sweeney, & van Kasteren, 2012; Vargo & Lusch, 2017) is indicative of this broader “practice turn” (Schatzki & Knorr Cetina, 2000; Whittington, 2011).

Orlikowski (2000) was among the first researchers to articulate a practice theory lens for research on interactions between human and information technology, arguing that social structures are not embedded in technology designs but are enacted in practices (see also Giddens, 1984). Oborn et al. (2011) examined how practice elements—emotions, objects, and activities (Reckwitz, 2002)—interrelated with an information system to engender different patterns of information system use, such as nonuse, idiosyncratic use, or extensive use. In a study of the interactions between robots and human actors, Barrett et al. (2012) argued that material (technology) and human actors resist and accom modate one another, creating tensions that alter boundaries. They conclude that “practices and technologies thus configure and reconfigure each other while at the same time shifting the nature of the boundaries involved” (Barrett et al., 2012, p. 1451). In a longitudinal study of an online health community, Barrett et al. (2016, p. 12) illustrate how different types of value were “performed through specific sociomaterial configurations of OC strategies, digita platform designs, and forms of engagement by various stakeholders.” Configurations of these elements evolved over several years as new stakeholders and technological features were mutually configured in the community's practices.

Such studies highlight how practice theory helps us theorize configuring as a dynamic unfolding of interactions between humans and smart technologies in which elements are mutually configured. That is, interactions betwee elements are reciprocal rather than linear, one-way effects. To do so, we must account for smart technologies as active and semiautonomous agents that share capabilities previously attributed to humans (eg, sensing, learning, and adapting) but without losing sight of human elements or their capabilities and influence (Beverungen et al., 2019 Orlikowski & Scott, 2015). Three assumptions drawn from practice theory are particularly relevant in this regard First, practices represent recurrent and durable patterns of activities that convey a collective sense of “what needs to be done” (Nicolini, 2009, p. 1404). Historically, humans have been best positioned “to understand what is going on, what direction things ought to take, what is expected of them, and what is appropriate” (Nicolini, 2009, p. 1405) Whether and how this type of sensemaking shifts to nonhuman actors (that is, smart technologies) has potentia implications for how configuring develops and for human autonomy and agency in their practices. Second, practices are subject to different interpretations and tensions so that enactments are dynamic and provisional (Nicolini, 2009 p. 1405). Day-to-day enactments of practices thus present unstable and shifting conditions in which humans and smart technologies interact, adapt, and configure and reconfigure each other. Third, practices are “immersed in a thick texture of interconnections” (Nicolini, 2009, p. 1407), maintained in part by boundary objects, protocols, and so on. Nicolini (2009, p. 1406) posits that the tools and instruments integral to a practice, which may embed the designer's intent, account for some of the durability of a practice and stability of these interconnections. However self-adjusting and reconfiguring smart technologies may stimulate innovative or deviant enactments that undermin the durability of a practice or destabilize interconnections among practice elements and with the smart technology.

To summarize, these literatures bring to our study an appreciation of configuration as an important property of smart service systems (Calza et al., 2015; Lusch, Vargo, & Tanniru, 2010; Spohrer, Piciocchi, & Bassano, 2012) but one that requires elaboration of human–smart technology interactions in configuring; a conceptual vocabulary for “unboxing” and thus examining configuration closely; and an analytic lens for theorizing configuring as an ongoing, emergent process through which configurations may develop (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Orlikowski, 2000). We now draw on these insights in an inductive analysis of the intervention study to consider how humans and smart technologies interact in configuration processes and what consequences may arise from these processes.

## 3 | EMPIRICAL SETTING, RESEARCH DESIGN, AND METHODS

Inquiry into configuration(s) of humans, their needs, and smart technologies calls for interdisciplinary researc (Maglio, 2014, 2015; Medina-Borja, 2015). Here, we combine a medical intervention study (Brodaty et al., 2003; Wallerstein & Duran, 2010) and a theory-building study (Breidbach & Maglio, 2016) informed by service science and information systems research. This approach has a strong potential for impact on practice and contributing novel the oretical insights.

## 3.1 | Study context

The ageing of populations has increased the prevalence of chronic health conditions such as cancer, cardiovascu lar diseases, and neurodegenerative diseases around the globe (World Health Organization [WHO], 2011). Caring for persons with chronic conditions requires integration of data and expertise across diverse service providers (Breidbach et al., 2016; WHO, 2015). One mission of the service science field is to contribute innovative models, tools, and methods to address these health system challenges (Maglio et al., 2015). Innovations in chronic care service systems, such as the use of “orchestrators” (Breidbach et al., 2016), are beginning to appear, while patients themselves are expected to be active cocreators of their own health (Frow, McColl-Kennedy, & Payne, 2016; McColl-Kennedy, Vargo, Dagger, Sweeney, & Kasteren, 2012). Mobile information technologies and wearabl devices (eg, the Apple Watch and Health Toolkit) are rapidly being developed and diffused for this purpose. Family service systems are also an essential component of the health care sector, as families increasingly provide care giving to persons with chronic conditions (Burgio, Gaugler, & Hilgeman, 2016) rather than relying on costl institutions such as care homes.

A dramatic example for what it means when families care for persons with chronic conditions is dementia (WHO 2012). Dementia, a neurodegenerative disease, is “a syndrome – usually of a chronic or progressive nature – in which there is deterioration in cognitive function (i.e. the ability to process thought) beyond what might be expected from normal ageing” (WHO, 2017a, p. 1). Deaths due to dementia more than doubled between 2000 and 2015, makin dementia seventh out of the 10 top causes for death worldwide (WHO, 2017b). The societal costs for caring for dementia are estimated at 1.4% of the gross domestic product (GDP) in high-income countries (WHO, 2017a), with 45% of that arising from care provided outside of professional settings (WHO, 2012, p. 2), mainly within family set tings (Etters, Goodall, & Harrison, 2008).

Caring for PwDs strains family members and puts substantive burden on their health, social relationships, and overall quality of life (WHO, 2017b). Caregiver burden develops in part from the fear that a PwD might wander off unnoticed and get hurt (Etters et al., 2008; Ory, Hoffman, Yee, Tennstedt, & Schulz, 1999). Information on the loca tion of the PwD is key to controlling such situations as it enables the CG to act and potentially save the PwD from harm. Interest in intelligent, or smart, assistive technologies is growing in geriatric and psychiatric research (Ienc et al., 2017). Assistive technologies (Bharucha et al., 2009) such as watches, wrist bands, or carpets (Gagnon-Roy et al., 2017) often have built-in sensors to track an individuals' bodily movements and assess whether a person is endangered or not (Bowes, Dawson, & Greasley-Adams, 2013).

## 3.2 | Our intervention study

The intervention study, carried out at the Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy at a German university hospi tal, was designed to assess whether introducing locating systems comprising a GPS watch linked to a mobile phone app (a smart technology) within family care could reduce CG burden while supporting the PwD's ability to maintain daily activities and routines. Maintaining familiar routines is important for PwDs in general, as routinized behaviours create less strain on their cognitive functions, and social activity and exercise support cognitive functioning. The uni of analysis in this study was the dyad (CG and PwD), not the whole service system, and their daily life experiences and practices.

Twenty dyads participated in the intervention study. Dyads were selected according to the rules and protocols for clinical research at the Department of Psychiatry.<sup>1</sup> Participation in the study was completely voluntary, and participants could opt out at any point. One person in the study suffered from advanced dementia, half of the partici pants had mild dementia, and the others had moderate dementia.

Appendix A provides details regarding the locating systems' designs and capabilities. Both systems included a watch, which was equipped with sensors that would collect data on the location of the PwD.<sup>2</sup> The CG could retrieve this data via an app on her/his smartphone. Each locating system had three core functions: (a) The CG and the PwD could call each other via the watch, and the PwD could set off emergency calls; (b) the locating function allowed th CG to locate the PwD on the mobile app, and one GPS watch could track the route that the PwD has taken; and (c) the so-called “geofencing” function allowed the CG to define a zone where the PwD normally moves, and an alarm would be set off when the PwD moved outside this zone. Each dyad was provided with one of two systems to use for a month. After a month, the locating system was switched to the alternative watch. Results from the intervention study were published separately (Megges et al., 2018).

## 3.3 | Our theory-building study

Complementary to the intervention, our theory-building study assessed whether and how the dyads integrated th locating systems into their practices. We used an inductive analytical process to develop theoretical explanations and thus to analytically generalize the empirical findings (Gioia et al., 2013; Lee & Baskerville, 2003). Our analytical goa was to investigate how emotions, mental activities, physical activities, and smart technologies interacted in configur ing processes and to explain how these elements were mutually configured. We focused on the practice of location control (described below in findings) that PwDs and CGs enacted frequently. Locating systems were intended to sup port this practice by introducing technological capabilities to monitor and report on the PwD's location

## 3.4 | Data collection

There were three rounds of data collection. In the first round, dyads were interviewed about their life situations, how they managed situations in which the PwD could get lost or wander off, and what promise they would see in assistive technologies such as the locating systems that were provided to them. Right after the first interview. they partici pated in an interactive training session on how to use the locating systems. Members of the research team observe the dyad's use of these technologies during the training for about an hour in the hospital setting.<sup>3</sup> In the last step of the first round of data collection, dyads were interviewed again and asked whether their expectations had changed All interviews were recorded and transcribed.

## 3.5 | Data analysis

Using the inductive analytical approach proposed by Gioia et al. (2013), we immersed ourselves in the data, iterating between theory and data to generate first-order concepts, second-order themes, and aggregate theoretical dimensions (Breidbach & Maglio, 2016; Gioia et al., 2013; Klein & Myers, 1999; Miles & Huberman, 1994). We began by descriptively coding data using the software MaxODA. Text fragment codes reflect the informants' language. These first-order codes related to the location control practice included, as examples, “CG and PwD agree on when PwD will be home,” “PwD can go out on her/his own,” “CG and PwD fight,” “CG and PwD do not speak about locating system,” and “CG remains insecure whether system really works.” Building on first-order codes, we iterated between data interpretation, coding, and existing theory as we considered different theoretical approaches. We found that the literature on smart service systems (Beverungen et al., 2019; Lim et al., 2016; Lim & Maglio, 2018), theory on configuration in organization science (Siggelkow, 2001, 2002), and practice theory (Oborn et al., 2011; Reckwitz, 2002; Vaara & Whittington, 2012) best captured our evolving understanding of the data, and we subsequently integrated key theoretical concepts with empirically grounded codes to develop the analytical structure depicted in Figure 1. ## 4.2 | Enhanced smartness

We identified enhanced smartness as a configuration of the location control practice in which the smart technolog contributed to dyads experiencing reduced CG burden and enhanced quality of life. The following narrative illustrate how practice elements and the smart technology interacted as location control was carried out and thus how config uring unfolded.

It was a warm day in a small village that is located in a larger metropolitan area in Germany. Maria, a 72 year old person with dementia, and her husband Tim recently drove into town to see a psychiatric treatment unit where they received a GPS locating system. It consisted in a wearable, a watch, that Maria wore and an app that Tim used on his smartphone. The wearable provided data about Maria's location that Tim could retrieve in order to locate his wife. This option granted Tim increasing comfort that helped to ease his worries and fear that his wife might wander off and get lost when she was out alone. On this day, Maria and Tim drove to their daughter's house where Maria picked up Christina, their newborn granddaughter, and went for a walk with her. Maria enjoyed these walks and the time she got to spend with Christina when taking her for walks in the wood nearby their daughter's house. Meanwhile, Tim stayed at home with his daughter and prepared lunch. Maria cared deeply for her granddaughter and wanted Christina to be safe. Therefore, she stayed on trails that she felt she knew well and did her best to be back at her daughter's house in time. While Maria was out with Christina, she barely noticed that she wore the GPS watch. Instead, she focused on Christina and the love that she felt for her granddaughter. Maria also was happy that she could actually help her daughter by taking Christina out for a while. During the time that Maria and Christina were gone, Tim and his daughter frequently used the mobile app to locate Maria. They began locating her simply to test whether the locating system would function. When lunch time approached, both were keen to know where Maria and Christina were and located them close to the house, which meant all was well and that both were about to be back for the family having lunch together.

Maria and Tim's narrative illustrates how this configuration emerged through mutual configuring of the elements of practice and the smart technology. First, the locating system reinforced Tim's positive emotions (calmness, reduced concern) and Maria's emotions (self-confidence, enjoyment, love) as she engaged in new activities independently The locating system's ability to monitor and track Maria also substituted for Tim's physical presence. Both Tim and Maria were comfortable with the watch and mobile phone, so the locating system integrated seamlessly with their physical activities (wearing the GPS watch and using the mobile app). Importantly, the locating system provided Tim with enhanced information on where Maria was so he could decide what steps, if any, were needed. The system's capabilities for monitoring and communicating location data reinforced the mental activities that Tim routinely under took to keep track of Maria. Tim and Maria were thus able to maintain location control while engaging in other prac tices independently (walking with the granddaughter, making lunch), which otherwise they would not have done Caregiver burden on Tim was reduced, and Maria's quality of life enhanced.

The enhanced smartness configuration emerged through the four interrelated processes of configuring indi cated in Figure 1 as second-order themes. First, the smart technology reinforced positive emotions (1). PwD felt relieved because they knew that their loved ones could locate them. Rainer (72), who suffered from mild Alzheimer dementia, enjoyed cycling “sometimes [even] some 50 to 60 kilometers a day.” However, his wif could not keep pace, and Rainer often felt bad for his wife having to worry about him. Bianca, who cared fo Rüdiger, also experienced that the location data help her focus on positive emotions and avoid negative ones when Rüdiger was cycline alone:

It is great to know that I do not have to worry. I can go to sleep and do not have to sit outside and wait for him. I know he is on his way and I can remain calm. (Interview Bianca)

Mental activities such as learning how to use the locating system along with friends or family members not only hel ped dyads to use the system but also increased their self-confidence and pleasure in doing so. For example, Lothar who cared for his mother, explained:

It really helps when there is a second person around that helps her learning the system. (Interview Lothar)

In addition to reinforcing positive emotions, the locating systems substituted for physical activities, such as the CG having to collocate with the PwD all the time (2). Clara (CG) would normally not allow Wolfgang (PwD) to go out alone, but with the GPS watch, she allowed him to walk around more freely.

He went to the mailbox. This meant that he had to cross the main road and walk around some corners alone. Normally we would have not done this. (Interview Clara)

By freeing the dyad from the need to be always collocated, both the CG and PwD engaged in other activities inde pendently (as with Maria and Tim). Even a mundane day-to-day practice like walking the dog can be challenging fo PwDs. When Ernst (PwD) went out with the dog, Manuela (CG) grew concerned if Ernst was not back on time. With the GPS locating system monitoring him, this simple and enjoyable activity was more comfortable for both of them Manuela explained:

I already look at the watch when Ernst is a little bit late. But this happens so often, he meets some other people with their dogs and they start talking. Locating systems just help with that. (Interview Manuela)

The above quotation also illustrates how the locating systems reinforced new mental and physical activities (3). Man uela used the locating system to assess whether she needed to take action or not, whereas Ernst took on activitie by himself. To do so, it was important that the physical properties of the locating system integrated seamlessly wit physical activities. This was particularly important for PwDs as well as CGs, who are often elderly people. For exam ple, Maria (72) told us that she did not notice the watch much, while Rainer recognized the watch simply as new watch. Such designs encouraged routine and reliable use of the watches and thus ensured that the PwD and CG were actually connected through the locating system. As dyads gained confidence in the locating system, they wer willing to explore more opportunities to use it, its use became increasingly routine to them, and they could enjo more and varied activities as a result.

The location data provided by the locating system reinforced mental activities typical of the location control practice (4). The CG needs to know where the PwD is in order to know better what actions he or she should do. Th locating system provided that information to the CG. Knowing that the PwD was in a particular place then informed knowing how the CG should respond to the situation. For example, Rüdiger (PwD) worked night and early mornin shifts. His wife Bianca was concerned because he cycled to work and back, taking 90 minutes per trip. When she saw that he was on his way back from work, she knew he was likely safe and the situation did not demand a response Similarly, Bianca would not call Rüdiger at work because she felt he should be able to focus on working. Some CG did not use the call function, depending on where the GPS watch located the PwD, to reduce potential embarrassment for the PwD, as Sabine recalled:

If I call him when he plays table tennis, for example, his friends will wonder what he is carrying there […] even though they all know about this [that he tested a locating system] […] I tried to call him when I knew he was alone. (Interview Sabine)

Through these reinforcing interactions, the locating system elaborated dyads' location control practices: CGs were better informed about the PwD's location and could make better decisions on how to respond, and both CG and PwD could undertake more activities with greater enjoyment and confidence. The accounts detailed above emphasize how the locating system reconfigured practice elements to engender these positive outcomes. Of course, dyad also had to configure the locating system to some degree; for instance, the PwD had to wear the watch consistently and the CG had to keep it charged. These reciprocal interactions were not problematic in the enhanced smartnes configuration. When this was not the case, configuring unfolded differently, leading to different configurations of location control.

## 4.3 | Degraded smartness

We identified degraded smartness as a configuration in which interactions between practice elements and the locat ing system disrupted dyads' location control practices. The CG and the PwD experienced stress, frustration, and anger, often leading them to stop using the locating system and revert to pre-intervention practices as colocation Dieter and Ute's account illustrates this negative reinforcing pattern.

Dieter and Ute were initially excited when a psychiatric treatment unit invited them to test a locating system. Dieter, who suffered from dementia, was a former engineer and technology freak who enjoyed trying out new tools and experimenting with them. Hence, both had no doubt that testing the locating system would help them cope with Dieter's cognitive impairment, which began having noticeable effects on his memory and capacity to handle everyday life. However, when Dieter and Ute returned home from their first visit to the treatment unit, things began to turn worse. True to his passion for IT, Dieter unpacked the locating system and wanted to learn its functions immediately. He looked at all the buttons on the watch trying to make sense of what they would mean and memorize all their details. Moreover, he was keen to learn the user manual and understand the many details that were described. When trying to learn these technical details, Dieter began feeling overwhelmed. He simply could not remember everything in the same way that he was able to when he worked as engineer. Consequently, Dieter felt a sense of frustration and was reluctant to use the locating system. The situation worsened when Ute sensed that her husband would not use the locating system to the extent that she had expected. She approached him about it, but that made him afraid that Ute would recognize his difficulties with learning to use the system. He turned her down saying that he disliked the system. Ute waited for a day and then tried to talk to her husband again, feeling that the situation grew increasingly tense. This made Ute try harder to convince her husband to use the locating system but the harder she tried the more difficult the situation became. Ultimately Dieter got so mad at Ute that she feared that he would destroy the locating system. Days passed and every time Ute tried to approach Dieter about using the locating system he walked away from her. Ute began to lock the entire house leaving open only one door allowing Dieter to enter the garden so that she could be sure to be around him.

Dieter and Ute's vignette reveals how disruptive interactions could develop. The material design of the locating system overwhelmed Dieter's mental ability to learn how it worked, reinforcing negative emotions about his condition. Dieter's distress reinforced negative emotions for Ute, as she became increasingly frustrated with hi refusal to wear the GPS watch. Ute's attempts to convince Dieter to use the system made him more resistant and defiant, for instance, avoiding her in the house and threatening to run away or to destroy the watch. Disappointed that the locating system had not helped, Ute reverted to their earlier control practice, such as locking Dieter i the house.

The degraded smartness configuration developed through three related configuring processes (see Figure 1, second-order themes). First, smart technology reinforced negative emotions for the PwD and sometimes the CG (5). In some instances, learning how to use or maintain the locating system was too challenging because of the material design of the locating system and the dyads' cognitive impairments or age. This frustrated or angered the PwD, as it did for Dieter. Others found the material design unpleasant and embarrassing. The PwD Hildegard complained about one locating system, saying “my arms are so thin and this is such a monster. If I would wear it, everyone would ask me ‘what on earth are you wearing?’” (Interview Hildegard). Heinrich, Hildegard's CG, shared with us that Hildegard felt overwhelmed and discriminated against when being asked to use the locating system:

This watch was just too complicated. And she denies that she needs such technology […] She simply rejects it [locating system] and there is nothing that you can do about this. […] But I have to say that she also feels discriminated by it [locating system]. (Interview Heinrich)

Reinforcing negative emotions disrupted interactions among other elements of the practice. When Ute tried to convince Dieter to use the locating system, Ute reported that “he became really aggressive because I kept on trying to convince him” (Interview Ute). When Heinrich “threatened” and “tried to force” Hildegard to wear the watch, her defences increased against putting on the watch (Interview Heinrich). For Rainer and Sabine, having to expend men tal activities to monitor and maintain the watch heightened negative emotions:

I always have to charge the watch sometime in the afternoon. This makes him angry. He says ‘this is shit why would I need that’? (Interview Sabine)

With reinforced negative emotions, continuing attempts to integrate the locating system with the dyads' physica activities were futile (6). This was evident when Dieter began avoiding Ute so that she would not pressure him abou using the locating system. This frustrated her ability to even collocate with him, leading her to lock doors to restrict his movements. Similarly, Heinrich reported that Hildegard tried to hide the watch or left the watch on the kitchen table and simply walked away, sometimes out of their apartment and leaving him without any information on her location or movements.

She did not take the watch with her. It was in our apartment all the time in the kitchen or on the board. (Interview Heinrich)

More generally, attempts to use the locating system could destabilize a dyad's ability to practice location control. Udo's moderate dementia meant that he could no longer put the GPS watch on himself. His CG Annegret would put the watch around his arm before she left for work in the morning. One day, Udo unintentionally damaged the watch when he forgot to take it off before showering, thus neutralizing its ability to monitor and communicate his location to Annegret while she was at work. Another time, Annegret simply forgot to use the locating system when Udo wan dered off. Annegret explained:

Udo went to the bath room in our flat but on his way back he just walked out of the door, into stairway, and two floors up. We immediately felt that he was away for too long, panicked, and went to search him. But I simply forgot to use the locating system in that hectic moment. (Interview Annegret)

Technical problems such as the need for frequent battery charging contributed to some dyads giving up on the locat ing system and reverting to colocation as a means of enacting control:

He was home all the time because the battery was empty all the time. (Interview Manuela)

Marion (CG) resented the need to constantly be colocated with Volker (PwD), but the dyad still reverted to this approach when they were unable to make the locating system work for them. She reported:

He does not leave the house without me. We are together 24/7. It is terrible. (Interview Marion)

Technical difficulties with the locating system increased the mental burdens on dyads (7). Maintaining the locating system required additional efforts that proved disruptive. For instance, CG Marion described how maintaining th GPS watch further disrupted her life:

This was so stressful because I constantly watched the charger and this is strenuous, especially at night. I sleep very badly anyway but now I was watching the charger all night waiting for it to flip from red to green. (Interview Marion)

In Volker and Marion's case, Volker did not want to use the GPS watch because he already had a regular watch. Mar ion had him wear it on one arm and his regular watch on the other arm, but keeping track of the locating system wa a problem, because Volker began “taking the locating system on and off, leading to me searching for it all the time” (Interview Marion).

The locating system at times provided apparently erroneous information that the CG then had to interpret, whic also increased mental burdens. Bettina (PwD) and Thorsten (CG) got increasingly frustrated when the locating sys tems send locations that were obviously wrong. Thorsten explained tha

So we are out in the country side, maybe 100 kilometers away from home. We are walking around the lake and all of the sudden she is away. I locate her and the system tells me that she is in the middle of that lake. I was asking myself ‘seriously?! Why should she be lying in the middle of that lake?! (Interview Thorsten)

In such instances, the information provided by the locating system was not valuable to the CG or the PwD to know what the situation was or know how to resolve it. When the CG had to assess the validity of the location the GPS watch provided, knowing how to respond to the situation was not improved. Manuela, for example, said that “eve with this system I could never leave him alone,” a statement that arguably contradicts why locating systems were introduced in the first place.

Through these configuring processes, the locating system failed to elaborate the core elements of dyads' location control practices so as to enhance the practice and their quality of life. Instead. their attempts to use it resulted ir negative interactions that disrupted other aspects of their practices, diminished quality of life, and increased CG burden. Degraded smartness thus illustrates how practice elements and smart technologies can be mutually configure so that the smart technology remains an external, or even threatening, element of the configuration

## 4.4 | Fragile smartness

We identified fragile smartness as a configuration of the location control practice that engendered tentative, and at times contradictory, outcomes. In this configuration, dyads frequently experienced technical problems with the locating systems or concerns about its possible breakdown. However, unlike with degraded smartness, dyads also experienced some comfort when using the locating systems. The following narrative illustrates this configuration:

Clara and Wolfgang were used to spending most of their time together. Clara estimated that, due to her husband's dementia, she accompanied him about 85 per cent of the time. However, Wolfgang stil went out on his own, for example, to cycle, to go for a walk, and to see a social service twice a week. In these instances, Clara grew increasingly worried that something might happen to Wolfgang. Therefore, she was excited to work with the psychiatric treatment unit that supplied her and Wolfgang with a locating system that would enable her to find him when he was out on his own. She became a keen user of the locating system as she frequently tried to spot her husband. She even sent Wolfgang out by himself once in order to see if she could locate him. However, while the system sometimes worked well, she experienced increasing frustration with technical difficulties. Her frequent use strained the battery of Wolfgang's watch as locating services demand a lot of power. As a result the battery died frequently when Wolfgang was out. Clara kept trying to fix the problem because she felt a little bit of relief with the locating system. The additional effort to make the system work infuriated her and she began sharing her anger with Wolfgang. Nonetheless she stated in her final interview: ‘This [locating system] is basically a very good idea.

This narrative reveals how mutual configuring of smart technologies and practice elements entailed interactions that were both positively and negatively reinforcing. For Clara, when the locating systems could locate Wolfgang, she felt relief, and Wolfgang could enjoy doing activities on his own. However, the locating system was a source of uncer tainty, requiring Clara to attend to the system as well as to Wolfgang. Her own actions using the system frequentl contributed to problems. The added burdens of trying to make the system work heightened her frustration and anger directed to the device but reinforced her desire to do so.

Fragile smartness developed through three related configuring processes (see Figure 1, second-order themes) First, the smart technology reinforced both positive and negative emotions (8). This was evident when Clara experi enced hopefulness and relief using the locating system but was extremely critical and frustrated that the system did not work reliably, She also experienced technical and design issues reguiring additional mental and physical activities such as attending constantly to the battery or figuring out the system interfaces. Similarly, CG Bianca expressed posi tive emotions about the potential of the locating system but frustration and insecurity over its features. She com mented that “Yes this [locating system] was good” but bemoaned:

Well the design was not so good. […] He always pushed this one button but that was for regulating the sound level […] He became increasingly insecure because of this and I had no idea which button he needed to push. That was a real problem. (Interview Bianca)

Marion (CG) also related her mixed interactions with the locating systems: “Well if you take the problems away, it is fairly good” (Interview Marion).

Provided that locating systems reinforced positive as well as negative emotions, dyads continued to use the sys tems. However, erratic systems at times destabilized mental and physical activities in their practices (9). The locating systems could not be relied on to function properly all the time. Dyads reported interrupted connectivity of the GPS watch or the mobile phone to the Internet. This meant the GPS watch could not monitor and report the PwD's location. For example, Diane (PwD) frequently wore the watch while walking out in the woods. However, the watch could not send signals in the woods, and the digital interconnection between her and Detlef (CG) disappeared Detlef explained this disrupted his ability to locate her precisely but still gave him information to act on if a problem arose:

Diane was out in the wood and I wanted to locate her. The app did not work so I tried to call the watch. She did not respond but called back explaining that she was close to some gate and many branches around her. While I did not know where exactly she was, it was good to know she was safe. (Interview Detlef)

The dyads also had to use the locating system correctly to enable the system to work as intended. In particular, PwD had to remember to wear the watch reliably. When the CG and PwD were separated, the CG first had to determin if the PwD was actually wearing the watch. If not, the CG had to resort to other information sources to decide what to do. Detlef related, “sometimes she forgot to take the watch and before I had realized she was already gone […] but in the end, we used the watch quite a bit” (Interview Detlef). Similarly, Bianca, who identified the number of button as a design issue, associated her husband's problems using the watch with his tendency to forget to wear it. Sh explained:

When he turned it on, he pushed the button and nothing happened. I must say he had his difficulties and I think that he sometimes forgot the watch because of that. (Interview Bianca)

The locating system only sporadically reinforced the dyads' mental activities, because the system did not always accurately report location data (10). When the locating system did accurately locate the PwD and communicate this information, the CG could then decide what to do (eg, go in search or wait for return). However, sometimes the information was wrong or confusing, and the CG had to interpret what the information actually implied about the PwD's location by relying on his or her own knowledge of the terrain, the locating system, and the PwD's habits. Bianc explained her thought processes:

I would say I could not find him 30 per cent of the time. I checked his route on the Internet, too, but it said ‘location unavailable.’ I think the connection was bad and when he cycles through the wood or other areas, it [locating system] simply had no connection to GPS. (Interview Bianca)

Horst's experience also illustrates that locating the PwD with the locating system required additional sensemaking on his part:

I could not locate her inside the mall. I mean, I know my wife, I know how long it takes her to walk to the mall and when she was going there. I located her at the bus stop right before the mall, right in front of the mall but no longer when she was in the mall. (Interview Horst)

To make the locating system work for them, dyads developed know how around dealing with and solving technica problems. Some CGs expended considerable mental effort bug fixing and experimenting with the locating system For example, Reinhard discovered that “You could only solve this technical problem when you switched the watc off at night and switched it on again in the morning” (Interview Reinhard). He also received messages from the app that he could not make sense off and encountered problems with GPS connections, so that he could not alway depend on information provided by the locating system:

It was completely weird. Sometimes it worked sometimes it did not […] So I put quite some effort into trial and error learning how this system works. Since this thing is only useful when you know how it behaves. […] But the idea is great. (Interview Reinhard)

To summarize, the fragile smartness configuration developed through interactions of both positive and negative rein forcement between the locating system and practice elements. Dyads experienced some improvements in quality of life and safety for the PwD but also disappointment and even anger due to the system being unreliable and hard to use. They had to tolerate instability and heightened variability in their location control practice and to expend pre cious mental capacity to gain some value from the system.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

The intervention study provided a nuanced view on the experiences of 20 dyads composed of a family CG and PwD as they attempted to integrate smart technologies (locating systems) into their practices for maintaining control of the PwD's location. Some dyads found the locating systems could ease CG burden and enhance qual ity of life, at least part of the time, while others experienced little or no value from the systems. We analyse these experiences by attending closely to how interactions between the locating systems and elements of prac tices arose in configuring processes. This allowed us to identify varied configurations in how location contro was enacted (enhanced, degraded, or fragile smartness). (see Figure 1). We now theoretically generalize from this study (Lee & Baskerville, 2003), and the theoretical foundations outlined earlier to articulate a proces model of configuring in practice. Figure 2 serves as a guide to this discussion.

To begin, we view configurations as tentatively enacted arrangements of core and elaborating elements and their interactions, which characterize human practices (Oborn et al., 2011; Reckwitz, 2002). Underlying configurations (a noun-like concept), configuring (a verb-like concept) is the process through which core elements of practices (such as location control) and elaborating elements of smart technologies mutually configure one another (Barrett et al. 2016, 2012; Siggelkow, 2002). Our approach contrasts with views of configuration as primarily self-adjusting actions by smart technologies (Barile & Polese, 2010; Demirkan et al., 2016; Spohrer et al., 2017) or as states that complex systems evolve towards (Misangyi et al., 2017; Short et al., 2008). We also allow that configuring may progress i unexpected ways and with unexpected results, such as configurations that lack coherence and cohesion (Barrett et al., 2016, 2012).

On the basis of Siggelkow's (2002) distinction between core and elaborating elements and drawing on practice theory, we highlight emotions, mental activities, and physical activities as core elements of practice. These element were particularly salient in our study of assistive health care technologies and are generally of analytic interest (Ortiz de Guinea & Markus, 2009; Reckwitz, 2002; Stein, Newell, Wagner, & Galliers, 2014, 2015). To articulate a finer grained analysis of the elaborating elements introduced through the locating system, we use Lim and Maglio's (2018 dimensions (connection, collection, computation, and communication) as the bundle of capabilities the smart technol ogy presented. This allows us to trace how both humans and smart technology participated in configuring the dyads location control practices.

The purpose of the intervention study was to introduce smart technologies (locating systems) to dyads and to assess whether the CG and PwD could utilize them to reduce CG burden and improve quality of life. The locating systems introduced elaborating elements into the dyads' practices: the locating systems' ability to collect data on th PwD's location (connection and collection) and to remotely report location data to the CG (computation and commu nication) (Figure 2. point A). The locating systems were intended to allow the CG to track and locate the PwD without constantly colocating with him or her and at the same time to reduce worry and concern (reinforcing interactions among connection, collection, communication, mental and physical activities, and emotion). Such interac tions would have to develop in the mutual configuring of elements through enactments of the location control prac tice (Figure 2, point B).

Mutual configuring of reinforcing interactions was apparent in dyads' reports of enhanced smartness (Figure 2, point C). For example, some CGs reported checking on the PwD's location data because they were worried about the PwD (interactions of emotion, connection, and collection). If location data showed that the PwD was with friends the CG knew the PwD was safe and refrained from calling to avoid embarrassing the PwD (interactions of emotion, connection, collection, and mental activity). Generally, enhanced smartness developed when the PwD found the GPS watch acceptable and readily wore it so that the watch could monitor the PwD's location (interactions of physica activities, connection, and collection). The CG then retrieved location data on the mobile app (interactions of menta activities, computation, and communication); one locating system also warned the CG when the PwD moved outsid designated boundaries (interaction of mental activities, computation, and communication). When the dvad was confi dent and competent in its use, the locating system substituted for the CG and PwD being colocated all the time (interaction of mental and physical activities, connection, collection, and communication). This then allowed the CG and PwD to undertake activities such as babysitting, hiking, or biking independently, which stimulated pleasure, joy, and so on and lessened worry or embarrassment (interactions emotion and physical activity).

Through these dynamics of reinforcing interactions, the enhanced smartness configuration developed. Achieving and maintaining enhanced smartness were not effortless, however, as the CG and PwD also had to adopt mental and physical activities to reinforce smart technology's capabilities. In this setting (assistive technologies, elderly people and dementia home care), these activities could be burdensome. For instance, the CGs had to keep track of the GP watch (as well as the PwD), monitor and charge its battery, and encourage and assist the PwD to wear the device The PwD had to remember to wear the watch and comprehend its simplified features for calling. That is, mutually reinforcing (two way) interactions between core practice elements and the locating system's elaborating element were necessary for enhanced smartness to develop

Proposition 1. Enhanced smartness develops when mutual configuring of core practice elements and elaboratin smart technology elements unfolds through reinforcing interactions.

This foundational proposition allows us to consider how and why configuring unfolds as it does. When many mutually reinforcing interactions with elaborating elements develop, elaborating elements can become tightl networked with core elements and evolve into core elements of the practice (Siggelkow, 2002) (see Figure 2, point C). Core elements are important in configuring processes, as changes in core elements are likely to affect other core elements and also influence how future configurations develop (Siggelkow, 2001, 2002). For instance, some dyads reported that as they grew more comfortable with the locating systems, they enjoyed greater freedom to engage with activities independently and with less worry about maintaining the PwD's safety, and they grew more reliant on the locating system. The elaborating elements (the locating system's connection and communication capabilities) thu evolved to be more core to practice as the locating system increasingly substituted for the mental and physical activi ties of colocation.

Proposition 2. Enhanced smartness configurations are more likely to persist when dense networks of reinforcing interactions develop so that elaborating elements evolve into core elements.

It is important to account as well for why enhanced smartness does not develop as expected. In our study, th locating system's elaborating elements (its capabilities) could become disconnected from and thus could not reinforce core practice elements if mutually reinforcing interactions did not develop. For instance, if the PwD did not wear the GPS watch or the dyad could not keep the batteries charged, the locating system could not reinforce the CGs' menta activities or substitute for physical activities such as colocation, and reinforcement of positive emotions did not fol low. Such actions may seem trivial, but they proved to be substantial barriers for some of the dyads we studied. Th CG's or PwD's actions sometimes even disrupted interaction between location system elements, for instance, when the CG overused the locating app and accidently ran down the GPS watch battery (negative reinforcement of menta activities, connection, and collection) or the PwD forgot to take the GPS watch off in the shower (disruptive interac tion of mental and physical activities and connection).

Negative reinforcing or disruptive interactions contributed to the dyads enacting degraded smartness (Figure 2, point D) or fragile smartness (Figure 2, point E). With degraded smartness, dyads reported the location system's prop erties and operation (size and complexity of the watch and battery issues) triggered negative emotions by challenging the PwD's cognitive abilities, embarrassing the PwD, and so on. As a result, the PwD refused to wear the watch or even threatened to hide or damage it. If the PwD did not wear the GPS watch, positive reinforcing interactions (such as substituting connection and communication for colocation) could not develop. Instead, the dyad reverted to earlie patterns. This was not simply a return to the status quo, however. Simply trying to use the locating system could b burdensome for the dyads and degrade their practice. For instance, when a CG tried to force the PwD to wear th watch, emotional tensions worsened and the PwD acted out of defiance (hiding the watch or avoiding the CG); th CG then took more extreme actions such as locking the PwD in the house. Another CG described how monitoring its battery power disrupted her night's sleep.

In fragile smartness, interactions involved both positive emotions (hope and relief) and negative emotions (frus tration and anger) when the dyads tried using the locating system. Mental and physical activities were intermittentl positively reinforced, when the locating system operated as expected, but there was additional mental burden on th CG when the system failed to work as expected. The locating system could not always adjust to its environment such as when the GPS watch could not maintain connection in wooded areas or communicate location data to the mobile phone's locating app. This placed additional burden on the CG to draw on their understanding and knowledg of terrain, GPS operation, the PwD's habits, and so on to interpret the limited information provided by locating system and to assess the PwD's likely situation (mental activities reinforcing connection and communication). Nonethe less, some dyads kept working with the locating systems despite difficulties.

Proposition 3. Fragile smartness develops when negatively reinforcing or disruptive interactions are counterbalanced by positively reinforcing interactions among the smart technology's elaborating elements and th practice's core elements. Degraded smartness develops when negatively reinforcing or disruptive interactions hinder any positively reinforcing elements from developing

Emotions typical of the location control practice—worry, concern, frustration, and embarrassment—had a piv otal influence on how mutual configuring among elements unfolded. Smart service systems scholars have tended to subsume emotion under terms such as “human-centeredness” (Maglio et al., 2015) or “value co-creation” (Lim & Maglio, 2018). However, recent information systems research has demonstrated that emotions play critica roles in humans' responses to technology (Stein et al., 2014, 2015). In enhanced smartness, there were no disrup tive or negative reinforcing interactions between the locating system and dyads' emotions. Instead, the location system's connection and communication elements positively reinforced the dyad's mental and physical activities, which then reinforced positive emotions. With fragile smartness, the locating system reinforced both positive and negative emotions. These countervailing interactions with emotion allowed additional positively and negativel reinforcing interactions to develop when the dyads persisted trying to use the locating system. With degraded smartness, the locating system reinforced negative emotions (particularly for the PwD), which hindered any rein forcing interactions from developing between the locating system elements and other practice elements. These findings are consistent with configuration theory, which allows that interactions among elements may differ in dif ferent configurations (Siggelkow, 2001, 2002). We also echo the argument of Stein et al. (2015) that oppositiona and explicitly mixed emotions (positive and negative) are distinct responses compared with purely positive or neg ative responses to information systems, and they may elicit distinctive use strategies.

Proposition 4. How emotions and smart technology elements mutually configure influences significantly whether enhanced, fragile, or degraded smartness configurations develop. At a minimum, countervailing positive and negativ interactions between smart technologies and emotions may be necessary to allow for other reinforcing interaction to develop.

The model of configuring in practice and these propositions analytically generalize our study to inform researc in other contexts (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). However, there are boundary conditions to our theorizing it is important to consider. Mutual configuring between humans and smart technologies is likely to have more substantial implica tions for how smart service systems perform when smart technologies are introduced into intimate human practices, such as the family caregiving practices we studied. In such settings, humans and smart technologies will interact directly, each will be visible to the other, and interface design issues (such as the large GPS watches in our study and the watch batteries) will influence how configuring in practice unfolds. If smart technologies primarily interact wit other smart services on the “backstage,” mutual configuring in practice between human and technology actors may be less important to smart service systems performance. When smart technologies are introduced to the practices of collective actors, such as organizations, the core practice elements relevant to mutual configuring will differ. Fo instance, emotion was an important practice element in our study and we posit will be in many other practice contexts as well, but organizations do not, per se, have emotions. Research on organizational configurations such as Sig gelkow (2001, 2002) will be useful to identify core practice elements of collective actors in these cases

## 5.1 | Implications for theory

A foundational premise of smart service systems is that they can configure and reconfigure in response to changing environmental conditions in order to enhance value cocreation (Beverungen et al., 2019; Breidbach & Maglio, 2016 National Science Eoundation. 2014). To date, this literature has emphasized the capabilities of smart technologies to drive configuring and reconfiguring while saying little about how humans participate in these processes (Demirkan et al., 2015; Polese et al., 2016; Spohrer et al., 2017). Adopting a human-centred perspective is important to under stand and anticipate when configuring, may lead to anticipated or unintended conseguences (Maglio, 2015). To address these issues, the model of configuring in practice we develop in this paper advances theoretic understandin of configuration in smart service systems in several important ways.

Following Bakken and Hernes (2006), we theorize configuring as well as configuration to develop novel insights about configuration in smart service systems. These terms appear in the literature but without elaboration of what they mean (Beverungen et al., 2019; Maglio & Lim, 2016; Medina-Borja, 2015). Similarly, the term “element” is not well specified or is limited to technology features (Badinelli et al., 2012; Barile & Polese, 2010; Calza et al., 2015) The conceptual framework of configuring in practice offers a much more granular vocabulary to analyse those ele ments in smart service systems that are related to humans, ie, emotions, mental activity, and physical activity, and demonstrates their roles in configuring are substantive. Drawing from practice theory (Oborn et al., 2011; Reckwitz 2002), we explicate how configurations arise through mutually configuring interactions between smart technologies and elements of practice. This allows us to unpack interactions so as to detect conditions that facilitate or inhibit value cocreation. For instance, examining mutually configuring sequences of interactions could help explain why ele ments of a configuration exhibit consonance or resonance (Barile & Polese, 2010; Polese et al., 2016). Our stud identified reinforcing (positive and negative) and disruptive interactions among emotions, mental and physical activi ties, and smart technology capabilities (connection, collection, computation, and communication). This allowed us to explain how configuring developed through cascading changes in interactions, such as when the connection and communication capabilities of the locating system reinforced dyads' mental and physical activities and positive emo tions, as well as when these capabilities reinforced negative emotions, disrupted physical activities, and burdened mental activities. This suggests that consonance or resonance should be considered as emergent rather than static properties of smart service systems.

Smart technologies are generally portraved as supplementing or enriching human activities. for instance. by inte grating resources so that value propositions can be realized (Beverungen et al., 2017, 2019). We demonstrate that “smartness” is not simply a desirable state towards which service systems gravitate (Beverungen et al., 2019; Lim & Maglio, 2018) but instead may evolve in varied directions, such as enhanced, degraded, or fragile smartness. Ou model of configuring in practice also highlights reciprocal reinforcing interactions in which humans supplement th capabilities of smart technologies that are critical for value to be realized. In our study, the mental and physical activi ties dyads had to provide, such as wearing the GPS watch, maintaining the battery, and interpreting limited or errone ous location information, were commonplace but also essential to the locating system enacting its capabilities (connection and communication). Addressing configurations as tentatively enacted arrangements of core and elabo rating elements thus emphasizes configurations as the joint accomplishments of human and technology actors (Orliwkoski 2000).

In this regard, we draw on organizational configuration research (Siggelkow, 2001, 2002) in our treatment of practice elements as core elements and the capabilities of smart technologies as elaborating elements in configura tions. We do this to theorize humans as coequal agents in configuring and contribute to theoretic balance vis-à-vis technology-centric approaches (Demirkan et al., 2015; Polese et al., 2016; Spohrer et al., 2017). We acknowledge there are likely smart service applications in which practice elements may act to elaborate the core capabilities of smart technologies. Our approach problematizes this question, however, rather than assuming the primacy of smart technologies in configuring processes.

Theorizing how practice elements and the capabilities of smart technologies are mutually configured in practice expands inguiry into how smart technologies and smart services may redraw boundaries between humans and tech: nologies (Barrett et al., 2012, 2016). Smart technologies are designed to bring to service systems capabilities previ ously attributed to human beings, such as learning through experience, (semi-) autonomous adjusting of future actions, and making decisions. There are ethical implications as well as practical design concerns to address, as such capabilities and associated. “rights and responsibilities” shift towards nonhuman actors. Our configuring in practice model provides a conceptual framework and foundational propositions to address these issues. For instance, redesign of a problematic human-technology interface might allow reconfiguring of fragile or degraded smartness into enhanced smartness by removing negative reinforcing or disruptive interactions. Substituting smart technologies fo human's physical activities and mental activities presents more complex issues. Replacing the CG with a caregiving robot, for example, would profoundly redraw the boundaries of dvads' location control practice: careful analysis of configuring in practice would be essential to the design, implementation, and evaluation of such a smart, service Whether and how the boundaries of emotions and smart technology capabilities will be (re)configured is an intriguing theoretical and ethical question We posit that at, least some positive reinforcing, interactions between smart technologies and emotions are necessary for other reinforcing interactions to develop (Proposition 4). How smart technologies can sense and respond to human emotions is a question that technology innovators are activel pursuing. An even more profound question is whether smart technologies could (or should) take responsibility fo emotions associated with human practices (such as the CG feeling concern for the PwD) in a meaningful and authen tic manner.

Finally, our study expands the applications of practice theory in the information systems field (Barrett et al. 2012, 2016; Orlikowski, 2000; Oborn et al., 2011) to address configuration in smart service systems. Earlier practice theory accounts tend to cast information technologies as passive information objects that actors draw on (Obor et al., 2011) and that have no ontological reality outside of their use in practice (Orlikowski, 2000). More recent work theorizes how the materiality of technologies influence practices (Barrett et al., 2012, 2016) but has yet to theorize the extent to which smart technologies are (and will be) designed to take action and express agency autonomously from the actions of human actors (Beverungen et al., 2017, 2019; Medina-Borja, 2015). Our configuring in practice framework brings human actors to the theoretical foreground while also allowing for these human-like capabilities of smart technologies. In our discussion of configuring in practice (Figure 2), we suggest Lim and Maglio's (2018) dimen sions of connection, collection, computation, and communication as a starting point for. “unboxing” smart systems capabilities in such studies

## 5.2 | Implications for practice

Our study holds several important implications for business practice. The hope is that smart service systems wil enhance economic and societal outcomes. However, technology innovations are often informed primarily by engi neering-driven approaches that grant technical design weight while discounting how such smart technologies become used. A practice theory approach can inform the design of smart technologies and help to illuminate how smartness is configured (or does not develop) through this interplay. Unless practitioners consider these tempora developments, we are unlikely to uncover potentially adverse effects of smart technologies. Practitioners must thu be mindful to not only collect and analyse fine-grained data about how individuals use smart technologies but also attend to how individual emotion and mental and physical activity influence and are influenced by smar technologies.

The domain of our empirical study is health care, where the use of assistive smart technologies to enhance care of persons with chronic conditions such as dementia could play a vital role in improving quality of life and restraining societal and economic costs. This study highlights several key challenges and opportunities to realizing these out comes. First, our practice-theory account of how and when the locating systems were integrated into participants day-to-day practices illustrated the multifaceted challenges of developing and deploying smart service systems to address the needs of patients. The less-than-perfect systems provided some relief for some dyads, but, unexpectedly, they also increased CG burden and stress for others. Anticipating and addressing issues such as the PwD's resistanc to using large, unattractive devices and Internet connectivity problems will require innovators to consider a wide range of practice-related issues. The practice theory approach we adapt here can be useful to learn about thes issues during design and early roll-out phases.

On a more general note, to our knowledge, our study is among the few combining an intervention study from medicine with a social science study attempted to generate theoretical insights. Our study revealed that through attending to practice in these real-life settings, we can disclose in much detail how interventions intended to help users can have unintended effects. In combination, such interdisciplinary studies can examine whether or not smar technologies are helpful but also explain why this was the case or not. Analytic theorization from one particular set ting can then inform design of interventions in other settings in which specific empirical outcomes may not be as relevant.

## 5.3 | Limitations and future research

As with all research, our study has several limitations. First, while the interdisciplinary design of the intervention study reported here provided the uncommon opportunity to study closely the experiences of users of this assistive technology, it required some compromise relative to the ideal for research design and methods. For instance, the clin ical ethics protocol did not allow for in-home observation, which would have provided deeper insights into the loca tion control practices of study participants. We relied on the dyads' narrative reports in interviews for our analysis which are subject to some post-hoc rationalization and problems with remembering situations. The psychiatrists observations of dyads in the clinic and their observation notes provided insights in addition to interviews but did not substitute for observations in the home setting. Even if observation is not possible in other settings because of pri vacy concerns, future studies might draw unobtrusive observational data such as from the smart technologies them selves, for instance, on amount of use, timing of use, and so on, and study participants could be encouraged to keep detailed diaries to enhance recall in interviews.

A limitation of our study for fully addressing smart service systems is our focus on dyads of PwD and CG. This allowed us to explore configuration within smart service systems in depth, but it did not capture all aspects of famil care service systems, for instance, when other family members played a part, when police were called to search fo PwD, or how PwD and CG interacted with other community resources. A broader scope of study for a smart servic system involving multiple, intersecting practices could provide valuable insights on how configuring unfolds acros (as well as within) practices and smart technologies.

The practice that we studied—family members locating a PwD—is arguably a specialized yet important practice given the growing social and economic costs of dementia. For our study, this practice served as revelatory case (Yin 2013) enabling analytical generalization (Lee & Baskerville, 2003) to literature on smart service systems. That being said, it is important to test and refine our arguments across practices, industries, and cultural backgrounds. Similar studies in other settings could deepen the practice perspective on configuration we developed here and may revea other practice elements or additional variants of configuration patterns that are important in other contexts. Addi tional study, and particularly longitudinal study, will provide more insights on why unexpected outcomes such a degraded smartness develop and whether degraded smartness configurations might shift to enhanced smartness.

## 6 | CONCLUDING REMARKS

Smart service systems bring together humans and smart technologies in intimate ways. But how will they interact and with what consequences? This paper offers one approach to investigating these broad challenges to technology innovation, business development, and to human societies in our technology-infused world. Our paper develops a human-centred perspective on the basis of practice theory to investigate how humans and smart tech nologies interact to configure smart service systems in day-to-day practices and thus how value may (or may not) be cocreated with smart service systems. We demonstrate that “smartness” cannot be taken for granted as desir able state towards which service systems gravitate (Beverungen et al., 2019; Lim & Maglio, 2018) but instead may become configured in varied, and unexpected, ways such as enhanced, degraded, or fragile smartness. Whil much remains to be done in research, practice, and policy, we hope that our model of configuring in practice wil be instructive for these endeavours.

## AUTHOR BIOGRAPHIES

Lauri Wessel is an associate professor of management and organization at the University of Bremen, Germany. He uses organization theory in order to understand and design health care IT. Lauri's work has been published in an interdisciplinary range of journals including the Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Business and Information Systems Engineering, Research in the Sociology of Organizations, and Alzheimer's & Dementia: Transla tional Research & Clinical Interventions. He serves as an associate editor for Communications of the Association for Information Systems and sits on the editorial board of Information and Organization. He has won awards from the International Conference on Information Systems and the Alcatel-Lucent Foundation.

Elizabeth Davidson is the W. Ruel Johnson Professor of Information Technology Management, Shidler College of Business, University of Hawai'i Manoa. Her health care research includes studies of the adoption and use of integrated clinical systems into hospitals and small physician practices, the evolution of personal health records as patient portals and mobile health apps, user experience with mobile health applications for chronic care management, and organizational forms of personal health data governance. In other research areas, she investigates sociocognitive processes in information systems development in organizations and has studied the emergence of new actor types and innovation practices on social media platforms. Her work has been published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of MIS, European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of the AIS, and Information and Organization. Dr Davidson has served as associate and senior editor for MISQ, JAIS, and EJIS and currently serves as editor-in-chief of Information and Organization.

Ana Paula Bezerra Barquet is a graduate in Pharmacy and Food Technology at the Federal University of Santa Catarina, Brazil, where she obtained her master's in industrial engineering with a focus in product development. She has a PhD from the University of Sao Paulo (Industrial Engineering Department) with a focus in prod uct-service systems as well as postdoc experiences from Technical University of Berlin and Freie Universitat Berlin, Germany. During her academic career. Dr. Bezerra Barguet has been involved in the coordination and execution of international projects and consulting projects. She has published in journals such as the Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Industrial Marketing Management, Journal of Cleaner Production, and Business Process Management Journal and presented her research on conferences worldwide. Her research interests and teaching skills include topics such as: product-service systems, product and service development process, business models, design science research, and re-manufacturing.

Hannes Rothe is an assistant professor at the Digital Entrepreneurship Hub, Freie Universitat Berlin. He earned his doctoral degree at Freie Universitat Berlin in 2015, where he is responsible for coordinating entrepreneurship education since 2013. He has been a visiting researcher at the University of Cambridge (UK) and TU Graz (Austria). Hannes' research interests focus on organizing data and knowledge, digital entrepreneurship, and digita infrastructures. His work has been published in journals such as Journal of the Association for Information Systems and, Communications of the Association for Information Systems and was presented at leading information systems conferences. He was runner-up and winner of multiple paper awards, including the Claudio Ciborra Award for the most innovative paper.

Oliver Peters is full professor at the Charite—Universitatsmedizin Berlin and currently chief of the branch Geriatric Neuropsychiatry at the Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy, Charite Campus Benjamin Franklin. He is principal investigator at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE e.V.) and board member of the German Dementia Competence Network (KND e.V.). He is interested in the establishment of, and has extensively worked on, new biomarkers for early diagnosis of chronic neurodegenerative diseases. He has participated as principal investigator in numerous observational and interventional clinical trials. Oliver's work has been published in numerous journals, and his latest publications can be found in Alzheimer's Research and Therapy, Lancet Neurology, PLoS ONE, Molecular Psychiatry, and Alzheimer's & Dementia.

Herlind Megges completed her undergraduate studies in social work at the University of Applied Sciences, Regensburg, Germany, and her Master of Science degree in gerontology at the Friedrich-Alexander University, Nuremberg, Germany. Since 2012, she has been working as a research assistant and later on as a PhD candidate at the Memory Clinic of the Charite—Universitatsmedizin Berlin, Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy. Herlind has acted as a study coordinator, project manager, and neuropsychological rater in numerous observational and interventional clinical trials. Her research activities focus on assistive technologies for persons with dementia and their caregivers. She has received funding from the German Alzheimer's Association, the Alzheimer Forschung Initiative, and most recently from the Berlin Institute of Health. Among others, her latest publications were published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: Translational Research & Clinical Interventions.
