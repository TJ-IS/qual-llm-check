## Abstract

This research focuses on information technology (IT) project managers' growth mindset concerning IT project management (PM) ability and investigates how such a mindset can promote escalation of commitment to a troubled IT project. Specifically, we suggest that the growth mindset of IT PM ability promotes prospective achievement motivation and can lead IT project managers to escalate their commitment to an IT project despite negative feedback suggesting that the project may not be completed successfully. Through a series of three experiments with 351 IT project managers, we obtained consistent support for our hypotheses suggesting that the growth mindset of IT PM ability promotes IT project escalation and this effect is partially mediated by anticipated regret about project failure and anticipated likelihood of project success. In addition, in the three experiments we found that when the project involves an unfamiliar technology the indirect effect of the growth mindset of IT PM ability through anticipated likelihood of project success is stronger than when the project involves a familiar technology.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Information technology (IT) project managers often face a difficult situation in which they must decide whether or not to continue a troubled project. In such situations, IT project managers often escalate their commitment, choosing to continue the project despite signs that it may not be successful, a phenomenon often referred to as IT project escalation (Mähring & Keil, 2008). While escalation of commitment can occur across a broad range of situations, IT projects are said to be particularly prone to escalation of commitment (Keil, Mann, & Rai, 2000) due to requirement volatility and the intangible nature of software (Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1991; Brooks, 1987). Moreover, prior infor mation systems (IS) research has shown that the consequences of IT project escalation can be quite negative, ofte resulting in large financial losses (Keil, 1995).

Since the mid-1990s, IS researchers have focused attention on the problem of IT project escalation (Keil, 1995 Newman & Sabherwal, 1996) due to frequent media reports of runaway IT projects that seem to take on a life of their own (Asbrand, 1993). Over the years, this topic has become one of enduring interest to both IS researcher (Lee, Keil, & Kasi, 2012; Lee, Lee, & Keil, 2018; Mähring, Keil, Mathiassen, & Pries-Heje, 2008; Pan, Pan, & Flynn 2004; Pan, Pan, Newman, & Flynn, 2006) and practitioners (Fichman, Keil, & Tiwana, 2005; Keil & Mähring, 2010 because mismanagement of IT projects can lead to multimillion dollar cost overruns (GAO, 2017). While som advances have been made in understanding IT project escalation, industry reports indicate that IT projects continue to experience significant cost and schedule overruns suggesting that the problem has not yet been solved. Indeed, in a study conducted by McKinsey and the University of Oxford which examined more than 5400 IT projects, the average cost overrun was 66% and the average schedule overrun was 33% (Bloch, 2012). In addition, an annual survey from project management company Innotas recently found that more than 50% of IT projects fail, and this was 3 years after the same annual survey found nearly identical statistics (Florentine, 2016). This suggests that IT project escalation remains an important IS phenomenon and furthering our understanding of it can be of immense value to both research and practice.

In this research, we draw on Dweck's mindset theory (Dweck, 2006) in which the core idea is the belief that “everyone can change and grow through application and experience” (Dweck, 2006, p. 7). Furthermore, growt mindset is defined as one's belief that his or her ability is a malleable attribute and can be improved through effort (Dweck, 2006), and prior growth mindset research found that having a growth mindset can shape an individual's achievement motivation and goals (Dweck, 2000). We suggest that drawing on mindset theory can offer new theo retical insights into understanding IT project escalation. Specifically, although prior research highlighted achievement motivation as an important factor driving IT project escalation, the focus has been on what we refer to as project achievement motivation in that it has been assumed that achievement motivation is related to the project itself (eg completing the project). This assumption, though, is not entirely accurate as decision makers' choices in organizations are often driven by their own self-interest rather than project-related goals (Eisenhardt, 1989). Therefore, in this research we aim to advance our understanding concerning the role of achievement motivation in IT project escala tion by showing that not only project achievement motivation but individual achievement motivation (eg, learning) can cause IT project escalation to occur.

In this research, we suggest that a growth mindset concerning IT project management (PM) ability (hereafter referred to simply as a growth mindset) can cause IT project escalation to occur by promoting prospective achievement motivation. Furthermore, we suggest that such a growth mindset influences two prospective factors (antici pated likelihood of project success and anticipated regret about project failure), which in turn drive IT project escalation. In addition, projects that involve unfamiliar technologies pose a greater level of uncertainty and unpredictability and are inherently more difficult to manage (McFarlan, 1981). As a result, project managers may be less optimistic about the likelihood of success when managing these types of projects. Thus, our research investi gates the impact of technology unfamiliarity on anticipated likelihood of project success and whether technology unfamiliarity moderates the indirect effect of growth mindset on IT project escalation that is theorized to be mediated by anticipated likelihood of project success (moderated mediation). In the remainder of this article, we discuss our research objectives and the theoretical background of this research. Next, we present our research model and hypotheses. We then discuss the experiments conducted to test the hypotheses followed by the results obtained Finally, we discuss the implications of our research along with limitations and directions for future research.

## 2 | RESEARCH OBJECTIVES

When faced with a decision of whether or not to continue a failing course of action, decision makers consider “th potential outcomes that may arise from escalating vs deescalating (eg, recouping a loss or losing even more resources) as well as the likelihood that each outcome may occur” (Sleesman, Conlon, McNamara, & Miles, 2012, p. 542). This indicates that decision makers look forward and consider what can be achieved should they choose to continue a failing course of action. Furthermore, prior research found that project managers can shift their focus from a financial goal to a different one in escalation situations, a process commonly known as goal substitution (Conlon & Garland, 1993). Specifically, goal substitution in escalation situations often occurs due to the completion effect; project managers become increasingly focused on finishing the project as it nears completion and this desir to complete the project leads to IT project escalation (Keil, Truex, & Mixon, 1995). While prior research highlighted the role of project achievement motivation in driving escalation decisions, there exists limited understanding concerning individual achievement motivation that may be relevant to escalation decisions, and how this can drive th decision to continue a troubled IT project. In this research, we draw on mindset theory to generate new insights into individual achievement motivation in IT project escalation. In doing so, we also expect to advance our understanding of IT project escalation in the following ways.

First, our research investigates the effect of a growth mindset on anticipated regret about IT project failure in escalation situations, and anticipated regret has been found to be important in the context of escalation decisions (Wong & Kwong, 2007). In this research, we suggest that IT project managers with a growth mindset care about learning and may not anticipate a high level of regret even if the project ultimately fails because they will hav accrued learning (another valuable outcome) by working on the troubled project. In this sense, we believe that learn ing can become a substitute goal in escalation situation. We know from prior research that in escalation situations individuals may substitute a new goal (eg, completion of a project) that is different from their initial goal (Conlon & Garland, 1993). Therefore, our research has the potential to advance our understanding of goal substitution in esca lation situations by showing that IT project managers' desire to learn can be a substitute goal driving IT project escalation.

Second, our research investigates the effect of a growth mindset on the anticipated likelihood of IT project success which has been found to be an important escalation driver. It is well known that technology professionals tend to be optimistic about future innovation and inventions (Pontin, 2009) and IT project managers can be optimistic when managing project risks (Lee, Keil, & Shalev, 2019). In the IT PM literature, optimism has been recognized as an important and persistent problem with respect to project planning<sup>1</sup> (Abdel-Hamid & Madnick, 1989; Brooks, 1995 and risk management (Lee et al., 2019). Moreover, when faced with a decision of whether or not to continue a trou bled project, IT project managers tend to optimistically believe that the project will eventually be successful (Keil, 1995). Yet, little is known about why project managers are optimistic about successfully completing an IT project in the face of negative feedback. Thus, further research is warranted to understand what drives project managers' opti mism in escalation situations, and our research aims to offer new insights in this area.

Third, project managers tend to be less optimistic about successfully managing projects that involve unfamiliar technologies which are known to be risky and involve uncertainty (McFarlan, 1981). Following this line of thought technology unfamiliarity should limit IT project escalation as it reduces decision makers' anticipated likelihood of suc cess. Contrary to this line of thinking, there is some research suggesting that IT project escalation may be more likel to occur when projects are perceived as research and development (R&D) type projects, as decision makers tend to be more patient when managing such projects (Keil, 1995). Extant literature does not offer an adequate explanation for what appears to be two contradictory views regarding the impact of technology unfamiliarity on perception about IT project success. Against this backdrop, our research aims to leverage and integrate technology unfamiliarit in understanding the indirect effect of growth mindset on IT project escalation that is theorized to be mediated b anticipated likelihood of project success.

## 3 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 3.1 | Mindset theory

The notion of a growth mindset (Dweck, 2006) was developed by Dweck et al. Growth mindset is centered on one' belief about the connection between effort and how malleable a particular ability is. In the context of IT PM, one may believe that effort leads to increased PM abilities (growth mindset). By contrast, one may believe that effort does not necessarily lead to increased PM abilities because PM abilities are not malleable (fixed mindset).

Prior research has generally regarded growth mindset as a “stable individual differences variable that may b influenced by situational characteristics” (Button, Mathieu, & Zajac, 1996, p. 28). This indicates that while people may have a general belief concerning growth mindset, external stimuli can prime people to adopt a different mindset Consistent with this view, a number of experimental studies have demonstrated that individuals can be induced to adopt a particular mindset (growth or fixed) based on experimental stimuli, such as achievement attribution (Dweck & Leggett, 1988) and task framing (Wood & Bandura, 1989). Such growth mindset manipulations do not necessarily change one's permanent traits, instead they “temporarily put people in a fixed (or growth) mindset” (Dweck 2006, p. 10), thus allowing researchers to observe the effects of a growth vs fixed mindset in the context of an experiment.

Early research on growth mindset focused on the motivational implications of such a mindset among childre and students (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Kamins & Dweck, 1999), and a more recent longitudinal study found that stu dents who had a growth mindset were more successful in STEM (science, technology, engineering, and mathematics fields (Nix, Perez-Felkner, & Thomas, 2015). Management scholars also started to pay attention to the growth mindset and its implications in organizational settings. This line of research demonstrated that the growth mindset has significant effects on managers' judgment and decision making. For example, Heslin, Latham, and VandeWall (2005) found that managers with a growth mindset are less likely to be influenced by their prior impressions of employee performance and more likely to acknowledge an improvement in employee performance than managers with a fixed mindset. This is because managers with a growth mindset believe that employees can grow and change and are thus more willing to revise their prior impressions. Prior research also found that training managers to have a growth mindset can lead to more accurate performance appraisals and helpful employee coaching (Heslin, VandeWalle, & Latham, 2008).

Overall, the current body of knowledge in this area views growth mindset as a positive attribute that both stu dents and managers can benefit from having—and we believe this might be particularly true for IT project managers who work in a dynamic and changing environment and must often learn new management and technical skills. However, we contend that while a growth mindset may be beneficial for employee motivation and achievement, its potential harmful side effects must also be considered. Specifically, managers with a growth mindset may behave dif ferently from others when faced with a situation in which a serious setback has been encountered. A growth mindset may lead them to perceive such situations differently from others—because a serious setback, if overcome, can be a valuable learning experience. In fact, Dweck (2006, p. 9) suggests that people with a growth mindset tend to “confront the challenges, and keep working at them.” In sum, while a growth mindset is generally known to have man benefits, its potential harmful side effects have received relatively little attention in the literature.

## 3.2 | IT project escalation

In the IS literature, IT project escalation was first studied by Keil et al. (1995). Keil et al. (1995) found that many fac tors previously known to influence escalation of commitment in general also play an important role in IT project escalation, but he also identified some additional factors that can be prominent in the context of IT PM such as emo tional attachment to the project, empire building, slack resources, and loose management controls. Building on an escalation-factors typology advanced by Staw and Ross (1987), Newman and Sabherwal (1996) then showed that both project and psychological determinants affected the decision to continue a troubled IT project. Later, Keil et al (2000) discussed the role of four major theoretical perspectives in IT project escalation (self-justification theory, prospect theory, agency theory, and approach-avoidance theory) and empirically tested key constructs associated with each theory. We believe integrating mindset theory with IT project escalation research has the potential to expand the approach-avoidance perspective in IT project escalation. From the approach-avoidance perspective, an escalation situation is viewed as an instance in which driving forces that propel us toward a goal (approach) and restraining forces (avoidance) collide and escalation results when the driving forces outweighs the restraining force (Brockner & Rubin, 1985). The completion effect captures the idea that when driving forces become stronger (the motivation to achieve the goal of completing a project) IT project escalation is more likely to occur (Keil et al., 2000) However, besides the project completion effect, little is known about other forms of approach motivation that ma drive project managers to continue a troubled IT project. In addition, while IT project managers' decisions may b driven by their individual achievement motivation rather than project achievement motivation, there is a lack of empirical research showing the connection between individual achievement motivation and IT project escalation

## 4.1 | Anticipated regret about project failure and anticipated likelihood of project success

Prior research on escalation has demonstrated that prospective factors, such as anticipated regret (Wong & Kwong 2007) and anticipated likelihood of success (Heath, 1995), can influence escalation decisions. Anticipated regret is a prospective type of regret that individuals experience by imagining how they will feel in response to a future event if they make a certain decision (Lankton & Luft, 2014; Zeelenberg, 1999). For instance, a customer may experience a high level of anticipated regret by imagining that the price of a car s/he recently purchased is later reduced. Anticipated likelihood of success is also prospective in nature because it involves taking a situation that is fraught wit uncertainty and imagining what the future may hold. In what follows, we theorize how growth mindset can influence anticipated regret about project failure and anticipated likelihood of project success.

One key proposition of mindset theory relates to the way individuals cope with failure; individuals holding a growth mindset view failure not as evidence of a lack of ability but as a springboard for growth and for stretching their existing abilities (Dweck, 2006). ## 4.2 | Technology unfamiliarity

Although innovation is often driven by the development or introduction of new technology into an organization, new and unfamiliar technology also introduces greater risk for IT project managers to have to deal with. Based on an international Delphi study, Schmidt, Lyytinen, Keil, and Cule (2001) identified “introduction of new technology” as key risk associated with IT projects. From a project manager's standpoint, a project that involves new and unfamilia technology poses a greater level of uncertainty and unpredictability and is inherently more difficult to manage (McFarlan, 1981). Based on this, we suggest that technology unfamiliarity may have a negative impact on project managers' perception about successfully turning around and completing a troubled IT project. Thus, we propose th following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 3 Technology unfamiliarity has a negative effect on anticipated likelihood of project success

Leading a project that involves new and unfamiliar technology offers an opportunity for project managers to obtai new technical knowledge, which is considered one of the key skills required for IT project managers (Keil, Lee, & Deng, 2013) and thus may offer an ideal environment in which to achieve growth and learning. Based on this, w believe that technology unfamiliarity represents an important potential moderator of the effect of a growth mindse on IT project escalation. In escalation situations, when a project that involves new and unfamiliar technology gener ates negative feedback IT project managers holding a growth mindset may believe that they can continue to lear about the new technology and improve their IT PM ability, allowing them to eventually complete the project successfully. By contrast, when a project that involves a familiar technology generates negative feedback IT project managers holding a growth mindset may not perceive a learning opportunity and may therefore be less optimistic about the prospect of successfully completing the troubled project. This line of reasoning suggests that technolog unfamiliarity moderates the effect of a growth mindset on anticipated likelihood of project success. This theorizing and H2 (involving the mediating effect of anticipated likelihood of project success) lead us to propose the following moderated mediation hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4 Anticipated likelihood of project success mediates the effect of a growth mindset on IT project escalation and this mediation effect is moderated by technology unfamiliarity such that the mediation effect of anticipate likelihood of project success is stronger when the project involves an unfamiliar technology rather than a familiar technology.

## 4.3 | The direct effect of a growth mindset on IT project escalation

Individuals with a growth mindset believe that their ability can be improved through effort and hard work (Dweck 2006). Furthermore, it is generally believed that people adopting a growth mindset are superior performers, as they tend to enjoy challenges, and strive to improve their ability and develop new skills (Perlick, 2014). Based on this, we suggest that an IT project manager's decision to escalate is driven by a motivation to improve his/her PM ability b overcoming the challenges associated with turning around a troubled IT project. Specifically, we suggest that whe faced with a decision of whether or not to continue a troubled IT project, IT project managers holding a growth mindset may view continuing the troubled IT project as an opportunity to improve their PM ability by overcomin the challenges. Such an achievement motivation may cause IT project managers to escalate their commitment to the troubled IT project. By contrast, IT project managers holding a fixed mindset may not desire to engage further in troubled IT project because they will not perceive a benefit in terms of improving their PM ability. Based on this, w propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5 A growth mindset causes greater IT project escalation than a fixed mindset.

## 5 | OVERVIEW OF RESEARCH METHOD

In order to test our hypotheses, we conducted three experiments using a scenario-based approach that has bee widely used both in prior escalation studies (Moon, 2001; Wong & Kwong, 2006) and in prior growth mindset studie (Blackwell, Trzesniewski, & Dweck, 2007; Hong, Chiu, Dweck, & Sacks, 1997). Furthermore, this approach has also been shown to be useful and appropriate for understanding decision making in IT PM (Lee et al., 2012, 2018, 2019). The scenario-based experiment allows researchers to create a decision setting that may not be easily accessible to researchers (eg, troubled projects), manipulate variables of interest, and observe their effects in a highly controlled setting, offering “a glimpse into how individuals' thoughts, feelings, behaviors, and decisions are affected by factors that may not be easily accessible in real-life situations because of confounding sources of variability that cannot b controlled” (Evans et al., 2015, p. 161). In their seminal work (for which Kahneman received the Nobel Prize) Kahneman and Tversky (1979, p. 265) state “by default, the method of hypothetical choices emerges as the simplest procedure by which a large number of theoretical questions can be investigated.” In our research context, the use of a scenario-based approach was justifiable because it would not have been feasible to have project managers mak decisions based on the actual projects that they were managing in their respective organizations.

In addition, in choosing to use the scenario-based experiment, our primary aim was to maximize internal validity as this gave us the ability to test causal relationships in a highly controlled setting. However, experiments of this type necessitate focusing on a relatively small number of variables and cannot replicate the complexities of real organiza tional settings. As a consequence, experiments necessarily trade off external validity for internal validity to some extent. While it is natural to think of generalizability in terms of external and ecological validity, it is important to remember that there are other ways in which we can generalize. In particular, experiments rely on analytic generali zation. With analytic generalization, the researcher is interested in generalizing a “particular set of results to som broader theory” (Yin, 2009, p. 43). Given our interest in generalizing to theory, the experimental design should not be evaluated on the extent to which it mimics organizational settings, but rather, on the extent to which it has the potential to increase our theoretical understanding of work behavior (Dobbins, Lane, & Steiner, 1988). Consistent with our discussion above, Calder, Phillips, and Tybout (1981) argue that when we think of generalizability we should distinguish between “effects application” research, which aims to obtain findings that can be applied directly to th real world situation of interest, and “theory application,” which focuses on advancing scientific theory. Therefore, w believe our study has value because it has the potential to provide new empirical insight concerning the causal rela tionship between growth mindset and IT project escalation

Moreover, each of the decision scenarios used in our experiments was designed to be consistent with how escalation of commitment is conceptualized in the literature and contained characteristics that are viewed as being central to escalation situations. Specifically, escalation of commitment is defined as a decision to continue a previousl chosen course of action (eg, a project) despite negative feedback (Brockner, 1992). Escalation situations involve (a) initiating a course of action and (b) deciding whether or not to continue the previously chosen course of action after receiving negative feedback (Lee et al., 2012; Lee, Keil, & Wong, 2015). Since there can be no escalation deci sion unless a prior course of action has been initiated (eg, initiating a project) and negative feedback has occurred most escalation studies focus on studying the actual escalation decision (ie, whether or not to continue a previousl chosen course of action). In other words, escalation situations, by definition, must involve an initial course of actio and negative feedback as to the viability of that course of action (Brockner, 1992; Garland, 1990; Staw, 1997). In ou experiments, all subjects were placed in a situation that involved a project that had been initiated and for which neg ative feedback had materialized (both of which occurred prior to having to make the escalation decision). Subject were then given the decision task of whether or not to continue the project.

In terms of experimental design, in Experiment 1 we manipulated growth mindset and technology unfamiliarit and tested their effects on IT project escalation and the underlying mechanisms through which these effects occur In Experiment 2, we sought to further validate the findings obtained in the first experiment by using a different manipulation for growth mindset. In Experiment 3, we adopted a different decision scenario and further tested our research model, this time measuring growth mindset as a trait instead of manipulating it. Mindset theory suggests that a growth mindset should be viewed as “relatively stable but malleable” (Dweck, Chiu, & Hong, 1995, p. 279) Thus, growth mindset researchers have either manipulated it or measured it as a trait. In our research, we sought to empirically test if the findings obtained in the first and second experiments (where growth mindset was manipulated) would hold when growth mindset was measured as a trait. Confirming if our research model holds whether growt mindset is measured or manipulated can add to the robustness of our research findings. In Table 2, we provide summary of the three experiments

## 6 | EXPERIMENT 1

## 6.1 | Participants, experimental design, and procedure

Using Qualtrics we recruited 140 project managers working in the IT industry to take part in our experiment<sup>2</sup> (79 men and 61 women; mean age = 40.44 years, SD = 10.00, age range = 22-64; mean experience as an IT project man ager = 7.13 years, SD = 5.38, experience range = 1-26 years). IT project managers were deemed suitable for our study, as they typically have the responsibility to manage and control a project. They are, by nature of their role, placed in th position to either make decisions in escalation situations or to recommend whether or not to continue a troubled project to senior management. The experiment was conducted online and participants were given a link to a web-based experi ment hosted on Qualtrics. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four treatment conditions in a $2 \times 2$ factorial design in which growth mindset (fixed vs growth) and technology unfamiliarity (familiar vs unfamiliar) were manipulated independently.<sup>3</sup> Participants were given the decision scenario and asked to play the role of the IT project manager described in the scenario (see Appendix A for the decision scenario and the manipulations).

## 9 | GENERAL DISCUSSION

IT is a prospectively focused discipline and it is not uncommon for IT professionals to anticipate the future and seek to keep themselves up-to-date with technological advances, while also embracing the learning opportunities that arise from overcoming new challenges. Therefore, we believe the need to learn can in fact be an important consideration for project managers when they are managing IT projects, and as such IT project managers with a growt mindset may be particularly vulnerable to escalating their commitment to troubled IT projects. However, extant liter ature largely ignores the potential role of a growth mindset in promoting IT project escalation. In this research, we offer a growth mindset model of IT project escalation, which explains how a desire to learn and improve one's PM ability represents a form of prospective achievement motivation that can drive IT project escalation.

## 9.1 | Theoretical implications

The findings that we obtained through a series of three experiments offer important contributions to IT project esca lation, IT PM, and mindset theory.

## 9.1.1 | Contributions to IT project escalation and IT PM

First, our research offers a fresh take on the prospective aspect of IT project escalation by drawing on mindset theory and providing the first empirical evidence indicating that the prospective achievement motivation that is inherent in a growth mindset causes IT project escalation to occur. Our research demonstrates that the effect of a growth mindset on IT project escalation is mediated by IT project managers' anticipation and perception about what will hap pen to the project in the future. Specifically, IT project managers holding a growth mindset anticipate a low level of regret about a potential negative outcome that can result from continuing a troubled IT project. This suggests that a growth mindset produces a motivation that places value on the future improvement in PM ability that can result from trying to turn around a troubled IT project, even if the project itself should ultimately fail. Our research finding shed light on the fact that project managers may focus on achieving learning when faced with challenges and obsta cles in managing an IT project. In addition, our results highlight the fact that IT project escalation may be driven not only by project achievement motivation but by individual achievement motivation. While prior research found that project achievement motivation (eg, project completion motivation) is an important factor driving IT project escala tion (Keil et al., 1995), to the best of our knowledge the role of individual achievement motivation in IT project esca lation has not been previously investigated. Thus, our research contributes to the literature by showing that IT project managers' desire to achieve learning (which is promoted by a growth mindset) can be a factor driving IT pro ject escalation.

Second, our research demonstrates that IT project managers' holding a growth mindset escalate in part because they perceive a greater likelihood of project success. IT project managers holding a growth mindset believe that their PM ability can be improved by working through challenging situations and that they can overcome challenges with their improved PM ability. This is an interesting finding that adds to the IT PM literature where optimism has been recognized as a chronic problem; for example, prior research on IT PM highlights how being optimistic about the future can cause problems such as the planning fallacy (Shmueli, Pliskin, & Fink, 2016). Mindset theory offers a new perspective in understanding why IT project managers may be optimistic when managing a project even in the presence of negative feedback and why this could lead to a decision problem, such as IT project escalation. Moreover, in managing IT projects technology unfamiliarity represents a key risk (Schmidt et al., 2001) introducing greater uncertainty and unpredictability (McFarlan, 1981), and thus it is likely to have a negative effect on anticipated likelihood of project success. However, in this research we show that a growth mindset leads IT project managers to be more optimistic when the project involves an unfamiliar technology than when the project involves a familiar technology This is due to the greater learning potential associated with the unfamiliar technology. We believe this is a new an interesting finding that adds to the body of literature on IT PM

Finally, by connecting mindset theory with prospective thinking in IT project escalation, we believe the contributions of our work extend beyond the IT project escalation literature and add to the broader body of literature on escalation of com mitment. Specifically, while recent escalation research underscored the importance of temporal thinking in understanding the escalation phenomenon (Moon, 2001; Wong & Kwong, 2007) there is a paucity of research focused on advancing our understanding of prospective achievement motivation in escalation of commitment. In this regard, our research is the firs to use mindset theory to explain a form of prospective achievement motivation that is relevant to escalation decisions

## 9.1.2 | Contributions to mindset theory

Our research adds to the literature on growth mindset research by shedding light on a negative effect that can b caused by having a growth mindset. Having a growth mindset is known to have many benefits and can be particularly useful in IT project contexts; for example, IT project managers having a growth mindset may be willing to learn new technical skills or new PM practices and methodologies. However, our research indicates that when IT project man agers believe that their PM abilities can be improved through effort they may be more willing to escalate commit ment to a troubled IT project. We believe this is an important finding that contributes to growth mindset theory b empirically demonstrating that a growth mindset can backfire and cause IT project escalation.

Our research context (IT project escalation) is characterized by a sequence of three events: (a) initiating a course of action, (b) receiving negative feedback regarding the previously chosen course of action, and (c) deciding whether or not to continue the same course of action. Negative feedback in escalation situations indicates not meeting the initial expec tations. In escalation situations, managers recognize the performance deviation (ie, the perception that the project is in trouble). However, recognizing the performance deviation would not necessarily mean that project managers who have a growth mindset will give up on the project, because in escalation situations decision makers will anticipate whether or not the project will eventually be successful. Drawing on the core idea of mindset theory that one's ability or performanc can improve over time through effort, we suggest that project managers who have a growth mindset choose to continue the troubled project anticipating that the project can eventually be turned around if they invest further effort. By contrast project managers who have a fixed mindset are more likely to give up anticipating that the project is unlikely to be suc cessful due to a lack of necessary PM abilities. In fact, prior growth mindset research showed that people who have growth vs fixed mindsets tend to have different reactions to negative feedback concerning task performance. Specifically when negative performance feedback is received individuals who have a growth mindset tend to believe that their perfor mance can be improved if they invest further effort, whereas individuals who have a fixed mindset believe that abilities are mostly innate and interpret negative performance feedback as the lack of necessary basic abilities (Dweck, 2006). In other words, mindset theory explains that people with a growth mindset are not easily discouraged by setbacks and “envi sion that increased effort leads to final success” (Gong & Fan, 2006, p. 177). Based on this, we believe our theorizing that a growth mindset leads to IT project escalation is fully consistent with the core idea of mindset theory

Therefore, our research findings are valuable in the sense that they stand in contrast to the existing body of research on mindset theory in organizational settings which emphasizes the positive results that can be derived from a growth mindset. Our findings can spark further discussion and research as to when and how a growth mindset can backfire. In sum, we believe our work contributes to the literature on mindset theory, which has focused almost exclusively on the positive effects associated with a growth mindset both in education (Kamins & Dweck, 1999) and management settings (Heslin et al., 2005, 2008)

## 9.2 | Practical implications

The benefits of having a growth mindset have been well documented across educational and managerial settings and we certainly do not mean to suggest that project managers with a growth mindset are to be avoided. In fact, w believe that a growth mindset is generally a desirable quality for IT project managers who must continually adapt to the dynamic IT field in which required skillsets are constantly evolving (Overby, 2007). However, based on this research we suggest that having a growth mindset may not be beneficial when managing troubled IT projects. This i because IT project managers holding a growth mindset look at “what may happen” and incorporate this prospectiv thinking into their decision to continue troubled projects. Our work suggests that this prospective achievement moti vation can lead IT project managers to be optimistic despite negative feedback in escalation situations and focus more on anticipated “learning.” Based on this, if the goal is to limit further investment into a troubled project, it ma be advisable for project managers having a growth mindset to evaluate the merits of the project based on its current status rather than their anticipation about the future.

In addition, project managers having a growth mindset should be more mindful when their project involves a new and unfamiliar technology. Specifically, based on our research we suggest that IT project managers having a growth mindset are more likely to have an optimistic perception about potential outcomes than IT project manager having a fixed mindset. While projects that involve a new and unfamiliar technology tend to have high potential and offer a great opportunity to learn, such projects also create a condition in which IT project managers with a growt mindset are more likely to fall victim to IT project escalation

Finally, our work raises a question as to what it means to be a learning organization. We often hear phrases lik “learn from the failure” or “fail to succeed.” Some even suggest that “many successful business leaders (are) urging their companies and colleagues to make more mistakes and embrace more failures” (Taylor, 2017). From a PM stand point, it may be beneficial to be explicit about the learning goals associated with an IT project and the extent to which such goals would justify IT project escalation even if it results in a project failure.

## 9.3 | Limitations and directions for future research

In this research, we relied on scenario-based decision-making experiments in order to test the idea that a growt mindset can promote IT project escalation. While this approach may not capture all of complexities associated with actual IT projects, we believe it is a useful and scientifically valid way to test new ideas. Specifically, scenario-based experiments allow researchers to manipulate specific variables of interest while controlling other factors. In actua PM settings, there are a number of factors that are difficult to control (eg, project size, project nature, project culture, and so on) which makes it difficult to investigate causal relationships. Nonetheless, we suggest that further research is warranted to extend our research by determining if our study findings can be generalized to a different project context. For example, the three experiments reported in this research involved IT projects that are like R&D project in nature (ie, developing a new technology) where trial and error is somewhat expected (Shirahada & Hamazaki 2013). We suggest that an IT project context similar to our experimental context would be one in which the effect of a growth mindset (vs a fixed mindset) on IT project escalation is quite salient and most likely to be discovered Therefore, we suggest that a fruitful direction for future research would be to investigate the effect of a growt mindset in a different project context that may involve more tangible or direct deliverables such as an enterprise implementation project. In addition, using scenario-based decision-making experiments we focused on studying th escalation decision in a situation that involved a project that had been initiated and for which negative feedback had materialized. However, given the nature of escalation situations that involve some temporal aspects (initiating a course of action, followed by receiving negative feedback, then followed by having to make an escalation decision) a research design that is longitudinal in nature can help generate additional insights into the effect of a growth mindset on IT project escalation.

Thaler (2015) suggests that the use of scenario-based experiments are valid when there is no strong reason to believe that participants would behave differently from what they say they would do. While the findings from scenario-based experiments reported in the escalation literature have generally been consistent with the finding reported from field studies (Staw, Barsade, & Koput, 1997; Staw & Hoang, 1995), it would clearly be desirable to further investigate the role of the growth mindset of IT PM ability on IT project escalation in a field setting. Moreover, in our research there were certain differences in decision scenarios used in Experiments 1 and 2 vs Experiment 3. For example, the decision scenario used in the first two experiments involved spending 15% of one's time to develop ideas that may lead to new products and was more investigational than the decision scenario used in th third experiment. While we believe that testing our research model in two somewhat different decision scenarios adds to the generalizability of our research findings, we believe that whether or not a project is investigational may moderate the effect of growth mindset on IT project escalation. Since our research did not investigate this empiri cally, we point to the need for further research focused on investigating the role of different project types in moder ating the effect of a growth mindset on IT project escalation.

In addition, the findings of our research suggest that there exists a residual effect concerning growth mindset and IT project escalation. Based on this, we suggest that further research is warranted to probe a potential mecha nism mediating this residual effect. Specifically, we believe focusing on IT project managers' anticipation regarding learning outcomes and/or a loss of learning opportunities would offer additional insights into the mechanism under lying the relationship between the growth mindset of IT PM ability and IT project escalation. Furthermore, Dweck (2006) suggests that individuals having a growth mindset are more willing to take risk and tend to accept challenging tasks even when successful outcomes are uncertain. This indicates that having a growth mindset may lower individuals' risk aversion.<sup>10</sup> In our research context, choosing to continue a troubled IT project can be understood as a decision that involves a high level of uncertainty and risk but also one that can lead to a high return if the troubled project can successfully be turned around. Based on this, it is reasonable to think that risk aversion may play an important role in mediating the relationship between a growth mindset and IT project escalation. Thus, we believ one fruitful direction for future research is to incorporate risk aversion in understanding the relationship between growth mindset and IT project escalation.

The findings of our research also show that a growth mindset has a positive effect on IT project managers' antic ipated likelihood of project success and we believe this optimistic perception that results from having a growth mindset might have important implications for research on IT project risk management and risk management in gen eral. IT project risk management is a set of activities aimed at identifying potential risks, estimating both their likeli hood of occurrence and potential impact, and developing a plan to reduce their chance of occurring or to mitigate their impact (Lyytinen, Mathiassen, & Ropponen, 1998; Schmidt et al., 2001) and optimism has been cited as a potential problem in managing IT project risks (Shalev, Keil, Lee, & Ganzach, 2014). Therefore, we suggest that one fruitfu direction for future research is to investigate how IT project managers' growth mindset may influence optimistic per ception and judgment in managing risks.

Another direction for future research is to investigate the role of group-level factors (ie, group decision making in the context of IT project escalation. In our experiments, the unit of analysis was individual decision-making con cerning whether or not to continue a troubled IT project. However, when IT project escalation decisions are made as a group, group-level factors, such as gender ratio of the group, may play an important role

Furthermore, in this research we did not explicitly consider the PM approach (eg, agile ys waterfall) in designing the experiment and defining the research context, and this can be viewed as a limitation. We believe further research is warranted to extend our study and investigate how the effect of growth mindset on IT project escalation manifests itself across different project environments. For example, one of the key principles of agile is to welcome trial-anderror and embrace changes (as opposed to sticking to the plan) (AgileManifesto, 2001). Furthermore, some researchers even suggest that learning be part of agile practices (Babb, Hoda, & Nørbjerg, 2014). This indicates that IT project managers' motivation to learn may be enhanced under the agile project environment and could have a greater impact on their decision to continue an IT project despite negative feedback. Therefore, we point to the need for further research to explore whether the type of development approach (eg, agile vs waterfall) moderates th effect of a growth mindset on IT project escalation.

Finally, in this research we focused on establishing a causal relationship between the growth mindset of IT PM ability and IT project escalation using lab experiments while controlling for other factors; thus, another fruitfu direction for future research is to investigate how IT project manager's growth mindset affects IT project escalation relative to other factors that may play a prominent role in IT project escalation. Moreover, further research i warranted to advance our understanding of IT project managers' growth mindset in the context of IT PM and investi gating its benefits as well as side effects concerning other behavioral issues, such as reluctance to report negative project news (Smith, Keil, & Depledge, 2001), conflict management (Jiang, Chang, Chen, Wang, & Klein, 2014), and team coordination (Mastrogiacomo, Missonier, & Bonazzi, 2014).

## 9.4 | Conclusion

Escalation of commitment, often understood as “throwing good money after bad,” is a problem that can waste valuabl organizational resources and has attracted significant attention among psychology, management, and IS scholars. While we cannot assume that just because a project is severely troubled continuing the project will always lead to a negativ outcome, escalating commitment to a troubled project is likely to end in failure or to cost more to turn around than it is worth. Our research connects mindset theory with IT project escalation by showing that a growth mindset associated with prospective achievement motivation can drive IT project escalation. We believe the findings of this research ar useful not only to IS scholars focused on the challenges of managing IT projects, but also represent a valuable addition to the broader body of literature on escalation. Furthermore, in spite of the benefits that are known to be associated with a growth mindset, our research provides an instance in which a growth mindset can backfire and cause IT project escalation, and this represents a valuable addition to the literature on growth mindset research.
