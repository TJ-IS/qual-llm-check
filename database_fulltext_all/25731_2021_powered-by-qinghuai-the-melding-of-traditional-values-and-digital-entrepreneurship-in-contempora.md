# Powered by “Qinghuai”: The melding of traditional values and digital entrepreneurship in contemporary China

## Abstract

Based on three case studies of Chinese Internet start-ups, this study seeks to address the research question: “How is digital entrepreneurship enacted in China?” Our findings reveal that there was a common theme that underpinned the start-ups we studied, which we termed ‘Qinghuai’ in the language of the informants we spoke with. In this paper, we explain the roots of the concept and its six constituent elements at the individual, organizational, and ecosystem levels. These elements are then abstracted into two dimensions: (a) spiritual idealism and (b) perpetual development. We argue that Qinghuai as a concept is a product and reflection of the cultural and institutional complexity of contemporary China. Further, we discuss how Qinghuai facilitates digital entrepreneurship across the business, organizational, and technological domains. This explanation is substantiated by data from our three cases and juxtaposed with what has been discussed in the existing digital entrepreneurship literature. Finally, as we present the contributions of our study, we elaborate on (a) how Qinghuai reflects the contemporary context of China; (b) how Qinghuai is instrumental to digital entrepreneurship in China; and (c) how Qinghuai is different from other related concepts including Guanxi, collectivism, collective action and social entrepreneurship. We conclude the paper by discussing its limitations, future research opportunities, as well as its practical implications.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Digital entrepreneurship is defined as “the pursuit of opportunities based on the use of digital media and other information and communication technologies” (Davidson & Vaast, 2010, p. 2), which leads to the transformation of existing business models or the invention of new ones (Hitt, Ireland, Camp, & Sexton, 2001). Especially since the advent of the Internet, we have witnessed the phenomenal success of numerous technology-enabled entrepreneurial ventures, which not only demonstrates the strategic potential of digital technologies, but also showcases their ability to challenge or disrupt the status quo. Some industries have been quicker than others to embrace the digital revolutio (eg, disintermediation and reintermediation in the travel industry), with innovative business models constantl emerging to provide alternative ways of consumption and hence shift the landscape of the industry (Davidson & Vaast, 2010). Other industries tend to be more insulated (eg, the financial services and public transportation indus tries), but even in these fields, we are now witnessing upheavals and fundamental structural changes driven by th development of digital phenomena such as FinTech and the sharing economy paradigm (see Leong, Tan, Xiao, Tan, & Sun, 2017; Tan, Cahalane, Tan, & Englert, 2017).

The existing literature has articulated a number of unique traits that are associated with digital entrepreneurship For instance, Nambisan (2017) argued that the nature of uncertainty inherent in entrepreneurship has been transformed, in the sense that we deal with entrepreneurial outcomes and processes that are less bounded in the digita context, as well as entrepreneurial agency that is less predefined (eg, the open source movement). It should be noted here that the uncertainty in this context not only entails the unpredictability of entrepreneurial activities, but more importantly underscores the generative nature of digital technologies (eg, see Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010; Yoo, Henfridsson, & Lyytinen, 2010), where “the solution” is never complete and constantly evolves with the environment and the market (Huang, Henfridsson, Liu, & Newell, 2017). Such fluidity demands less rigidity in the execution of entrepreneurial activities, in order to cope with the dynamism of the contemporary business landscape and better leverage the evolving nature of digital solutions (see Ries, 2011). In addition, it has been argued that digital technolo gies have the potential to empower and emancipate, as the playing field has been leveled via greater and more equi table access to resources (eg, Leong, Pan, Newell, & Cui, 2016). Consequently, entrepreneurs are able to rely on existing digital infrastructures and to utilize cheaper and more flexible IT tools (eg, cloud computing) to pursue their business ideas.

Against this backdrop, many scholars have called for “fresh” conceptualizations of digital entrepreneurship, especially in terms of how we can evaluate, inform and effect its success (Nambisan, 2017). In particular, existing theories of entrepreneurship tend to assume that entrepreneurial processes and outcomes have a fixed boundary. Conse quently, these theories emphasize the entrepreneurs' capability to execute a plan (eg, Brinckmann, Grichnik, & Kapsa, 2010), and posit entrepreneurship opportunities as identifiable a priori, while assuming a predictable futur (Fisher, 2012). Meanwhile, the creation of opportunities and the reshaping of goals in the process of enactment tha uniquely defines digital entrepreneurships have been downplayed (Sarasvathy, 2001, 2009).

## 1.1 | Digital entrepreneurship in China

A discussion of digital entrepreneurship would not be complete without an examination of what has been unfolding in China in recent years. Industry observers in the West have noted the exponential growth of digital entrepreneuria activities in China (Tse, 2016), underscored by a 6-fold increase in venture capital investment in Chinese firms from 2011 to 2016 and an accelerating number of Chinese tech unicorns (ie, firms valued at over US\$1billion) that have had or are planning an Initial Public Offering (Fannin, 2018). The same factors that are believed to have constrained the development of the public sector are also contributing to this entrepreneurial surge, as start-ups seize opportuni ties left by state-owned laggards. On the other hand, this surge can also be attributed to the economic growth in China that has taken place since the liberalization of its economy, as well as the growing pervasiveness of Internet adoption, especially via mobile devices, which has cultivated a huge market with “voracious” consumers (The Econo mist, 2017). Moreover, the entrepreneurial mind-set is no longer limited to the private sector. As part of its efforts to transition from a manufacturing-based economy to a knowledge-based economy, the Chinese government has offi cially embraced innovation and entrepreneurship in its national economic strategy (Tse, 2016).

Research on entrepreneurship in the context of China has historically focused on the issue of how entrepreneur overcome obstacles that are specific to China or other emerging economies, such as a volatile institutional environ ment, a lack of critical resources, and insufficient government support (eg, see Guo & Miller, 2010; Mahmood & Mitchell, 2004; Xin & Pearce, 1996). In fact, this stream of research has seen extensive efforts towards the conceptu alization of indigenous Chinese concepts and traditional values in order to explain how Chinese entrepreneurs achieve success. For instance, Guanxi, which means “interpersonal relationship” in Mandarin, has become crucial to entrepreneurs in China in terms of forming business networks and acquiring the necessary resources (Guo & Miller, 2010; Peng, 2000).

Despite being the focus of much discussion over the years, we contend that the concept of Guanxi may be limited in providing an understanding of entrepreneurial success in China in the new digital age, for a number of reasons. Firstly, and most importantly, the external environment within which Guanxi operates has fundamentally changed. As discussed previously, we have seen tremendous government efforts towards promoting and supporting entrepreneurship and innovation in recent years, meaning that the institutional constraints that entrepreneurs have had to overcome through the formation of formal and informal Guanxi ties may not be as pertinent as they once were (Xin & Pearce, 1996). Sec ondly, digital entrepreneurship creates different business dynamics, as discussed above. The challenges associated with entrepreneurial efforts in the digital landscape are very much embedded in the inherent uncertainties of its processe and outcomes (Nambisan, 2017). This is an even more daunting issue in the context of China, where competition is fierce and is relentlessly fuelled by the growth of this environment (The Economist, 2017). Finally, while Guanxi explains a great deal of how entrepreneurs gather resources from, and deal with, the external environment (Guo & Miller, 2010), it explains very little about the internal development of the business itself.

Thus, a key question that remains is “how is digital entrepreneurship enacted in contemporary China?” This is the research question that we will seek to address in this paper. We pursue this research with a strong emphasis on the contextual conditions surrounding the phenomenon. Davison and Martinsons (2016) highlight that the contex tual inadequacies in IS research hinder generalizability and theory building, and subsequently advocate that researchers should “explicitly acknowledge the specific context wherein it is undertaken” to formulate theory from particularistic research (p. 242). One way to do so is to conduct more context-explicit and context-aware studies and to use/develop indigenous constructs (Fernández, 2016). We respond to these calls here with the aim of developing indigenous concepts that will advance our understanding of digital entrepreneurship in the specific context of contemporary China.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: we first present a brief discussion of how the knowledge sur rounding digital entrepreneurship has evolved over time, before introducing China and its cultural and institutiona contexts, which provide the theoretical background for the study. Next, we present our research method and th ways in which we collected and analysed our data. This is followed by the findings section, where we present the indigenous theoretical concept of Qinghuai, and discuss how Qinghuai plays an instrumental role in the successfu enactment of digital entrepreneurship. We then compare Qinghuai with related concepts in existing literature, such as Guanxi and collective action, and reflect on its theoretical contributions. Finally, we discuss the limitations of ou study and future research opportunities in further investigating the concept of Qinghuai

## 2 | DIGITAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP

The existing literature offers different perspectives on digital entrepreneurship. and to a large extent reflects how our view of the relationship between Information Technologies (IT) and entrepreneurial activities has changed over time (eg, see Steininger, 2019). Historically, IT has been seen as instrumental to entrepreneurial activities (De Giudice & Straub, 2011), both at the national (eg, Meso, Musa, Straub, & Mbarika, 2009) and organizational levels (eg, Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2003). This stream of research is closely linked to studies of IT innovation, in which the rela tionship between IT and organizational innovation is explored (eg, Fichman, 2004; Swanson, 1994). However, in recent years, the rise of digitalization has prompted scholars in the fields of both innovation management and entre preneurship to revisit this unidimensional conceptualization of digital entrepreneurship. For instance, a recent review of the digital entrepreneurship literature has revealed that digital technologies can be ubiquitous in every aspect of entrepreneurial operations (eg, throughout the value chain as well as the operations of a firm—see Steininger, 2019) Similarly, Davidson and Vaast (2010) argue that digital entrepreneurship phenomena are sociomaterial in nature suggesting a kind of “inextricably inscribed” relationship between technology, entrepreneurial agents, and the socia and historical contexts around them.

Against this backdrop, Nambisan (2017) challenges the traditional assumptions associated with entrepreneuria activities and argues that the new characteristics of digital entrepreneurship require new conceptualizations of th phenomenon. More specifically, while digital entrepreneurship intersects with conventional entrepreneurship in many ways, what distinguishes digital entrepreneurial activities from general entrepreneurial activities, in particular, is that they are fundamentally underpinned by IT, such that alternative, non-IT-enabled means of enacting those activities are plainly impossible or significantly less viable (Du, Pan, Zhou, & Ouyang, 2018). In other words, in th context of digital entrepreneurship, IT is integral to, and inextricable from, the entrepreneurial activities associated with the operations of a firm, the nature of its market offerings, and/or the way it connects to its customer (Steininger, 2019). Nambisan (2017) further points out that digital entrepreneurship is characterized by (a) les bounded outcomes and processes; and (b) less predefined entrepreneurial agency. More specifically, fluidity is enabled or driven by the open and evolving nature of digital artifacts (Yoo et al., 2010), the generative nature of digi tal platforms (Zittrain, 2006), and the scalability of digital infrastructure (eg, cloud-based infrastructure) (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). On the other hand, emerging digital platforms and infrastructures such as social media and crowdsourcing (eg, Thies, Wessel, & Benlian, 2018) enable the enactment of entrepreneurial activities by collectives via co-creation (Nambisan, 2017).

The implications of the changing dynamics of entrepreneurship due to digitalization are multifold. The fluidity of the process and outcome requires entrepreneurial actions to facilitate “a continuously evolving value proposition,” and leads to a more dynamic path that involves constant adjustment (Nambisan, 2017). At the same time, this adds to the uncertainty associated with entrepreneurial actions. The collective mode of digital entrepreneurship further heightens such uncertainty and requires entrepreneurs to involve and coordinate a wider range of stakeholders often via digital technologies (Nambisan, 2017). It is therefore important for us to formulate new understandings of how digital entrepreneurship can be successfully enacted (Nambisan, 2017; Shen, Lindsay, & Xu, 2018; Steininger, 2019).

## 3 | THE CULTURAL AND INSTITUTIONAL CONTEXTS IN CHINA

Much has been written about China in terms of its cultural and institutional contexts. At the national level Hofstede's (1984, 1991) seminal work on national culture has identified the characteristics of Chinese culture as high power distance, strong uncertainty avoidance, collectivism, masculinity, and long-term orientation. Although widely used, this theorization has been criticized as being over-simplified and too positivistic (eg, Baskerville, 2003). Whe it comes to culture in China, nuances are important, and this is especially the case after decades of economi development. For instance, the traits of collectivism and uncertainty avoidance are at odds with the imperative to accumulate personal wealth and the national drive toward entrepreneurship, and such seemingly contrasting trait can manifest in different contexts/situations (Meyer & Shen, 2010; Tian, 1998). Another stream of research on Chi nese culture has focused on the influence of Confucianism (King, 1991; Tsui & Farh, 1997). However, while Confucianist philosophy certainly still holds sway in Chinese society, many of its values have evolved or been adapted ove time (Wu, 2015), and the extent and nature of its present influence is unclear.

Efforts to discover the unique nature of the Chinese context in general and the Chinese business environment in particular have led to the development of concepts such as Guanxi (Farh, Tsui, Xin, & Cheng, 1998; Jacobs, 1980 Ou, Pavlou, & Davison, 2014; Tsui & Farh, 1997), network capitalism (Boisot & Child, 1996) and relationship-based e-commerce (Martinsons, 2008). In essence, these scholars have identified the importance of interpersonal relationships, “close and pervasive ties”, informal and tacit communication and information sharing in economic activities in China (Davison & Martinsons, 2016).

These contextual traits of China are evidently associated with or attributed to the institutional environment. It has been argued that a relationship-based culture prevails due to a lack of trust in rule-based systems (Mar tinsons, 2008). There is also an absence of well-established institutions that fully support a market-based econ omy, despite the economic achievements that have been made over the past decades (Holiday & Yep, 2005) Furthermore, national level policies are still planned via a top-down approach, and the central government con tinues to play a leading role in launching major developmental programs (Gao, 2007; Luoma & Nyberg, 2011). Con sequently, it is believed that the ability to manage relationships with the government (and especially loca authorities) and to foresee changes in the relevant regulations and policies are crucial to the survival of businesse in China (Pearson, 2005).

Finally, at the group level, the cultural context in China appears to be increasingly fragmented across different age groups and geographical regions. For instance, both organizational and consumer studies have applied the con cept of “generation cohorts” to highlight the distinctive cultures and value systems associated with different genera tions (Egri & Ralston, 2004; Hung, Gu, & Yim, 2007). These studies have further demonstrated the complexity of the cultural landscape in China, in which elements of traditional Confucian values, Western capitalism, and Chinese com munist ideologies have been blended together due to the major political revolutions and reforms that have take place in recent decades (Vohra, 2000)

It can be naïve and futile to try to describe the Chinese context in simple terms. The long history of the country as well as the recent reforms and economic development, have rendered this environment a peculiar mix, in which various traits can manifest selectively depending on circumstances (Hung et al., 2007). We therefore believe tha such particularism provides a fertile ground for developing indigenous theories or theoretical concepts (Davison & Martinsons, 2016). In the next section, we describe our research approach.

## 4 | RESEARCH METHOD

In order to examine the contemporary and underexplored phenomenon of digital entrepreneurship in China, we adopt the qualitative case research method (Siggelkow, 2007; Walsham, 1995). More specifically, we utilize a multi ple-case study design (Yin, 2003) to examine how digital entrepreneurship is successfully enacted in China

## 4.1 | Data collection

Our case selection criteria were that the organizations we study must not only represent entrepreneurial ventures based in China but should also have achieved a significant measure of commercial success. This is because we are seeking to develop theory based on proven, if not best, practices, so as to strengthen the potential theoretical and practical contributions of our study (eg, see Pan & Tan, 2011). After screening a number of candidate firms, we selected three Internet start-ups as the foci of our study: FinTech X, Parking X, and Hotel X (all pseudonyms). These start-ups represented digital entrepreneurship ventures across the three very different industries of finance, parking and accommodation. More importantly, all three firms have proven to be phenomenally successful within their respective areas at the time of data collection (as evidenced by the growth of their business, number of media men tions, and amount of venture investment that they were able to secure), making them revelatory or extreme case (see Gerring, 2008) for the purposes of our study.<sup>1</sup>

A team of researchers carried out extensive fieldwork at the headquarters of each of the case companies. W followed similar fieldwork procedures at all case sites. First, a meeting was held with the founder or CEO of the com pany, during which an overview of the background and the business model of the company was presented. We then collected secondary data in the form of the corporate presentation slides, internal reports, and brochures that wer presented at these meetings. These briefing meetings were then followed by in-depth presentations on the organiza tional operations. The research team had the opportunity to visit multiple departments, observing and taking videos and photographs of the working environment, and in some cases were given a demonstration of the company's main products (eg, mobile applications and devices). We then started primary data collection, consisting of 14 to 16 inter views within each organization.<sup>2</sup> Given the fledgling nature of each of the start-ups, sampling of interviewees was done with the aim of capturing a holistic representation of the key business units of each organization. Conse quently, we interviewed people across different departments and different levels of the organizational hierarchy (consisting of senior executives, middle managers and staff). An interview guideline can be found in Appendix A. Al interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed. ## 4.2 | Data analysis

Since this paper aims to develop theory, we adopted and adapted the techniques of grounded theory methodolog when engaging in data analysis (see Glaser, 1978; Urquhart, Lehmann, & Myers, 2010). This process unfolded in three stages. First, we focused on each individual case company. Through a systematic analysis of the transcripts, we developed a case narrative and visual maps (eg, developmental timelines captured in diagrammatic form) for each case company (Langley, 1999), which detailed its business model and the milestone events in its development to date. In doing so, a voluminous amount of data was condensed into a manageable form, and we were able to gain holistic understanding of the nature of each company and its business.

In the second stage, the first author conducted open coding by carefully studying each of the interview tran scripts (see Urquhart, 2012). At this stage, over 80 open codes were generated. We then engaged in axial coding b comparing and aggregating the open codes, and generated initial concepts related to digital entrepreneurship (Urquhart, 2012). At this point, the concept of Qinghuai, which we later defined as the moral commitment toward and pursuit of, a spiritual ideal, emerged as the primary concept underpinning the operations and success of each case company. The concept was brought up organically by various interviewees across all three cases without being prompted by the research team. Consequently, we came to the realization that Qinghuai is a core, indigenous concept that is common to the three cases, and may hold an answer to our research question: how is digital entrepre neurship enacted in China?

In the third stage, we engaged in theoretical coding to further flesh out the concept of Qinghua (Urquhart, 2012). More specifically, we revisited the transcripts and the codes generated via axial coding, with the aim of further clarifying our conceptualization of Qinghuai and its role in supporting digital entrepreneurship. This theoretical coding resulted in a concept map of Qinghuai, which captured the linkages between Qinghuai and various organizational activities related to business development (Stake, 2013). A sample coding table (see Table B2) and a data structure (see Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013) that illustrates how the concept of Qinghuai was derived (see Figure B1) are presented in Appendix B.

## 5 | FINDINGS

Based on our research objectives, we sought to gain an understanding of how these three start-ups managed to thrive and attain success in their respective industries through the enactment of digital entrepreneurship. As ou investigation unfolded, a concept was independently raised within each of the case firms, without prompting on our part. This concept was Qinghuai, and every time it was brought up by the various informants, the concept was used to describe different aspects of both the individuals and the organization. This led to our conclusion that Qinghuai was central to the success of digital entrepreneurship across all three cases. It also drove us to fur ther develop the conceptualization of this notion, based on the data collected, and this is explained in the follow ing subsections.

## 5.1 | The roots of Qinghuai

Qinghuai is not a contemporary invention, but a term that has historically existed in the Chinese language. Figure 1 illus trates how the phrase is written in simplified Chinese and shows that it consists of two Chinese characters. Linguistically these two characters share the same radical - the radical of heart, which signals that the character is associated with feelings. Qing can be translated as “love” or “affection,” while Huai can be translated as “bosom” or “mind”. Together the literal translation of Qinghuai is “the feelings and emotions that an individual possesses”. However, in a similar wa to Guanxi and other Chinese phrases, the meaning of Qinghuai is both nuanced and multi-faceted

Historically, Qinghuai as a phrase is closely related to a particular literary genre—the “wuxia” or martial arts novels, which form an important part of Chinese art and pop culture. In these novels (the most famous of which were authored by a write named Louis Cha from Hong Kong), the protagonists, who usually possesses indomitable martial art skills, are almost always described as people with Qinghuai. For these protagonists, a noble pursuit of life on a spiritual level is the most important thing, especially in comparison with pragmatic pursuits such as money or fame (ie, materialism). These noble pursuits can take many different forms, manifesting in values such as patriotism, humanism, romanticism, loyalty, righteousness, and so on. In this sense, Qinghuai is a way of life that is associated with following one's heart (as opposed to perfect rationality).

In the contemporary context, the term is used to describe a moral commitment toward a spiritual ideal (eg, con tributing to society, conducting oneself in a righteous manner, etc.), as opposed to the pursuit of material or eco nomic gains. However, we also discovered in our investigation that Qinghuai may be used to describe traits that go beyond personal virtues (eg, an organization can be described as possessing Qinghuai). Consequently, our effort towards conceptualizing Qinghuai aim to provide an understanding of the concept and its (new) meanings by identi fying its constituent elements on a number of levels.

## 5.2 | What is Qinghuai?

As discussed above, Qinghuai was independently and organically brought up by various informants at each of th three case organizations, to describe different aspects of their business. This led to our conceptualization of Qinghua as a multi-level, multi-dimensional construct. Figure 2 and Table 2 provide a summary of our conceptualization of th key elements of Qinghuai across three distinct levels: the (a) individual, (b) organizational, and (c) ecosystem levels More specifically, Qinghuai at the individual level concerns individual virtues; Qinghuai at the organizational leve concerns relationships between individuals within a collective, as well as intra-organizational values and norms; Qinghuai at the ecosystem level is related to the overarching mission of the organization, and its relationships to external stakeholders and society in general.

At the individual level, Qinghuai is a form of self-cultivation, which refers to an individual's pursuit of spiritua and aspirational (as opposed to material) goals. The emphasis for the individual is on improving oneself and “doing things that are meaningful,” rather than becoming rich or well-known for what he or she does. For instance, many of our informants at the three organizations, and especially those holding senior positions who had already reached a certain status, mentioned Qinghuai and the desire to participate in a meaningful cause as forming part of their moti vation for founding or joining the organization to begin with, while simultaneously acknowledging the risks and diffi culties of being part of an entrepreneurial start-up. For instance, the co-founder of Parking X told the story of his reasons for leaving his previous job, a senior manager at a state-owned real estate conglomerate, to pursue a differ ent path of life: “I thought I needed to do something different. There is a saying: life needs different journeys. When I informed my then boss of my decision, he told me that I should think about the risks, and asked if I wanted to do it part-time. But I was very committed, and I think entrepreneurship is a highly risky business, even if you do it full-time and work on it 200%… therefore you should do something whole-heartedly or not do it at all… in a way, I chose suc hardship myself, but all these struggles or hesitations come and go [in the face of a passion for entrepreneurship].”

Self-cultivation is in turn driven by and coupled with selflessness, which refers to a spirit of self-sacrifice and th willingness of an individual to set aside material interests for his/her spiritual values. For example, across the three case organizations, there were many similar accounts of informants sacrificing leisure and time with their loved ones for the sake of their organization. which they saw as having an important societal purpose. to the extent that some reported regularly living in the office. For instance, we noticed a tent in the meeting room in which we interviewed the staff of FinTech X, who told us that it was set up by some employees who would camp at the office when work ing late into the night (almost always voluntarily). The most frequently cited reason for this was not because th informants were expected to behave in this way by the organizations or their top management, but simply becaus they believed that they were chasing a spiritual goal and that “it was the right thing to do”. Unanimously and inde pendently, the informants from the three case organizations all mentioned a desire (and commitment) to “create something from nothing”, and hence contribute toward a meaningful cause (in terms of the organization and societ in general).

At the organizational level, Qinghuai manifests as solidarity, which represents a collaborative ethos among coworkers who share the same spiritual ideals (eg, openness and willingness to share) and regard the organization's cause as noble and righteous. The latter, in particular, results in the employees' prioritization of the interests of th organization as a whole. On the one hand, many of the informants indicated that there was a “family-like” atmo sphere. And since they were in an environment of likeminded others. they felt no gualms about sharing knowledge and ideas with each other as they saw this as contributing toward the attainment of their shared ideals. Certain employees even used the Chinese term “zhanyou” (meaning comrade-at-arms) to describe their co-workers. For instance, when describing his relationship with another colleague, the Marketing and Operations Director of Hotel X drew the analogy: “We have the relationship of comrades… it is as if we have been through the long march together”. On the other hand, there is both a sense of unity and a strong, explicit collective identity, which enables the joint pursuit of what is seen to be a noble and righteous organizational cause. It was apparent to us that informants from all three cases firmly believed in the vision and mission of their organization. And consequently, their capacity for working together to achieve the organization's aims was not because their own individual interests were being met by working together, but simply because the organization's cause was something that they believed in. For instance numerous informants reported that their organization possessed Qinghuai because they were collectively creating and building something meaningful together. The Market Procurement Director of Hotel X provided this example:

“… a main characteristic of this company is that… there is a common dream… there is a shared backbone… the direc tions and goals of different departments are aligned, and our progress is unified”.

In addition, a second dimension of Qinghuai at the organizational level is adventurism, which refers to the collec tive willingness to take risks with the aim of sustaining the pursuit of the organizations' cause despite uncertainty. It is a mind-set that goes beyond the innovative and experimental nature that is inherent to technology start-ups (see Ries, 2011), and instead emphasizes a “fighting spirit” that allows the organization to persevere and continuousl improve itself in line with its ideals in the face of all challenges. The HR Manager of FinTech X explained: “Qinghua [as possessed by the staff of FinTech X] is determination, a strong belief, a working-to-death spirit”

At the ecosystem level, Qinghuai involves both balance and harmony in relation to how the organizations manage their relationships with external entities and business partners. The former refers to balance in the pursuit of business and pro-social objectives (ie, the Yin and Yang of entrepreneurial ventures), which was observed at all three case organizations, so that societal contributions are made even as each organization is seeking to develop and offer a novel business model. For instance, the vision espoused by FinTech X, the microloans platform, is to “enable the youth of China to change the world”. Although this may sound grandiose or romantic, it reflects the company's determination and commitment to educate their customers (college students) on the importance of building credibil ity by providing a combination of financial services. These services would allow their customers to not only obtain loans to buy what they want, but also to earn an income or even to generate savings and wealth. The organization also imposes a very stringent credit assessment system when approving loans to their customers. Although established at the expense of business growth and profitability, the organization nevertheless believes that its stringent lending criteria are crucial to their societal role of responsible lender. Similarly, Parking X, the parking space sharing platform, was created because its founders identified a misalignment between demand for and supply of parking spaces in metropolitan China, and saw its business model as a solution to traffic congestion. Consequently, the organization firmly believes that their business involves Qinghuai, since they are creating benefits for the genera population and helping to solve societal problems. As stated by one of Parking X's Product Developers: “I very muc agree with one thing our CEO said: you need to do something that is valuable for society. I think what Parking X does is to deliver promise.”

The element of harmony refers to a sense of respect and consideration for other players within the industry, including competitors and complementors. For example, across the three organizations, we observed clear efforts toward including and catering to the interests of even minor players within their respective industries. In the case of Hotel X, the hotel services platform, a dedicated mobile app was created to recruit housekeepers (ie, cleaning staff and provide central cleaning services for all its managed properties. When developing this app, the needs of th housekeepers were one of the foremost considerations, and Hotel X catered to these needs by incorporating various tools to simplify and streamline their work. The organization even went a step further by negotiating an agreement with an insurance company to provide workplace insurance for these part-time workers whenever they were on assignment for Hotel X. Likewise, when Parking X were designing their business model, they sought to ensure that the security guards who looked after their parking spaces would also receive a commission whenever a transaction was made.

At the beginning of this section, we defined Qinghuai as “a moral commitment toward a spiritual ideal”. Aligned with this definition, the further delineation of Qinghuai at the three different levels discussed above largely falls into two dimensions: (a) spiritual idealism and (b) perpetual development. These findings resonate with and reflect the cultural and contextual complexity of modern China (see Hung et al., 2007) highlighted previously. Firstly, traditiona Chinese values are rooted in Confucianism, which emphasizes the virtues of benevolence, righteousness, wisdom and trustworthiness (Xing, 1995). These are manifested in Qinghuai through the values of selflessness, solidarity, harmony, and balance. Secondly, Chinese communist ideologies, which are rooted in Maoist and Marxist-Leninist thought, advocate collective interests and individual sacrifice (Ladany, 1988). These are manifested in Qinghua through the values of selflessness, solidarity, and harmony. The first dimension of Qinghuai, spiritual idealism, is therefore a reflection of traditional Confucian values adapted to the features of Chinese communism. However, the second dimension of Qinghuai—perpetual development—is a product of the Social Reform era (ie, from 1978 on— see Egri & Ralston, 2004), when individual achievement, economic efficiency and entrepreneurship are encouraged and valued (Rosen, 1990; Tian, 1998). These are manifested in Qinghuai through the values of self-cultivation and adventurism.

To sum up, the concept of Qinghuai, consisting of six elements at three levels, can be integrated through th dimensions of spiritual idealism and perpetual development. These two dimensions reflect the cultural context of modern China, in which the strongly rooted traditional Confucianist values co-exist in parallel with communist ideol ogies and capitalist ideals (Sabet, 2011). Indeed, China presents a unique context that has been shaped by multiple drastic social changes over the last 100 years (Egri & Ralston, 2004; Hung et al., 2007; Vohra, 2000). Our indigenou concept Qinghuai appears to further affirm this view.

## 5.5 | Qinghuai in the organizational domain

In the organizational domain, the embracement of Qinghuai has facilitated the internal development of these start ups in a number of ways. First, evidence from our case studies suggests that the possession of Qinghuai can help these companies cultivate a distinct organizational identity and culture. In particular, we observed that the employees of all three companies exhibited a sense of Solidarity, and seemed to strongly identify with what thei company stood for: in the case of FinTech X, this was the mission to “enable Chinese youth to change the world” b providing them with access to financial services; in the case of Parking X, it was the mission to make parking spaces accessible to everyone; in the case of Hotel X, it was the mission to revolutionize the traditional hotel industry. A mentioned previously, all of these missions were driven by the desire for Harmony and Balance—to solve certain societal issues and to contribute to a better world, and this helped to attract and unite people with similar aspira tions. Moreover, traces of the two dimensions of Qinghuai identified here can be found in the organizational cultur across all three cases. FinTech X has its “Ten Commandments,” ten principles followed by everyone that emphasize both an entrepreneurial spirit and traditional Chinese family values (eg, being hardworking and self-sacrificing, supporting each other, being open and straightforward, etc.). Similarly, Parking X has an established value system that consists of the core values of “collaboration, sharing, win-win, innovation, passion, and inclusiveness”. In a simi lar vein, Hotel X embraces three principles that illustrate their aspirations on three levels: (a) “warmth” at the product level, suggesting that the product they offer should be “heart-warming”, (b) connection at the innovation level, suggesting that they are part of a larger network and have a role to play at this level; and (c) freedom at the spiritua level, indicating that the essence of their product or the consumption culture they promote is freedom of choice. In other words, what we can observe from these three companies is that both managers and employees are motivated by Adventurism (as manifested in the pursuit of innovation and business growth) while at the same time being knit ted together in a spirit of Solidarity and collaboration in their pursuit of a higher cause.

Second, the embodiment of Qinghuai has enabled continuous learning, which is deemed crucial for entrepre neurial ventures in general (Ries, 2011) and digital entrepreneurship in particular (Nambisan, 2017). At the individua level, self-cultivation (the commitment to continuous self-improvement) seemed to be the common driver behin the decisions of many of the interviewees to join the three start-up companies and to work continuously towar their goals. For instance, the importance of gaining experience was often brought up, especially by entry-level hire (who were often millennials) at the three organizations. For example, an employee from Parking X explained: “Yo learn much more in one year working in a start-up than in three years working in an established company”. To cop with the speed at which the start-ups evolved, many interviewees across the three organizations indicated that the worked overtime of their own accord, committing to the organization in Solidarity with their co-workers, and spent spare time reading and studying to enhance their skills. For instance, one interviewee from FinTech X spent so muc time in the office that he had brought a sleeping bag to enable him to stay overnight in the conference room of th organization during the week, in order to reduce the time and cost of commuting. This spirit of continuous learning and improvement also permeated the organizational level. All three companies had organized a formal series of col lective brain-storming or problem-solving sessions. Informally, some interviewees also mentioned “an atmosphere of learning” across the three organizations, and reported that this atmosphere was contagious, influencing almost everyone who worked at the company. Interestingly, this eagerness to learn and improve was attributed to th entrepreneurial nature of the companies, as explained by the Product Development Director of Parking X: “Th source [of the learning atmosphere] is still the innovative business model, which demands that the new functionali ties (of our products) are different from other companies. This means that you have to think from scratch, both in terms of functionality and the structure. This cannot be done by following the rules, and hence creates a sense of excitement”.

Third, the embracement of Qinghuai can help start-ups to overcome resource constraints, another key challenge for new entrepreneurial ventures (Starr & MacMillan, 1990). Qinghuai enables start-ups to compete with establishe technology firms (such as Baidu, Alibaba and Tencent in China) for talent, since although they may not be able to offer the same material benefits as these resource-rich organizations, they are able to offer opportunities for their employees to “chase their dreams”, “make a difference” and “constantly learn”. Indeed, many of the informants we spoke with described their Solidarity with their co-workers and the open, exciting and family-like environment as some of the key reasons for their willingness to forego a higher salary and better benefits at a more established firm and to work for a start-up instead. The embracement of Qinghuai not only makes it easier for start-ups to recruit tal ent, but also to get the most out of their employees. This is because the value of Selflessness drives employees to contribute more toward collective goals, and through effective collectivization, the start-ups are able to maximiz their limited resources and overcome their existing constraints. This is consistent with existing research on entrepre neurship in China, which suggests that the initial stage of firm creation is heavily dependent on “ganqing”, or the affection that an employee feels toward an organization (Guo & Miller, 2010).

In summary, the embracement of Qinghuai in the organizational domain can be instrumental in facilitating th internal development of a company. The two dimensions of Qinghuai can help to attract and unite talented peopl with similar aspirations (ie, to be part of an entrepreneurial venture and useful to society at the same time), to creat a common identity, and to establish an atmosphere of learning and self-improvement. However, it should be note that the three case companies were careful only to recruit people who shared similar values or, in their words, “peo ple with Qinghuai”. The Chief Front-End Architect of FinTech X provided an illustration: “We are quite strict at our job interviews… we mainly focus on two things: one is how passionate the candidate is about learning and how demanding he/she is on self-improvement; the other is his/her attitude [toward contributing to a collective goal]”.

## 6 | DISCUSSION

Our case studies of three Chinese start-up companies resulted in the development of the indigenous concept of Qinghuai and the illustration of its role in the successful enactment of digital entrepreneurship in China. Figure 3 summarizes our main findings. We argue that the concept of Qinghuai both reflects the context in which it was origi nated (ie, contemporary China at a historic crossroads, and influenced by multiple forces), and the uniqueness of digi tal entrepreneurship in China. In the rest of the section, we will elaborate further on these two points.

## 6.1 | Qinghuai as a product of contemporary China

As shown in Figure 3, we have conceptualized Qinghuai as a construct of two dimensions permeating across thre levels. The origins of Qinghuai in wuxia are echoed in the dimension of spiritual idealism, which is manifested through the traits of selflessness, solidarity, balance, and harmony. From our case studies, we observed a common set of ideals that all three start-ups were striving for: contribution to something meaningful, improving Chinese soci ety, and being open and collaborative toward a higher cause. This determined pursuit of ideals can be partially attrib uted to traditional Chinese values (see Xing, 1995), and also partially attributed to a desire to shun the materialisti and capitalistic tendencies that are often observed in contemporary China (Bartolini & Sarracino, 2015).

The second dimension of Qinghuai, perpetual development, is manifested in the traits of self-cultivation and adventurism. We argue that this dimension can be regarded as the modern version of Qinghuai's origins in wuxia, and is a result of the “social reform era” (post 1978) during which rapid industrialization and modernization led to unprecedented economic growth (Tian, 1998). To draw an analogy, protagonists with Qinghuai in wuxia novels would pursue their spiritual ideals through their martial arts skills, whereas individuals and collectives with Qinghua pursue their ideals through entrepreneurial endeavours. The commonality lies in the desire for perpetual self-devel opment, and hence a commitment to invention and innovation was apparent in all three start-ups. For these workers the excitement of being part of an entrepreneurial venture lay in the adventure of creating something of their ow that required continuous self-cultivation and improvement to sustain

In this sense, we argue that Qinghuai is the manifestation of the above two forces at work, and hence a product of this particular era in China. It reflects both the traditional values that are deeply rooted in Chinese culture and th significant social and economic changes the country has undergone in the past century. The existing literature on human resource management and consumer behaviour has explored the complexity of the Chinese context by revealing the different value systems held by different generational cohorts (eg, Egri & Ralston, 2004; Farh, Zhong, & Organ, 2004; Hung et al., 2007). However, our conceptualization of Qinghuai has highlighted that forces exerted b Confucian values, communist ideologies, and capitalist beliefs interact in a dynamic and integral manner on individ uals or groups of individuals, regardless of the generational divide. We are therefore experiencing a China that is nei ther traditional nor modern, neither communist nor capitalist. Its identity is unique in the current era.

## 6.2 | Qinghuai and digital entrepreneurship in China

Our three case studies have also provided an indication of the role of Qinghuai in driving digital entrepreneurship in China. We argue that what is at work here is the integrated concept of Qinghuai, rather than its disparate constitu ent elements. In particular, both dimensions of Qinghuai, as manifested in the different elements of Qinghuai across the various levels, have contributed to the business, organizational, and technological domains of digital entrepre neurship in a synergistic manner. Take the identified role of “crafting a distinct value proposition” for example: the dimension of spiritual idealism has, in a way, led the three start-ups to aim not for an offering that maximizes eco nomic rationality or business growth, but which also takes into account the long-term wellbeing of society. This was achieved in each of our case organizations by focusing on a specific societal issue: for FinTech X, it was a lack of access to financial services by younger people and college students; for Parking X, it was a shortage of parking spaces and the problem of congestion in urban China; and for Hotel X, it was the operational challenges (eg, declining profits, competition from Airbnb) of the hospitality industry. At the same time, the dimension of perpetual develop ment ensured the viability of their business models, as the companies sought to solve these problems through inven tion and continuous innovation (constantly aiming for the development of something new and revolutionary). For example, FinTech X developed a closed-loop financial ecosystem with digital offerings; Parking X adopted a nove C2B2C model supported by an expansive digital infrastructure; and Hotel X came up with a dual-sharing model (ie sharing accommodation and housekeepers) that utilized various digital technologies. In this sense, the ideal is pur sued via innovation, and the innovation is realized in the ideal

Moreover, our study reveals that the embodiment of Qinghuai is instrumental for digital entrepreneurship in China, and especially in the way it enables technology start-ups to overcome the typical challenges of entrepreneur ship. These challenges include the need to quickly attract a critical mass of customers (Eisenmann, Parker, & Van Alstyne, 2006), ensure the long-term viability of the business (Finkelstein, 2001), navigate the uncertainties withi the regulatory environment (Hoffmann et al., 2009), and overcome resource constraints (Starr & MacMillan, 1990) As indicated earlier, when powered by Qinghuai, a new digital entrepreneurship venture may find it easier to craft a unique value proposition with a distinct social angle that helps to set it apart from its competitors, thus facilitating the attraction of an initial customer base (Tan et al., 2017). By corollary, this implies that established firms may also be able to take advantage of Qinghuai to continuously evolve their existing value propositions in accordance to market needs, thereby meet the challenge of constant adjustment that is associated with digital entrepreneurship (se Nambisan, 2017).

In addition, by demonstrating a commitment to this higher societal cause over time, these ventures may be abl to improve their image and cultivate goodwill among both their customers and policy makers. In doing so, these ven tures may attain a form of organizational legitimacy (see Aldrich & Fiol, 1994), which would not only enhance th long-term viability of their business and their ability to navigate changes in the regulatory landscape (Park & Luo, 2001; Tsang, 1998), but allow them to attract and work with a greater variety of external resource owners (Fisher, Kotha, & Lahiri, 2015) and engage in collective value co-creation (Nambisan, 2017) as well. Moreover, by focusing on the recruitment of employees who embody the values of Qinghuai, a start-up may be able to attract and retain talent to enable the start-up to better compete with more established competitors. Our study reveals that thi may be because employees with Qinghuai are more likely to be willing to forego economic gain to some extent, an to dedicate themselves more fully to the organizational cause.

We also propose that Qinghuai may be equally instrumental in equipping the three start-ups to cope with the unique challenges of digital entrepreneurship. For instance, one trait that the three companies shared was their determination to develop their digital offerings and core technological systems in-house (eg, see Steininger, 2019) As explained by our informants, this was driven by the desire, based on a spirit of Adventurism, to invent something from scratch and “leap from zero to one”. The capability to develop the requisite technological skills within the com pany allowed these start-ups to reduce the level of uncertainty to some extent, and have more control over th dynamism that digital entrepreneurship entails (Nambisan, 2017). To illustrate, FinTech X's decision to develop their own risk-control system had given them a competitive edge in a turbulent environment infused with regulatory uncertainties. This technical choice was, in turn, a result of embracing the spiritual idealism of Qinghuai, as their goa was to educate young people on responsible spending and credit building (as opposed to growing the business at al costs).

Furthermore, the embodiment of perpetual development had also enabled the three startups to continuousl improve their technologies and to adapt to the changing environment. One phrase that was used very often by the informants engaged in product development was “iterate” (or “diedai” in Chinese), suggesting that the products and or the technological platforms are constantly being changed and updated. This fixation with improving iterativel was driven in part by the desire to innovate, and in part by the individuals' motivation to learn about the newes technologies. The perpetual development of the firms' technological capabilities therefore facilitated the continuou evolution of value propositions, which, as explained previously, is deemed a key challenge of digital entrepreneurshi (Nambisan, 2017). For instance, in all three cases, the business models of the start-ups had evolved over time, whic would not have happened if it were not for adjustments or innovations on the technological front

In addition, Qinghuai also enabled the three start-ups to develop platforms that were generative in nature, whic further enhanced their capacity to involve and engage with a wide range of stakeholders and to establish an ecosystem around the business model. Both dimensions of Qinghuai were instrumental here. For instance, informants from Hotel X spoke about how they went the extra mile to keep the platforms open, because they wanted to use state of-art technology. This later enabled the business side stakeholders to leverage these open platforms to connect with other stakeholders, beyond the original design. On the other hand, the embodiment of spiritual idealism enabled the companies to attend to bigger and broader issues. In the case of FinTech X, they connected their platform with other portals because they realized that offering a full portfolio of services (eg, borrowing, spending, and earnin money), rather than simply micro-loan services, would allow them to better serve and educate college students

Finally, we would like to underscore the influence of Qinghuai in the technological domain. Besides its role in facilitating the development of innovative and generative digital offerings, Qinghuai appears to be manifested in the technology itself. For instance, many of our informants at FinTech X labelled their digital product “a platform with Qinghuai” due to the goal of helping youths and college students access financial services in a responsible way. In other words, Qinghuai was embedded in their offerings through specific technological configurations. From the firm's perspective, the leverage of technological advancements to enhance risk controls was their way of showing responsibility and care for their end customers. This finding, in which social values were embedded in technological design, resonates with recent calls in the field of IS for the deployment of ICT for the betterment of humanity (see Lee, Cho, & Lim, 2018; Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2016), an approach which has also been termed positive computin (see Tondello, 2017) and responsible innovation (see Stahl, 2013).

## 6.3 | Qinghuai and other related concepts

To further highlight the unique contributions of our proposed indigenous concept, we draw a comparison between Qinghuai and other related concepts in the existing literature (see Table 4). These concepts include Guanxi, collectivism, collective action and social entrepreneurship.

Although there is some overlap in terms of the values attributed to both concepts (eg, the emphasis on harmony or “hexie”—see Arnesen & Foster, 2018), Qinghuai operates on an entirely different level than Guanxi. More specifi cally, although it has been argued that Guanxi is important in the creation and development of entrepreneurial firms in China (Guo & Miller, 2010), it is mainly concerned with external network building and dyadic inter-personal relationships (Chen, Chen, & Huang, 2013). Conversely, Qinghuai signifies a certain virtue possessed by individuals and organizations, and is mainly concerned with internal self-cultivation in the context of the creation of an entrepreneurial firm. Qinghuai is also not relationship-specific, but instead is a general sense of affection toward a higher cause. In addition, the development of Guanxi can be a strategic choice, and may be established in a contrived man ner for the purpose of attaining favors and benefits such as better career prospects and job placements (Xiao & Tsui, 2007). Qinghuai, on the other hand, is a natural inclination that cannot be artificially acquired; it can only be cul tivated over an extended period of time.

As compared with collectivism, an attribute often associated with the Chinese culture (Hofstede, 1984, 1991) Qinghuai is a more encompassing and multi-faceted concept. We posit that individuals or entities with Qinghuai wil necessarily exhibit collectivist traits, but not vice versa. This is because it is possible for an individual to exhibit col lectivist traits without feeling genuine affection for the organization he/she works for or the cause the organizatio is striving for. For instance, Healy, Bradley, and Mukherjee (2004) discuss the concept of “instrumental collectivism”, in which individuals act in concert with the collective to maximize self-interests. In addition to Qinghuai, th motivation for collectivism may stem from cognitive (eg, social categorization) and behavioural (eg, interdependence) factors (Henry et al., 1999).

Related to collectivism is the notion of collective action, defined as “joint action in pursuit of a common objec tive” (Okamoto, 2003, p. 813). While the former refers to a mindset, the latter pertains to actions that are taken in a concerted manner and was originally proposed as a theoretical construct to focus attention on solving the “socia dilemma,” in which short-term individual self-interests are at odds with long-term collective interests (Ostrom, 1998) More specifically, the social dilemma stems from the “common good” nature of the benefits of participating in a col lective (Willer, 2009). Individuals are motivated to participate in collective activities because they anticipate synergies from pooling their resources (Kitts, 2006). However, the benefits of participating in a collective tend to be characterized by (a) the impossibility of exclusion, defined as the inability to exclude members of the collective from enjoying the benefits of membership even if they do not contribute to it; and (b) the jointness of supply, where one member's enjoyment of the benefits do not diminish the level of benefits to other members (Monge et al., 1998) Consequently, free-riding (Albanese & Van Fleet, 1985) or the inclination to contribute as little as possible, is a signifi cant problem. This premise is distinct from our conceptualization of Qinghuai, which posits that effective joint actio is due less to the alignment of individual interests than to a natural manifestation of its inherent values (eg, a share belief in a higher cause). In other words, we are similarly arguing here that individuals or entities with Qinghuai wil tend to act collectively, but that not all joint actions are due to the workings of Qinghuai.

Finally, Qinghuai, with its emphasis on a higher (and often social) cause, may be deemed to be related to socia entrepreneurship, which is defined as entrepreneurship exercised for the purpose of “… creating social value, eithe exclusively or at least in some prominent way…” (Peredo & McLean, 2006, p. 64). However, our conceptualization of Qinghuai is distinct from social entrepreneurship in a number of ways. Firstly, social entrepreneurship describes a form of entrepreneurship, while Qinghuai refers to a particular virtue. In other words, although we have examine Qinghuai in the context of digital entrepreneurship, it can also manifest in other domains (eg, social behaviour). Sec ondly, social entrepreneurship places “social value” at the centre of the entrepreneurial activities. For Qinghuai, th pursuit of a higher cause is certainly important, but there are also other values and dimensions that we have identified as being just as important.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

In answering the research question “How is digital entrepreneurship enacted in China?”, we have developed th indigenous concept of Qinghuai through three case studies of digital entrepreneurship ventures. We have presented Qinghuai as a multi-level and multi-dimensional concept, and have showcased its role in driving digital entrepreneur ship. We argue that Qinghuai is on the one hand a product of the peculiarity and complexity of contemporary China and on the other hand the means through which digital entrepreneurship can be successfully enacted in such a con text. We believe that the development of this concept has enhanced our understanding of China and its institutiona and cultural setting, and has contributed to efforts towards seeking contextualized explanations (see Davison & Mar tinsons, 2016) and consequently the theorization of the digital entrepreneurship phenomenon

## 7.1 | Limitations and future research

This study is not without its limitations. First, although the conduct of multiple case studies is generally deemed to be more robust than a research design based on a single case (Eisenhardt, 1989), a common criticism of the case research method is the problem of generalizability or external validity (Walsham, 2006). One issue in particular tha limits the generalizability of our study may lie in its singular context (ie, all of our three cases are technology start ups, and may not be fully representative of all forms of digital entrepreneurship). However, this study invokes the principles of analytic generalization (see Silva & Hirschheim, 2007) or what some researchers refer to as “generalizin from description to theory” (Lee & Baskerville, 2003, p. 235). In any case, future research should be directed at vali dating the propositions of our research framework, so that the boundary conditions of our study can be bette defined. In addition, future research should also investigate whether a similar concept or constellation of concept exist in other contexts, in other contexts that are both similar to and different from that of China

Second, while we have developed a conceptualization of Qinghuai that consists of two dimensions and six ele ments, and have articulated eight potential roles of Qinghuai in facilitating digital entrepreneurship in China, it is necessary to acknowledge that we are bounded by the extent of the data collected (Pan & Tan, 2011). In other words although we have tried to be as comprehensive and holistic as possible in our development of this concept, we are by no means suggesting that our specification of the constituent elements of the construct and its influence on digi tal entrepreneurship is complete or exhaustive, as this is impossible to achieve within the scope of a single researc article (eg, see Tan, Pan, Lu, & Huang, 2015). Nevertheless, the fact that Qinghuai was organically raised as a concept by interviewees at three separate organizations without prompting on our part is, in our view, an indication of its importance and relevance. Our hope is that this paper will serve as a catalyst for future studies of this concept, so that our understanding of the nature and implications of Qinghuai can be advanced. In particular, future studies should seek to validate the findings of our study (ie, Figures 2 and 3), or to extend them by fleshing out the concept of Qinghuai and its influence on digital entrepreneurship more fully. It may also be fruitful to explore whether there is a sequence in terms of how Qinghuai influences the various aspects of digital entrepreneurship, and to conduct comparative study of entrepreneurial ventures with (ie, where Qinghuai occupies a key position in the organization's system of values) and without Qinghuai (ie, where Qinghuai is absent or has minimal influence). In this way, th necessity and significance of Qinghuai's role in facilitating digital entrepreneurship can be better understood and ascertained. Finally, a longitudinal study with an ethnographic approach that traces the development of a start-u and examines how the role of Qinghuai changes as the start-up grows and matures may also be a fruitful avenue fo future research.

## 7.2 | Practical implications

In spite of these limitations, we believe that the results of our study have significant implications for practice, particularly for two stakeholder groups operating within the domain of digital entrepreneurship in China. The first of these stakeholder groups would, of course, consist of digital entrepreneurs and managers of technology start-ups. For thi group, our study can be a source of empirically supported prescriptions on how to overcome some of the key chal lenges that they may be facing. For instance, our study suggests that the cultivation of Qinghuai within their organi zation and the recruitment of employees with Qinghuai can be crucial, particularly if their business is in the fledgling startup phase. This is because Qinghuai can help them to attract an initial base of customers (see Eisenman et al., 2006) and overcome their resource constraints, which may be particularly severe compared to their mor established competitors (Starr & MacMillan, 1990). More importantly, although it is not the primary focus of our cur rent study, our findings also hint at how Qinghuai can be cultivated; in particular, our study suggests that Qinghua must be embedded into the culture of the focal organization. While culture is notoriously difficult to influence (Mey erson & Martin, 1987), individuals with Qinghuai whose aspirations resonate with that of the organization should b recruited as a priority, in order to seed Qinghuai within the organizational culture. In addition, while it may be rela tively straightforward to recruit and/or retain employees who are aligned with the higher cause pursued by the organization, our study suggests that there is a certain momentum that needs to be maintained through the cultivation of a spirit of self-improvement and continuous innovation. In other words, spiritual idealism in isolation is insufficient The potential of Qinghuai in facilitating digital entrepreneurship will only be fully realized when coupled with th dimension of perpetual development.

The second stakeholder group that may be potentially informed by our study consists of government authorities and policy makers who are concerned with the development and maintenance of vibrant entrepreneurial ecosystems within the country (especially those centered on Beijing and Shenzhen and the other technology hubs springing up across the country - see Soo & Chen, 2017). While it is almost inevitable that the majority of start-ups will fail in China (see Banjo, 2018), our study can help this stakeholder group to identify the ventures that are more likely to succeed, since they possess the traits (ie, the dimensions and elements of Qinghuai) that render them more suited for survival within the entrepreneurial landscape (eg, see Tan, Pan, & Hackney, 2010). Resources can then subse quently be allocated or diverted to these ventures as a priority, so that their probability of survival is furthe increased. Conversely, our study can also help government authorities and policy makers to identify those ventures that are less likely to succeed. The appropriate remedial actions can then be taken (eg, channelling the efforts of those ventures toward social causes, to help them differentiate themselves from established businesses), in order to preserve the resources that have been invested in the establishment of these ventures and potentially mitigate th rate of digital entrepreneurship failure

Finally, our findings can also benefit employees, the third stakeholder group. Our investigation suggests that individuals who see Qinghuai as a legitimate cause for pursuit seem to align better with organizations that also embody the spirit of Qinghuai. Therefore, in the current environment of China, where materialistic causes are regarded as highly important (Bartolini & Sarracino, 2015), it is an encouraging sign for individuals who have more spiri tual or aspirational aims that companies or startups striving for a greater cause still exist. It may therefore b beneficial for these employees to target organizations that embody the traits of Qinghuai when seeking employment opportunities. This is especially the case for people who have already established careers and are now at the stage of looking for new, meaningful challenges.
