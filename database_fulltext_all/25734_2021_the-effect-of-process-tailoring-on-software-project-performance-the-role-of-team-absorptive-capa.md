# The effect of process tailoring on software project performance: The role of team absorptive capacity and its knowledge-based enablers

## Abstract

Software process tailoring (SPT) is a team-based and learning-intensive activity that addresses the particular dynamic characteristics of a development project. Because SPT critically influences how projects are conducted, its performance should be investigated. However, the extant literature lacks empirical evidence on how the underlying effects of SPT performance and its team-supportive factors operate and influence software project performance. From the knowledge perspective, this study adopts dynamic capabilities theory and considers the learning ability and absorptive capacity of software project teams to develop a theoretical model to address this gap. The results of an empirical examination of the model with 135 software project teams advance our understanding of how team-level learning antecedents—experience, communication quality and trust—dynamically facilitate teams' absorptive capacity (AC) when they conduct SPT, which in turn reinforces project performance. The mediating effects of the proposed model are unveiled and discussed, and theoretical implications as well as practical guidance for how AC and these factors promote SPT and project performance are suggested.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Contemporary software projects have the characteristics of uniqueness and volatility, leading development teams to modify standard processes and continue to make adjustments as needed. Adjusting software project development to respond to the variance and dynamics across the project is a process known as software process tailoring (SPT (Campanelli & Parreiras, 2015; Park & Bae, 2013; Tripp & Armstrong, 2016; Xu & Ramesh, 2008). SPT is used i either sequential (eg, waterfall) or iterative (eg, agile) development (Bass, 2016; Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010; Park & Bae, 2013). In addition to its typical usage in a project's planning stage to fit the development to unique settings SPT is particularly important for agile development, where it is repeatedly conducted during the development cycles to enable the project to evolve to better fulfil the users' needs or changes (Campanelli, Camilo, & Parreiras, 2018 Lee, Wang, & Chen, 2020).

For software teams, SPT is a challenging task as it requires intensive knowledge and determination in the modifi cation of processes followed by experimentation throughout the development period. The existing SPT research mostly focuses on the development of technical approaches or methods to better customise standard processes (eg Alegría, Bastarrica, Quispe, & Ochoa, 2014; Bass, 2016; Campanelli et al., 2018; Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010; Park & Bae, 2013). From a management perspective, the further exploration of contributive team efforts to the performanc of SPT and project outcomes, that is, the project performance for meeting the quality, schedule and budget, merit an empirical investigation (Campanelli et al., 2018; Campanelli & Parreiras, 2015; Xu & Ramesh, 2007, 2008). Specifi cally, SPT is deemed a collaborative decision task that solicits and leverages various domain knowledge and experi ence from project team members (Coleman & O'Connor, 2008; Xu & Ramesh, 2008). In this regard, team learning becomes a critical effort in conducting SPT, and understanding the performance of SPT would help the learning efforts in a software team lead to efficient and effective tailoring decisions that might, in turn, impact the project performance.

Since SPT reflects the fact that software projects must accommodate their particularities and volatility, softwar teams should learn to be flexible and adaptable when performing SPT. We argue that this important aspect of team learning can be addressed using the dynamic capabilities theory. To understand dynamic capabilities, Lin, Su, and Higgins (2016) proposed a useful typology in which absorptive capacity (AC) is the major construct for an organisa tion's learning ability to identify, assimilate and apply valuable external knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990) Researchers have extended organisation-level AC to team-level AC and investigated how it helps improve and inno vate knowledge-related teamwork (Ali, Musawir, & Ali, 2018; Bjorvatn & Wald, 2018; Popaitoon & Siengthai, 2014; Tiwana & Mclean, 2005). Therefore, in this study, we consider AC as a mechanism for realising team learning. Fur thermore, because SPT is knowledge intensive and self-adaptable (Xu & Ramesh, 2008), in examining the dynamic capabilities of teams in SPT, we focus on team-level AC in terms of the knowledge acquisition, assimilation, transfor mation and exploitation (Leal-Rodríguez, Roldán, Ariza-Montes, & Leal-Millán, 2014; Pavlou & Sawy, 2006) of soft ware teams in responding to different SPT contexts.

However, one issue arises when AC is applied to SPT in which team members are encouraged to challenge exis ting processes, to solicit opinions and experiences that may be unfavourable and conflictual, as well as to critiqu and/or accommodate one another to reach integrative and tailored solutions. Given these challenges, it is unclear how teams acquire, assimilate, and exploit knowledge to nevertheless yield efficient and effective tailoring results (Xu & Ramesh, 2008). Hence, to determine how AC enables SPT, we further consider the knowledge-based team enablers—focusing on communication quality, experience, and trust—to address the aforementioned SPT context The details of the examination of these enablers are further explained in Section 2.3. According to the literature team communication quality determines the extent to which the members of a team effectively share critical infor mation to reach a consensus and generate knowledge (Boies, Fiset, & Gill, 2015; Chen, Li, Clark, & Dietrich, 2013 Gonzálezromá & Hernández, 2014). Additionally, team trust refers to a secure climate among team members that prompts their willingness to speak freely (Gong, Cheung, Wang, & Huang). Experience is a key influencer in knowl edge-intensive collaborative decision processes. Although these factors are largely known, this study seeks to explore their deeper and previously undescribed effects, specifically in the SPT context, to help software teams bet ter navigate the turbulent development of SPT to obtain project success.

Based on the above descriptions, several research questions are addressed in this study: (a) Does a soft ware team's AC effectively facilitate SPT performance, and, if so, how? (b) How do the three team-based knowledge antecedents influence AC, which in turn promotes SPT performance? (c) How do AC and SPT affect project performance? To answer these questions, this study proposes a research model that utilises the partia least squares (PLS) technique and examines 135 in-house software development project teams collected from Taiwanese firms. The findings of this paper provide insight into SPT learning requirements to address unstabl software development. Our results contribute to the adoption of dynamic capabilities theory and its embodi ment of AC to foster SPT. The findings in this paper advance our understanding of how the three team-based learning factors facilitate distinct mediating effects of teams' AC in conducting SPT. We also offer suggestions for how AC and these factors promote SPT performance, which in turn contributes to project performance. The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 presents the theoretical development of the proposed model and hypotheses. Section 3 describes the research methodology. Section 4 presents the sample collectio and data analyses. Section 5 details the findings and implications of this study. Section 6 describes the limita tions of the study and outlines future research opportunities

## 2 | RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

## 2.1 | Software process tailoring and its performance

A software process is defined as a set of activities, tools, methods, and artefacts that are the elements for com posing software development (Park & Bae, 2013). Many process reference models can be used to standardise development; however, those models cannot always be directly applied to a project's process given the particular development and project conditions (Ginsberg & Quinn, 1995; Xu & Ramesh, 2007). However, designing new processes to fit each project would require a considerable amount of time and resources (Slaughter, Levine, Ramesh, Pries-Heje, & Baskerville, 2006; Xu & Ramesh, 2008). Therefore, software teams use their orga nisation's existing software process standard and adjust and fine-tune its software processes to meet the condi tions of the individual project. The way in which a software process is tailored and modified can be specified based on attributes such as formality, which refers to the degree of execution of the processes or their activities and artefacts, for example, full application, simplification, replacement or exemption; frequency, which refer to how often the processes are performed; granularity, which refers to the level of detail with which the processes are defined; and scope, which refers to the area addressed by the software processes (Ginsberg & Quinn, 1995; Xu & Ramesh, 2007).

SPT is suitable for planning software development to meet the particularities of a given project. SPT also sup ports the flexibility of the development process in responding to project dynamics. For example, in agile develop ment, SPT is needed to adjust the process for upcoming development cycles or support development sprints to better meet the project's ongoing needs (Bass, 2016; Campanelli et al., 2018; Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010; Tripp & Armstrong, 2016). Since SPT is critical to the effort and work delivered in a project, its performance should be investigated. To study SPT performance, Xu and Ramesh (2008) defined it based on its effectiveness and efficiency. Spe cifically, the effectiveness of SPT is defined as “the quality of tailoring decisions,” and the efficiency of process tailoring is defined as “the effort needed for process tailoring” (Xu & Ramesh, 2008, p. 291). Correspondingly, the present study examines SPT performance by considering how the effort of performing the task and the quality of tailoring decisions influence project performance. In this study, which scrutinises team learning in SPT to obtain bette performance, we first explore team absorptive capacity and then address team knowledge antecedents in the follow ing sections.

## 2.2 | Absorptive capacity

The existing literature has shown that absorptive capacity (AC) can be a team-level learning construct (Ali et al., 2018 Bjorvatn & Wald, 2018; Tiwana & Mclean, 2005). This is true when AC reflects an interdependent combination of individuals' knowledge structures and learning activities (Roberts, Galluch, Dinger, & Grover, 2012). AC is rooted in dynamic capabilities theory, the importance of which is emphasised in a project-based context (Bjorvatn & Wald, 2018; Leal-Rodríguez et al., 2014; Popaitoon & Siengthai, 2014). The dynamic, complex and unique characteristics of software development create the need for intensive knowledge concerning different process implementations for different project contexts in performing SPT (Campanelli & Parreiras, 2015; Xu & Ramesh, 2007) Note that project contexts refer to, for example, problem domains and clients' business environments, system designs and functionalities, requirements status, and user platforms, which require development to be highly innova tive and changeable. Moreover, each tailoring task, despite its innovativeness and uniqueness, may need to engage knowledge from other projects to obtain repeatable success as well as to avoid repeating mistakes. Hence, a team must first have the ability to absorb external SPT knowledge and then the ability to apply that knowledge to the task.

Drawing from the definitions of AC from Zahra and George (2002) and Pavlou and Sawy (2006), we define AC as a team's self-learning capabilities in terms of acquiring, assimilating, transforming and exploiting knowledge to generate new knowledge to better respond to environmental changes within a project. Table 1 below further summarises the definition of four AC components, that is, the acquisition, assimilation, transformation and exploitation of knowledge. Thus, AC can be conceptualised and operationalised as a construct consisting of composite indicators (Sarstedt, Hair, Ringle, Thiele, & Gudergan, 2016) of the four components (Bjorvatn & Wald, 2018; Jansen, Bosch, & Volberda, 2005). Note that team AC does not reside in any single individual but depends on a mosaic of collectiv capabilities that are dynamically held by the team (Roberts et al., 2012; Tiwana & Mclean, 2005). Therefore, AC does not refer to a static team learning process but, rather, the extent to which that process can be sufficiently flexible to cope with dynamic project requirements and environments (Lee, Chen, & Shiue, 2017; Lee, Hsu, & Chen, 2018) Therefore, in the SPT context, we adopt AC as a team's knowledge-based dynamic capability to address how the per formance of SPT can be enhanced by a team's AC as well as to determine the factors that could increase team AC These factors (ie, experience, communication quality and trust) are further explained in the following sections (Sec tions 2.3-2.6).

## 2.3 | Selected enablers of absorptive capacity in SPT

SPT is a special type of teamwork involving task conflicts and diverse professional perspectives of members (Le et al., 2020). Therefore, determining how to increase shared understanding and coordination among members to effectively support team learning (ie, AC) in software teams under a conflictual SPT context is critical. We consider experience, trust, and communication quality for the following reasons. First, SPT is a knowledge-intensive and expe rience-oriented activity (Xu & Ramesh, 2007, 2008), and in this context, members who possess tailoring experiences appear to address conflicts and coordinate task knowledge among members effectively when planning the develop ment. Second, trust and communication are reportedly key factors in investigations exploring team operation and performance (Boies et al., 2015). Specifically, in the IS development context, quality communication helps resolve disagreements and misunderstanding and is deemed the most elementary component in learning-oriented teamwork (Hsu, Shih, Chiang, & Liu, 2012). This is particularly true as the success of IS development is cultivated by a share understanding (Bittner & Leimeister, 2014; Corvera Charaf, Rosenkranz, & Holten, 2013; Rosenkranz, Charaf, & Hol ten, 2013). Furthermore, trust can increase social norms and relationships in collaborative software development (Ibbott & O'Keefe, 2004; McHugh, Conboy, & Lang, 2012). Trust is also a key factor in resolving task conflict (Curs¸eu & Schruijer, 2010) and facilitating knowledge sharing (Costa, Fulmer, & Anderson, 2017; Lin, Hsu, Cheng, & Wu, 2011; Staples & Webster, 2008). These three factors appear to facilitate software teams' ability to foster AC and we theorise and empirically investigate their deeper relationships and mechanisms in AC in the context of SPT.

Moreover, in the literature, team trust and communication are often discussed with psychological safety (Car meli & Gittell, 2009; Gong et al., 2012; Yanchus, Derickson, Moore, Bologna, & Osatuke, 2014). This study individually explores and investigates team communication and trust instead of psychological safety for the following reasons. First, trust is considered a foundation of psychological safety (Carmeli & Gittell, 2009; Edmondson, 1999). As psychological safety stems from interpersonal trust (Carmeli & Gittell, 2009; Edmondson & Lei, 2014; Hennel & Rosenkranz, 2020; Vandekerkhof, Steijvers, Hendriks, & Voordeckers, 2018), it is useful to examine trust rather than psychological safety to increase traceability (Gong et al., 2012). Second, communication is considered the primary means by which psychological safety is manifested and observed (Yanchus et al., 2014). In addition, the nature of a team is dynamic in composition, and as psychological safety may not be achievable in all situations involving th team, quality communication remains a necessity (Jain, Fennell, Chagpar, Connolly, & Nembhard, 2016). Therefore in this study, separate investigations of team trust and team communication quality allow us to understand more of their distinct mediating effects on AC and SPT performance.

## 2.4 | The effect of team experience on absorptive capacity

Scholars have shown that AC largely depends on the level of prior relevant knowledge or experience already possessed by an organisation (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002). However, organisations and teams hav inherent differences. Unlike teams, organisations are stable and formal social structures with more institutionalised procedures to gather, apply and permanently store knowledge. By contrast, project teams are loose and time-limited social structures (Cohen & Bailey, 1997). They may lack steady and firm effort in collecting and storing knowledge resulting in less or no permanent support for continuous knowledge creation (Fong, 2003)

In studying team-level AC, having experienced team members, in general, effectively improves the team and enhances the team's ability to appropriately leverage suitable knowledge and effectively apply it to make better judgements and decisions (Haas, 2006). However, a software project team that is engaged in software development usually involves a more dynamic composition. In addition, SPT is a repetitive process that requires a continuous learning effort on the part of the team in making ad hoc tailoring decisions throughout a dynamic development process. Within a software project team, individual members may have rich and varied experience; however, whethe the experience and knowledge are effective and sustainable in continually performing SPT is questionable in such temporary and unstable environment. Hence, it is important to determine whether a software team's learning capa bility in SPT would be hampered if it no longer had contributing members on the team. Correspondingly, we hypo thesise the following:

H1: Software team experience has a positive influence on absorptive capacity.

## 2.5 | The effect of team communication quality on absorptive capacity

Team communication quality describes how clearly, effectively, completely, fluently and timely messages and infor mation are delivered among members of a team (Gonzálezromá & Hernández, 2014). A higher level of team commu nication quality can increase the accuracy, timeliness, adequacy, and credibility of the information and knowledge exchange (Han, Lee, & Seo, 2008; Lee, Park, & Lee, 2015). Higher quality also minimises the lack of understanding reduces errors in reasoning and misinterpretations, enhances decision quality and reinforces team consensus and acceptance by providing timely and relevant information and knowledge (Akgun, Lynn, Keskin, & Dogan, 2014).

Software development requires harmony and consensus since it relies heavily on teamwork (Faraj & Sproull, 2000). This is especially true as the development may embrace conflicts in search of innovative and diverse ideas or opinions that are then digested, absorbed and collaboratively realised (Crawford, Soto, de la Barra, Crawford, & Olguín, 2014). Thus, communication appears to be an important driver in the learning- and knowledge-intensive software development process. In SPT that alters development, we also expect communication to be critical becaus process change also involves conflicts due to the different functional and professional interests in the potentiall altered tasks (Lee et al., 2020; Xu & Ramesh, 2007). If a team possesses sufficient communication quality, members can effectively acquire, comprehend and combine diverse opinions and concerns by comprehensively addressing th tailoring needs. In this sense, SPT requires team communication that increases members' shared cognitions and con sensus that supports the team's ability to identify an integrative tailoring solution in which the tasks are harmoni ously aligned. In other words, high communication quality allows the team to effectively absorb SPT knowledge to facilitate interpersonal learning (Akgun et al., 2014; Troster, Mehra, & Van Knippenberg, 2014) and thus boost th team's AC. Therefore, we hypothesise the following:

H2: Software team communication quality has a positive influence on absorptive capacity.

## 2.6 | The effect of team trust on absorptive capacity

Team trust has been identified as team members' confidence in each other's dependability and expertise (D Jong, Dirks, & Gillespie, 2016; De Jong & Elfring, 2010). Edmondson (1999) indicated that such a team factor can build a sense of psychological safety among team members, which helps create a non-judgmental atmosphere. This atmosphere is one in which team members are willing to accept their own vulnerability based on the positive expectations of their teammates' competence, integrity, and benevolence (Bunderson & Boumgarden, 2010; Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995; Schulte, Cohen, & Klein, 2012). In the context of software development, team trust may be an important condition in facilitating a software team's AC in SPT. If the team can create a trusted and safe environment, members will feel relaxed when asking questions, seek feed back, embrace errors or even feel encouraged to voice concerns or critique a plan or the team's proposed changes because they do not expect to be penalised or bear the brunt of other team members' negative per ceptions (Kostopoulos & Bozionelos, 2011; Ortega, Den Bossche, Sanchezmanzanares, Rico, & Gil, 2014). Fur thermore, a trusted SPT environment may contribute to the solicitation of individuals' acquired knowledge and experiences, both good and bad, leading members to discuss the tailoring scheme in greater depth as well as to gather complementary SPI knowledge, thereby facilitating mutual learning and resulting in more thorough tailoring solutions. In this sense, team trust helps team members to more easily acquire SPT knowledge and mor effectively apply the acquired SPT knowledge to the alteration process

However, team trust may lead to groupthink (McAvoy & Butler, 2009). Members may attempt to maintain team cohesion, preventing them from realistically appreciating those who take the role of devil's advocate (Meyer, 2000 or evaluating constructive viewpoints, creative ideas, or impartial opinions about SPT from other members, thu leading to a poor decision-making process (Baron, 2005). This phenomenon will decrease the team's ability to objec tively identify, value and acquire suitable SPT knowledge as well as influence its ability to exploit and apply that knowledge for tailoring tasks. Therefore, we consider whether team trust can amplify the team's AC in the correc direction. Consequently, we hypothesise the following:

H3: Software team trust has a positive influence on absorptive capacity.

## 2.7 | The effects of absorptive capacity on SPT performance

By effectively gathering and applying appropriate SPT knowledge, AC might be deemed a key mechanism in facilitating a software team's learning capability in the acquisition of SPT knowledge. Specifically, as in the definitions set forth in Table 1, AC in SPT begins with the acquisition of knowledge that helps the team seek, evaluate and filter th external SPT knowledge required to respond to and address the particular tailoring needs. Then, assimilation facili tates the translation of the acquired knowledge into a “local language” (Lee et al., 2017) according to the tailored pro ject's contexts. Specifically, translation refers to the decomposition, classification and analysis of the acquired knowledge to enable the team to interpret and understand external SPT knowledge from various external sources to fit to the project's conditions. Next, transformation helps the team integrate the newly acquired knowledge with it existing SPT know-how, for example, the existing process plan or model (Ali et al., 2018). The transformation of knowledge also includes the dissemination of the synthesised knowledge across the team (Lee et al., 2017). Finally, the exploitation capability allows the transformed knowledge to be incorporated into the team's knowledge stock (Lee & Chen, 2019b; Zahra & George, 2002) for application to future SPT needs. In sum, this study assumes that the aforementioned work of AC can be recognised and serve as a useful knowledge process for software teams in SPT operations. With this assumption, AC appears to enable the flow of the absorption and creation of the knowledge required and to facilitate “the reconfiguration of the team's knowledge stock” (Lee et al., 2017, 2018) for the contin ual operation of SPT, resulting in a more efficient SPT process. Thus, we hypothesise the following:

H4: Absorptive capacity has a positive influence on SPT efficiency.

According to several reports, AC helps team members to recognise and integrate peers' knowledge, know-how and skills, thereby renewing and strengthening a team's knowledge-processing mechanism (Ali et al., 2018; Pop aitoon & Siengthai, 2014; Tiwana & Mclean, 2005). In this study, these effects are also considered to be contributors to quality tailoring decision making in SPT, which refers to the holistic and integrative tailoring solutions obtained b the team. In SPT, a quality tailoring decision comprehensively identifies and justifies the formality, the frequency, the granularity and the scope of the modified processes and associated work products (Ginsberg & Quinn, 1995; Xu & Ramesh, 2007). Such a decision is expected to be collaboratively made because modifying highly interrelated soft ware tasks involves intensive and diverse opinions and knowledge exchange among the team members. Through the acquisition and assimilation of AC, an SPT decision may simultaneously and sympathetically consider ad hoc sub goals, constraints, relationships, and external case references of the tasks of identifying an appropriate tailorin scope and the granularity of the modification proposed for the tasks. Through transformation and exploitation of AC. an SPT proposal may be able to be integrated with the existing process or model into an optimal action plan including the feasible formality and frequency of task execution that is effective for development. Correspondingly we hypothesise the following:

H5: Absorptive capacity has a positive influence on SPT effectiveness.

## 2.8 | The effect of SPT efficiency and effectiveness on software project performance

Theoretically, the overall performance of a project may be affected if the development process is altered. With this assumption, software process tailoring can be extended to consider a beneficial effect on the project's overall perfor mance in terms of schedule, budget and quality goals. However, according to some studies, the work of SPT is not always a “silver bullet” and may be risky (Alegría et al., 2014; Ocampo, Bella, & Munch, 2005). Software project team are often seen as unfamiliar with all of the technical tailoring methods or as lacking sufficient knowledge or know how to conduct the SPT task (Alegría et al., 2014; Xu & Ramesh, 2007). Therefore, they tend to perform such task in an easier and more effortless way (Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010).

In addition, when the development is uncertain, software teams may employ a watch-and-go or divide-and-con quer strategy (Balmelli, Brown, Cantor, & Mott, 2006; Feiler, 2018) and tend to perform SPT tasks in a safer and simpler way by focusing on the development scope and goals on which they are currently working (Alegría et al., 2014; Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010). Hence, fewer tailoring efforts (ie, higher SPT efficiency) are more likely to be expecte to save time such that the development may be expedited to reach the current goal. This condition can also aris given the time pressures on project teams due to, for example, earlier releases of working software or the decision to rush the tailoring of a project and then later evaluate and modify it at the next stage-gate or development loop sprint (Bass, 2012). In turn, a project's goals can be better and incrementally met by evolutionarily following a plan that is continuously modified (Lee & Chen, 2020; Torrecilla-Salinas, Sedeño, Escalona, & Mejías, 2015). Therefore we hypothesise the following:

H6: SPT efficiency has a positive influence on software project performance.

A software project team that makes higher quality tailoring decisions (ie, higher SPT effectiveness) can determine the potential impact areas and plan corresponding actions to yield better project consequences (Con boy & Fitzgerald, 2010; Xu & Ramesh, 2008). For example, a software team may consider modifying the system design and the associated development tasks, not only to better satisfy clients' needs but also to improve the cost and schedule performance of the project. However, software project teams are also often seen as limite in scope to only the part of the project on which they are working (Alegría et al., 2014). Therefore, they tend to have a narrower and segmented tailoring scope that fails to consider its potential impact on the entire pro ject (Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010).

In addition, SPT can be a “double-edged sword,” and its performance may have positive or negative impacts on a project. For instance, an improper tailoring decision may increase the likelihood of poor product or process design, resulting in a project being reworked, with more time and costs needed for that reworking. In other words, quality SPT implies that a project's tailoring decisions are consistent with the project's business strategy and performance goals, which can be achieved since the team members conducting the tasks not only include software development professionals but also managerial roles (eg, project managers). Therefore, the tailoring decisions that are made base on valid assumptions are presumed to help software teams conduct SPT to maximise the fulfilment of project goals Accordingly, a higher level of SPT effectiveness makes it possible to increase a project's overall performance. Hence we hypothesise the following:

H7: SPT effectiveness has a positive influence on software project performance.

Based on the aforementioned hypotheses, the research model is proposed in Figure 1. With the learning perspective of SPT, and based on dynamic capabilities theory, the model is constructed to address how a software team's AC effectively facilitates SPT performance as well as how the three team-based knowledge antecedents influ ence the facilitation, which in turn promotes SPT performance and software project performance.

## 3 | RESEARCH METHOD

## 3.1 | Data collection and sample

A survey research approach was utilised to empirically examine the proposed model. The original instrument of this study was in English. Then, a back-translation method (Brislin, 1970) was employed to develop the Mandarin version of the questionnaires in consideration of the survey participants whose first language is Mandarin. To ensure content and face validity, three MIS experts were included in the development of the questionnaire to examine the instru ment relevant to the SPT domain and to ensure that the survey items were clear and meaningful. Then, a pilot testing process was conducted with 15 software project managers from different Taiwanese firms to help ensure the understandability of the survey questions.

F I G U RE 1 The research model

The initial sampling frame consisted of 200 organisations in Taiwan. These firms are selected based on the top 200 largest firms list published by CommonWealth magazine, one of Taiwan's most influential economic news media outlets (https://english.cw.com.tw/). We first contacted these organisations by e-mail or telephone and then explained the study's purpose to them to ensure that they understood and agreed to participate. This process also helped ensure that the participants were serious in answering the questions. Of the 200 organisations contacted 135 organisations were willing to participate in the survey. The unit of analysis in this study is the team, and the target population refers to the in-house software development teams that are directly involved in SPT. To increase th causality of the proposed model, in our questionnaires, we adopted a matched pairs design. Specifically, we asked one team manager/leader and two members (IS professionals), who were familiar and knowledgeable with the SPT context and practically involved in SPT tasks, to complete the questionnaires. We collected the questionnaires at two time points. At Time 1, team experience, team trust, and communication quality were rated by the team mem bers. At Time 2, the team managers/leaders were invited to report demographic information and software project information and to rate AC, SPT performance and software project performance. Time 1 and Time 2 were separated by 2 months. This design of the questionnaires can also be regarded as an ex ante method used to avoid common method bias (CMB). In addition, CMB may occur due to the effect of social desirability bias (Nederhof, 1985). Thus, this study used an online questionnaire via email instead of distributing paper questionnaires. In the questionnaire we also declared that the data would be used only for academic purposes and that personal information would be kept confidential to encourage the respondents to complete the questionnaire honestly. These treatments were use to help reduce the social desirability bias effect (Nederhof, 1985; Srivastava, Chandra, & Shirish, 2015). Moreover before the formal data collection, we performed a sample power analysis using G\*Power software (Faul, Erdfelder Buchner, & Lang, 2009) and found that the minimum sample size needed for the proposed model is 146. The parameters of this software consider an effect size of 0.15 (average value), a power level of 0.95, and a maximum allowed error of 0.05 as recommended by Cohen (1988) and Campanelli et al. (2018).

The survey was conducted by e-mail; 135 software teams (including 135 team managers/leaders and 270 mem bers) participated in this survey. Therefore, a total of 270 matched samples were obtained. ## 3.2 | Measures

In this study, the constructs were adapted from previously developed constructs that had been validated in prior stud ies. All constructs in the survey were measured with a seven-point Likert rating system, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The measurement items for the constructs are listed in the appendix. Specifically, team experience was a reflective construct that was measured using two items adapted from the measurement scales of Akgun, Keskin Byrne, and Imamoglu (2007). Software team trust was operationalised as a reflective construct with the four items derived from Akgun et al. (2007). Software team communication quality was a reflective construct measured using three items adapted from Stewart and Gosain (2006). Absorptive capacity was operationalised as a second-order formativ (composite) construct with four first-order reflective components (ie, acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation). The measurement items for acquisition (five items), assimilation (two items), transformation (six items) and exploitation (five items) are adapted from Ali et al. (2018) and Popaitoon and Siengthai (2014).

SPT performance was represented with the two constructs: SPT efficiency and SPT effectiveness. SPT efficiency was measured by two items adapted from Xu and Ramesh (2008). SPT effectiveness referred to the quality of th tailoring decisions, and its measurement items were established based on Xu and Ramesh (2008), Slaughter et al. (2006) and Dooley and Fryxell (1999). Further, a referent shift approach was applied for the measurements (Kuenzi & Schminke, 2009; Lee & Chen, 2019a) to modify the focal referent from the organisation (ie, “this firm”) to the project (ie, “the project”). We also consulted the two SPT studies (ie, Xu and Ramesh (2008) and Slaughter et al. (2006)), and we fine-tuned the expressions of the measurement items to fit the SPT context. Software project performance was operationalised as a reflective construct with the six items. The scale for project performance was adapted from Parolia, Goodman, Li, and Jiang (2007)

## 3.3 | Control variables

Five control variables (team size, project duration, software development type, industry type and firm size) wer selected in the proposed model. Team size refers to the number of members associated with their project (Stewart & Gosain, 2006). This variable essentially represents the structure and composition of a team, which can be a confounding factor in investigating the performance of software projects (Lin, Chen, Hsu, & Fu, 2015; Stewart & Gosain, 2006). In this study, team size is measured by the logarithm of the number of members. Project duration refers to a project's length of time, which affects how team members interact and exchange knowledge that is critica to project performance (Lin et al., 2015). In this regard, we controlled for the possible influence of the variable of project duration in the hypothesis-testing analyses

Regarding the software development type, this study tests to determine whether different major types of devel opment (ie, sequential development approach vs agile approach) affect tailoring results and project performanc (Campanelli & Parreiras, 2015; Kang, Hahn, & De, 2017; Xu & Ramesh, 2007). Accordingly, a binary variable is used to represent whether the project development is based on an agile model (1) or on a sequential model (0) (Kang et al., 2017). Moreover, large firms may possess high-level software engineering competence (Lee et al., 2018), which may influence project performance (Cui, Wu, & Tong, 2018). Thus, firm size was controlled for and measured by the natural logarithm of the number of employees. Different industry sectors (using industry dummies) may have distinct levels of software development capabilities (Lee et al., 2018) and thus may affect project performance (Cu et al., 2018).

## 3.4 | Data aggregation

We aggregated the team members' responses at the team level by examining several statistics to justify aggregation: Rwg (James, Demaree, & Wolf, 1984) and intraclass correlation coefficients ICC (1) and ICC (2) (Schneider, Hanges, Smith, & Salvaggio, 2003). The values of Rwg and ICC (2) were greater than the accepted value of 0.70, and th values of ICC (1) exceeded the threshold of 0.12, indicating a reasonable level of agreement (Schneider et al., 2003) There was support for the aggregation of the following constructs: for experience, Rwg = 0.82, ICC (1) = 0.33, ICC (2) = 0.86; for communication quality, Rwg = 0.78, ICC (1) = 0.36, ICC (2) = 0.81; for trust, Rwg = 0.81, ICC (1) = 0.32, ICC (2) = 0.83. Therefore, the team members' ratings were appropriate for team-level aggregation.

## 4 | DATA ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

In recent research, scholars have suggested that partial least squares (PLS) is appropriate if a model includes forma tive measurements or higher-order constructs (Hair, Sarstedt, & Ringle, 2019; Nitzl & Chin, 2017; Rigdon, 2016; Sarstedt et al., 2016). The present study consists of a reflective-formative type higher-order construct (Becker, Klein, & Wetzels, 2012; Hair et al., 2019) in which absorptive capacity is operationalised as a second-order formative construct with four first-order reflective components/sub-constructs, that is, acquisition, assimilation, transformatio and exploitation. Therefore, the statistical technique of PLS was selected to examine the proposed research model In addition, this study adopted the repeated indicator approach (Becker et al., 2012) to estimate the hierarchica latent variable (ie, absorptive capacity). This approach takes the entire nomological network into account rather tha only the higher- or lower-level model, and it estimates all constructs simultaneously instead of estimating all dimensions separately (Guhr, Lebek, & Breitner, 2019). In the data analysis section, the first step examined the measurement model to determine the reliability and validity of the constructs, and the second step assessed the strength of the hypothesised links among the variables. In this study, SmartPLS 3 software (Ringle, Wende, & Becker, 2015) was used for the analysis, and the bootstrap resampling method (5000 resamples) was used to determine the significance of the paths within the structural model.

## 4.1 | Measurement model

For the reflective constructs, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and item reliability were assessed and vali dated in the measurement model. We first examined individual item reliabilities based on item loadings between an indicator and its posited underlying factor. If the loading was lower than the suggested acceptable value of 0.5, th item was dropped from the construct (Hair, Hult, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2013) (see Appendix A). All retained item load ings were greater than the suggested acceptable value (see Table 3). Moreover, reliability was also examined using composite reliability (CR) values. All of the CR values were above 0.7, ranging between 0.790 and 0.917, which is a commonly accepted range (Hair, Hult, et al., 2013) (see Table 3). The results showed that all CR values were reliable

To meet the requirements of convergent validity, the average variance extracted (AVE) from each construct should exceed the threshold value of 0.50 as this indicates that 50% or more of the variance is explained by the indicators of the latent variable (Hair, Hult, et al., 2013). In this study, the AVE values ranged between 0.562 and 0.839 and all values were above the acceptable level of 0.50 (see Table 3). All the factor loadings and AVEs support the convergent validity of the constructs. For the discriminant validity, we employed a heterotrait-monotrait ratio of cor relations (HTMT) approach. According to the results (see Table 4), all HTMT values, ranging from 0.278 to 0.845, were below the threshold value of 0.85 (Hair et al., 2019). This finding indicates that discriminant validity was established between the two constructs, supporting discriminant validity. Overall, convergent and discriminant valid ity are empirically supported, demonstrating the sufficient construct validity of the reflective constructs

## 4.3 | Mediation analysis

We also examined the mediation effects of the research model. As shown in Table 7, there are eight mediating paths in the research model. We adopted the variance accounted for (VAF), which is the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect (indirect + direct effect), to examine the mediating effects. If the VAF is less than 0.2, there is no mediating effect, while if $0 . 2 < \mathsf { V A F } < 0 . 8 ,$ , a partial mediating effect exists. If the VAF is greater than 0.8, then the relationship is fully mediated (Hair, Hult, et al., 2013). The mediating results are summarised in Table 8. According to the results, path 1 (TE AC EY) and path 2 (TE AC EE) are partial mediations; the others are full mediations (paths 3 to 8). Specifically, AC partially mediates the relationship of TE (team experiences) and SPT efficiency as wel as that of TE and SPT effectiveness. In other words, TE directly fosters SPT performance; it also amplifies AC, which in turn enhances SPT performance. In a cross check with the survey data, we realized that the demographic charac teristic of smaller team size possibly links to the finding of the direct effect of TE on SPT, while the characteristic of having less development experience helps explain its conditional effect on SPT. This finding is rational as an experi enced team with fewer members can soon conclude a tailoring plan without putting extra effort into learning and integration, whereas a less-experienced team will rely on AC for learning to collaborate in the SPT process.

## 5 | CONTRIBUTIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

## 5.1 | Theoretical contributions

This study explores and provides insights into whether and how the underlying mechanism of SPT performance and its knowledge-based enablers operate and how they influence project performance. The model adopted in this study takes a fresh look at the dynamic capabilities theory in SPT to address how SPT performance is reinforced through a team's AC. The results of this study empirically demonstrate that higher levels of SPT performance significantly contribute to software project performance. An overall insight is that, from the organisation's point of view, the SPT result (ie, tailoring solutions) that benefits a project, in theory, may help to altogether increase the performance of future similar projects. In this regard, we encourage project teams to take a broader view of SPT as it can further pro vide stimuli to enable an organisation's SPI (software processes improvement).

An interesting finding of this study is that SPT performance, in terms of its efficiency and effectiveness, fully mediates the linkages between AC and software project performance. In other words, AC does not directly foster project performance; it exerts the influence on project performance by contributing to SPT performance. Compared to the existing literature (Ali et al., 2018; Cui et al., 2018; Lee, Lee, & Park, 2014; Popaitoon & Siengthai, 2014), which has shown that AC has a direct effect on project performance, this study further reveals a mediator in this relationship in the context of SPT. In the mediation analysis, we found that the direct effect of AC on project performance becomes relatively nonsignificant compared to the indirect effect that occurs through SPT (see Table 8). Thus, SPT plays an intermediary or contextual role in the AC-project performance relationship, and such intermediaries or factors may significantly change the utility of AC in project performance. The results further showed that even if the team possesses a higher level of AC, the effect of AC on project performance may be attenuated if it cannot be directly exerted on the work of SPT. Thus, SPT performance is found to be the point of application for AC, theoreti cally implying that AC may rely on learning-based teamwork (eg, SPT) to demonstrate its effect. This study advances our understanding by providing insight into the intervening factors by which AC fosters project performance

Accordingly, we suggest that research concerning team absorptive capacity in projects should consider the mediating effects when comparing contextual relationships to project outcomes.

In theory, the result of a software project is determined by its process. This study additionally shows that project performance is largely affected by how the process is planned through SPT, whereby AC assists in the planning, which is holistic and integrative. Although the traditional plan-based approach is discouraged when dynamism is cru cial to development (Ahimbisibwe, Cavana, & Daellenbach, 2015; Ceschi, Sillitti, Succi, & De Panfilis, 2005), in thi study, the strong linkage between SPT and project performance confirms that planning continues to play a major rol in driving a project through changes to achieve success. This finding reflects the misconception that process planning is less important or ignorable in empirical process-based software development. Instead, in agile development, the Scrum theory (Schwaber, 2004) promotes the idea of continual retrospection for the purpose of inspecting how the Scrum process worked during the last development cycle and adjusting it to improve the subsequent run of develop ment. This study clarifies the concept and unravels the more profound value of planning, that is, continually adjusting the process for improvement rather than merely defining development. Nevertheless, a project team should not trea SPT as a means of pursuing sophisticated work plans but, rather, as a learning and social process through which to better understand not only the dynamics but also the team members. This perspective is also important because every software team is unique, and software teams in human-centered development (Capretz, 2014; Jerome & Kazman, 2005; Seffah, Vanderdonckt, & Desmarais, 2009) should learn to find the best way for team members to collaboratively plan suitable development solutions that are integrative for all.

With the team factors studied in this paper, AC is found to act as a partial mediator between team experience and SPT performance, meaning that past tailoring experiences not only directly prompt the team to make quality tai loring decisions and reduce the effort in performing SPT, but they also upgrade the team's AC in conducting SPT tasks. However, trust and communication quality do not directly affect SPT performance; instead, both factors exert their positive effects by improving AC. which eventually contributes to SPT performance. While recent studies have promoted the importance of knowledge in collaborative SPT (eg, Bass, 2016; Campanelli & Parreiras, 2015; Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010; Dingsøyr, Moe, Fægri, & Seim, 2018; Xu & Ramesh, 2008), the present study contributes to th existing literature by exploring the team-based enablers of SPT and how such enablers influence a software team's dynamic knowledge-processing capability (ie, AC) to increase SPT performance. The findings regarding team experi ence, trust, and communication quality and their profound effects on SPT are further discussed below.

The extant literature has emphasised the importance of team experience in SPT (Bass, 2016; Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010; Slaughter et al., 2006; Xu & Ramesh, 2007). This study extends the literature by identifying the partia mediating effect of team experience on AC and reveals two types of team experience, that is, tailoring content expe rience and SPT process experience, that work distinctly in two tailoring situations. First, when dealing with a new SPT case that requires the team's learning effort, experience in the SPT process helps guide and facilitate the team to better acquire, assimilate and exploit SPT knowledge. In other words, in this situation, team experience refers to the established process know-how of increasing the team's AC to more effectively develop innovative SPT solutions. When addressing an SPT situation that has been previously encountered, the effect of experience on the tailoring content, for example, contextualised or case-based knowledge (Campanelli et al., 2018; Campanelli & Parreiras, 2015 Pillat, Oliveira, Alencar, & Cowan, 2015; Xu & Ramesh, 2008), emerges and enables the team to rapidly select and adapt tailoring criteria and solutions. In this case, the team chooses to bypass AC, resulting in a reduced SPT work load and referable tailoring consequences. Members' prior experiences are known to enable the team to have “sen semaking capabilities” (Haas, 2006). In this study, the finding that team experiences represent a direct enabler further indicates that although a software project team has a transient composition, the prior experience of individ ual members continuously influences SPT decisions. This finding implies that when a member leaves the team, his or her prior experience is retained in the team's memory, which continuously contributes to future similar tailoring cases.

Scholars have shown the criticality of communication quality to software development in various aspects (eg, Chen et al., 2013; DeFranco & Laplante, 2017; Hsu et al., 2012; Lu, Xiang, Wang, & Wang, 2011; Tang, 2014; Treud & Storey, 2012). This study refreshes the literature by demonstrating that communication quality does not have a direct influence on SPT performance. This finding reflects the fact that SPT is based on collaborative decisions made by members with various functional roles and diverse views. Further, it sheds light on an implicit condition that is based on the characteristic of survey results: even for a smaller team, communication would not be effective without the team's effort to assimilate and exploit the diverse knowledge and opinions of its members to produce a process redesign that benefits the most aspects. With respect to team trust, the existing literature has deemed it a majo contributor in software development (McHugh et al., 2012; Stewart & Gosain, 2006). This study further identifie team trust as an indirect enabler of SPT, implying that it does not directly influence SPT decisions to avoid group think. This finding is critical as trust might be misused when members tend to directly buy into an SPT decision proposal without first critically analysing and assimilating the potential impacts of the decision for themselves. In othe words, trust should be used to encourage members to voice their concerns and opinions in the process of SPT rathe than simply endorsing team decisions.

## 5.2 | Practical implications

Lessons learned from this study have several practical implications for organisations and practitioners. First, the mixed findings of the effect of team experience on SPT suggest that to increase the direct effect of team experience to SPT performance, the composition of a project team should include at least one member who has previously par ticipated in SPT. However, it may be difficult to include SPT-experienced members on every software team. In thi regard, organisations may consider having knowledge management systems or scenario-based process asset libraries (PAL) (Lee, Shiue, & Chen, 2016) that can store and classify prior tailoring experiences and provide useful information for project teams to consider when making tailoring decisions. This process should also be associated with esta blishing an organisation's incentive policies to enthusiastically encourage SPT in projects. To support the indirect effect of experience on SPT, it becomes critical to establish the operating know-how of team learning (ie, AC). Therefore, for teams that develop innovative software or those with less tailoring content experience (eg, junior teams another characteristic of the participants in the survey), we suggest providing complementary training on the learn ing-based SPT process, that is, how to learn together to make tailoring decisions rather than simply a large numbe of ad hoc SPT cases.

Second, face-to-face communication is commonly suggested as an efficient means by which to facilitate quality team communication. In SPT tasks, team members are solicited to freely share domain knowledge, personal experi ences or sincere opinions. However, not every team member feels comfortable speaking out in a face-to-face context, which hampers the quality of communication in the discussion. In this regard, an IT-based group support system (GSS) or social media may be useful since it is not only convenient to overcome the limitation of team members being geographically dispersed, but it also promotes the effectiveness of a team's communication, which in tur increases the quality of collaborative decisions. In addition, specific operational means such as joint application design (JAD), the Delphi converging discussion or voting processes provide advanced anonymity and a parallel and flattened hierarchy of communication and are particularly helpful technological SPT environments.

Team trust can be viewed from two perspectives. One is that individual team members should not be afraid of speaking out, especially publicly sharing their past failures, problems, and concerns. Additionally, the entire team should be open to and appreciate any input, whether trivial or critical, from any members. Team managers play an important role in nurturing a psychologically secure SPT environment. From the psychological perspective, the attri bution theory of leadership (Martinko, Harvey, & Douglas, 2007) can be extended to suggest that managers shoul not justify a problem and attribute it to a certain individual member due to her or his record. Conversely, managers may consider establishing a neutral environment with no individual attribution while also praising individual contribu tions in front of the team and giving confidence to team members, thus creating a “positive Hawthorne effect” (Che & Chong, 2011) for the team. This environment helps team members to open their minds and relax, thereby enhancing manager-member relationships as well as reducing groupthink, which in turn contributes to the making of effective tailoring decisions accomplished by the team as a whole.

Finally, with the aforementioned facilitation of AC and the three enablers, an appropriate learning feedbac mechanism is suggested. As the development of a software project is dynamic and contingent, SPT is not a one-tim task that is conducted only in the planning phase of the project. Once a tailoring decision is made, we suggest that project teams adopt a learning-by-doing strategy of SPT by reviewing the effect periodically to determine whether the experimental results of the redesigned process and the aforementioned environments obtained the proper modi fications to accommodate the project's current situation. For example, in an agile-based (Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010) development project, the software team may continually conduct such a review as well as a sprint-review meeting after a development cycle (ie, a sprint), which is completed to fine tune the process and the SPT environment to increase the performance of SPT development. Alternatively, the team should consider utilising the retrospectiv meeting to consolidate the evolutionary results of the process design evidenced by the software team and submit ting the results to the organisation to contribute to the improvement of its standard processes.

## 6 | LIMITATIONS AND FURTHER RESEARCH

Despite its contribution to both theory and practise, this study has several limitations that should be addressed fo future research. First, this study primarily focused on teams and projects that are transient and utilised cross-sectional data in the investigation. Therefore, the cause-and-effect relationships might not be inferred from the result of the current study. In this regard, future research could be extended to conducting a longitudinal study to furthe identify the relationships among these variables as they develop over time. Second, by focusing on the knowledge perspective, this study has investigated several key team variables that are found to be significant in strengthening a software team's AC when performing SPT. However, there are other team-related variables from different aspects that may need recognition. For example, from a power perspective, power distance is a specific cultural value that may influence a team's social relationships and interactions among individuals (Cole, Carter, & Zhang, 2013; Yang et al., 2007). This factor may determine the utility of AC, which subsequently influences SPT and project perfor mance. Future research could further explore and investigate how power distance influences software teams' opera tional mechanisms in performing SPT.

Third, the target samples in this study are software teams in a typical work mode, that is. working at the same time and place. In cases where team members are geographically distributed, for example, different countries, such a team may suffer from challenges and threats related to task coordination due to certain geographic limitations. Hence, follow-up studies can be extended to consider how distributed teams coordinate and synchronise in con ducting tailoring work and inspect whether there are differences in the proposed model between in-house teams and distributed teams. Fourth, SPT might also involve outsourced software projects in which clients and vendors must orchestrate the collaborative determination and management of the development of a project. With such cli ent-vendor relationships, what types and how such inter-organizational collaborations are performed to achieve superior SPT and project performance merit further exploration in future SPT research

Finally, according to the analysis of the differential effects of the development types on the model, agile devel opment facilitates a trusted environment and strengthens effective communication to foster AC in the SPT context Therefore, the value of the practices of agile development can be further identified. For example, agile practices, such as retrospective meetings, help exchange knowledge among team members, thus facilitating the team's continu ous learning in the evolution of project development (Dingsøyr et al., 2018; Wang, Conboy, & Pikkarainen, 2012) Therefore, the finding of this study, that is, the need for continual process planning (SPT) through learning, can be further embedded in retrospective meetings that convey the lessons learned from previous development cycles to achieve better planning for the following round of development. In this case, cultivating agile teams and their behav ioural models in retrospection to achieve continuous improvement and learning should be explored in future studies.
