R E S E A R C H O P I N I O N

# Digital enforcement: Rethinking the pursuit of a digitally-enabled society

Antonio Díaz Andrade | Angsana A. Techatassanasoontorn

Business Information Systems, Auckland University of Technology, Auckland, New Zealand

Correspondence Antonio Díaz Andrade, Business Information Systems, Auckland University of Technology, Auckland, New Zealand. Email: antonio.diaz@aut.ac.nz

## Abstract

In this article, we aim to sensitise the information systems community about the dispossession of choice that the extended reliance on Internet technology creates for individuals. The overemphasis of digital inclusion as a solution to the digital divide problem frames Internet use as desirable in a progressive society but labels non-use as problematic or a deficiency that needs to be remedied. This situation, we argue, creates a new modality of inequality that we term digital enforcement, defined as the process of dispossession that reduces choices for individuals who prefer to minimise their reliance on the Internet if given the opportunity or those who want to live their lives offline altogether. We present digital enforcement as an ethical problem and draw on the concepts of governmentality and technologies of power to explain how practices around Internet use in society result in digital enforcement. We conclude with a hopeful perspective to call for an ethical agenda to develop desirable futures.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

When Sir Tim Berners-Lee made the first web browser available to the public in August 1991, barely 0.5% of th world population was connected to the Internet (Roser, Ritchie, & Ortiz-Ospina, 2020). In 2018 we achieved a signif icant milestone when we reached the 50% mark of the world population online for the first time (ITU, 2019). As w may expect, online and offline populations are not equally distributed across regions and countries. We witness a high Internet penetration rate in wealthy countries—as of June 2018, over 98% of the citizens of Andorra, Iceland and Liechtenstein have access to the Internet (ITU, 2018)—and a rapidly growing Internet penetration rate in lowincome countries—Bangladesh experienced growth from 0.2% in 2005 to 18.0% in 2017 (Doan, 2019). To be sure there are still pockets of deficient access to the Internet—for example, Central African Republic with 4.3%, Guinea Bissau with 3.9% and Somalia with 2.0% (ITU, 2018). The confluence of social, political, technological and/or cultura factors that explain the low Internet penetration rates in these countries is beyond the scope of this exposition. In this article, we focus our attention on societies with a high level of Internet penetration

In societies where Internet use encompasses more and more aspects of living, the Internet ostensibly reflect modernity and societal progress (Feenberg, 2019). Users cannot resist the lure of the abundance of available online resources and services that promise to simplify their lives; for many of them, living life offline is an inconceivable prospect. Consequentially, a growing number of Internet users define new means of social interaction (eg, posts on social media) and modes of transaction (eg, online banking); to put it simply, they epitomise a way of living in modern society. Interactions and transactions—once predominantly conducted face to face—are now being transferred to the digital realm. Online transactions are becoming the default option, and those who opt for conducting transac tions offline often have to pay the price, literally. For instance, it is rather common in the airline industry to charge fee for reservations made over the telephone or in person, even when no alternatives are available (Klint, 2017). Th trend of banks shutting down their branches and pushing their customers to online banking (Arnold, 2018) consti tutes another example of an “imposed option.”

In contemporary society, technologies are forms of power that subject human beings to technical contro (Foucault, 1979). Therefore, the spread of technology is not innocent. In other words, “[t]he tools that we use shap our way of life in modern societies… How we do things determines who and what we are. Technological develop ment transforms what it is to be human” (Feenberg, 1999, p. 2). The concentration of the Internet around a few companies such as Facebook and Google raises concerns on privacy, data protection, surveillance, and the use of data to manipulate online publics, among others (Zuboff, 2019). Despite the growing concerns, some people may be willing to use the Internet because it is a convenient choice or reluctant to use the Internet, yet have no choice in the mat ter. We contend that this issue, which we label “digital enforcement,” deserves our attention. We define digita enforcement as the process of dispossession that reduces choices for individuals who prefer to minimise their reli ance on the Internet if given the opportunity or those who want to live their lives offline altogether.

In this article, we raise concerns about digital enforcement and trace the policy and scholarly discourses on digi tal inclusion and the stigmatisation of Internet non-users that leads us to the current state of affairs. We draw on Foucault's (1979) concepts of governmentality and technologies of power to elaborate on the mechanisms that cre ate digital enforcement. We conclude with an invitation for intellectual conversations and actions to create a better world where individuals have the right to choose whether and how they use technology. In particular, we explore the ideas of democratisation of technology as a plausible approach to ensure that the Internet serves a wider range of interests.

## 2 | FROM DIGITAL DIVIDE TO DIGITAL INCLUSION: ARE WE MOVING CLOSER TO SOCIAL INCLUSION?

As information and communication technology (ICT) started permeating many aspects of human activities researchers, as well as policymakers, realised its potential to make a difference in political, economic and social life for people in both wealthy and low-income countries (Walsham, 2012). The information systems (IS) field has been closely paying attention to the promising role ICT can play in solving wicked problems affecting society. An important area of research revolved around the concern of the widening digital divide or the gap separating the “haves” from the “have-nots” (Compaine, 2001). In the mid-1990s, the digital divide was defined as the gap between thos with access to computers and the Internet and those without. Accordingly, the focus of many studies was on th physical availability of computers and access to the Internet among various groups. While some scholars challenged the assumption that the Internet would become the “great equaliser” (Sidney Howland, 1998), others urged policymakers to take action to avoid exacerbating the marginalisation of certain groups (Hoffman & Novak, 1998).

However, the dichotomous notion of haves and have-nots was subsequently criticised as it builds on a techno logical deterministic logic, which assumes that access to technology would lead to use and social change would auto matically ensue (Warschauer, 2003). The implicit premise was that universal access to ICT would “create wealth spread access to information and therefore power around more democratically, and foster community among their users” (Fukuyama, 2002, p. 182). Consequentially, the discourse on the digital divide evolved into concerns aroun inequality in the ability to use technology, access to social support networks and quality of technology (Dewan & Riggins, 2005; DiMaggio, Hargittai, Neuman, & Robinson, 2001; Hargittai & Hinnant, 2008). This type of digita divide is referred to as the second-level digital divide and includes differences in digital skills and use (Scheerder, van Deursen, & van Dijk, 2017), while the gap in access is described as the first-level digital divide. Later, some scholar made a call for taking a broader perspective on the digital divide issue by considering not only access and use but also the outcomes and consequences of Internet use (Warschauer & Matuchniak, 2010). This approach that linke Internet access and use to differences in the outcomes and consequences from Internet use was labelled as the third-level digital divide (Wei, Teo, Chan, & Tan, 2011).

As Internet use grew from the mid-1990s onward, the practical uses of the Internet became almost unlimited Today, individuals can access and use the Internet to fulfil their needs, such as getting information, communicating with family and friends, completing business transactions, enjoying leisure time and expressing political views. The observation that individuals who do not access or use the Internet miss out on the innumerable resources availabl online that supplement and facilitate life offline has attracted considerable attention of policymakers internationally who anticipated the negative implications that being digitally excluded could have on disadvantaged and vulnerabl communities (Dewan & Riggins, 2005; Helsper, 2008; Warschauer, 2003). The spotlight on the digital divide and research evidence that shows the links between digital exclusion and social exclusion (Helsper, 2012; Park, 2017a 2017b; Warren, 2007) made the need to address the inequalities around access and use of the Internet a policy pri ority (Helsper, 2008). However, it is important to emphasise that social inclusion goes beyond the prospects of eco nomic prosperity and wealth; social inclusion, according to Giddens (2008), “also refers to opportunities and to involvement in public space” (p. 102). Conversely, social “exclusion is not about graduations of inequality, but about the mechanisms that act to detach groups of people from the social mainstream” (Giddens, 2008, p. 104).

Despite the shifting focus on the debates around the digital divide, access and the ability to use the Internet were relentlessly emphasised as critical in creating opportunities for individuals in the emerging network society (Castells, 2000a, 2000b). This promise of technology explains, at least in part, the formulation of policies intended to encourage Internet adoption and use as well as the planning of digital inclusion projects as a response to the chal lenges of the digital divide (Al-Jaghoub & Westrup, 2009; Jaeger, Bertot, Thompson, Katz, & DeCoster, 2012; Madon, Reinhard, Roode, & Walsham, 2009). In other words, digital inclusion was conceived as the perfect solution to the problem of the digital divide (Phipps, 2000). This conceptualisation led to a simplistic classification, in our opinion, of two groups of individuals: Internet users, who constitute the normalised group, and Internet non-users, who represent the problematic other (Holden, 2012) and whose “deficiency [needs] to be remedied” (Wyatt, 2003, p. 68) Moreover, this conceptualisation implies that the digital divide is a technological problem that requires technologica solutions—that is, making everyone an Internet user. However, we suggest that it is more sensible to understand the digital divide as a political and social issue rather than a technological one (Klecun, 2008). Stigmatisation happens in the highly digitised world we live in now. Wearable technology, virtual reality, artificia intelligence, augmented reality and the Internet of Things, among others, contribute to a digital environment that shapes how we live our lives. Slowly yet steadily, we are being pushed to be part of the digital world directly or indirectly with the promise it offers of the benefits of the low transaction costs, manifested as immediacy an convenience. In vast regions of the world, humans have now developed a symbiotic relationship with digital technol ogy. They act upon the world through Internet technology, as evidenced by the growing adoption of the multiple modalities of the sharing economy (eg, Uber, Airbnb), the popularity of online dating sites (eg, Tinder, Bumble) and the expansion of e-learning services (eg, Coursera, Khan Academy), among other technology-enabled services. In thi regard, the Internet constitutes “a means of experience, not an object of experience in use” (Ihde, 2009, p. 42— emphasis in the original). However, in this quest for the universalisation of online services, government policies and the ever-increasing technological services offered by the corporate sector can become the instruments of disposses sion through digital enforcement.

## 3 | AN IMPORTANT GROUP OF CITIZENS THAT SHOULD NOT BE IGNORED

The literature in the area of information and communication technology for development (ICT4D) has largely focused, for valid reasons, on the benefits that individuals can derive from digital technology, especially for socia inclusion (eg, Díaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016; Ferguson & Soekijad, 2016; Joia & Paschoeto dos Santos, 2019). How ever, the findings of these studies might be incorrectly interpreted if we were to infer that Internet use is the onl way for people to realise their potential. While it can be argued that access to and use of the Internet contribute to social inclusion, the latter is not an automatic outcome of the former (Phipps, 2000). Social inclusion involves mor than just access to and use of the Internet; social inclusion requires other arrangements to be in place (Zheng & Walsham, 2008). Moreover, Internet use can have harmful consequences on multiple aspects of life. These advers effects include Internet addiction disorder (Young, 2015), the deterioration or loss of human touch in interpersona relationships (Turkle, 2011), the propensity to adopt narcissistic behaviours (Verhaeghe, 2014), threats to persona privacy (Marwick & boyd, 2014), cyberbullying (Kowalski, Giumetti, Schoreder, & Lattaner, 2014) and using the Inter net as a refuge to compensate for lack of offline life (Mazzoni, Baiocco, Cannata, & Dimas, 2016), among others Thus, we can conclude that living an online life does not take us to wonderland; engaging in online activities ma expose us to the detrimental effects of Internet use on people's lives.

## 3.1 | The dispossession of choices

However, our focus is not on the harmful effects of the Internet on users. Our concern here is about the depletion o choices for citizens who decide not to engage in Internet activities. To illustrate our point, we present three vignettes The first one describes the predicament that Internet non-users currently experience in their dealings with government agencies and imagines the future challenges they may face in the United States where, according to a recent Pew Research Center report, 10% of the adult population does not use the Internet (Anderson, Perrin, Jiang, & Kumar, 2019).

## Vignette 1. Interacting with government agencies and beyond

“As just one example of life dysfunction caused by not going online, consider the Social Security Administration, which now offers application forms for some basic benefits only online or at an office. These forms are no longer available by mail … If applying for a basic Social Security application is challenging today for those who are offline, imagine the plight of the non-user in five years when trying to apply for retirement benefits, connect with government agencies, or conduct business with a bank, retailer, doctor or any other online resource that is part of the daily and routine American experience for internet users.” USC Annenberg Center for the Digital Future (Lebo, 2017)

The second vignette describes a similar problem, although the problematic situation here concerns the issues associated with the shutting down of bank branches for elderly New Zealanders.

Stuff News (Mitchell, 2019)

We can only speculate on the reasons why Ms Hazelwood does not switch to the convenience of online banking given that broadband Internet is available in her hometown, Pahīatua, and pretty much everywhere else in New Zealand. However, we observe that nudging customers to use online services by shutting down bank branches and ATMs, a cost-cutting, commercially-driven decision, is widely adopted in the banking industry (Scott, 2019) to the point that it is predicted that in the UK ATMs will disappear by 2037 and bank branches will disappear by 2041 (Guta, 2018). Although a business writer in a Forbes magazine article contends that bank customers are not inter ested in conducting face-to-face transactions and argues that banks should invest in technology to improve customer service (Shevlin, 2019), the press often reports the negative consequences that the shutting down of branches has on bank customers. For instance, a recent article documents that individuals most affected by the clo sure of bank branches in approximately half of the counties in the United States between 2012 and 2017 ar residents of low-income communities who lack transportation means to reach the nearest branch (Mulhere, 2019) Regrettably, leaving individuals with no other option than using online services is not unique to the banking industry a similar pattern is observed in other commercial and government services. We observe that choices outside of the Internet are rapidly disappearing for many citizens

The third vignette presents a compelling illustration of the disappearance of choice in the context of the current pandemic. In the face of the lockdown that many governments imposed as a response to the health emergency cre ated by the Covid-19 outbreak, many workers had no other option than working remotely.

## Vignette 3. Working remotely

“Virtual work has no knockoff time: email, WhatsApp and the dreaded, supposedly upbeat, Zoom meetings mean workers are available all the time.

The content of the third vignette alerts us to the situation of how workers can be deprived of choices. Dominant discourses plagued with euphemisms can redefine what work is and dispossess workers of the opportunity to do their job as they used to do. Remote work supported by digital technology over the Internet, which was initially con ceived as an emergency response, may become the new normal.

## 3.2 | Simply not interested in using the Internet

As discussed earlier, digital enforcement is the dispossession of choices for people to engage or not engage wit Internet use. As we have shown in Vignette 3, people who have experienced digital enforcement include Interne users and Internet non-users. Since we cannot find reliable statistics to present a complete picture of those, whethe users or non-users, who experience digital enforcement, in this sub-section we present data on Internet non-users only. Given that this group is likely to be severely impacted by digital enforcement, we refer to them to illustrate that people's choice should matter.

We use the results of the World Internet Project survey (Díaz Andrade, Hedges, Karimikia, & Techatassanasoontorn, 2018), which we conducted in New Zealand in 2017, to demonstrate the existence of non users along with their reasons for not using the Internet. While the survey, part of an international initiative in over 40 countries, is intended to capture information about uses and perceptions of the Internet, we discovered that 6% of respondents were self-declared Internet non-users in a sample of 2012 nationwide individuals (16 years old and over). Their answers gave us valuable insights into why this group of New Zealanders do not use the Internet.

Before elaborating on the evidence, we feel compelled to present a few clarifications that help us accuratel interpret the results. A closer inspection of the answers given by the 6% of self-declared non-users reveals that some individuals are not entirely alien to the Internet. Thus, we classify the respondents into three categories. We labe the first group as “former users.” These are the people who decided to stop using digital technology, for example “I have not used a computer since I retired 10 to 12 years ago. I had just finished working for the Police and had been using a computer for 30 years.” We designate the second group as “indirect users.” These are people who rely on someone else to access information from the Internet, for example, “I do not seem to reclaim information about how to use the Internet, my son just does it for me.” The third group is perhaps the most interesting and we characterise them as “unaware users,” for example, “I do not understand the things on the Internet, only Facebook, as English is my second language.” After removing instances like these ones, we were left with a group of participant who can be unquestionably identified as “real” non-users. ## 4 | GOVERNMENTALITY: FROM DIGITAL INCLUSION FOR SOCIAL INCLUSION TO DIGITAL ENFORCEMENT

We propose using the concepts of “governmentality” and “technologies of power” (Foucault, 1979) as a useful way of further understanding how technology practices in society result in digital enforcement. In a broad sense governmentality refers to techniques and procedures for directing human behaviour (Rose, O'Malley, & Valverde, 2006). Governmentality constitutes a modern form of power reflected in a convergence of sophisticated methods “formed by the institutions, procedures, analyses and reflections, the calculations and tactics” (Foucault, 1979, p. 20), built on a type of regulated freedom that encourages individuals to compare what they are with what they could or should be (Rose, 1999). In this sense, the capacity of the individual for action is used to secure the ends of dominant actors (McKee, 2009).

The term governmentality should not be mistakenly attributed to the realm of the state as the sole responsibility for managing the conduct of citizens (Rose et al., 2006). This conceptualisation draws attention to “the diversity of forces and groups beyond the government that have, in heterogeneous ways, sought to regulate the lives of individuals” (Rose & Miller, 2008, p. 27). The contemporary forms of social control do not necessarily repress but coax most of the population to conform voluntarily to norms by standardisation of individual and collective conduct through normalisation of individuals and collective subjectivities. A normalising society is the outcome of a technology of power centred on self-governing subjects (Foucault, 1981; Willcoks, 2004). Also, the notion of governmentality helps us understand that “the activity of problematising is intrinsically linked to devising ways to seek to remedy it” (Rose & Miller, 2008, p. 15). Viewing the development of policy and research on digital divide and inclusion in the last few decades, we can clearly see the process of problematising by constructing and making visible the digita divide as a problem with many issues and concerns that are in need of some measures to rectify them in the form of “digital inclusion” interventions.

Governmentality involves not only ways of thinking but also technologies for intervening. Technologies of power are described as “actual mechanisms through which authorities of various sorts have sought to shape, normalise, and instrumentalise the conduct, thought, decisions and aspirations of others in order to achieve the objectives they consider desirable” (Miller & Rose, 1990, p. 8). Over the years, we have observed that the governing of digita inclusion has been done through systems of planning. These systems of planning come from governments in the form of digital inclusion frameworks as well as attempts from the private sector to nudge or coerce people by making Internet-enabled options more attractive or simply removing a non-Internet alternative altogether (see Table 3)

The examples presented in Table 3 constitute instances of digital enforcement manifested through the imposi tion of desirable behaviours as defined by dominant actors. A case in point is the strategy devised by the Canadia government to become a “model user” of information technology reported in Fraser's (2007) study. As part of this strategy, the Canadian government defined the use of technology as a norm of conduct for other organisations an as the means through which citizens interact with the state. The discourse that the Canadian government used to justify the need to transition towards a network society as “not only inevitable, but the only way in which Canadian can maintain the conditions for the national community to exist” (Fraser, 2007, p. 210) may resonate well in othe countries where access to the Internet is widely available. However, it is not only government agencies who envisage a future in which interactions and transactions with citizens are conducted through the Internet. Commercial actor are the front runners in the race for promoting digitisation because they are the ones who can reap the most signifi cant benefits in a digital economy (Mosco, 2004).

We contend that the prevalence of online interactions and transactions is increasingly making Internet non-users deviant individuals. Moreover, in the haste of the digitisation process, the choices of citizens are being seriously compromised. Since the online world is “what makes sense” to the majority, Internet users claim a position of legitimacy that affects the others, who are dispossessed from spaces of interactions and transactions (cf. Bourdieu's (1992 notion of symbolic domination). By making online interactions and transactions the default option for conducting most aspects of our lives, we are endorsing the hegemony of the majority. As a consequence, a group of people ma be losing control over how they want to live their lives.

We acknowledge that our exposition raises ethical questions.<sup>1</sup> We argue that there is nothing wrong with promoting access to the Internet; indeed, our personal belief is that the benefits of using the Internet in contemporar life generally outweigh the negatives. However, we cannot ignore a group of individuals who, for a diversity of rea sons, would rather not make the Internet part of their lives. Faced with the ethical dilemma of assessing the positive and negatives of living online or offline, we adopt a Kantian approach that emphasises the quality of humans a active, free agents who are entitled to make their own choices (Kant, Gregor, & Timmermann, 2011). While th classical utilitarian perspective considers a moral obligation “to augment the happiness of the community” (Long, 1990, p. 35), by adopting a Kantian approach, we try to protect and promote the plurality of individual inter ests in a fair manner. Assuming that the interests of Internet users, no matter how large this group is, are more important than those who may not want to use the Internet constitutes not only an arbitrary but also an unethica act. The interests of members of both groups are equally important because they both, owing to their human condi tion, have the freedom to make decisions. As Nussbaum (2011) forcibly argues, “[o]ptions are freedoms, and freedom has intrinsic value” (p. 25) and posits that “[t]here's a great difference between a public policy that aims to take care of people and a public policy that aims to honor choice” (p. 56). In this regard, she echoes Sen's (1999) criticism of paternalism: “There is a difference between ‘nannying’ an individual's choices and creating more opportunity for choice and for substantive decisions for individuals who can then act responsibly on that basis” (p. 284). Without freedom, individuals cannot exercise responsibility.

In the case that concerns us, we emphasise the intrinsic value of the opportunity for choice of the individual over the consequence of any particular choice. That is, the capability to choose to be an Internet user matters more than the benefits the individual can derive from using the Internet. It is equally important to recognise that the choice for not using the Internet matters more than the benefits the individual can derive from that choice. In the first case, the individual chooses to use the Internet because she may value the convenience of conducting some activities online (eg, online grocery shopping). In the second case, the individual chooses not to use the Internet because she relishes the prospect of human interaction or simply wants to prevent businesses or governments from exploiting her personal data. In societies where access to the Internet is widespread, it is about having the opportu nity to choose to use the Internet or not to do things the individual values doing. To be sure, denying access to the Internet when it is widely available would constitute an unfair act to members of society.

While we, like most of our IS colleagues, especially in the ICT4D area, believe in the benefits that can be derived from technology, we deliberately refrain from passing judgement on the choices individuals make about whether to use the Internet or not. Our concern is about depriving individuals of the ability to exercise their choices on how they want to live their lives in our quest for digital inclusion. We risk acting as the Marxist claiming to liberate the peasant by confiscating their harvest or the missionary civilising the pagan by imposing alien creeds. Without adopting a neo-Luddite position, we do not want to be complicit in punishing individuals because of their choice of not using the Internet; their choices must be respected (Sen, 1999).

## 5 | TECHNOLOGY AND SOCIAL INCLUSION: AN ETHICAL AGENDA OF MAKING A BETTER WORLD

Foucault's (1979) concepts of governmentality and technologies of power demonstrate the sophisticated use of modern power through “regulated freedom” to make individuals “voluntarily conform” to the social outcomes tha are considered desirable by dominant stakeholders who assert that all citizens should be Internet users. This raises an ethical question that has not been much explored in IS research: How can we create a better world where individ uals have a choice to use or not to use technology and are able to maintain their quality of life?

Inspired by Walsham's (2012) call for the IS field to develop an ethical agenda to help make a better world, we contend that there is a need for a critical examination of the role of technology to promote social inclusion in bot research and policy discourse. We conclude this article by offering some ideas that IS researchers can take to chal lenge a normative premise of seeing “technology as a solution, not the cause of problems” (Rowe, 2018, p. 381) These thoughts should not be viewed as a prescribed research agenda; instead, they should be treated as an invita tion for more intellectual conversations and actions among IS academics on this topic.

Chiasson, Davidson, and Winter (2018) propose an ethics-based IS research agenda “to explore potential, alter native futures, and identify areas where policy interventions (broadly defined) may act to bring about a more desired outcome” (p. 368). Implementing this research agenda requires IS researchers to take an anticipatory stance on th likely consequences of digital technology on multiple stakeholders (Stahl, Eden, Jirotka, & Coeckelbergh, 2014). In this regard, technology constitutes a platform for political debates among social actors with different interests and agendas that are to be inscribed in the always-evolving technological ecosystem (Feenberg, 2001). This idea of democratisation of technology opens up debates and choices to citizens, particularly those who are affected by such technology.

We do realise that our exposition raises ethical questions about the unintended consequences of digitisation which we cannot address here. However, we would like to invite our colleagues in the IS community to explore th issues discussed here without the preconceived notion that Internet use is always desirable. Thus, we propose fou different research avenues that could enhance our understanding of the causes, nature and consequences of digita enforcement.

Firstly, IS researchers may want to engage more deeply with those who experience digital enforcement to dis cover their values and interests in life and their perceptions about the role the Internet might play. This line of inves tigation may reveal their fears of and hesitances about the Internet. Perhaps it can even show the irrelevance of Internet technology to achieve what they consider essential in life. However, researchers should acknowledge that engagement with the Internet is not a binary condition, that is, either use or not use.

Secondly, IS researchers may want to focus on the analysis of current digital inclusion policies and corporat practices that deprive individuals of offline choices. This analysis can expose how the growing transfer of offlin interactions and transactions to the online sphere rapidly diminishes the choices available to individuals and their “right to choose.” This line of investigation can identify the global, powerful actors that have a vested interest in pro moting the universalisation of online interactions and transactions.

Thirdly, IS researchers may want to scrutinise the ethical implications that emerge with digital enforcement Given the benefits that Internet technology can provide in education and healthcare, particularly in the context of current debates about the sustainable development goals sanctioned by the United Nations, researchers may want to analyse the contradictions that emerge by forcing individuals to use the Internet.

Fourthly, IS researchers may want to focus on the supply side of online services. A legitimate question can b asked about the moral obligation, and the implications for efficiency, of government agencies providing duplicated online and offline services to cater for both those individuals who choose to use online channels and those who do not. A similar question can be asked about the right of private organisations, whose primary goal is profit maximisation, to offer online channels only.

Of course, the research avenues we propose here require scholars to challenge the status quo that is dominated by studies on Internet users. Pursuing the ethical agenda that we propose may require researchers to go beyond con ventional approaches and take on the role of what Davison (2020) calls an iconoclast. However, researchers alon cannot advance innovative ideas and approaches; for their effort to come to fruition, the cooperation of perceptiv and open-minded reviewers is required.

We conclude with an optimistic note on the development of desirable futures even though we recognise that the window of actions is quickly narrowing down for non-users or reluctant users of the Internet. We remain hopefu that active influence by individuals and groups with IS scholars among them can help us realise desirable futures for all.
