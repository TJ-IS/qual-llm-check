## Abstract

Digital social innovation (DSI)—the novel use of digital technology to address societal challenges—plays a critical role in our collective pursuit of sustainable development. In this practitioner paper, we present an in-depth DSI case study where the grassroots communities in a remote county leveraged e-commerce to leapfrog out of poverty, becoming successful entrepreneurs with online businesses that thrive on a global scale. Based on this case study, we first discuss three leapfrogging opportunities afforded by e-commerce, which were successfully embraced by the grassroots communities to begin the transformative journey. We then reveal three obstacles that emerged during the technology leapfrogging process, which have challenged the sustainability of the grassroots DSI endeavour. Lastly, we discuss three top-down interventions that have been instrumental in overcoming the bottleneck of grassroots leapfrogging development and subsequently nurture a self-sustaining, resilient community. Overall, by discussing some of the common barriers to the growth and sustainability of DSI, and how they have been addressed, this article offers insights and recommendations for policymakers, public and private sector practitioners, and communities in underdeveloped regions to navigate both the potentials and pitfalls of technology leapfrogging, and ultimately build a pathway towards resilience and sustainability.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The United Nations sustainable development goals have provoked a global movement in addressing the world's most pressing challenges—from poverty to climate change—to create a more prosperous and sustainable future (Pan & Zhang, 2020). Digital social innovation (DSI)—the novel use of digital technology to address major societa challenges—has been the bedrock of sustainable development and has therefore garnered increasing attention amongst researchers and practitioners particularly in recent years (Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2016; Pan & Zhang, 2020). Information systems (IS) research has shed light on various DSI initiatives and their impact on sustainable development. From improving health and well-being (Venkatesh, Rai, Sykes, & Aljafari, 2016), relieving hunger (Zheng & Yu, 2016), to promoting environmental sustainability (Malhotra, Melville, & Watson, 2013; Tim, Pan Bahri, & Fauzi, 2018) and civic engagement (Leong, Pan, Bahri, & Fauzi, 2018; Tim, Pan, Bahri, & Fauzi, 2017), existing DSI research has painted an optimistic picture of resilience and sustainability in the face of societal challenges.

Nevertheless, we are far from making the most of DSI's transformative potential (Faik, Barrett, & Oborn, 2020) Sustainable development strategies need to effectively encompass the potential of digital technology to create accel erated and further-reaching impact (Pan & Zhang, 2020), but many possibilities offered by DSI remain underexplored (Faik et al., 2020; Majchrzak et al., 2016). In this practitioner paper, we contribute to the growing body of knowledge on DSI and sustainable development by presenting several learnings derived from our longitudinal research on the emergence of ‘e-commerce villages’ in remote China—a DSI phenomenon that has since flourished into a nationwid development program that bridges inequality and alleviates poverty.

In the past two decades, we have experienced a massive wave of growth in e-commerce and observed its inte gration into our everyday life. E-commerce has since developed into a relatively well-researched topic in the IS discipline. However, most existing studies have investigated e-commerce as an economic phenomenon. For example, handful of existing research examines how e-commerce adoption led to business value creation (Du & Mao, 2018 X. Li, Troutt, Brandyberry, & Wang, 2011; Tan, Pan, Lu, & Huang, 2015). Strategies in catering for consumer preferences and maximise sales performance were discussed (Chen, Huang, & Davison, 2017; Fang et al., 2014; Huang Chen, Ou, Davison, & Hua, 2017; Martinsons, 2008), including product categorisation (Lin & Wang, 2018), time limited discounts (H. Li, Fang, Lim, & Wang, 2019) and the use of computer-mediated communication tools on e commerce platforms (Ou, Pavlou, & Davison, 2014).

However, we know little about the social impact of e-commerce (Leong, Pan, Newell, & Cui, 2016), especially in terms of how e-commerce can be effectively integrated into the lives of the underprivileged (Lund & McGuire, 2005) to achieve significant developmental outcomes, such as poverty alleviation. Investigating the social impact of e commerce is of both research and practical importance. Existing research has indicated that e-commerce can empower underserved communities to achieve societal development at an accelerated rate (Cui, Pan, & Cui, 2019 Datta, 2011; Hanna, 2016). For consumers in underdeveloped regions, e-commerce could help break geographica barriers, allowing consumers to access a larger choice of goods at lower prices. For producers in underdeveloped regions, e-commerce releases them from reliance on expensive intermediaries and allows them to sell their products to a much larger market, both of which largely improve their income and standard of living. E-commerce adoption in underdeveloped regions also has the potential to bring transformation at a larger scale. When brick-and-mortar retai stores—especially mega-store chains such as Walmart and the like—were growing in the urban region, such develop ment not only had a significant impact on the local economy, but also generated unprecedented transformations in employment, supply chain, logistics, and consumer lifestyle and behaviours (Gereffi & Christian, 2009). Online retailing, as the next wave in the retail industry, is likely to bring even greater changes to society, especially in underdeveloped regions which were largely untapped in the previous retail era

In this article, we shed light on how e-commerce can be adopted to bring forth sustainable development amon the once marginalised communities through a case study of one of the earliest and largest e-commerce counties<sup>1</sup> i rural China. We conceptualise the process of e-commerce adoption in rural China as a technology leapfrogging pro cess. Technology leapfrogging is the action of adopting a technology in an area where the prior version of such a technology had not been adopted (Davison, Vogel, Harris, & Jones, 2000). In our case study, the rural communities in China leapfrogged the brick-and-mortar retail era and dove straight into online retail through a large-scale adoption of e-commerce. Despite being latecomers, rural communities in China have created a thriving e-commerce ecosystem through community-driven initiatives and a close collaboration with one of the largest e-commerce platform providers in the world—Alibaba. Based on this case study, we derive insights and lessons learned that can inform both research and practice in technology leapfrogging and, more broadly, the social impact of e-commerce in enabling the building of a digitally resilient and sustainable community.

Firstly, aligning with discussions from existing research (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019; Davison et al., 2000), our case study demonstrates that the success of DSI demands more than just having access to a particular technology. In rura China, for example, despite having access to an e-commerce platform and supporting infrastructure, several challenges limit the further progress of the leapfrogging development—including the absence of accumulated expertise and experi ences, as well as resource constraints. Secondly, our case study demonstrates how an effective grafting of collective bottom-up actions and top-down interventions can be effective in overcoming the bottlenecks of leapfrogging devel opment and bringing forth sustainable development. Overall, by presenting findings derived from an in-depth research on a resilient community that has successfully achieved advancement through DSI, this article contributes furthe understanding on the opportunities and challenges of technology leapfrogging (Davison et al., 2000; Fong, 2009), and offers actionable insights for policymakers, public and private sector practitioners, and civil society to better wor together in nurturing DSI for societal advancement (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019; Majchrzak et al., 2016)

For the communities in underdeveloped regions, insights developed from this research can help to develop a better understanding on the common issues pertaining to technology leapfrogging, and to identify ways in which col lective effort and top-down support can be leveraged to overcome constraints and advance development. For deci sion makers, including local governments in underdeveloped regions, public sector practitioners and technology providers, the findings presented in this article highlight key vulnerabilities in the technology leapfrogging proces and provide recommendations on effective interventions that can be implemented to better support the sustainabl growth of DSI initiatives.

This article is organised as follows. In Section 2, we present the background for our case study—Suichang, th first e-commerce county in rural China. In Section 3, we present rich descriptions developed from our case study to illustrate how communities in the remote county successfully leveraged the Taobao e-commerce platform of Alibab to begin a transformative entrepreneurial journey. Our rich descriptions also unveil three challenges that the commu nities encountered during the technology leapfrogging process and discuss three top-down interventions that wer implemented to overcome the challenges. In Section 4, we discuss lessons learned developed from our case study on the emergence and management of technology leapfrogging and their practical implications. In Section 5, we con clude the article with future research directions

## 2 | THE EMERGENCE OF ‘TAOBAO VILLAGES’ IN RURAL CHINA

## 2.1 | Alibaba and its Taobao platform

With the proliferation of Internet usage and mobile devices, e-commerce has grown and continues to grow rapidly in China, becoming one of the key foci in the nation's development strategies (Press, 2016), By 2022, China's e commerce market is expected to hit 1.8 trillion USD, more than twice the size of the US e-commerce market (713 bil lion USD by 2022) and larger than that of the United States, Britain, Japan, Germany and France combined (Forrester, 2018). The explosive growth of e-commerce in China hit headlines as e-commerce giant, Alibaba, marke its record-breaking 25 billion USD IPO in September 2014. Alibaba has since emerged as an immensely powerfu driver of e-commerce in China. In their 2019 fiscal year, e-commerce transactions in China exceeded 5 trillion RMB (853 billion USD), more than double the transactions of Amazon and eBay combined (Alibaba Group, 2019)

Alibaba was founded in 1999 by Jack Ma, a former English teacher from Hangzhou, China, who saw the need for a B2B platform to connect millions of small and medium-sized enterprises in China with customers all over th world. Today, Alibaba provides C2C, B2C and B2B services through its three main sites: Taobao, Tmall and Alibaba com, respectively. Taobao, which translates as ‘digging for treasure’, is Alibaba's biggest e-commerce site, and has a near monopoly on C2C online trading in China. Founded in 2003, Taobao is now home to more than 9 million mer chants, selling everything from pickled vegetables to car spoilers

Taobao distinguishes itself from eBay by leveraging a deep understanding of Chinese consumers to infuse loca cultural preferences into the platform and the services offered (Davison & Ou, 2008; Ou et al., 2014; Ou & Davison, 2009). For example, the busy, compact layout and strong colours of Taobao's webpages were designed to suit the preferences of Chinese consumers. Several services such as Alipay (an escrow service designed to ensur secure transactions) and AliWangWang (a chat function designed to allow buyers and sellers to communicate) were also created to establish the trust needed for Chinese consumers to embrace e-commerce. All these services are pro vided free of charge for Taobao users.<sup>2</sup>

In December 2014, Alibaba announced that one of the company's top priorities will be to promote e-commerce adoption in the rural regions of China (South China Morning Post, 2015). While the announcement came as a surprise for many, e-commerce has in fact been gaining traction in the rural regions of China since 2009. Over the past few decades, unequal development and the subsequent unequal distribution of income, education and healthcare in the Chinese countryside has led to a series of societal issues, such as brain drain, poverty and a widening rural-urban disparity. Nearly half of China's 1.36 billion people live in rural areas with limited access to resources and opportunities.

In 2009, clusters of ‘Taobao Villages’—villages in which at least 10% of residents operate an online store on Taobao and generate annual sales of at least 10 million RMB (1.6 million USD)—surfaced in the Chinese countrysid and have since transformed the lives of rural Chinese residents. By September 2020, 5,425 Taobao Villages had emerged. In addition, 1,756 ‘Taobao Counties’ (a county in which there are more than 10 Taobao Villages) had also emerged in the country (see Figure 1). For Alibaba, more than 70% of the increase in active users in the third quarter of 2018 came from underdeveloped areas (SCMP, 2019).

## 2.2 | A case study of Suichang—The first Taobao county in China

Taobao will come to the villages, we just knew it. Suichang is their first stop. Did you see the ecommerce slogans we painted on the wall when you guys walked around in the village? That was an idea we developed with the Taobao Marketing department. They actually work very well: they convey a strong message: don't underestimate the power of rural areas… We set up a good starting point [for rural e-commerce]. Last year we were given “The Best E-merchant” award by Alibaba and the China E-commerce Association at the National E-commerce Conference. The first and only medal in the country. (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association)

In this article, we present a case study of Suichang, the first Taobao County in China, to discuss how rural communities leapfrogged into becoming e-commerce entrepreneurs with online businesses that are thriving on a globa scale. Suichang is a county located in the Zhejiang province, in Eastern China. Suichang is a small and mountainous county that is known for its beautiful bamboo forest and natural scenery. Prior to the emergence of e-commerce, more than 70% of Suichang's 50 000 residents were working as farmers. More than 18 000 residents in the count subsisted on an annual net income of less than 3,000 RMB (400 USD). Like many other rural regions in China, Suichang faced problems of depopulation and rural hollowing before the rise of e-commerce. Most of the young people had left the county in search of jobs in the city areas, whilst the elderly, who stayed behind, made a living by tending the fields.

## 3 | CASE FINDINGS

## 3.1 | The grassroots breakthrough and the limits of leapfrogging

## 3.1.1 | Low entry barrier and the steep learning curve

The flourishing of e-commerce in rural China was an unexpected development. Like most other remote areas, th technological and infrastructure developments in rural China had been lacking and communities had limited exposure to digital technologies. Adding to the lack of digital literacy was the lack of knowledge and experience in busines management, a crucial skillset to sustain a profitable e-commerce venture. In our case study, for example, these two issues were highlighted by one of the Suichang pioneers as the first hurdle in introducing e-commerce to the resi dents in Suichang county:

Other than basic computer skills, they still needed to master online marketing strategies. Not only that, they had low bargaining power in getting supplies: they could hardly convince the suppliers that they would be able to sell well, let alone negotiate for a lower purchase price. (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association)

These initial roadblocks, however, did not stop the grassroots adoption of e-commerce as the availability of th Taobao platform and a growing community-based support system established a promising outlook for this transformative journey. The Taobao platform was created to realise one of Alibaba's core objectives in empowering the growth of small businesses. The platform was made available to individuals to start their businesses at zero cost Technically, only basic technology infrastructure such as access to the Internet is required for anyone to start an online business on the platform.

Nevertheless, a low setup cost was not the only factor that empowered rural communities to take the leap. From our case study, we observed how a few pioneers played a significant role in cultivating confidence and developing a community to fuel the adoption of e-commerce. Mr. Dongming Pan, for example, was one of the few pioneers lead ing the adoption of e-commerce in Suichang county. Pan left the county when he was young to look for better job opportunities in larger cities. In 2010, Pan saw an opportunity in e-commerce, and decided to return to Suichan with a strong drive to market the rich agricultural products of his hometown through the Taobao platform.

Knowing that the local community had limited knowledge in technology and business, Pan immediately identi fied the need to establish a support system for the grassroots endeavour. Working with a few other pioneers and the local government, Pan established the ‘Suichang E-commerce Association’ in March 2010 and has served as th chairman ever since. The main objective of this association is to better support and coordinate the organic growth of e-commerce in the county. The association brings together all stakeholders, including e-merchants, suppliers, logistic companies, financial service companies and more, to consolidate available resources, expertise and provide services to address unmet needs as they arise. Through the establishment of the association and the initial success demon strated by a few pioneers, e-commerce adoption in the county started to grow steadily. Most of the early adopters started their first online stores selling agricultural produce that was readily available across the region, including char coal, tea, sweet potatoes and bamboo products. One of our interviewees provided a succinct summary when asked about how their e-commerce journey started:

We have trainings offered by the association. If we're unsure about anything we can also ask them [the association]. We [rural residents] all also know each other, we always get together and talk about this [e-commerce]. (E-merchant (1))

The initial growth of e-commerce however, soon faced some issues. While the availability of a free-for-use e commerce platform and the encouraging success stories of early adopters provided a positive outlook for the e commerce venture, the locals who had taken the leap to join the entrepreneurship journey soon recognised that the had just scratched the surface in terms of running a business. The ability to leapfrog brought new opportunities but at the same time also exposed the skill gaps of the newly minted entrepreneurs

In Suichang, many e-merchants started their online businesses through imitation and quick learning from their peers and the association. It was relatively easy to create a new online store on the Taobao platform, selling simila local agricultural products that were available to everyone in the county. Nevertheless, managing an online business required a new set of capabilities and know-how, which many rural entrepreneurs were not well-equipped with. In addition to the lack of expertise and experience in business management, the rural entrepreneurs also faced signifi cant resource constraints, which limited further growth of their businesses. For example, our interviewees constantly highlighted the issue of having limited human resources. Attracting talent from urban areas was very difficult, given the limited venture capital and the lower standard of living in rural areas. As the e-commerce business continues to grow, there is an increasing demand for different expertise, which is difficult to fulfil in the rural context:

Actually, I think our [rural entrepreneurs] most significant disadvantage is our limited access to talent. As I mentioned before, people who are skilled, the businessmen, will not be willing to come to here. We are not there yet… right now, we really need people who can manage the operations and product design. Design and operation – really difficult to get. (E-merchant (2))

## 3.1.2 | The quick wins and the long-term strategies

Despite initial difficulties, the influx of online businesses has undeniably contributed significantly to the economic development of rural China. E-commerce offers rural residents the possibility of earning a decent income with off farm work, while also creating numerous job opportunities that have attracted many more individuals to return from the cities to begin their entrepreneurial journey in their rural hometowns.

In Suichang, e-commerce adoption has boosted the stagnant rural economy by reviving the county's agricultura industry. The Taobao platform offers a means for agricultural products to be supplied directly to the consumers, cut ting out the middleman's long-standing control of the market. The transparency of the platform also provides accu rate market information for the e-merchants, eliminating the problem of suppressed prices which is common in th agricultural industry. One of our interviewees explains how e-commerce has allowed the rural residents to bring thei long-established agricultural business to the wider market:

Most of us here [living in Suichang] know how to make our own tea leaves. Our handmade tea tastes much better than industrialised tea you know… so tea became a big part of our Taobao [business]… our tea wasn't that well-known before but now we are one of the top 3 of best-selling products on Taobao. That wasn't easy to achieve. (Representative (1) from the Suichang E-commerce Association)

The increased visibility of rural produce on the e-commerce platform generated a renewed interest amongst consumers and stimulated both domestic and international consumption of rural produce. In 2012, approximatel 2 years after e-commerce business started to grow in Suichang, the total transaction value recorded by all member of Suichang E-commerce Association, who collectively run more than 1,200 online shops on Taobao, reached 1.5 million RMB.

However, while the unique agricultural and handmade products of Suichang have offered local e-merchants a niche position to break into the e-commerce market, it was just a matter of time before similar products from other Taobao vil lages started to flood the market. Given that e-commerce adoption in the rural regions was mainly driven by imitation, the highly similar product base and business model quickly led to product homogenisation and cut-throat competitio amongst the community. Rural entrepreneurs, who have little experience in product differentiation and branding strate gies, did not respond well to the intensification of competition. Many of them started price wars and repeatedly cut price for short-term wins. Over time, the price wars significantly reduced the profit margins for rural products. The lack of a proper branding strategy and product differentiation hampered the sustainability of the business:

Very soon we faced a new problem—even when we [the association] trained many e-merchants, we realised many of them were unable to sustain their businesses. We did our investigation, and what we learned is, the e-merchants all think they have no competitive advantage over others. Some said they really didn't know what to sell. (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association)

The reliance on agricultural products also created unprecedented issues ranging from branding to logistics. For example, selling fresh food products online was a new experience for both the e-merchants and consumers. The emerchants faced a new set of challenges related to storage and logistics. At the same time, consumers were demand ing more assurance in terms of the quality and safety of these products. Some consumers were hesitant to purchas fresh food products online, especially when hundreds of similar products were available on the market yet very few indicators on how these products compare in terms of quality and safety.

Recognising these issues, the Suichang E-commerce Association started a new initiative in 2011 called MyStore, offering end-to-end supply chain services to novice entrepreneurs:

There were many issues, for example, negotiating a good price with suppliers, and even selecting an appropriate product to sell. And then there was the issue of storage and logistics. These challenges are like a series of mountains, creating obstacles for the e-merchants… so now with [the store], the English name is MyStore. Why? Because it is a store for us: the e-merchants, the suppliers, the association. MyStore has a physical presence, we have a physical place now, the association did not have one. (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association)

MyStore is a one-stop service centre established to support the rural entrepreneurs in all things, ranging from product selection (see Figure 3), photography, to storage and quality control. Managed by the association, MyStor sources a wide variety of products from suppliers, packages the products and even provides hundreds of profession ally taken photos for each product for the e-merchants to choose from. The store also offers a service acting as an intermediary: it allows e-merchants to direct customers' purchases to the store, which will then deliver the products to the customers on behalf of the e-merchants. In this way, MyStore reduces the burden on e-merchants in investin in storage space and managing logistics

With such a support structure, rural residents are given the flexibility to play different roles in the e-commerce ecosystem, without having to handle all the complexities of managing a business

We help a lot in the product development and design for agricultural produce. Most of the suppliers of agricultural products are farmers working in the field, it is really difficult to ask them to think about marketing, products, etc. Now in our Marketing Department we have about 20 to 30 people that specialise in this product development. (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association)

MyStore also plays an important role in resolving the cut-throat competition among rural e-merchants by culti vating more awareness in product differentiation and branding:

We always remind our members: don't sell everything you can get here from the store. Try to identify what is unique for your online shop, don't sell everything but push for one or two signature products that are unique for your business. (Vice Chairman of Suichang E-commerce Association)

However, while making significant progress, the e-commerce champions in Suichang were well aware of th challenges ahead. Many of the e-merchants we interviewed pointed out their concerns, especially around the sus tainability of their businesses. For example, one of our interviewees revealed a pessimistic view when asked to com ment about the prospect of selling agricultural products on Taobao:

I don't think we can do anything for now. There is no existing standard for agricultural products online, and it is beyond our ability to set this standard. Now we just need to keep trying to find a way to make our customers feel safe about our products. I think we need at least one or two years to do this. So I don't think selling agricultural products gives us any advantage at this stage. (E-merchant (2))

## 3.1.3 | Revitalisation of traditional industries and the disruption of social fabric

As e-commerce continued to grow in Suichang, the substantial increase in income and infrastructure development brought significant improvements to the standard of living. In addition to business value, the availability of ecommerce also brought unprecedented options and convenience for the rural residents. Products from cities could now be delivered to the most remote regions, saving rural residents time and money that used to be spent travelling thousands of miles to purchase necessities and desired goods. One of our interviewees summarised thi development:

I think this initiative is a very meaningful one especially for villagers. What problems have we solved? We addressed a major inconvenience in buying and selling. For those of you who have not visited a rural village before, you should go see this one in the afternoon. Try imagine this: all young people have left the village, leaving the older people who have limited literacy… they have very limited choice, the only places they can go are those little shops, but what can you get from there? (Loca government representative)

The growth of e-commerce in rural regions also creates many job opportunities (see Figure 4). Local residents are now able to take on a variety of roles, including joining a logistics company, working in customer service, photog raphy, product packaging, website development and much more. In Suichang, much of the young workforce that had left the county in search of better opportunities have now returned to participate in the e-commerce ventures Leveraging on their exposure and experience in the cities, many of these young returnees have transformed their decades-long traditional family businesses into lucrative e-commerce businesses on Taobao. Many of these tradi tional businesses, such as weaving and bamboo craft, have long suffered a decline from limited profitability and an ageing workforce. By harnessing the potential of e-commerce, the young returnees have played a part in revitalisin these traditional businesses and preserving the local culture and values

However, such a strong drive and commitment in the e-commerce business has also brought about significant disruption in rural living. One of the unexpected changes observed through our site visit and from interviews was a forced shift in the lifestyle of rural residents. Many rural residents had to leave behind their relaxed pace of life to keep up with the growth of e-commerce. Our interviewees repeatedly pointed out their stressful new lifestyle du to having limited resources and facing fierce competition. For example, to effectively communicate with customers who often do online shopping at night, rural e-merchants had to work on customer service from late evening until midnight:

During the day, they are occupied with other tasks such as product packaging, inventory management, delivery and more. These lifestyle changes indirectly caused the often-close-knit rural communities to become isolated focusing entirely on their own business ventures. During our site visit, some of our interviewees repeatedly pointe out how occupied they are, not just with their business operations, but also with media and interview requests:

We have been interviewed by two to three hundred media outlets already, all requesting a site visit as well. Everyone around here has had so many interviews, so please try to keep it brief today. They have a very tight schedule. Starting from early noon they have to start organising product delivery. The boss has to do everything himself. (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association)

Another surprising consequence of e-commerce development was the reoccurrence of out-migration. Whil some young talent has returned to the village to pursue the entrepreneurial opportunity enabled by e-commerce the limited capacity and resources in the rural context have constrained further growth of their businesses. Some of the more successful e-merchants were forced to move their operations to nearby cities for better access to resources. The limited scalability of the rural context has therefore triggered a population outflow. Some of ou interviewees have also pointed out how e-commerce growth has steered interest away from other jobs, such as kindergarten teachers, because most people are attracted to e-commerce related roles that are more financiall endowed.

Taobao first came to us in 2011. They were all very senior people. After their visit, we officially registered our association as a company. I still recall, during their first visit, it was winter and really, really cold. We didn't even have a heater here. But we talked for many, many hours. Everyone was reall excited. We talked about how e-commerce can help the agricultural industry so much. They said we just have to continue the good work we have been doing, Taobao would want to work with us. They are one of the largest e-commerce platforms in the world! This gave us confidence and inspiration (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association

Overall, the grassroots-driven development of e-commerce in Suichang has demonstrated immense possibili ties for underserved communities to leapfrog to a digitally enabled future. However, as previously discussed, th grassroots effort has been met with several challenges which constrain its further development. These challenge mainly stem from the limitations around resources and competence, which are common in underserved contexts In this section, we present several lessons learned from our research that shed light on the role Alibaba played in empowering the further growth of the grassroots endeavour. These lessons can inform future top-down effort that aim to address the bottlenecks found in underdeveloped contexts and uplift self-organised, local DS initiatives.

## 3.2.1 | Bridging the competency gaps

As previously discussed, the accelerated yet disjointed development of e-commerce revealed a gap in the technica and managerial skills of rural e-merchants. While local initiatives such as peer sharing and training sessions have hel ped tremendously in getting the rural e-merchants started in a short time, most rural e-merchants have limited digita proficiency, no prior entrepreneurial experience, and demand much more holistic training and support. One of ou interviewees from the Suichang E-commerce Association highlighted this significant need in upskilling:

We don't have young people here. Most of us are middle-aged and older, our literacy level is below average. (Representative (2) from the Suichang E-commerce Association)

As the platform provider, Alibaba recognised the massive potential of Suichang and was eager to support its fur ther growth through upskilling. One significant investment by Alibaba in this area was related to the launch of severa targeted programs and initiatives by Taobao University. Taobao University was founded by Alibaba in 2006 as a training platform to cultivate a talent pool to support e-commerce development in China. Recognising the significant skills gaps in rural e-commerce development, Taobao University launched the ‘Taobao Village E-commerce Talent Development Plan’ in 2017 to provide support for the Taobao Villages:

To alleviate poverty, we need to motivate the people and equip the people with knowledge. At Taobao University, we believe that mindset and knowledge are the key in poverty alleviation. (Representative (1) from Alibaba)

Three key initiatives were offered as part of this plan. Firstly, Taobao University brought their highly experienced instructors to the remote villages to run bespoke, professional lessons and seminars. These trainings were tailored based on the specific needs of the local context, including taking into consideration the resources and specialty prod ucts available locally and the maturity of e-commerce development. The trainings were also holistic—they wer offered not only to the e-merchants, but to all key players including wholesalers, association leaders and the loca government. This professional leadership and management training helped nurture a self-sustaining local support system, which was critical for an independent, healthy growth at the county level:

Taobao University is leveraging our professional teaching staff and courses on e-commerce to provide targeted training for local government in regions in poverty. We tailor made courses and training programs for local leaders, including our “targeted poverty alleviation class”… to equip the local leaders with the latest insights, approaches, and models, so together we can work on promoting the growth of e-commerce. (Representative (1) from Alibaba)

Secondly, Taobao University offers thousands of online training courses to maximise the scale and impact of upskilling. These courses, covering a wide range of topics including business management, product promotion, data analysis, customer services and more, have provided highly accessible upskilling opportunities for the rural communi ties. Lastly, recognising that creating longer term impact requires deep involvement of local stakeholders, Taobao University worked closely with local leaders and communities in Suichang to co-create initiatives that could best serve their needs. For example, combining Taobao University's expertise in professional curriculum development and coaching with the domain expertise of Suichang representatives, a group of local trainers were nurtured. These trainers work closely with Taobao University to continuously communicate local demands and bring in Alibaba's lat est resources and know-how to support the grassroots development.

In addition to offering training, Alibaba also provided significant financial support to the e-merchants through Ant Financial, its investment body. Ant Financial offers a range of exclusive financial loan packages to all e-merchant in Taobao Villages, both existing and upcoming. One of our interviewees summarised how Ant Financial supports the further development of Taobao villages:

In our village, if someone wants to start something, they will submit a request to us. For example, they want to be a supplier, want to start poultry farming. We will evaluate their request: how big a place they need, how much money they need to get started. We don't provide the financial loan, but Alibaba does. The loan provided is very targeted, you have to spend the money on the things you were approved to spend on. In this way, we not only empower individuals on their entrepreneurshi journey, but through the approval process and targeted loan, we also help in shaping the development and direction of rural e-commerce. (Representative (2) from the Suichang E-commerce Association)

## 3.2.2 | Paving the way to scalability

Most online shops of rural entrepreneurs rely on local produce such as agricultural or handcrafted products, whic are products that often do not have an innately strong or unique selling point on the online marketplace. The nature of these goods, compounded with entrepreneurs' inexperience in creating a unique value proposition, severely lim ited growth in the highly competitive market of e-commerce. With more clusters of Taobao villages emerging from different rural regions in China, Alibaba soon identified the need for better coordination and organisation. Whe managed effectively, these self-organised clusters can form strong identities, which collectively promote variety and enrich the e-commerce market.

Alibaba worked on creating unique brand identities for rural produce and in effectively marketing these prod ucts. One initiative worth noting was the creation of a specialised microsite on Taobao.com, which features a ‘Uniquely China’ branding to promote regional Chinese specialties. This initiative allowed individual entrepreneurs to craft a personalised image for the rural produce they sell and to project the unique value of such products onto th global marketplace. Suichang was the first county-level unit to be featured on the site, with a ‘Taste of Home’ brand ing. Local products such as baked sweet potatoes, charcoal peanuts and bamboo shoots were marketed as the tradi tional, handmade snacks you would find at home. To further promote the unique positioning of Suichang as a place close to nature, the website also sold tickets to several tourist attractions in Suichang to encourage more people to visit the county and enjoy the unique food and scenery. One of our interviewees elaborated on the impact of such an innovative collaboration:

We also worked on our collaboration with Taobao. Around the end of 2011, we basically confirmed a strategic partnership with Taobao. But it was not until 17th May 2012 that we officially signed a con tract with them, the first of its kind in China. The official collaboration has triggered many changes, including all the attention we now receive from the news media, experts and university. Everyday there are so many interview requests I need to attend to, CCTV News China, Xinhua, China News, they have all visited before. These media reports significantly helped us in promotion. Immediately after signing a collaboration with Taobao, we began to prepare for something. It's called “Uniquely China”. (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association)

Other counties which later joined the e-commerce journey were subsequently featured on the site with their own unique selling point. For example, the Shandong county was featured as the ‘Producer of Grains’ for selling qual ity staple foods such as grains, wheat and maize, which are the key agricultural products of the county. With the ‘Uniquely China’ branding initiative, Alibaba has created differentiation and a unique identity for each region thereby reducing the cut-throat competition and fostering a healthy development of the e-commerce ecosystem. An e-merchant in Suichang summarised how getting support from Alibaba significantly helped their e-business:

## 3.2.3 | Creating a sustainable ecosystem

In addition to providing support in business operations and management, Alibaba was also committed to a bigge vision: creating a sustainable ecosystem for rural e-commerce to prosper. To nurture a healthy e-commerce ecosystem in Suichang, Alibaba collaborated with Suichang's local leaders and government in an innovative initiative—th ‘Ganjie’ platform (which translates to ‘going to the market together’). The Ganjie platform initiative started in 2014 to increase the reach of e-commerce to the last mile. The core mission of the Ganjie platform is to embed e-commerc in every remote area and ‘connect rural community with the digital society’ (translated from a slogan spotted durin our site visit). To offer the most comprehensive and inclusive service possible, the Ganjie platform collaborates wit small convenience stores and groceries shops in the most remote areas of Suichang to set up ‘Ganjie service points’

Computers and internet access were provided to these service points, and individuals were trained to serve as intermediaries to assist residents in the area in purchasing products on Taobao. The service points also receive deliveries on behalf of the residents. More interestingly, the service points offer additional services beyond e-commerce, such as helping local residents pay their electricity bills online and booking train tickets, with the aim of promoting acceptance of a digitally enabled lifestyle in these most isolated regions (see Figure 5). The tight bonds and increased community cohesion nurtured through these service points were also essential to developing a self-sustainable and digitally resilient community.

In a few years, the Ganjie platform developed rapidly into an exemplary model of rural e-commerce, given its positive impact in promoting the adoption of e-commerce and subsequent improvement in the standard of living in the most remote regions. In 2017, the platform received a significant investment from Alibaba, which became th second largest shareholder of the platform. By 2019, the platform had received more than 1 billion RMB in tota investment and developed into the signature platform of agricultural e-commerce. Overall, the ‘Suichang Model became the first exemplary model of agricultural e-commerce in China, demonstrating how the proactiv involvement of grassroots and a close collaboration with leaders can revitalise the rural agricultural industry and transform rural living. A succinct comment from one of our interviewees serves as a great summary for this encour aging phenomenon:

Overall, I see the development of rural e-commerce as the creation of an ecosystem, a ‘value system’. It includes collaboration with Alibaba (Do you know about the strategic agreement we signed with them?) as well as the value we show to our local government. Actually, we didn't need to convince them or sell this [e-commerce] to them. What we did was simply execution. After we demonstrated the value created for the people, the industry, the young villagers, everyone saw that. They all aligned. (Representative (2) from the Suichang E-commerce Association)

I actually think, for places like here [Suichang], actually the more under-developed a place is, the more we need technologies. Overall, based on our rich empirical data, we have developed an understanding on the rise of grassroots-driven leapfrogging, its limitations, and the role of top-down interventions in leading the way to its further development and sustainability. The successful adoption of e-commerce in rural China has demonstrated the potential for underserved communities to benefit from technological development. DSI can enable significant transformations even in the most con strained contexts and the flourishing of online businesses in rural China has indeed painted a hopeful picture for technology leapfrogging. Nevertheless, our research also highlights that having access to a DSI alone does not guar antee successful leapfrogging and social changes (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019; Davison et al., 2000). In this research, we demonstrate that a successful leapfrogging process requires more than just access to a technology—it requires an effective integration of both bottom-up and top-down efforts.

## 4.1 | The grassroots breakthrough and the limits of leapfrogging

Bottom-up development is crucial in ensuring that the digital initiative is community-driven and operationalisable in the local context. As shown in our case study, the organic growth in the early stages demonstrates that the grassroots can tap into their indigenous knowledge base and local expertise to establish a unique positioning in the globa market. For example, the long-established expertise in producing high-quality agricultural products and traditiona crafts was one of the early driving forces that instilled confidence in the local community in Suichang to adopt e commerce. Despite the local community being totally unfamiliar with digital technologies and business management a strong drive to pursue an improved life and a close-knit relationship existing within the community in Suichang suc cessfully triggered significant e-commerce adoption in the early stages. Overall, the initial bottom-up development was a process that was largely dependent on pre-existing resources, network and culture. The community sold loca products, learned from their peers and worked towards a common goal of economic development

Top-down support becomes increasingly instrumental as the grassroots development grows. From our case study, we unveil three limitations of bottom-up development that can impede the success, scale and sustainability of technology leapfrogging. Firstly, while the availability of an e-commerce platform affords a low barrier for entry, th ability for anyone to register an online store and deploy a compelling website overnight paints an unrealistically sim ple picture for the leapfrogging venture. Many of the grassroots-turned-entrepreneurs in Suichang soon faced chal lenges in sustaining the growth of their businesses. Living in a deprived environment with limited access to resources and expertise made sustaining the leapfrogging process particularly challenging.

Secondly, our case study also demonstrates that many grassroots endeavours were short-lived. For example most e-commerce businesses established in the early stages of the leapfrogging process in Suichang were based on peer sharing of knowledge and imitation. While the initial flourishing of these highly localised and consistent onlin businesses created a unique positioning for the rural e-commerce venture, this largely unmonitored developmen soon created intense competition that jeopardised the collective growth. Given that these businesses rely heavily on niche local produce and specialties, rural e-merchants also face challenges in expanding their businesses to a wide audience.

Finally, our case study also reveals how rapid adoption of e-commerce disrupts the local social fabric. While e-commerce adoption creates benefits in the forms of new jobs and improved economic performance, the leapfrog ging process also generates social disruption in the form of conflicts (eg, in competition for scarce resources, employ ment and business opportunities), reduced participation in other alternative economic options (eg, jobs not related to e-commerce) and out-migration. These disruptions reduce resilience at the community level and make efforts in sus taining the leapfrogging development more difficult.

## 4.2 | Charting a resilient and sustainable pathway for technology leapfrogging

Based on our case study, we derive three top-down interventions that serve as recommendations for decision makers, including local governments in underdeveloped regions, public sector practitioners and technology providers to better support a community-driven leapfrogging initiative. Firstly, provide support in the forms of upskillin opportunities and financial resources. Professional support in identifying skills gaps and offering tailor mad upskilling opportunities will help to instil further confidence amongst the community and nurture digital resilience Seed funding is also essential to promote initial uptake and reach critical mass, which is an important catalyst tha can stimulate further organic growth

Secondly, resources can be strategically allocated to establish an institutional structure to better govern th often less-coordinated community-driven development. A good example from our case study is Alibaba's ‘Uniquel China’ initiative that effectively brings together the isolated ventures of rural e-merchants and nurtures a collectiv effort to revitalise the agricultural industry. The initiative has empowered a more consolidated effort in bringing together local agricultural products to build a branding that is attractive to global consumers, resulting in a mor lucrative market where all businesses can flourish

Finally, as the development matures, decision makers should focus on cultivating an inclusive ecosystem. For top-down initiatives to be positively received and effectively integrated at the community level, building deep tie with grassroots-based initiatives is essential. Alibaba has demonstrated the impact of such an approach in our cas study. For example, instead of imposing a top-down, one-size-fits-all model, Alibaba strategically invests in grassroots-based initiatives that have proven to be operationalisable. The investment in the Ganjie platform is a goo example, demonstrating how top-down support can empower bottom-up initiatives to realise greater potential and achieve diffusion at a much larger scale. Nurturing grassroots-driven initiatives, instead of imposing standardise models, is an effective way for top-down interventions to overcome local inertia. Resistance is common when under served communities are challenged to leapfrog into unfamiliar domains that often seem far-fetched. Effective top down interventions should therefore serve as catalysts that stimulate more governed developments of grassroots ini tiatives, instead of being forceful implementations of change.

## 5 | CONCLUSION

E-commerce is big enough to involve everyone. If you see a farmer in the field, you might think he is not involved in e-commerce. But he is just not involved in the sales process. In fact, he is one of our producers. So this is how it includes everyone. (Pan, Chairman of the Suichang E-commerce Association)

The staggering growth of e-commerce in the rural regions of China has inspired many big e-commerc players to derive strategies and plans in the hopes of attracting more rural users. For example, JD.com, China's second largest e-commerce player, has shifted the company's strategic focus to smaller cities and villages sinc 2018, investing 20 billion RMB to launch new initiatives such as drone delivery to expand its logistics network, and offering localised services to gain a foothold in the rural e-commerce space (South China Morning Post, 2018). These growing initiatives signal how rural regions represent a lucrative new market opportunity fo technology companies looking to expand their consumer bases, especially as higher tier e-commerce market approach saturation.

Digital technology, such as e-commerce, can be a powerful enabler and equalizer, but driving sustainable chang through technology involves much more than offering technical solutions to people. This article presents a successfu example of how local communities in an underdeveloped region collaborated effectively with decision makers to lever age a technology for sustainable leapfrogging development. The rich descriptions and lessons learned presented in thi article can be used to guide future technology leapfrogging initiatives in underdeveloped regions. Specifically, by mak ing explicit the roles of both bottom-up and top-down initiatives in a technology leapfrogging process, this article first provides guidance for communities in underdeveloped regions to better understand how to leverage opportunitie afforded by digital technology, and work effectively as a collective as well as with decision makers to advance and sustain digitally enabled societal developments. Secondly, this article unveils the role of decision makers in supporting community-driven pursuits. Leaders such as the local government and technology providers can gain a better under standing on the requirements and vulnerabilities in a community-driven technology leapfrogging process, and mak more informed decisions in terms of resource allocation and strategy formulation to better support the endeavours.

Existing research has shown that many large-scale, top-down DSI investments have had limited success in making developmental impact (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019; L. Li, Du, Zhang, & Mao, 2019). A recent literature review on digitally enabled societal development suggests that engaging the target beneficiary communities and empowering them to have an active role in the development process can increase the success of DSI initiatives, but such inclusiv processes are often missing in more paternalistic DSI projects (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019). By unpacking the impor tant roles communities play in leveraging digital technology for social impact and how top-down support can b effectively implemented to support and sustain their engagement in the process, we hope to generate more research attention in understanding the critical success factors of DSI initiatives, especially around how bottom-up and top down efforts from multiple and diverse stakeholders can effectively complement each other to nurture DSI fo greater impact and sustainability (George, Howard-Grenville, Joshi, & Tihanyi, 2016; Liu & Xu, 2018). We also hop to inspire more leaders to engage grassroots communities in crafting DSI initiatives that could generate sustainabl societal benefits.

## APPENDIX: RESEARCH DESIGN

Since 2013, we have engaged in research into the emergence of e-commerce in rural China, conducting in-dept case studies at a large number of e-commerce villages and counties in several provinces, including Zhejiang (highlighted in this article), Shandong and Fujian. We selected the qualitative case study methodology (Pan & Tan, 2011; Walsham, 1995) to develop new insights for the understudied phenomenon (Bansal, Smith, & Vaara, 2018; Trauth & Jessup, 2000). As one of the first counties to venture into e-commerce in rural China Suichang county was selected as the representative case study for this article.

The primary data source for this case study was the onsite interviews we conducted with the different stakeholders involved in Suichang's e-commerce development (see Table 2 for a full list of interviewees). In addition to the interview data, we also collected a large number of secondary data sources prior to, during and after our onsite visit. Prior to our onsite visit, we collected publicly available data, including news arti cles, press releases and reports published by AliResearch to establish an initial understanding of the emergence of e-commerce in Suichang county. During our site visit, we requested documentation and presentations from our interviewees whenever possible and took notes and pictures to document our observations. After the site visit, we also attended several forums hosted by Alibaba to keep abreast of the latest developments in rural e-commerce in China. It was in these forums that representatives from Alibaba.com, Taobao.com, Taobao University, rural entrepreneurs, policy makers and researchers gathered to discuss the latest strategies and share progress on the adoption of e-commerce in rural China. Overall, since 2013, we have visited nine counties in four provinces, and interviewed various stakeholders who have played a part in the e-commerce journey of rural China—including government officials, community leaders, rural entrepreneurs, e-merchants and rural residents. This rich qualitative data have allowed us to develop a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon in general and helped tremendously in our analysis of individual cases (such as the case of Suichang presented in this article).

Our onsite interviews at Suichang were semi-structured and composed of non-leading, open-ended question (Myers & Newman, 2007). Questions were modified throughout the interview process in order to gather dat more relevant to the interviewees' role in the e-commerce adoption process. Additional questions were also cre ated throughout the process as we developed a deeper understanding of the phenomenon. To control for retro spective bias, we cross-checked information against publicly available materials or official documentation after the interviews.

In this practitioner paper, we describe the opportunities afforded by the adoption of e-commerce in underserved communities in rural China for technology leapfrogging, the limitations that arise from the process and importan lessons learned that can serve as recommendations for both rural communities and decision makers (ie, effective col laboration and interventions that can overcome the limitations and bring forth sustainable development).
