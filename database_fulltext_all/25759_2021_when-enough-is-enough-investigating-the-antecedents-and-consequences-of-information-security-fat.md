## Abstract

Despite concerns raised by practitioners, the potential downside of the information security demands imposed by organizations on their employees has received limited scholarly attention. Our research focuses on information security fatigue (hereafter security fatigue), which is defined as a socio-emotional state experienced by an individual who is tired of and disillusioned with security policies and their associated guidelines and procedures. This research delves into the security fatigue concept, investigates its antecedents and reports how fatigue affects employee security policy compliance (and non-compliance). Since security fatigue is not well articulated in the literature and there is limited understanding of its antecedents and consequences, we take a research approach that affords novel insight into this phenomenon. Specifically, we conduct 38 in-depth interviews with business and IT professionals, and then use a qualitative approach to construct a model, including seven research propositions, to highlight the key aspects of security fatigue. Our results indicate that four distinct antecedents contribute to security fatigue, which result in three unique consequences. We discuss security fatigue in relation to past theoretical views and related concepts within the security policy compliance literature and identify directions for future research.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

It's called ‘security fatigue’, and it exposes online users to risk and costs businesses money in lost customers. (Ritchey, 2018).

The continued frequency of information security incidents in today's organizations has established the ongoing need for managers to implement a robust collection of internal controls. In addition to technical measures such a access control, intrusion prevention, and malware detection, most companies recognize the importance of esta blishing formal security policies in order to specify both acceptable and unacceptable behaviours when using th organization's technology assets (Cram, Proudfoot, & D'Arcy, 2017). From an employee (end user)<sup>1</sup> perspective, a security policy is ‘a set of formalized procedures, guidelines, roles and responsibilities to which employees ar required to adhere to safeguard and use properly the information and technology resources of their organizations (Lowry & Moody, 2015, p. 434). Research indicates that these measures are at least somewhat effective in reducin a variety of security incidents, although there are many contingent factors (Cram, D'Arcy, & Proudfoot, 2019)

Commensurate with the rise of security policies in organizations, there are the increasing demands placed on employees to adhere to the specified guidelines and procedures (e.g., maintaining multiple and complex passwords, multifactor authentication procedures, attending periodic security training sessions, etc.). Employees are often put int the difficult position of trying to adhere to security policy requirements while at the same time trying to accomplish their primary job responsibilities in an efficient manner; these objectives can come into conflict, resulting in unintende consequences. Employees can become inundated with security policy requirements and the associated security-related communications. To this end, research suggests that organizations may be placing too much emphasis on securit (in the minds of employees), in terms of policy guidelines, procedures and ongoing training (D'Arcy, Herath, & Shoss, 2014; Posey, Bennett, Roberts, & Lowry, 2011; Stanton et al., 2016). A phenomenon known as security fatigue has emerged, as illustrated in the quotations at the opening of this article, which can lead users to risky computing behaviour at work and in their personal lives. In this paper, we focus on security fatigue in a work context

Originally identified in the practitioner literature (Furnell & Thomson, 2009), security fatigue is defined as socio-emotional state experienced by an individual who is tired and disillusioned with security policy requirements In a work context, employees who experience security fatigue and engage in non-compliant security behaviour fo this reason are distinct from those employees who consistently ignore or refuse to comply with security policies Rather, security-fatigued employees may have at one time been very inclined to comply with security policies, and may still be, but strict compliance is less likely for these employees due to their fatigued state (Furnell & Thomson, 2009). Simply put, these employees are weary of, and worn out by, the demands imposed by security pol icy requirements, which can have negative consequences for organizational information security efforts. Exploring security fatigue is particularly salient in light of evidence that a large percentage of security breaches can be traced to the behaviour of well-meaning employees, who for one reason or another failed to enact a secure behaviour in a particular instance (rather than those employees who more generally have malicious intent towards their organiza tion) (Posey, Roberts, Lowry, & Hightower, 2014). In this vein, an industry report found that non-malicious insiders were responsible for the loss of roughly two-thirds of the billions of records compromised over a 12-month period (IBM, 2018).

To more fully articulate the security fatigue concept, consider, for example, an employee who receives monthly email reminders outlining good security practices (e.g., avoiding phishing schemes, password hygiene). The employe may find the messages helpful in promoting security policy compliance, but if they are also required to complete regular security training, be on constant alert for IT department internal phishing attacks and change passwords ever 90 days, the employee may begin to feel worn out by security policy requirements and accompanying controls, to the point that their compliance declines.

Crucially, security fatigue has not been empirically validated as a distinct concept within the academic literature As such, the literature is also missing a comprehensive set of antecedents to, and outcomes of, security fatigue. Thi study therefore poses two research questions. First, why do employees become fatigued by security policy require ments? Second, how does this affect their security policy compliance?

In addressing these questions, this study seeks to contribute to the research and practice of information securit management and, particularly, the subject of behavioural compliance (Hui, Vance, & Zhdanov, 2016). By investigatin security fatigue along with a set of its antecedents and consequences, and linking these topics to security policy compliance, we provide a more nuanced understanding of the behavioural aspect of information security manage ment. For example, most prior research on security policy compliance considers this behaviour in a strictly dichoto mous sense – compliance or non-compliance. Our findings peel back the layers of security policy compliance and reveal ignorance of security policy requirements, security policy workarounds and minimization of security efforts as negative, compliance-related consequences of security fatigue. Further, despite the recognition that sanctions fo non-compliance and security training, education and awareness efforts can be beneficial in encouraging employe compliance with security policies, our findings suggest that these factors can also lead employees to experience security fatigue. Managers can use this information to help prevent, as well as reduce, instances of security fatigue in employees.

To conduct our research, we interviewed 38 business and IT professionals on their experiences with securit policy requirements in the workplace and their perspectives on security fatigue. Because of the lack of prior attention to security fatigue in the academic literature, we adopted a qualitative approach that granted us the flexibility to identify and refine the complex views of practitioners, yet allowed us to more rigorously evaluate and articulate th security fatigue construct as it relates to the information systems (IS) security literature. We analysed the patterns emerging from these extensive discussions and constructed a model that highlights a set of core antecedents and consequences of security fatigue. Based on our observations, we outline seven research propositions and discuss the concept of security fatigue within the context of the existing theoretical insights from the security policy compli ance literature. We conclude by identifying several future research directions.

## 2 | CONCEPTUAL FOUNDATIONS

Any employee belonging to an organization with a security policy has a degree of latitude in terms of whether to comply or violate the established guidelines. Some individuals will remain steadfastly compliant, irrespective of orga nizational or personal factors, while others, such as those with malicious intent, will regularly violate policies. Anothe group of individuals will have their compliance behaviour fluctuate over time, depending on a range of personal, contextual and organizational factors. Gaining increased clarity on those factors that are most influential in steering an employee away from compliance to violation behaviours is of particular importance to organizations as they seek to minimize damaging security incidents. In particular, practitioners have noted that security fatigue can pull even steadfastly compliant individual towards non-compliant behaviour. As such, it is important for an organization to b able to recognize those individual users which have the potential to be affected [by security fatigue] and implement preventative actions accordingly’ (Furnell & Thomson, 2009, p. 11).

Existing accounts in the practitioner literature, such as Stanton et al. (2016), have established the possible exis tence of security fatigue, including some of the associated symptoms. However, detailed insights into th antecedents and behavioural consequences of security fatigue have not yet been established. As well, these prelimi nary practitioner observations stop short of theorizing relationships among the elements of security fatigue. In th following section, we begin to address these shortcomings by introducing the concept of decision fatigue as a valu able conceptual foundation for security fatigue.

## 2.1 | Security fatigue as a type of decision fatigue

In order to better understand why employees may become fatigued by security requirements, we look at the plausi ble explanation that they are exposed to an excess of information regarding organizational security issues and that over time they become worn down from the continual reminders, training and guidelines. In order to explore thi viewpoint, we draw on past research on the topic of decision fatigue, which refers to the impairment of an individ ual's self-control following the need to make many deliberate and effortful choices (Baumeister, 2002a; Voh et al., 2008).

Although we are unaware of any decision fatigue studies within IS literature, extensive work has been con ducted in other disciplines, including consumer behaviour (e.g., Vohs et al., 2008), financial analysis (e.g., Hirshleifer, Levi, Lourie, & Teoh, 2019), law (Danziger, Levav, & Avnaim-Pesso, 2011), and healthcare (Allan et al., 2019; Linder et al., 2014; Persson, Barrafrem, Meunier, & Tinghög, 2019; Pignatiello, Martin, & Hickman Jr., 2020). For example judges making consecutive case decisions exhibit a continuously decreasing number of favourable decisions unti having a break (after which their rate of favourable decisions returns to roughly the same initial level as at the onset of the first decision-making session) (Danziger et al., 2011). Likewise, the quality of financial analysts' decisions is found to decrease as more decisions are made throughout the day (Hirshleifer et al., 2019), and similar results ar found concerning surgeons' decisions at the end of long shifts (Persson et al., 2019).

Other findings indicate that performance (e.g., quality of decisions/reasoning) is positively influenced by information exposure, but only up to a point, after which performance declines (Eppler & Mengis, 2004). Schroder, Driver, and Streufert (1967) represents this relationship between performance (i.e., decision accuracy) and information load as an inverted U-curve, with performance dropping off after reaching this overload threshold. Information overload has been found to have a number of negative outcomes, including ‘lack of perspective (Schick, Gorden, & Haka, 1990), cognitive strain and stress (Malhotra, 1982; Schick et al., 1990), a greater tolerance of error (Sparrow, 1999), lower job satisfaction (Jacoby, 1984), or the inability to use information to make a decisio (Bawden, 2001)—the so-called paralysis by analysis (Eppler & Mengis, 2004, p. 19).

The consequences of decision fatigue revolve around reduced self-control. Since the activity of weighing alter natives and making choices requires individual effort, the concept of decision fatigue suggests that an individual' ability to make continued choices is ‘a limited resource that resembles a form of strength or energy’ (Vohs et al., 2008, p. 884). Past research from psychology explains these types of decision-making decrements by counting them as by-products of decreasing self-control, described as the ability to change or overpower our more viscera reactions to the stimuli around us (e.g., thoughts, emotions and actions) (Baumeister, 2002a, 2002b; Baumeister, Gailliot, DeWall, & Oaten, 2006). Baumeister (2002a) explains that self-control operates on the basis of a limited resource, akin to energy or strength, that can become depleted through use. The depleted self is then less able to carry out further acts of self-control. The resource is used not only in self-control, but also in other acts of volition and indeed it may be the basis for the self's entire executive function’ (p. 130).

In conceptualizing security fatigue, we draw on the characteristics of decision fatigue in terms of the relationshi between having to make many decisions and a decreasing level of self-control associated with declining decision quality. Just as past research has found such links within the fields of financial analysis or medicine, we apply this phenomenon within a security context. In doing so, we view security fatigue as a particular type of decision fatigue As such, increasing numbers of decisions are posed to end users, such as when or whether to attend security train ing, reuse passwords or encrypt email. While such actions are typically mandated as part of security policies, research indicates that the extent to which employees follow such mandates is to some degree discretionary and varies widely (Boss, Kirsch, Angermeier, Shingler, & Boss, 2009). Hence, individual decision-making comes into play. Mem bers of an organization may therefore encounter reduced self-control regarding their willingness and intention to comply with security policy requirements as the number of security decisions they have to make increases. ## 3 | RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

This study adopts a qualitative approach that utilizes selected principles associated with grounded theory. Grounded theory refers to ‘an inductive theory discovery methodology that allows the researcher to develop a theoretica account of the general features of a topic while simultaneously grounding the account in empirical observations o data (Martin & Turner, 1986, p. 141). We adopted this approach because of two key factors. First, although ther was a lack of explicit attention in the academic literature to security fatigue, practitioner articles had previously mad reference to the concept at a high level (e.g., Furnell & Thomson, 2009; Stanton et al., 2016). Because of this existing recognition of the concept within the practitioner community, we believed that an in-depth, qualitative examination of the perspectives of selected practitioners could serve as a critical first step in defining exactly what securit fatigue entails and aiding in the construction of a theoretical model. Second, our initial view of the concept of secu rity fatigue was that it was relatively complex, in part because it needed to be distinguished from existing concepts (e.g., decision fatigue), but also because the concept appeared to have several possible interpretations (e.g., who i being fatigued, what different scenarios could be fatiguing, how fatigue might impact people differently). Past com mentators have argued that a qualitative approach can be appropriate for concept and theory development in com plex scenarios, while also being systematic and able to generate theory that is of interest to practitioners based on empirical phenomena (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Jones & Noble, 2007; Myers, 2009; Patton, 2002), which is consistent with the nature of this study.

We adopted selected features of the approach advocated by Strauss and Corbin (1990) and Corbin an Strauss (2008). For instance, the research does not begin with an a priori theory that is intended to be tested; rather, the research begins with an area of interest where the relevant concepts are allowed to inductively emerge from th empirical data. Although some proponents of grounded theory seek to avoid any pre-conceived notions or theoreti cal foundations, we followed the more flexible approach supported by Strauss and Corbin (1990) and Myers (2009) which allows for an awareness of the relevant literature, but not to the extent that it constrains creativity and ide generation.

## 3.1 | Data collection

We conceptualize security fatigue at an individual level of analysis, since each employee differs in his/her experience skills and understanding of security policy requirements. To gain an information-rich perspective on security fatigue, we adopted an interview-based data collection approach with purposeful sampling (Patton, 2002). In particular, w selected interviewees of varying ranks within their respective organizations and across different industries. We took this comprehensive data collection approach because the concept of security fatigue is not yet well articulated and it was important to get a range of different opinions and viewpoints on the topic.

A total of 38 interviews were conducted between November 2016 and November 2017. Eight of the interviews were initiated through personal contacts of the authors, while the remainder were facilitated through requests for participation that were sent via email to members of the local chapters of two professional organi zations that specialize in IT issues: ISACA and the International Information System Security Certification Con sortium. These organizations were asked to participate because their members are generally familiar with technology issues. Participants who were recruited via the two professional organizations were each compensated with a gift certificate to an online retailer. The interviewees were professionals from business departments (19 of 38 interviewees met this criterion) and IT departments (19 of 38 interviewees met this criterion), including end users and IT practitioners from across the senior (15 of 38), middle (13 of 38) and junior (10 of 38) ranks, as well as those from a variety of industries (participants came from a total of eight industries, including healthcare, financial services, education, technology, and professional services²). We included both end users and IT practitioners, as opposed to solely end users, because IT practitioners are not only required to adhere to security policies themselves but also are responsible for the implementation and day-to-day enforcement of security policy requirements. Thus, IT practitioners are well informed of the end-user experience and have an accumulated body of knowledge in this regard. Capturing the combined perspectives of end users, IT practitioners and those at the management level facilitates a more accurate assessment of the potential link ages between security fatigue and aspects of security policy compliance; this is because end users can be biased when it comes to relaying compliance/non-compliance behaviours due to social desirability concerns (Feng, Zhu, Wang, & Liang, 2019).

A total of 32 different companies are represented across the interviewee group. Although we recognize that interviewing only a specific group of employees (e.g., IT staff in the financial services industry) or end users from single organization could have provided us with a more focused assessment of security fatigue, the nature of ou research questions was better suited to the wide-ranging approach that we adopted.

The average interview length was 44 min and ranged from 18 to 70 min. All interviews except one were digitall recorded and transcribed (for the non-recorded interview, the interviewee cited privacy concerns, but permitted th authors to take detailed notes during the interview). A total of 616 transcribed pages were generated from the inter views (refer to Appendix A for a detailed listing of interviewees).

The interviews followed a semi-structured approach (refer to Appendix B for the outline of questions used to guide each interview), which was focused around the interviewee's perceptions of the concept of security fatigue including its antecedents and consequences. Although we used a set protocol to guide the broad direction of each interview, the discussions were adapted to account for the particular experiences or opinions of the interviewee. We periodically revisited the interview protocol throughout the data collection process and made a series of mino tweaks to account for new areas of inquiry that had been identified through participant interviews or during the coding. Of the 38 interviews, 16 were facilitated by the first author, 15 were facilitated by the second author and 7 wer co-facilitated by both the first and second authors.

## 3.2 | Data analysis

The interview transcripts were analysed with NVivo, beginning with open coding, which refers to analysing text and summarizing its properties and dimensions using a succinct, descriptive code (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Myers, 2009 Strauss & Corbin, 1990). When open coding, Bohm (2004) suggests asking questions such as ‘What is at issue?’ ‘What actors are involved?’ and ‘How is the phenomenon being addressed?’ During the open coding process, w iteratively compared and refined the coding labels as new data were reviewed. Refer to the following section, as wel as Appendix C, for details on the coding results and examples of coded interviewee comments

While conducting the open coding, we followed Corbin and Strauss's (2008) suggestion to simultaneously engage in analysis that relates the identified concepts and categories to each other. For example, as we began our analysis, several coding categories emerged in relation to concerns that were raised by interviewees regarding th communication of security policy requirements and related activities that take place within the organization, such as awareness campaigns or training. We coded the concepts independently, such as ‘policy length’ and ‘policy clarity’, but as we continued our analysis, we began to recognize a pattern that was being highlighted by interviewees in regard to the overall method their organizations used to communicate security policy requirements, which also included related concepts such as ‘communication medium’ and ‘communication format’. As a result, we followe the technique used by Boldosova (2019), Corley and Gioia (2004), and Wessel et al. (2019) by grouping the lower level, first-order concepts within a broader, second-order sub-category, in this case pertaining to the ‘method of communicating security policy requirements’. These sub-categories were then combined to form aggregate catego ries. We note that the ‘Fatigue Symptoms’ category consists of only first-order constructs, but no sub-categories Although previous guidance (e.g., Strauss & Corbin, 1990) suggests that this activity, referred to as axial coding, is stand-alone activity, Corbin and Strauss (2008) argue that it is more appropriate to conduct alongside open coding Refer to Figure 1 for details on the connections between the first-order constructs, coding subcategories and aggre gate coding categories.

Finally, the identified codes were analysed to generate predictive statements about the phenomena under inves tigation using causal and correlational linkages (Myers, 2009; Urquhart, 2013). In particular, we considered the tem poral aspects of security fatigue (e.g., what activities did interviewees tell us occurred first, and how did the describe responding to such events?) in order to identify patterns associated with the micro and macro condition present when security fatigue takes place, as well as the nature of employees' responses to security fatigue (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). For example, throughout our coding process we worked to clarify the extent that the second-order themes/sub-categories (e.g., method of communicating security policy requirements or security pol icy workarounds’) were connected with either causes of security fatigue or aftereffects of security fatigue (refer to Figure 1 for details). Further, we sought to corroborate if connections between the sub-categories and security fatigue that were raised by end users were echoed by IT practitioners (and vice versa). For example, where a pattern began to emerge from end-user interviews that suggested a potential link between the quantity of security commu nications and fatigue, we re-examined the IT practitioner data to see if those interviewees had also noted a similar pattern with end users. Where both end users and IT practitioners were consistent in their views, the connection between the sub-categories and security fatigue were viewed as being more robust. We then used these groupings as a basis for our research propositions (included later in the Discussion, Contributions, and Future Research sec tions), which focus on the antecedents and consequences of security fatigue. Although formal propositions are onl occasionally used within qualitative studies, their inclusion can make the work increasingly useful to other scholars including bridging the gap with quantitative researchers (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2012)

Throughout the coding process, the authors met regularly to discuss and refine the coding categories, based on new insights that were identified in the interviews. The second author reviewed a sample of six interviews (approxi mately 15% of the completed interviews) to verify the reliability of coding. This sample is consistent with past research that similarly evaluates the reliability of coding, such as with Webster (1998), who reviewed 10% of th transcribed data. A range of criteria can be used to assess the quality of qualitative research. Myers (2009) suggests that studie such as ours should be evaluated in terms of the rigour and validity of the analysis, as demonstrated by clear linkages in multiple instances between the findings and the data, as well as a demonstrated familiarity with the subject area Each of the coding categories identified in our analysis included comments from a minimum of 4 interviewees and an average of 17 interviewees. In our study, we sought to fulfil this criterio through the inclusion of formal theoretical propositions. Other commentators, such as Sikolia, Mason, Biros, an Weiser (2013), highlight the importance of conducting coding reliability checks and achieving theoretical saturatio in achieving trustworthy findings, which we also complete in our study, as explained above.

## 4 | RESULTS AND ANALYSIS

Three broad category groupings emerged from the data that inform our research questions. The first grouping concerned the characteristics of the security fatigue concept, including the types of emotions experienced by inter viewees (e.g., frustration). The second grouping focused on the specific antecedents of security fatigue, including th quantity of security policy communications and activities and the inconvenience of security policy requirements. Finally, the third grouping explored the consequences of security fatigue, including employees who ignored security policy requirements, adopted security policy workarounds and minimized their security efforts. We provide more details, including representative interviewee quotes, for each of the category groups in the section below.

## 4.1 | Understanding security fatigue

A total of 348 interviewee comments were coded to categories associated with the characteristics of security fatigue (refer to Table 2). In general, there was an overwhelming agreement across the interviewees that security fatigue was a valid and relevant phenomenon.

Consistent patterns emerged from the data regarding the symptoms of security fatigue (refer to Figure 1 fo additional details). In terms of the frustration symptom, interviewees noted that the security policy requirements they needed to fulfil were tedious and prevented them from doing their work. In addition, some interviewees discussed the areas of security where they saw fatigue as being particularly promi nent. Although we were told about a range of fatigue-inducing incidents, the following quotes highlight instance that include password management, website access and the use of network tools:

I certainly think there is fatigue around passwords…. People know enough that if it is a policy or not, you are technically forced to create a complex password and you have to change it twice a year. (CISO, Education)

I think the big part about what I would consider fatigue is the fact that people are aware that they are being restricted…all of these restrictions with your device or what you can do on your laptop or restrictions on what sites you can visit on your work PC and passwords. (Senior Manager, Professional Services)

Let's take that end-point solution. It requires a user to click and say yes or no or allow it or not. And see this on my own. I will get all these warnings that are coming out from the solution that I am using on my own computer and I don't know which ones are good or not and I am a security guy. So an end-point user will continue to click yes, yes, yes. (CEO, Technology)

Overall, the reality of security fatigue was reinforced in our interviews and was most commonly associated with feel ings of frustration, tiredness and hopelessness. It is important to note that the granular insights about securit fatigue, in terms of it being comprised of a comprehensive set of emotional states as reported to us by interviewees have yet to be articulated in the relevant literature. In this manner, our results provide fresh insight into the securit fatigue phenomenon as compared to the prior high-level practitioner accounts that mainly introduce it as a new phe nomenon. ## 4.2 | Security fatigue antecedents

A total of 218 interviewee comments were coded to categories that interviewees associated with the root causes of security fatigue, leading to four distinct antecedent categories that emerged from the data. Some instances of inconvenience were associated with lengthy security-related procedures that needed to be followed, while others were connected to relatively straightforward checks that needed to be performed but that disrupted the speed at which a process could otherwise be performed. However, based on our analysis, we find that the data support the notion of security fatigue standing as a distinct phenomenon that is activate by an individual employee's judgements of the security policy requirements that are in place, which contributes to feelings of frustration, tiredness and hopelessness

Specifically, we identified that decision-fatigued individuals will have issues with self-control and/or will expe rience reduced decision-making ability due to an inundation of information. ## 5 | DISCUSSION, CONTRIBUTIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

Our results indicate that four distinct antecedents contribute to security fatigue (inconvenience of security polic requirements, perceived legitimacy of security policy requirements, quantity of security policy communications and activities and method of communicating security policy requirements). In addition to identifying these antecedents to security fatigue and the consequences that follow, to our knowledge, our results are the first in the academic liter ature to articulate the security fatigue construct and provide evidence of a resultant decline in security polic compliance behaviour. This has been demonstrated through the statements made by organizational members at a variety of levels and in a variety of industries. Based on our analysis, this section outlines four research proposition for the relationship between the antecedents of security fatigue and fatigue itself, as well as three research proposi tions for the relationship between security fatigue and fatigue consequences. Following each set of propositions, w highlight how our findings compare with the existing IS security literature and why these new insights are important

## 5.1 | Research implications: Security fatigue antecedents

Based on the results presented in the preceding section pertaining to the antecedents of security fatigue, we propose the following:

Proposition 1: When employees perceive security policy requirements to be inconvenient, they will increasingly expe rience security fatigue.

Proposition 2: When employees perceive security policy requirements to be illegitimate, they will increasingly experi ence security fatigue.

Proposition 3: When employees perceive security policy requirements to be excessive in quantity, they will increas ingly experience security fatigue.

Proposition 4: When employees perceive security policy requirements to be conveyed with poorly chosen communi cation methods, they will increasingly experience security fatigue

The four antecedent categories we have uncovered and specified in Propositions 1–4 contribute to the limited research on the potential pitfalls of information security initiatives from the end-user's perspective. Our perceived legitimacy of security requirements and method of communicating security policy requirements categories are unique contributions in terms of drivers of security policy compliance. We note, however, that prior IS security research ha considered similar factors to our other antecedent categories. For instance, one might consider our inconvenience of security policy requirements and quantity of security policy communications and activities categories as similar to prior research that considers burdensome, excessive and constantly changing security policy requirements as stress indica tors that contribute to security policy noncompliance (e.g., D'Arcy et al., 2014; Hwang & Cha, 2018; Lee, Lee, & Kim, 2016). While acknowledging the overlap, we submit that there is a subtle and important distinction between ours and past work.

Specifically, prior work focuses on stress due to security policy requirements, which is apparent in the survey based measures used to capture the concepts (e.g., ‘I often find it too complex for me to understand and use new information security technologies Hwang & Cha, 2018). By contrast, and based on our qualitative approach, whic afforded deep insight into the security fatigue phenomenon, we found that employees did not necessarily experience stress-like symptoms from the (perceived) inconvenient and high quantity of security communications they experi enced. This is evident in the following interviewee's response, which we provided earlier: ‘I also get a lot of emails of the phishing attacks from [my employer] and my first inclination is to just click and delete. I think we get way too much information and because of that I have a large habit to just delete any email that I get’. In this and several othe responses, employees seemed weary and worn out by security policy requirements and reminders, as opposed to stressed, which is consistent with the negative effects of having to deal with too much information and too man choices, both of which result in decision fatigue. Thus, an important outcome of our study is the evidence that secu rity policy requirements do not always have to meet the threshold of ‘stress’ in order to have a negative influence on employees' security policy compliance

Past research investigating why end users comply with security policies has established a range of influentia antecedents, including attitude, self-efficacy and security education (Cram et al., 2019). Although we do not dispute the general importance of these constructs, our findings suggest that, as end users undergo security fatigue, the may experience changes in these key factors. For example, an end user with growing security fatigue may encounte a diminished security attitude (i.e., the extent that they view compliance as important) and lower self-efficac (i.e., confidence in their ability to perform in a compliant manner), while simultaneously being exposed to an increas ing quantity of security education activities. Since a large proportion of security policy compliance research to dat has been conducted using cross-sectional surveys (Cram et al., 2019), our findings suggest additional insights tha could be uncovered through longitudinal surveys that investigate changes in the factors associated with security pol icy compliance.

Another unique aspect of our work is that we consider the downstream impact of the antecedent categorie through a new intervening mechanism—security fatigue. Prior work has considered other intervening mechanisms such as security-related attitudes and beliefs, which link perceived negative aspects of security policy requirements to compliance intention or behaviour. This extant work suggests that the perceived negative aspects of security pol icy requirements (e.g., work impediment; Bulgurcu et al., 2010) lead to negative attitudes or beliefs related to secu rity, and thereby security policy violations. Our work is different in that we cast security fatigue as a socio-emotiona state made up of feelings of frustration, tiredness and hopelessness; it is not necessarily indicative of negative atti tudes or beliefs about security policy requirements

## 5.2 | Research implications: Security fatigue consequences

Our results also identified three unique consequences that stem from employees experiencing security fatigue Based on the results presented in the preceding section, we propose the following:

Proposition 5: Employees experiencing security fatigue will have a higher propensity to ignore security policy requirements.

Proposition 6: Employees experiencing security fatigue will have a higher propensity to utilize security policy workarounds.

Proposition 7: Employees experiencing security fatigue will have a higher propensity to minimize their level of security effort.

By contributing insights on these fatigue consequences in Propositions 5–7, our study provides novelty an nuance to the current understanding of security policy compliance. As noted, the extensive corpus of literature investigating security policy compliance tends to treat it as a dichotomous outcome (people comply with a policy or they do not). Our research introduces three behavioural consequences (ignore security policy requirements, securit policy workarounds and minimizing security effort) that articulate a variety of non-compliant behaviours that can occur as a result of security fatigue. While some similar behavioural responses to security policy requirements have been introduced in the practitioner literature (Furnell & Thomson, 2009; Stanton et al., 2016), our study extends thi work with a theoretical account of how such non-compliant behaviour is actually manifested in organizations. Dis tinct from the bulk of extant security policy compliance literature, our results suggest that compliance behaviour i not an ‘all or nothing’ proposition for employees. Furthermore, these behavioural consequences stemming from security fatigue can be thought of as employees' decreasing self-control in terms of their ability to comply with secu rity policies as they should. This pattern is consistent with the more general decision-fatigue phenomenon in whic our security fatigue construct is grounded.

Accordingly, our findings provide fresh insight into why seemingly well-meaning employees who may value their organization's information security efforts may still engage in security policy non-compliance. Furthermore, as secu rity fatigue is conceptualized in terms of three emotional states, our work contributes to the emerging research that considers emotions as important drivers of security policy compliance. As opposed to prior IS security studies on thi subject, which focused on emotions such as fear (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010), anxiety (Burns, Roberts, Posey, & Lowry, 2019) and reactance (Lowry & Moody, 2015), our identification of frustration, tiredness and hopelessness (as per the conceptualization of security fatigue) offers a fresh set of emotional states that are relevant to securit policy compliance, and so furthers IS security research on the non-rational drivers (i.e., not pure cognitive delibera tion) of this employee behaviour.

We note that the bulk of security policy compliance literature is based on the reconstruction and re-evaluation of similar research models that leverage a common core of theories (e.g., general deterrence theory, protection moti vation theory, theory of planned behaviour) (Cram et al., 2017). Our work provides a new construct, namely securit fatigue, which is unique to these theory bases and thus can help to account for some of the unexplained variance i these research models. Our conceptualization of security fatigue also provides an impetus for leveraging new theory bases for the study of security policy compliance, such as those incorporating emotions and other affective concept (e.g., moods) that are not predicated on a purely rational view of human behaviour.

Our work also makes an important contribution in terms of methodological diversity within the problem space of security policy compliance. A profound majority of this literature has used an empirical survey approach and onl a handful of studies have conducted interviews (Cram et al., 2017). Accordingly, our results help to provide an under used but valuable qualitative lens through which secure behaviour (or the lack thereof) in organizations can be bette understood. For example, in our discussion above, we noted that managers did not seem to consider security policy workarounds to be a serious offense, because these employee behaviours were not viewed as malicious. In this man ner, our qualitative approach provides additional contextual understanding of why security policy workarounds occur, which is also aided by the combined perspectives of the end user/IT practitioner that we used for this study

The results of our study also complement the IS security research that examines the effect of security warnings (also referred to as fear appeals) on end-user behaviour. A key study on fear appeals reports that these warnings do promote security policy compliance, but that this effect is not systematic and it is influenced by individual factors, including self-efficacy, threat severity, response efficacy and social influence (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010). Additionally, this area of inquiry has found that poor timing of security messages can lead to a tendency for end users to disregard those messages during times of high dual-task interference, defined as a limitation of the human cognitive system in which the human brain must rapidly switch attention between multiple tasks that are being attempted at the same time’ (Jenkins, Anderson, Vance, Kirwan, & Eargle, 2016, p. 1). Within our model, this could be interpreted as being consistent with the security fatigue antecedents of inconvenient security policy requirements, the quantit of security policy communications and activities and the method of communicating security policy requirements Furthermore, repeated exposure to security warnings can rapidly result in end users' habituation, and ultimate disre gard, of those precautions (Anderson, Jenkins, Vance, Kirwan, & Eargle, 2016), which is in line with our view on employees who ignore security policy requirements due to the effects of security fatigue

Finally, our findings are consistent with past research outside of IS that suggest links between repeated deci sions and declining self-control (e.g., Hirshleifer et al., 2019; Persson et al., 2019; Vohs et al., 2008). We recogniz that most security policies are established in an authoritative manner (i.e., without consultation by end users), but past research such as Moller, Deci, and Ryan (2006) find that increasing the autonomy of the choices made by indi viduals serves to counteract the depletion of self-control. Although past research recognizes that too many choices can limit the motivation of individuals (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000), we recognize the possibility that security fatigu could be reduced by offering at least some autonomy to end users in regard to their security-related behaviours Exploration of this issue is a topic for future research.

## 5.3 | Practical implications

From a practitioner perspective, it is a common perception that there is a positive linear relationship between efforts conducted to promote security policy compliance and observed security policy compliance in an organization. At th most basic level, our results suggest that managers should be aware that in many cases less is more , in that securit policy requirements should not focus on sheer volume but rather take a more targeted and nuanced approach. Spe cifically, as security policy requirements are increasingly perceived as inconvenient and illegitimate, it may contribut to more security policy violations rather than less. Furthermore, by frequently communicating security policy require ments using techniques that conflict with end-user needs (e.g., lengthy messages, using technical jargon), fatigue i likely to be amplified.

Accordingly, practitioners can benefit from drawing on our model and propositions to craft security polic requirements that minimize security fatigue as much as possible by fine-tuning fatigue antecedents to appropriate levels for the organization and/or end user. For example, we found that the most commonly noted security fatigue antecedents were those associated with inconvenient security policy requirements and the quantity of security pol icy communications and activities. Organizations wishing to address security fatigue could start with these two con cerns by making security policy requirements less arduous to adhere to and sending fewer security-related messages to staff. In addition, barraging high-level managers and executives with security reminders (high quantity of securit communication) via email (weak method of communicating) from low-level IT practitioners (low level of perceived legitimacy) will likely result in feelings of being tired and disillusioned with security policy requirements. A bette approach for this type of audience might be holding a semi-regular meeting with high-ranking IT/security managers or executives in which salient threats to the organization are identified and the need for corresponding security mea sures are presented. On the other hand, rank-and-file employees might be best suited to receive more regular (yet still conservative) reminders that clearly state the justification and legitimacy of various security policy requirements to mitigate any lingering resentment due to inconvenience. In short, each organization must determine the point at which security policy requirements begin to decline in effectiveness and start contributing to fatigue, and do so at a granular level that accounts for inter-organizational variations between geographic locations, departments and roles

## 5.4 | Future research directions

Several promising research directions stem from this research. One is the extension of this study by developing an instrument to quantitatively measure security fatigue. Such a tool could allow for the completion of a more broad based investigation of security fatigue employing surveys and/or experiments that could be used to further validate our findings. After the development of this measurement instrument, the research propositions outlined above coul be tested on an expanded population. In particular, the use of a longitudinal approach could capture the evolving nature of security fatigue over time. Additionally, such a tool could be used in a practical setting, in which securit practitioners could attempt to identify levels of security fatigue within their own organizations and thereby execute more targeted information security initiatives (e.g., work to rejuvenate fatigued individuals without adjusting securit policy requirements for individuals who are not yet exhibiting fatigue indicators at that time). Furthermore, by regu larly evaluating the security fatigue of a sample of employees within their organizations, security practitioners could have an evolving and updated picture of fatigue at any point in time. Such an effort could also be conducted covertl (e.g., analyzing keystrokes Jenkins, Grimes, Proudfoot, & Lowry, 2014) or mouse cursor behaviours Jenkins Proudfoot, Valacich, Grimes, & Nunamaker Jr, 2019)) in an effort to avoid contributing to security fatigue

Another research direction could examine potential techniques undertaken by managers to minimize the existence of the antecedent conditions we uncovered, as well as activities that organizations could engage in to counter act the existence of security fatigue in order to minimize its negative consequences. For example, we identified the quantity of communications about security policy requirements and a lack of perceived legitimacy for such require ments as antecedent factors in our research model, which can lead to security fatigue. We also referenced genera information-overload literature which has found that there exists a threshold at which additional information serve as a detriment to decision-making performance (i.e., information overload Eppler & Mengis, 2004; Schrode et al., 1967)). Future research can seek to illuminate the existence, and position, of such a threshold with respect to the antecedent categories in our model to thereby help practitioners fine-tune the frequency with which securit information is disseminated in an effort to avoid security fatigue.

Finally, further research could explore how security fatigue is manifested in different types of industries and at different levels within an organization. We earlier presented an example of how different strategies for conveying the importance of security policy requirements should be used depending on the targeted audience. Furthermore, the tolerance for security policy requirements and related communications and activities that lead to fatigue will var between different industries. For example, the inconvenience of a password policy requiring extremely long passwords that need to be changed regularly might be more tolerable at a defense contractor compared to a non-profit organization. In short, deciphering the perfect recipe of security policy requirements, and balancing the impact of th security fatigue antecedents we have identified, should be calibrated to fit the nature and needs of organization and the varying roles of employees within that organization. Illuminating the variations of our model and how it applies to different contexts is a rich area for future research.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

As with any research, this study has limitations. We recognize that although the antecedents and consequences identified in our model emerged from participant interviews, additional constructs may exist related to securit fatigue that have not yet been uncovered. Similarly, we note that as an individual-level phenomenon, security fatigu has the potential to affect each employee differently. The existence of the identified antecedents may not alway lead to security fatigue, and the existence of fatigue may not always lead to the identified consequences.

This research sought to understand the concept of security fatigue through in-depth discussions with 38 busi ness and IT professionals. The results of the study serve to establish an initial conceptualization of the factors that lead to security fatigue, as well as the compliance-related consequences of employees who experience fatigue. For organizations, our study can aid in increasing awareness for the position that increasing the quantity and intensity of security policy requirements may not always contribute to increased employee compliance. Rather, managers should carefully consider when enough is enough; that is, finding the point that employees are aware and engaged in secu rity policy initiatives but have not yet reached the point of becoming fatigued
