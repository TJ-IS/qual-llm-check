# A possible conceptualization of the information systems (IS) artifact: A general systems theory perspective<sup>1</sup>

## Abstract

This opinion paper addresses and contributes to the discourse on whether there is a central artifact that captures the essence of the information systems (IS) discipline. It argues that the IS discipline can, and should be, faithfully captured by an IS artifact. We offer a theoretical conception of the IS artifact by drawing upon General Systems Theory (GST). Key concepts of GST are distilled as meta-principles which inform our formulation of the IS artifact. We use the meta-principles of the IS artifact to develop salient assertions that theorize what an ‘IS’ is. To demonstrate the appropriateness of our conception, we illustrate how the assertions we developed are consistent with patterns related to emerging topics in IS research, notably, healthcare and IT, and Fintech. We formulate a research agenda on these emerging topics—based upon the conceptions developed in the paper—to guide future research. We conclude with the contributions and implications of our study, including the relevance for IT-enabled work in the era of the COVID-19 pandemic.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION AND OBJECTIVE

## 1.1 | The debate on the IT artifact

In the information systems (IS) discipline, the notion of the IT artifact continues to provoke passionate debat (Alter, 2015; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Cheikh-Ammar, 2018; Galliers, 2003; Iivari, 2017; Lee, Thomas, & Baskerville, 2015; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). The IT artifact has been broadly defined as bundles of material and cultural properties packaged in some socially recognizable form such as hardware and/or software’ (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001, p. 121). It has been argued that the notion of the IT artifact could guide IS research, define its bound aries, and promote new research perspectives and methods (Liu, Li, Goncalves, Kostakos, & Xiao, 2016). The salienc of the IT artifact to IS research can also be gauged by the often-asked question when authors submit their work to IS journals: Where is the IT artifact [in your research] (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015, p. 275). The primary reason for ask ing this question (to authors) is to ensure that IS research maintains its distinctiveness and that submitted papers ar not ‘in fact psychology, social science, or computer science papers masquerading as IS papers’ and to ensure that the IS aspect is not tangentially or superficially addressed (Lowry, Willison, & Dinev, 2017, p. 547)

Given its importance, there are extensive debates in the IS discipline on the usefulness, the nature, and—on a related note—the centrality of the IT artifact. Regarding its usefulness, IS scholars have argued that the IT artifact rep resents the essence of IS (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003). It has been observed that ‘the core research domain of IS schol arship includes the IT artifact and its immediate nomological net’ (Balijepally, Mangalaraj, & Iyengar, 2011, p. 376) and an ‘effort to articulate the general characteristics of IT artifacts is worthwhile because it can contribute to devel oping and enriching IS-centered paradigms (Cheikh-Ammar, 2018, p. 291). This viewpoint, in favour of the IT arti fact, generally argues that ‘most IS research centers around IT artifacts and their design, use, and consequences (Rai, 2017, p. iv). However, there have also been observations that the IT artifact has been so variedly defined that it has lost much of its usefulness (Alter, 2015); consequently there have been calls for disbanding it (Alter, 2015; Nunamaker & Briggs, 2011). For example, Nunamaker and Briggs (2011) observe that

… The term IT artifact may no longer be useful because its meaning has been diluted. It has been interpreted and reinterpreted by researchers, authors, reviewers, and editors until it has become so broad that it adds no value… (p. 8, emphasis added in bold).

Regarding the nature of IT artifact, some scholars believe that the IT artifact implies only technical artifacts (too view), others believe that it includes social artifacts interacting with such technical artifacts (ensemble view), whil still others maintain that it is sufficient to have the IT artifact nominally in the background (nominal view) (Akhlaghpour, Wu, Lapointe, & Pinsonneault, 2013; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Seidel, Müller-Wienbergen, & Becker, 2010). The varying conceptions of the IT artifact (please see Appendix, Table A1) have often caused differences of opinions among researchers. To some scholars, a clear notion of the IT artifact is essential (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001), while others doubt the value of such definitions and argue that it enforces rigid thinking that constricts creativity and the intellectual development of the IS field (Robey, 2003). The diverse views on the nature of the IT artifact create much confusion among IS academics (Matook & Brown, 2017), leadin to inconsistent standards of judging IS research depending on the reviewers' vision of the IT artifact. This leads to reviewers often rejecting sound and useful research just because the research seems unable to demonstrate consis tency with any particular view of the IT artifact (Nunamaker & Briggs, 2011).

Regarding the centrality of the IT artifact, some scholars doubt the value and appropriateness of the IT artifact (Lyytinen & King, 2004) considering the fast-changing landscapes in technology and business, and the arrival of new and unique problems (Agarwal, 2016). They argue that today's technologies are often embedded and unobservable (Agarwal & Tiwana, 2015), leading to difficulties in clearly locating the IT artifact. Indeed, prominent scholars embracing the tradition of sociomateriality have argued that it often becomes increasingly difficult to identify technologie separately from people who use them (Orlikowski, 2010; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008), thus rendering the IT artifact as a moot concept in need of updating.

## 1.2 | Our position on the need for a central artifact

From this ongoing debate, it is clear that the notion of the IT artifact is contentious, fuzzy, unsettled, and changing (Matook & Brown, 2017). Consequently, it has been argued that there is a need for scholarly effort to ground and conceptualize the IT artifact (Cheikh-Ammar, 2018). It seems that ‘there remains a fundamental misunderstanding of what the IT artifact is and of its utility in IS research’, and ‘this misunderstanding may impede the progress of the entire IS field’ (Lowry et al., 2017, p. 547). Crucially, it has been observed that ‘little progress has been made in term of the conceptualization of the IT artifact’ (Akhlaghpour et al., 2013, p. 157).

Against this background, we contend that the time is ripe to consider the conception of an artifact that could serve as a central artifact for the IS discipline. Importantly, we side with the scholars who recognize the value of a central artifact that can capture some of the unique and defining aspects of research and practice within the IS disci pline. Accordingly, we present our opinion of what that central artifact could be, as well as elaborate upon some of its salient aspects. Ultimately, defining this artifact helps us articulate our view of the nature of an information sys tem, a fundamental endeavour in capturing the essence of IS research and practice

We hope that our view on the nature of the central artifact and its usefulness to define the nature of an IS wil provide guidance on engaging with research on information systems (IS). Such clarifications will also help authors, editors, and reviewers decide whether a phenomenon under investigation is an IS one and hence within the remi of the IS field—hopefully imparting more clarity, systematicity, and fairness in how manuscripts submitted to IS journals are evaluated.

We contend that there is value to instituting a central artifact that captures the unique disciplinary space of IS and there is an inherent danger of not doing so. Not having a central artifact for the IS discipline inhibits ‘our abilit to differentiate our research space from that of the reference disciplines’ (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015, p. 283). In contrast, having a central artifact that uniquely captures the IS discipline allows us to investigate phenomena consistent with an IS-centric paradigm (Cheikh-Ammar, 2018; Weber, 1987). It also allows IS researchers to investigate how humans engage more fundamentally with technology and derive alternate forms of this engagement (Sarker, Chatter jee, Xiao, & Elbanna, 2019). The changing IT landscape necessitates the creation of an anchor to foster newer form of IS research (Liu et al., 2016); the central artifact serves as that anchor.

In sum, conceptualizing a central artifact for IS facilitates understanding of emerging IS questions, while also attending to disciplinary cohesion and distinctiveness (Grover, London, & Craig, 2016; Hirschheim & Klein, 2012) There have been calls to ensure that IS should have a solid and pervasive set of core values such that we do not lose our compass and soul as a discipline (Davison & Tarafdar, 2018, p. 588-589). In our opinion, a central artifact serves as a discipline's ‘compass and soul’. Crucially, a central artifact capturing the essence of the IS discipline is useful to make the ‘axis of cohesion’ for our discipline more concrete—thereby serving to preserve our disciplinar distinctiveness, yet providing avenues for coherent expansion (Sarker et al., 2019).<sup>2</sup>

## 1.3 | Information: A missing but essential component?

This effort to find a contemporary central artifact for IS assumes greater salience given that the notion of ‘infor mation —occurring in the very definition of IS—has either been neglected or inconsistently understood in prior IS research (Boell, 2017; Hassan, 2011; McKinney Jr & Yoos, 2010; Mingers & Standing, 2018). This problem remains despite the assertion of many scholars that information represents the core of the IS field (Le et al., 2015; Nunamaker, Briggs, Derrick, & Schwabe, 2015; Petter, Carter, Randolph, & Lee, 2018), and that ‘technologies come and go, but the need for information is persistent and ever increasing’ (Nunamaker & Briggs, 2011, p. 2)[emphasis added in bold]. Hence, there are passionate calls to pay attention to the information element in IS research:

we need to learn as a discipline how to theorize about information…[given that]…there is so much we do not know or understand about information and its relationship with systems and people (Petter et al., 2018, p. 16).

Tellingly, information is missing from prior conceptions of the IT artifact as well (Galliers, 2003; Lee et al., 2015). The lack of recognition of ‘information’ limits the relevance of the IT artifact, especially with the current need to focus on the ‘information’ aspect in contemporary areas such as business intelligence, data analytics, and Big Dat (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014). We have been cautioned that ‘we are squandering an opportunity to understand and lever age information in our [IS research] work because we are choosing to ignore it’ (Petter et al., 2018, p. 11)—and such a state of affairs is not helpful for the IS field.

The inadequate handling of information in prior IS research provides impetus to our objective of developing a contemporary conception of the central artifact that can accommodate information (Petter et al., 2018) and hel move the IS discipline forward. Specifically, if we are to formulate a conception of a central artifact for IS research we need to consider how information could be considered and ascribed a key role within it.

## 1.4 | Objective: Developing an IS artifact

Our discussion thus far reveals two issues. One is the debate on the usefulness, nature, and centrality of the IT arti fact, as well as its neglect of the information aspect. The second is the need for the IS discipline—or for that matter any discipline—to still structure itself around a cohesive framework with some commonly understood principle (Sarker et al., 2019).

Siding with the need for a central artifact to capture IS research, we contend that such a central artifact should comprise social, technical, and information elements, and these together should constitute the informa tion system . This position builds upon the rich legacy of sociotechnical thinking that has shaped the IS disciplin (Sarker et al., 2019). The sociotechnical perspective adopted in the IS discipline posits that a mutual interaction between social and technical components is what defines IS phenomena (Beath. Berente. Gallivan. & Lyytinen, 2013). Given the foundational role of the sociotechnical perspective in IS research, it is logical to build upon sociotechnical thinking in developing the conception of a central artifact that epitomizes IS research. How ever, while the sociotechnical perspective acknowledges the interactions of social and technical components in shaping IS phenomena, it is somewhat silent on the notion of information. Therefore, we argue that a useful wa forward is to not only to conceptualize the central artifact in IS research based upon the sociotechnical perspec tive, but also to expand sociotechnical thinking to incorporate information into this central artifact.<sup>3</sup>

Developing an artifact that incorporates social, technical, and information elements necessitates a conceptua move from an IT artifact to an IS artifact (Lee et al., 2015). This move is consistent with previous calls for morphing the IT artifact into an IS artifact (Lee et al., 2015; Lowry et al., 2017). Crucially, we need to better define and theoriz about the nature of the IS artifact and usher in valuable theoretical perspectives in order to develop it (Akhlaghpour et al., 2013). Such observations motivate our paper and enable us to pose the following research question (RQ):

RQ: How can we conceive a more contemporary view of the central artifact in IS (which we shall henceforth call the IS artifact), that:

• allows us to clearly define what an “IS” is

• ascribes, among others, a key role to information

The rest of the paper addresses this RQ. The following section draws upon the concepts of General Systems Theory (GST) and proposes a GST-inspired formulation of the IS artifact. While GST is not a new theory, there hav been renewed attempts to revisit it and derive fresh ideas from it that serve IS research (Burton-Jones, McLean, & Monod, 2015; Demetis & Lee, 2016, 2017; Lee, 2010). Indeed, there have been efforts to conceptualize a system theoretic view of the ‘IT artifact’ (Matook & Brown, 2017). Our work is not only stimulated by such research, but also moves the literature forward to formulate an IS artifact instead of an IT artifact and explicitly account for an include information.

Following our formulation of the GST-inspired IS artifact, the next section uses this formulation to present a suitable theorization of the nature of an IS, captured by four salient assertions. We then demonstrate how our assertions—that follow from our GST-inspired IS artifact—are both consistent with, and guide emerging IS research topics, namely healthcare and IT, and Fintech. This guidance is explicated in terms of a research agenda that can stimulate upcoming work on these contemporary topics. We believe that these emerging areas will play a substantia role in shaping the IS discipline in the near future and our research agenda could be deemed useful in that regard We conclude with the contributions and implications of our paper.

## 2 | USING GST TO DEVELOP THE IS ARTIFACT

There are obviously different ways to conceptualize the IS artifact—we present one possible way forward using the framework of GST (Boulding, 1956; Kast & Rosenzweig, 1972; Von Bertalanffy, 1972). To this end, we develop th systems aspect of IS, given that it is one of the key roots of our discipline represented in its very labe (Galliers, 2003). In this context, it is helpful to heed to Lee's (2010) call for a re-introduction of systems concepts to better understand what an IS means. This would be in line with the IS discipline's espoused position that it is a disci pline about information systems (Lee, 2010, p. 345, emphasis added in bold). Other scholars have supported Lee's perspective, and recommended that IS researchers need to draw upon the well-developed systems concepts prop ounded by GST—noting that in much of IS research, systems concepts are not explicitly recognized or discussed:

Where are the systems then? Taken for granted as a tacit assumption? Forgotten or abandoned? Shunted aside or obscured by the clouds? Socially deconstructed, reconstructed or transmogrified into something else? Or are they still here, even though ignored in much of our research and discourse and thus invisible to the audience? Further, what are the consequences of the disappearance or invisibility of systems? (Baskerville, Davison, Kaul, & Wong, 2014, p. 233).

Consequently we need to engage in a meaningful discussion of systems concepts so as to realize the value of GST to IS research (Baskerville et al., 2014).<sup>4</sup> Not doing so can have negative ramifications. For example, Baskerville et al. (2014 observe that there is substantial diversity in the conceptualization of IS—often causing confusion among IS researchers. This diversity may be partly attributed to the relegation of the systems concept into the background as part of the IS assumption ground’ (Baskerville et al., 2014, p. 235) and the fact that the systems aspect of IS has often been ‘assumed and ‘taken-for-granted’ (p. 236). Drawing upon the observations by Lee (2010) and Baskerville et al. (2014), we can con clude that while systems concepts may have been used in IS research (Walker, Stanton, Salmon, & Jenkins, 2008), their lin to GST is often not clear. In other words, researchers often end up using systems concepts without knowing that they are indeed concepts from GST or systems thinking. This obliviousness, first, to the concepts themselves, and second, to th implications of those concepts, is detrimental to cumulative knowledge building within the IS discipline.

There are other potential dangers of relegating GST and systems concepts to the background. One major issu of the ‘disappearance’ of systems in IS research is that our research works often lose the sense of the interconnec tedness of the various subsystems of complete, operational information systems. From a disciplinary standpoint, w lose sight of the distinctiveness and identity that can differentiate IS researchers from those in other disciplines— thus inhibiting the creation of our own unique and compelling disciplinary space (Lee, 2010). This lack of distinctive ness with other disciplines may have negative effects on long-term disciplinary survival and growth (Sarke et al., 2019). This state of affairs clearly calls for the need to more explicitly embrace systems thinking, which can strengthen disciplinary identity and distinctiveness, and thereby its continued vitality

… using systems concepts, among other things, could play a major part in contributing to distinguishing the IS discipline and thereby justify its existence. Ultimately, the IS discipline needs to employ systems concepts to a greater extent than it already has (Lee, 2010, p. 345, emphases in bold).

Apart from helping to develop our distinctiveness as IS scholars, GST is also helpful to embrace an interdisciplin ary approach; an approach that plays a salient role in the healthy expansion of the IS discipline (Tarafdar & Davison, 2018). This is because GST has the advantages as a generalized framework interpretable/adaptable to par ticular, specialized contexts:

[the goal of general systems theory is to] develop…generalized ears, and by developing a framework of general theory to enable one specialist [i.e., an IS researcher] to catch relevant communications from others [i.e., researchers in other disciplines]…a specialist…will be more sensitive to the contributions of other fields if he is aware of the many similarities…in…different…fields (Boulding, 1956, p. 1999, emphases in bold).

Indeed, IS researchers have demonstrated that GST can accommodate varying research objectives, and it pro vides a powerful lens to analyse a range of phenomena involving humans and technology (Porra, Hirschheim, & Parks, 2005). The intellectual range of GST is succinctly captured below:

… the systems perspective can be implemented in many ways…[from]…hard, mechanistic, closed, and relatively predictable to those that assume that systems are soft, organic, open, and inherently unpredictable…a systems perspective can underpin a wide variety of research (Burton-Jones et al., 2015, p. 669, emphasis added in bold).

It can thus be inferred that GST could serve as a powerful and much-needed framework to develop the IS artifact However, GST and its applicability to IS has been at the center of debate and it would be inappropriate to not acknowledge that debate and clarify our position with respect to it. In fact, we contend that being sensitive to thi debate and particularly mindful of the critiques of GST allow us to better leverage the power of GST in IS

The debate on GST started with Demetis and Lee (2016) calling for the IS community to re-focus on GST. In a set of articles (Demetis & Lee, 2016, 2017), the authors passionately called for an introduction of GST to IS theoriza tion, and accord GST its rightful place' within the IS discipline. Responding to this call. others have critigued this approach (Mingers, 2017; Robey & Mikhaeil, 2016; Schultze, 2017) The primary concerns raised against GST were that it encouraged a closed view of systems, focused on stability, discounted emergence and relationality, and inhibited reflexivity (Mingers, 2017; Robey & Mikhaeil, 2016; Schultze, 2017). However, even these critiques have acknowledged that a focus on GST still represents a ‘laudable aim’ (Mingers, 2017, p. 67) and that ‘revisiting GST is…rewarding …’ (Robey & Mikhaeil, 2016, p. 127).

Given the adaptability of GST, its varied intellectual range, and the observation that investing in GST is ‘laudable and ‘rewarding’, we propose that GST can be suitably adapted so as to develop a compelling yet parsimonious view of an IS artifact. Of course, we remain mindful of the potential pitfalls of blindly applying GST to IS phenomena, and are therefore sensitive to the suggestions made by Robey and Mikhaeil (2016), Mingers (2017), and Schultze (2017) as we develop our GST-based conception of the IS artifact. Notably. GST provides a flexible canvass. which allow: us to suitably interpret/adapt it so as to address many of the concerns raised in these critiques.

## 3 | A CONCEPTION OF THE IS ARTIFACT USING GST

## 3.1 | Key GST concepts

Our research objective necessitates a review of the basic GST concepts as summarized in Table 1 (Ackoff, 1971 Boulding, 1956; Kast & Rosenzweig, 1972); we draw upon and amalgamate these concepts as we present our adapted view of an IS artifact (Figure 1). Essentially, in this notion, the IS artifact is represented as a superordinat system composed of social and technical subsystems, with information playing a key role that captures the state and behavior of these superordinate systems

Formally, a system can be defined as follows:

A system can be defined as a complex of interacting elements p1, p2,…,pn. Interaction means that the elements stand in a certain relation, R, so that their behaviour in R is different from their behaviour in an another relation, R0. On the other hand, if the behaviour in R and R0 is not different, there is no interaction, and the elements behave independently with respect to the relations R and R0 (Von Bertalanffy, 1950, p. 143, as quoted in Demetis & Lee, 2016, p. 117).

The interacting elements that comprise a superordinate system could be subsystems, which could have furthe inter-related subsystems nested within them. In the context of the IS artifact, following sociotechnical thinking, the per tinent subsystems are the social subsystem and the technical subsystem (Sarker et al., 2019). The social subsystem con sists of the individuals (including their knowledge, skills, and values), structures (e.g., hierarchy, reward structures), and their inter-relationships (Lee et al., 2015), such that organized patterns emerge, along with shared norms, values, and symbols (Allon & Hanany, 2012). The technical subsystem can be defined as one that ‘comprises the devices, tools, and techniques needed to transform inputs into outputs in a way that enhances the…performance of the organization (Sykes, Venkatesh, & Johnson, 2014, p. 54). Further, it is noted that both subsystems (social and technical) are malleable with fluid and permeable boundaries and emerge with changing contextual conditions and mechanisms (Wynn Jr & Williams, 2012). In other words, the subsystems are open systems that interact with the environment surrounding the system as well as engage in exchanges with other subsystems (Pike, Bateman, & Butler, 2018). This interaction focuses on creating dynamic equilibrium with other subsystems in a loosely coupled manner (Gulati, Sytch, & Tatarynowicz, 2010). Note that in our conception, information is not a subsystem; it is a defining and emergent property of the superordinate system that shapes, and is shaped by, how the social and technical subsystems interact

While we present the key GST concepts in Table 1, we combine these concepts into certain basic meta principles to elaborate on how the subsystems should work together and enable us to formulate a parsimonious, yet revelatory, conception of the IS artifact (see Table 1 for the meta-principles that combine the GST concepts). This is because many of these GST concepts are logically related and utilizing them simultaneously under the same meta principle adds parsimony, coherence, as well as incisiveness to our approach. The meta-principles also help pave th way to place information as a key characteristic of such a superordinate system, as will be elaborated upon later in the paper (refer to Table 2 for explanations of each meta-principle).

## 3.2 | The four meta-principles of the IS artifact

## 3.2.1 | Meta-principle 1: The IS artifact is composed of inter-related and permeable subsystems combined in a holistic and synergistic manner

This meta-principle can be derived by integrating concepts 1, 2, 3, and 4 in Table 1. In line with the holistic and synergistic idea of systems (Kast & Rosenzweig, 1972), we contend that there should be a focus on how the inter-related subsystems combine to synergistically add value in the form of a superordinate system (i.e., the IS artifact). Nevo and Wade (2010) define synergy as positive emergent capabilities such as those between an IT asset and an organizationa resource that results in a system [named]…IT-enabled resource (p. 169, emphasis added). In the IT innovation domain for example, the notion of synergy has been particularly useful because concepts such as harmonious IT affordanc (an example of a holistic system) emerge from the synergy of IT affordances (which are each sociotechnical subsystems (Chatterjee, D Moody, Lowry, Chakraborty, & Hardin, 2021; Chatterjee, Moody, Lowry, Chakraborty, & Hardin, 2020).

This synergism is promoted by permeable boundaries of the subsystems. In order to achieve overall synergy, the subsystems should adapt and exchange subsystem resources through the subsystem boundaries. If the subsystems have closed boundaries (meaning they never import or export resources), then they can never change and adapt consequently, achieving synergy by combining the subsystems would be impossible. Therefore, the synergistic exis tence of the superordinate system (i.e., the IS artifact) requires this permeability across the various subsystem (Lomi, Lusher, Pattison, & Robins, 2013). For example, in an IS innovation system, the technical subsystem acts bot as a target and an engine of innovation by enhancing the permeability of knowledge across system boundaries allowing knowledge (epitomized by the information characteristic) to be shared and distributed (using the technologi cal subsystem) for dynamic learning across social structures in an organization (Boland, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2007).

The synergy may also be created through the concept of hierarchical subsystems in GST (Kast & Rosenzweig, 1972). For example, the superordinate system could be germane to the system of IT-enabled organizational decision making, which incorporates humans processing information as well as digital controls (Lee & Berente, 2011). The superordinate system could also ascribe greater importance to any of the subsystems (e.g., greater human autonomy or greater reliance on the technology), thus creating an inherent hierarchy (Tuertscher, Garud, & Kumaraswamy, 2014). During the life of such superordinate systems, different subsystems may change in hierarchy, so as to contribute to the overall synergy. For example, in the case of ubiquitous computing, technology is often in the con text/ background (Yoo, 2010); this apparent relegation in the hierarchy (from an explicit subsystem to a context) pro motes the synergy and allows us to accept technology-in-context (in some cases) as a valid form of the IS artifact.

## 3.2.2 | Meta-principle 2: IS artifact comprises of a purposive system that seeks multiple goals through multiple paths (multifinality and equifinality)

This meta-principle integrates concepts 5, 6, and 7 in Table 1. A purposive, multi-goal-seeking system that transforms inputs to outputs is one that can produce similar outcomes in different ways (equifinality) as well as lea to different outcomes (multifinality) (Ackoff, 1971). An example of equifinality is how two different social structure (CIO-CFO/CIO-CEO) combined with strategic positioning (e.g., information structure signalled to the outside world) may result in technological outcomes such as the acquisition of new IT tools and services (Banker, Hu, Pavlou, & Luftman, 2011; Elbanna, 2013).

## 3.2.3 | Meta-principle 3: IS artifact is best represented by an open system that receives inputs from the environment and produces outputs for that environment

This meta-principle integrates concepts 8 and 9 in Table 1. According to our perspective, the superordinate system can be considered as a classic example of an open system, as it is itself a collection of open subsystems, which can survive by importing more energy (inputs) from the environment than they export (Peery, 1972). Much like other organizational systems, information systems are often closely related to the environment in which they exist, and which the system is connected to, or embedded in (Davison & Martinsons, 2016). Organizational systems ar characterized by permeability where such systems react to, and exchange resources, ideas, and people with the out side environment into which it is embedded (Faik, Thompson, & Walsham, 2019). For example, environmental turbu lence (an environmental input) is faced by organizations who counter it by developing improvisational capabilitie using the IT infrastructure (technical subsystem) as well as human beings (social subsystem) who leverage thes infrastructures (Chatterjee, Moody, Lowry, Chakraborty, & Hardin, 2015).

An important consideration here is the feedback loop where outputs feed back into the inputs so as to correct deviances from what is desired in the outputs. The idea of feedback is important to achieve system evolution (Kast & Rosenzweig, 1972). Negative feedback from the environment will allow for the correction of deviances so that the system does not become unstable, while positive feedback can have a spiralling effect for system improve ment. For example, in the context of an IT services supply chain model (Lee, An, & Connors, 2009), feedback contro schemes ‘can improve costs, utilization and stability of workforce …[and] can produce desirable policies of workforc resource management’ (p. 77).

## 3.2.4 | Meta-principle 4: The IS artifact is composed of mutually adaptable subsystems for promoting dynamic equilibrium and managing entropy

This meta-principle can be derived by integrating concepts 10 and 11 in Table 1. We conceive the subsystems as mutually adaptable and propose that IS researchers recognize the inter-relationship among the subsystems and th implications of such inter-relationships for the system as a whole. The core focus of the subsystems through this mutual adaptation is to manage overall entropy of the superordinate system. Entropy (of a system) measures th degree to which a system is disorganized (Swanson, Bailey, & Miller, 1997); in such a disorganized system, the outcomes are random and uncertain (McKinney Jr & Yoos, 2010). Put in another way, entropy captures how random and uncertain a system is. Negative entropy (negentropy), on the other hand, captures how stable the system is. Importantly, GST views that ‘open systems as capable of positive as well as negative entropy changes, making i possible for the system to attain a dynamic equilibrium or steady state, raising the chances of survival’ (Aksulu & Wade, 2010, p. 587). Thus, the IS artifact, as an integrated, superordinate system, gravitates towards dynamic equi librium by managing entropy.

This entropy level within the superordinate IS system is dependent upon and managed by interaction between the subsystems. For example. when the social and technical subsystems are jointly optimized and in harmony entropy level is low. In contrast, when the social and the technical subsystems are in disharmony, and perhaps in some creative tension—such as in the case of complex adaptive systems (Chae, 2014)—entropy level of the superor dinate IS artifact system is high. Importantly, both low entropy and high entropy can lead to beneficial outcomes When entropy is low, subsystems work in tandem to produce outcomes. Conversely, when entropy is high, subsys tems find innovative ways to reduce the entropy such that creative outcomes happen. Toh and Kim (2013) provide an example of this, noting that in situations of high technological entropy (uncertainty about technological innova tions and investments), organizations may tend to invest more in the technology so as to alleviate this uncertaint and stabilize the system

## 4 | THEORIZING ON THE NATURE OF AN IS

The GST-based formulation of an IS artifact, which, arguably, is parsimonious, revelatory, and flexible, emerges as a possible solution to the debates regarding the nature of the IT artifact. The meta-principles of the IS artifact give ris to normative assertions that need to be satisfied in order for the IS artifact to faithfully capture and define an IS. This definition of IS highlights, among others, the role that information plays in materializing the IS artifact in a well defined IS. We would like to note that IS research comprises different research phenomena (e.g., security, healthcare innovation, etc.) and approaches (e.g., behavioural, design science, econometric, etc.). Therfore, our conceptualizatio of the IS artifact should be broad enough to include these approaches. At the same time, it has to be sufficiently flex ible to accomodate the expanding IS research landscape stemming from studies of new technologies and their unique impacts on society and businesses. Therefore, when we develop each assertion, we are mindful of the variet in IS research, while also taking care that each assertion contributes to the uniqueness of the IS disciplinary space.

As we developed each assertion, we chose a prominent IS research stream—cybersecurity—to illustrate/support the arguments behind each assertion. This effort grounds our assertions in empirical and theoretical findings in cybersecurity research and shows how conceptions salient to this popular (and developing) IS topic are consistent with our formulation of the IS artifact. We feel that using cybersecurity as an example will improve the appeal of ou assertions.

## 4.1 | Assertion 1. An IS should promote holistic synergy among the constituent subsystems

This assertion follows primarily from meta-principle 1. We argue that an IS should be characterized by whether th overall synergy for the superordinate system is attained. Notably, we conceive that in an IS, each subsystem is important insofar as it contributes to the overall synergy. For example, when humans (i.e., the social subsystem ele ments) interact with the technical system elements, this relationship could be hierarchical and either one of them could enjoy greater autonomy (e.g., humans overruling the technology or accepting the recommendations of th technology). As long as such social–technical relationships increase the synergy in the system and the system pro gresses towards its goals in a desired manner, it is consistent with our understanding of an IS.

This salience of synergy to defining an IS is well exemplified by the research stream of cybersecurity. With regard to cybersecurity, for example, it has been observed that technical and social aspects of security should synergistically conjoin to produce desirable outcomes:

The best effort is often achieved when several security measures are brought to bear together. How should intrusion detection collaborate with other security mechanisms to this synergy effect? (Axelsson, 2000, p. 189, emphasis added in bold).

Often, such varying components of organizational security need to be optimally configured for this synerg (Cavusoglu, Raghunathan, & Cavusoglu, 2009). Clearly, therefore, synergy is an important concept in cybersecurit research, supporting our first assertion.

## 4.2 | Assertion 2. In an IS, the social and the technical components interact along an affording–constraining relationship

This assertion follows primarily from meta-principles 1 and 2. The interplay between the social and the techni cal subsystems is a fundamental issue in an IS artifact (Lee, 2004). Owing to the rapidly evolving technologies and their innovative uses, this interplay assumes various forms (Sarker et al., 2019) including mutual appropria tion (Goh, Gao, & Agarwal, 2011), unidirectional causality (Venkatesh, Zhang, & Sykes, 2011), social in a tech nology context (Alnuaimi, Robert, & Maruping, 2010), technology in a social context, (Li & Sarkar, 2011) embeddedness of the social within the technical (Friedman, Kahn Jr, & Borning, 2006), constitutive entanglements of the social and the technical (Orlikowski, 2010), or imbrications between the social and the technica (Leonardi, 2011). Please see Appendix, Table A2, for a summary of interplays between the social and technica components.

We contend that a meta-view to look at the relationship between the social and the technical components could be achieved through concepts such as affordances and constraints and their different variations (Leonardi, 2011). The notions of affordances–constraints highlight how arranging technology within socia structures (or vice versa) facilitates/constrains use/practice in social and organizational contexts (Zammuto Griffith, Majchrzak, Dougherty, & Faraj, 2007). Fundamentally, this arrangement could take any of the forms of interactions above.

This ‘affording–constraining’ relationship between the social and the technical gives rise to emergent action, which is dependent not only on the IT features but also on how it relates to ‘people's ability and predisposition to use these features in certain ways, and the organizational context within which this takes place’ (Gal, Blegind Jensen, & Lyytinen, 2014, p. 1372). The flexible relationship between the social and the technical may also accom modate temporal unfoldment of the interaction between the social and the technical. For example, working wit temporal separation using ubiquitous technologies is common (Chatterjee, Chakraborty, Sarker, Sarker, & Lau, 2009) In other words, an affording–constraining relationship also allows social and technical subsystems and their mutua influences to be spread across a much wider timespan, encouraging multifinality. Clearly, affordances/constraint could arise from context, causality, embeddedness, or even temporality (Sarker et al., 2019), thus allowing multipl manifestations of the IS artifact, adaptable to individual needs of IS researchers.

The appropriateness of affordances and constraints to define an IS is well captured by cybersecurity research. A cybersecurity system heavily draws upon the notion of affordances and constraints. Here, security technolog affords users certain actions, or may constrain them as well. For example, firewalls may constrain users from visiting certain websites. Or, loopholes in cybersecurity technologies can afford certain actions where organizational users (or intruders) can take advantage of such loopholes (Nazir, Patel, & Patel, 2017). Thus, cybersecurity technologies ar built with a consideration of what affordances (or constraints) they provide to the users.

## 4.3 | Assertion 3. In an IS, information results in a reduction of the level of entropy

This assertion follows primarily from meta-principles 3 and 4. Entropy is a critical concept in GST, defined as a measure of how random or uncertain the system is (McKinney & Yoos, 2019). The question is, what best captures entropy within an IS? We contend that information can be best understood as a concept that reduces the entropy which emerges out of interactions between the social and the technical subsystems. Within an IS, the amount of information is often understood to cause a reduction of uncertainty (McKinney & Yoos, 2019). From this perspective the amount of information can be best understood as causing a reduction of entropy (also called negentropy) of th superordinate (IS artifact) system.

Our conception of information and its salience in reducing uncertainty builds upon the general definition of information (GDI) proposed by Floridi (2009). This definition is represented by three clauses:

GDI) σ is an instance of information, understood as semantic content, if and only if

GDI.1) σ consists of n data…;

GDI.2) the data are well-formed (wfd);

GDI.3) the wfd are meaningful… (Floridi, 2009, p. 16).

The key ideas in this definition are data being ‘well-formed’ and ‘meaningful’, which are terms associated with increased orderliness and reduced randomness. This definition of information implies that the role of information is to help realize the path (by being well-formed and meaningful) of achieving the desired goals of a system. In othe words, information provides some sort of order to a goal-seeking system in its effort to realize those goals, thu reducing entropy. Therefore, Floridi's definition of information can be ultimately interpreted as highlighting the rol of information in reducing entropy. Given that entropy has been defined as a mathematical aggregation of data in order to reveal more insights (Ahituv, 1980), a conception of information as meaningful and well-formed data can be logically extended to propose the role of information as reducing entropy.

Understanding information as a contributor to reduced entropy allows us to situate it well within a GSTinformed IS artifact system. Within such a system ‘information always arises at the confluence of the social and the technical…and imparts dialog, meaning, and utility to sociotechnical interactions…’ (Sarker et al., 2019, p. 710). Th imparting of dialog, meaning, and utility to sociotechnical interactions ensures that the social and the technical com ponents work in alignment to realize the overall goals of the IS artifact system. Clearly, there is reduced randomnes in realizing the systemic goals if appropriate information is produced/available.

This conception of information, especially its role in reducing randomness and uncertainty, has been embraced in multiple disciplines including economics, information science, and physics. For example, Knight's (1985) classic work on risk, uncertainty, and profit in economics understands uncertainty (entropy) as imperfect information. A the information becomes more perfect (i.e., valuable) entropy is reduced. In the information science discipline, infor mation theorists have acknowledged ‘the similarity of the formal definitions of physical entropy, according to statistical mechanics, and of information, according to information theory (Bawden & Robinson, 2015, p. 1965). In addition, physicists have acknowledged that information can cause reduced entropy (Brillouin, 1951, 1953). Indeed, it has been observed that in physics, the idea of entropy has progressively merged ‘with the idea of…information (Holt, 2012, p. 61).

Perhaps inspired by such observations, we find instances in the IS literature which recommend understanding operationalizing information as that which reduces entropy in a system (Sarker et al., 2019). For example, information gain has been understood as resulting in a reduction of uncertainty (Chou, Sinha, & Zhao, 2010). Information is a fun damental systemic characteristic when the social and the technical subsystems interact dynamically, and mutually emerge to co-construct an IS (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Sawyer & Huang, 2007) and can play multiple roles within a sociotechnical system:

information can be conceptualized as a medium through which the social and the technical interact as the outcome of the interaction…, as the mediator between the social and technical components… or sometimes even as the agent shaping the social and the technical [and their interactions] (Sarker et al., 2019, p. 710).

However, regardless of the role it plays in the IS artifact system, the fundamental salience of information is in ensuring orderly and harmonized interactions between the social and the technical components, that is, reducing the uncertainty (entropy) in the IS artifact system. Indeed, given the varied conceptions of information, understanding it as a contributor, to reduced entropy has been proposed to be a unified understanding of information (Bawden & Robinson, 2020).

Crucially, the view of information as reducing entropy is a unifying conception that captures all five perspectives of information prevalent in IS research. These five views propose that information lies in environmental structures; information lies in the organization of signals; information resides in the coding and decoding of signals; information reflects the shared intentionality of actors; and finally, information lies in the coordination of join action (Beynon Davies & Wang, 2019).<sup>5</sup> It has been further argued that:

…it is possible to develop a view of information that encompasses all five positions identifiable within our ideal-type of information situation, but in one holistic, enacted whole. Indeed, we would go further and tentatively suggest that is not possible to engage with information situations effectively… without adopting this enacted viewpoint (Beynon-Davies & Wang, 2019, p. 481).

We posit that viewing information as causing reduced entropy allows for a holistic conception of information that encapsulates all five perspectives. For example, viewing information as a reduction of entropy allows us to acknowledge the environmental turbulence (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010) and the structures (such as industry-level idio syncrasies) that give rise to such turbulence, and how better information can either reduce the environmenta turbulence, or channelize orderly action in the presence of such turbulence. Understanding information in terms of organization of signals directly follows from the definition of negative entropy, which is the orderliness of a system. The same is true if we view information as residing in the coding and decoding of signals, which allow for bette understanding of environmental and system-level cues to guide purposeful decision-making. Finally, conceiving information as residing within shared intentionality and joint coordination by actors reflects the effort by actors to engage in collective sense-making to bring increasing orderliness for coordinated action (Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012). Al these five perspectives together highlight the relevance of information for increasing the orderliness of a system which, by definition, is a reduction of the system's entropy.

Based upon the above observations, we propose that viewing information as resulting in reduced entropy withi the IS artifact system is beneficial and strategically useful as we grow the discipline. There is support for this position with scholars opining that an explication of the relation between entropy and information creates a normal bridge to other disciplines, promoting interdisciplinary research:

IS research that helps explain the relationship between information and entropy, how this type of information should be measured, the limitations of measurement, and the criteria for measures would enhance the value of IS research to the larger scientific community (McKinney Jr & Yoos, 2010, p. 333-334).

By relating entropy to information, our paper addresses this normative call.

Cybersecurity research provides a suitable example supporting our conception of information as reducing entropy. For example, safeguarding information assets is the major focus of a cybersecurity system (von Solms & von Solms, 2018). Therefore, a key idea of a cybersecurity system is the notion of information integrity (Wang, Lau, & Gerdes, 2018). Cybersecurity systems are designed and implemented so that information is not compromised. Realizing that information is fundamental to effective decision-making, tampering with information raises the overall entropy of the system—bringing in more chaos among organizational stakeholders. Therefore, in cybersecurity, information (or more specifically, useful information) is understood to contribute to a reduction of chaos in the cybersecu rity system. This is consistent with our view of information as causing reduced entropy in the system

## 4.4 | Assertion 4. In an IS, equifinality and multifinality can emerge over time

This assertion follows primarily from meta-principle 2. We believe our revised understanding of IS offers an avenue for pursuing multiple outcomes, which could be both instrumental (economic) and humanistic. Instrumental outcomes could be, for example, greater revenues, and humanistic outcomes could be more along the lines of employe satisfaction or quality of work life. The GST concepts of equifinality and multifinality allow us to accommodate such varving outcomes of an IS. It is important to understand that multifinality for a system evolves longitudinally (Loeb Rai, Ramaprasad, & Sharma, 1998), and it is often the case that multiple goals are not pursued or achieved simulta neously. Thus, it is meaningful to investigate if one set of goals (say, economic) could lead, in time, to another set of goals (say, humanistic), or vice versa (Sarker et al., 2019).

We could also investigate how goals are being continually negotiated, and thus emergent; therefore it may hap pen that the multifinality that we finally achieve is quite different from the multifinality we aimed for (Winter, Ber ente, Howison, & Butler, 2014). In short, we need to consider the ‘big picture’ and focus on the temporal emergenc of such multiple goals, as well as the different paths that emerge to reach those goals. In pursuit of this tempora emergence, the feedback mechanism is arguably crucial. The feedback mechanism introduces a temporal orde where the multiple goals may follow one another in sequence (Lee et al., 2009). Apart from helping us achieve multi finality, the feedback mechanism also illuminates on different paths to achieve the same goal (Zollo & Winter, 2002)—thereby upholding equifinality.

The literature stream of cybersecurity captures the notion of multifinality and equifinality quite well. A cyberse curity system is not only designed to provide better technology to thwart malicious practices, but it is also important in raising security awareness among the social users in the system (Herath & Rao, 2009). This is an example of multi finality. On the other hand, outcomes of cybersecurity systems are achieved by not only designing better technolo gies but also by raising awareness of safe security practices through programmes, training, and policies (Vance Lowry, & Eggett, 2013; Warkentin & Willison, 2009), the effects of which often emerge over time. This is an example of equifinality.

## 5 | LOOKING FORWARD: THE USEFULNESS OF OUR CONCEPTION IN GUIDING RESEARCH ON CONTEMPORARY/EMERGING IS TOPICS

In this section, we demonstrate the value of our conception of the IS artifact. Specifically, we show how our con ception of an IS, which follows from our GST-inspired IS artifact, is useful to shape the emerging IS research land scape. For that purpose, we choose two particularly emerging research streams: healthcare and IT, and Fintech. The former is a dominant contemporary IS topic, with increased salience due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The lat ter is a relatively nascent IS research topic, which we feel will continue to dominate much of the intellectual pro gress in the IS discipline in the near future. For each research stream, we first show how research in that particular stream is well captured by our IS conception. Following that, we develop a research agenda for that research stream which builds upon our conception, thus demonstrating the usefulness of our conception to stimu late future research.

## 5.1 | Healthcare IT systems

Healthcare research is consistent with our formulation of the IS artifact and our subsequent discussion on the nature of an IS. Indeed, researchers have projected that the future of effective healthcare lies in connecting systems (tech nology), data (i.e., information), and people (i.e., the social, consisting of patients and healthcare providers) (Bardhan, Chen, & Karahanna, 2020)—all of these capture the fundamental components of our proposed IS artifact. First, the conception of synergy is inherent in achieving a compelling delivery of healthcare services (Jadad, Haynes, Hunt, & Browman, 2000). Efficient and effective delivery of healthcare services need collaborative synergy between the social aspects of the system (such as patients and healthcare providers) using technologies that are at their disposa (Noran, 2013). Chronic disease management programmes, for example, often develop their efficacy based upon how well individuals can synergistically embed the technologies in their daily routine (Jiang & Cameron, 2020). The fol lowing quote highlights the importance of synergy in a healthcare IT system

More effort is needed to untangle the effects of the ITSM [IT-enabled self-monitoring] intervention components in order to better assess impacts and design more effective ITSM interventions. Such untangling is necessary to understand the synergies between the ITSM and its multiple non-IT components (ibid, p. 473, emphasis added in bold).

Second, the notion of affordances and constraints in designing effective IT-based healthcare is also highlighted using affordance actualization theory (Strong et al., 2014) for organizing the ITSM literature. This literature observe that newer affordances replace older ones as the IT continuously innovates, and in such cases older IT systems can become constraining for users to effectively practice ITSM for system users. Clearly, how medical technologie afford (or constrain) newer forms of healthcare action is a matter of substantial interest within the healthcar community.

Third, the role of information is critical in healthcare as it can mean a difference between life and death. New healthcare devices, such as wearable and ingestible ones, are providing ‘data gathering, aggregation, and interpreta tion using AI’ (artificial intelligence) (Bardhan et al., 2020, p. 186). What is notable is that such techniques to gather data, and the subsequent analyses using AI techniques, can be applied to reduce the noise from the data and uncovering patterns so as to reduce chaos. In other words, medical technologies and their use are not only about col lecting information, but at the same time increasing information usefulness so as to improve the efficiency and effec tiveness of healthcare decisions and subsequent interventions. This exemplifies how the collection of usefu information serves to reduce the inherent chaos (entropy) in the healthcare system (Sabeti, Katebi, & Boostani, 2009) while emphasizing that the usefulness of information is fundamentally a sociotechnical construction.

Finally, a healthcare IT system embraces multifinality. For example, the goal is not only prevention, but also intervention and cure, as well as ensuring consistency and safety of healthcare practices over time. The achievement of these goals can also be approached through different paths. One could be by making patients (or high-risk individ uals) more aware of their potential healthcare dangers (Hwang, Lee, & Shin, 2016). Or it could be by designing IT to raise psychological empowerment among recipients of healthcare services (Ghosh, Khuntia, Chawla, & Deng, 2014). Consistent with the idea of equifinality, these are all different pathways that play out temporally to realize effectiv healthcare using IT.

## 5.1.1 | Guiding healthcare IT systems research through our IS conception

Our conception also provides for new investigations in the domain of IT and healthcare. The synergy in an IT healthcare system requires an understanding of the varying affordances (or constraints) placed by healthcare tech nology. For example, affordances for immediate feedback and patient monitoring in chronic disease management might help promote synergistic collaboration between healthcare providers and patients. Likewise, new informationa affordances (using new analytical modelling techniques) of IT can be investigated and specific IT features that help promote these informational affordances can be designed so as to reduce the entropy in the healthcare IT system Research could try to focus on how to define alternate desirable outcomes of healthcare services (multifinality) and different pathways to achieving those (equifinality). Our discussion is captured by the following sample researc questions:

1. What new affordances of healthcare IT promote collaborative synergy between healthcare providers and patients?

2. How can unique analytical and AI techniques be leveraged to create new models of public health that consider alter nate outcomes (such as prevention, cure, and emotional support)?

3. How can different contexts of healthcare implementation shape different mechanisms to achieve the synergies between patients and healthcare providers?

## 5.2 | Fintech ecosystems

Fintech (abbreviation for Financial Technologies) is quite prominent among the emerging research interests of the IS community (Gomber, Kauffman, Parker, & Weber, 2018b). Fintech ‘refers to the influx of technology tools, plat forms, and ecosystems that make financial services or products more accessible, efficient, and affordable (Hendershott, Zhang, Zhao, & Zheng, 2017, p. 885). The primary forces that are driving Fintech systems include tech nological innovations and transforming traditional financial services with the aid of digital technologies (Gomber Kauffman, Parker, & Weber, 2018a).

One of the key attributes of the Fintech research stream is the creation of an innovation ecosystem that sustains the financial products or services (Hendershott et al., 2017). The concept of an innovation ecosystem captures issues such as synergy (Tantalo & Priem, 2016). Importantly, from an IS standpoint, the synergy is not only between th social stakeholders in the innovation ecosystem, but also between those stakeholders and the IT infrastructure (Suseno, Laurell, & Sick, 2018). Therefore, a holistic financial ecosystem needs synergy between IT, ecosystem stakeholders, and end users.

The affording–constraining interaction between the social and technical needed in Fintech research has bee demonstrated by Du, Pan, Leidner, and Ying (2019). In their blockchain implementation study, they show how pay ments initiated by users can leverage the technological affordances to validate/broadcast transactions within th financial network. They also discuss how users interact with the system in a continuous manner so as to mitigat possible constraints and convert them into affordances.

Financial information is of fundamental salience in the Fintech ecosystem, which needs to safeguard financia information. Put simply, the social and technological components of an innovation ecosystem can work in a non chaotic manner only if the user is able to comprehend the information produced by the financial ecosystem. If th information produced by the financial ecosystem is not valid, or trustable, then it destabilizes the system—leading to increased entropy of the Fintech ecosystem. Finally, a Fintech ecosystem recognizes both multifinality and equi finality. The portfolio approach to financial management suggests that users need to balance both strong desire for money-multiplication with stable growth of funds (multifinality). Equifinality is also achievable in a Fintech ecosystem, where one could pursue similar outcomes satisfying interests in financial growth by digitally investing in differ ent financial instruments.

## 5.2.1 | Guiding Fintech research through our IS conception

Our conception of IS can guide the design of a Fintech ecosystem which provides organizational and inter organizational platform for Fintech transactions. The concept of an ecosystem considers issues such as modularit (into components of the ecosystem), complementarity (of the components), and coordination within ecosystems an governance of the ecosystems (Jacobides, Cennamo, & Gawer, 2018), in addition to stakeholder interests and demands. Our conception is useful to investigate these elements and their interactions. For example, research coul delve into designing the optimal modularity and coordination so as to promote synergy in Fintech ecosystems Technology-based Fintech ecosystems can stimulate research on affordances that support and sustain such ecosys tems. Specifically, given the sensitive nature of financial information, we can ask: how can we combine informationa affordances in Fintech ecosystems with cybersecurity affordances so as to safeguard information-keeping in Fintech ecosystems and make them more orderly (reduced entropy)? Are alternate governance mechanisms of Fintech eco systems similarly efficacious in actualizing outcomes (equifinality)? And, finally, do Fintech ecosystems only promote one outcome (better financial management) or other outcomes which can include psychological and behavioural out comes? If so, how? Based on this discussion, we can propose the following sample research questions:

1. How can a fintech ecosystem be optimally modularized and coordinated to promote synergy?

2. How can ground-level affordances (e.g., informational affordances and cybersecurity affordances) be combined t improve information-safekeeping in a fintech ecosystem?

3. Can we design alternate fintech ecosystem governance mechanisms which generate similar outcomes? In addition what other outcomes (e.g., psychological/behavioural outcomes) can a fintech ecosystem promote?

## 6 | CONTRIBUTIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

This opinion paper offers multiple contributions. First, this paper provides a basic and parsimonious understanding of what a central artifact might mean for current IS researchers. We hope that our work addresses some of the confu sion and debate regarding the IT artifact and ultimately provides concrete implications of what we can mean by an IS (Elbanna & Newman, 2013). Specifically, this paper provides a starting point not only to evaluate the nature of any I research (as reflected in the frequently posed question: why is your study an instance of IS research?), but also to help formulate research questions and studies in the IS discipline that are consistent with contemporary research interest in this discipline (as exemplified by our discussion on healthcare and IT, and Fintech). Notably, the broad and flexibl framework of GST, which is considered foundational for information and management sciences, and which we use to develop our view of the IS artifact, is open to multiple ontological and epistemological positions, and hence to dif ferent methodological approaches. This, we believe, is altogether consistent with our view of the IS discipline as being inclusive, diverse, and expansive. Our work espouses the sociotechnical philosophy that has shaped the IS discipline (Sarker et al., 2019) and concretizes sociotechnical thinking by developing a conception of IS that is undoubt edly sociotechnical, yet contemporary and forward-looking. We are optimistic that our conception will allow researchers to leverage the richness in sociotechnical philosophy; such efforts will not only preserve our disciplinar distinctiveness, but also enable us to welcome new areas of IS research.

Second, while information is central to the IS community (Galliers, 2003), our paper highlights the fact that information has often been inadequately addressed and defined in the IS literature (McKinney Jr & Yoos, 2010; Mingers & Standing, 2017). By explicitly conceptualizing information as resulting in reduced entropy in IS, we directly addressed this issue. However, this understanding is open to debate and we invite our IS colleagues to modify our conception to arrive at a greater consensus. We hope that this conceptualization moves the field a step forward towards a mor comprehensive and consensual understanding of information.

The third contribution is in terms of future research implications. Our work energizes IS research, particularly at the cutting-edge, as demonstrated in the surfacing of key research questions that can guide future investigations i healthcare and IT, and Fintech. While we used these two research streams to demonstrate the utility of our conception, the GST-inspired conceptualization of the IS artifact can be equally applicable to other contemporary and emerging research areas. Our formulation also has salience for varying IS researchers including those embedded in behavioural, econometric, and design science traditions. For example, our conceptualization allows IS researchers to capture human (social) behaviour with respect to technology, help in the design of technological systems that afford desirable outcomes, and define econometric formulations to capture the interaction of social and the technical systems. Thus, we believe that our conception will resonate broadly with IS researchers and help them conceptualize their next research project.

Our vision of the IS artifact not only captures emerging topics in IS research, but also can benefit continued research in many traditional IS research topics. As a case in point, information systems development (ISD), which is perhaps foundational to IS research and practice, and continues to be a prominent IS topic, can also benefit from our conception of the IS artifact. For example, much of ISD research seeks to synergize organizational needs to the technology being developed (Kampfner, 1997); in fact, synergy has been considered as an enabling metaphor for ISD (Hirschheim & Newman, 1991). Again, the notion of affordances have been used to understand some of the key con siderations in ISD, namely the delivery of quality in systems and information (Grgecic, Holten, & Rosenkranz, 2015). Critically, Leonardi (2011) uses the concepts of affordances and constraints to describe the development of ‘CrashLab' simulation technology. Elsewhere. the concept of entropy was used to analyse and revise ISD practices (Larsen, Päivärinta, & Smolander, 2013). Finally, ISD has often embraced both multifinality and equifinality. Multiple outcomes can emerge from an implemented IS design principle (multifinality) (Lukyanenko & Parsons, 2020). Again ISD ‘accepts the concept of equifinality—that there may be several equally effective, internally consistent patterns between the system and its context’ (Lycett & Paul, 1999, p. 129). Of course, as we discussed earlier, ISD research (like most other traditional IS research topics) does not often explicitly incorporate GST concepts and tends to leave them implied or in the background. Thus, explicitly (and consistently) surfacing GST concepts may bring valuable con siderations for traditional IS topics such as ISD and can open up different directions for these topics as well

We have demonstrated the usefulness of our conception of an IS to stimulate future research. This contribution, to energize and guide IS research, perhaps acquires greater salience in today's world, ravaged by the COVID-19 pandemic For example, our conception of IS research allows for the induction of novel technologies and practices that redefine the landscape of technology-enabled work (Jain, Padmanabhan, Pavlou, & Santanam, 2018) post COVID-19. A critical issue in the changing nature of IT-enabled work is the important concept of human–computer symbiosis (Jain et al., 2018). Given the COVID-19 crisis where workers are at home, with only their computers for company, the notion of human–compute symbiosis gains salience. We are at a stage where the lack of physical interaction due to social distancing guidelines neces sitates a seamless integration of human and machine problem-solving capabilities.

The question is, how can our conception of an IS help as we face this inevitably changing nature of organiza tional work? Our first assertion, which is related to synergy, argues for finding a synergy between the social and the technical systems and implies that the enhancement of human intelligence (or more broadly, abilities) by com puters can happen only when there is a synergy between humans and IT. Therefore, our work proposes that intel ligence augmentation is not only about ‘computers enhancing human intelligence’ (Jain et al., 2018, p. 250) but also about finding and promoting acceptable levels of synergy between them. Our second assertion, which is that the social and technical interact along an affording–constraining relationship, allows us to investigate the natur of affordances and constraints that the technology provides and how such affordances and constraints bolster or compromise intelligence augmentation. For example, when working in isolation and in the absence of co-located colleagues, will workers expect some unique affordances from the technologies at their disposal? Or will the excessive reliance on technologies constrain their work, and if so, how will they work around those constraints? Put simply, what are the affordances that are relevant to strengthen intelligence augmentation in a stay-at-hom workplace, and how can we design technologies that can deliver such affordances? These unique affordances sup port the increasingly distributed, fluid, and autonomous nature of work that happens during stay-at-home order and merit new investigation.

Using the view of information as reduction of entropy, we can investigate how information needs are being redefined in this increasingly fluid and autonomous workspace, where the workers are becoming increasingly de coupled due to lack of co-location. One may question whether (and how) the efficacy of such fluid work environ ments is compromised if the information needs of the workers are not met. Does the fluid workspace become mor chaotic in that case, and if so, how can organizations address some of the emerging chaos and bring about som semblance of order in the new home-based workplaces? These are interesting questions for future investigation

The new form of fluid work during stay-at-home orders also provides opportunities for interesting discussion (and investigations) of multifinality and equifinality. Workers are seemingly being seen as productive (or maybe even more productive) in such fluid and autonomous workspaces, leading us to conclude that the same work outcome can be realized in quite different ways—by being co-located in office space, or by staying at home and increasingly relying on information and communication technologies (an example of equifinality). If so, how can organization leverage this equifinality for achieving excellence and success?

In addition to investigations on equifinality, it would be interesting to study whether humanistic outcomes such as work–life balance are being supported/compromised along with the economic demands of productivity in such fluid and autonomous workspaces. Does being part of such workspaces increase (or decrease) worker satisfaction and place les (or more) strain on their personal lives? If so, how do the conceptions of affordances, synergy, and information contribut to understanding of such multifinal outcomes? Such questions can be fruitfully investigated by future research.

In sum, our conception of an IS allows for new investigations into the nature and factors salient to strengthening aug mented intelligence and new forms of work in a stay-at-home workplace. We believe this usefulness of our conception to studying emergent phenomena, such as the post COVID-19 work landscape, will be of value to the IS community

## 7 | CONCLUSION

To conclude, we hope that in this paper we have raised relevant issues related to the fundamental nature of IS research while presenting our own view on the conception of the IS artifact. We are optimistic that the IS academic communit will engage in this debate regarding this conception of what is central to defining our discipline, that is, the notion of what an IS artifact is, and consequently what an ‘IS’ is. It should be kept in mind that our formulation of the IS artifact is but one possibility, and there could be many others. For example, the IS discipline has see alternate conceptions such as work systems (Alter, 2013) or neo-STS (Winter et al., 2014) to capture the fun damental underpinnings of IS research and these could be used for alternate conceptions of the IS artifact Another way forward to conceptualize the IS artifact is to incorporate recent developments on complexity theory (CT) and complex adaptive systems (CAS) in IS (Benbya, Nan, Tanriverdi, & Yoo, 2020; Haki, Beese, Aier, & Winter, 2020). Indeed, there are commonalities between GST and CT (Schneider & Somers, 2006). CT does include some additional considerations such as the conception of edge of chaos <sup>6</sup> (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1998) and unpredictable emergence of outcomes (Benbya et al., 2020). Given the significant similarities between CT and GST, we feel that it would be a worthwhile attempt to integrate both GST and CT into a comprehensive, and perhaps new, framework to guide future IS research and practice, and we invite work in this area. In this regard, we hope that our articulation of the key tenets of GST will inspire future researchers to engage in a critical reflection of such alternate formulations to decipher commonalities and derive a meta conception of the unique nature of research and practice in our discipline. As IS researchers, we all gain to benefit from such discussions and debates.

T A B L E A 2 Various forms of interplay between the social and the technical components in an IS (Adapted from: Sarker et al. (2019))

<table><tr><td>Type of interplay</td><td>Definition and example(s)</td></tr><tr><td>Mutual appropriation</td><td>Here, the social and technical components of an IS mutually appropriate (shape) each other. An example of this interplay is a study of the coevolution of technology and work routines through the implementation of an IT system (Goh et al., 2011).</td></tr><tr><td>Unidirectional causality</td><td>Here, the conception is that either IT drives social outcomes or social structures and processes influence IT. An example of the former would be the Electronic Market Hypothesis (Malone, Yates, &amp; Benjamin, 1987), while an example of the latter would be a study that illustrates how organizational characteristics influence logical design of IT systems (Cooper, Watson, Wixom, &amp; Goodhue, 2000).</td></tr><tr><td>Social in a technology context</td><td>Here, IT is often the context of theorization but does not explicitly feature in the theorization itself. An example is a study on CIO reporting structure (Banker et al., 2011).</td></tr><tr><td>Technology in a social context</td><td>Here, the focus is primarily on the technical aspects of IS, such as development of software, or algorithms. Social issues are often in the background and not explicitly part of the study. An example is the design of a data-masking method with an aim to secure private information against record linkage disclosure (Li &amp; Sarkar, 2011).</td></tr><tr><td>Embeddedness of the social within the technical</td><td>Here, social considerations are often embedded within technical artifacts. An example is the design of group systems using theories of ethics (Chatterjee, Sarker, &amp; Fuller, 2009).</td></tr><tr><td>Constitutive entanglements of the social and the technical</td><td>Here, ontological differences between social and technical are ignored, and they are always treated as being ‘constitutively entangled’, that is, not existing separately from each other. An example would be to treat a mobile device and its user as constitutively entangled in the phenomenon of mobile communication (Orlikowski, 2007).</td></tr><tr><td>Imbrications between the social and the technical</td><td>Here, the social and the technical are considered distinct, but they can be arranged in patterns that overlap; such arrangement ensures they can function in an interdependent manner. An example is a study which shows how organizational routines and technologies get continuously shaped and re-shaped by changing imbrications between social and technical elements in a system (Leonardi, 2011).</td></tr></table>
