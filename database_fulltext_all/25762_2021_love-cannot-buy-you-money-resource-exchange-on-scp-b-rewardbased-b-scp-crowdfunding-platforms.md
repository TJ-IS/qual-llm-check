# Love cannot buy you money: Resource exchange on reward-based crowdfunding platforms

## Abstract

Reward-based crowdfunding platforms – in which campaigns exchange rewards for financial backing to develop a product or service – are one of the fastest-growing segments of the crowdfunding industry. We use an extension of social exchange theory (SET) called the resource theory of social exchange (RTSE) to examine resource exchanges through rewards on Kickstarter. A resource exchange occurs when a project backer and project creator exchange money (ie, financial backing) for a reward (eg, a thank you or a t-shirt). A project creator can develop a reward portfolio that contains various types of resources, which in the RTSE are categorised as love, status, information, money, goods and services. Our study provides a comprehensive examination of resource exchange on a major reward-based crowd funding platform, answering the call to investigate the effects of a key element of such platforms – rewards. We find that the types of resources project creators include in the reward portfolios they offer should be carefully considered. Specifically, our results indicate it is more beneficial to offer rewards that contain universal and concrete resources (eg, goods, services) than resources that are particularistic and symbolic (eg, love, status). However, the positive effect of offering universal and concrete resources as rewards is diminished as the fundraising goal is increased, which suggests that the optimal design of the reward portfolio is contingent on other characteristics of the campaign. Moreover, our findings reveal that while it is advantageous to offer more rewards, it is disadvantageous to offer too many different types of resources across those rewards. Overall, our study adds depth to the understanding of resource exchange in reward-based crowdfunding and provides practical insight into how to design reward portfolios.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Scholarly research defines crowdfunding as ‘a collective effort by people who network and pool their money together, usually via the Internet, in order to invest in and support efforts initiated by other people or organizations’ (Fisk et al., 2011 p. 444). Reward-based crowdfunding platforms are one of the largest and fastest-growing segments of th crowdfunding industry (Mollick, 2014). A crowdfunding platform (eg, Kickstarter, GoFundMe) provides the infrastructur (eg, website, payment processing) to support project creators, who are the entities or individuals with the project ide that they want to raise money to support, and project backers, who are the individuals providing money to the proposed projects (Gerber & Hui, 2013). A project creator uses the crowdfunding platform to create a project campaign, which is website that is used to explain the project idea, introduce the project creators and solicit funds from project backers dur ing a defined funding period. An exchange on Kickstarter consists of a project backer giving money to a project creator either with or without the promise of a defined benefit in return. A reward is a resource or bundle of resources offered by the project creator (ie, a defined benefit) in return for a defined financial contribution the project backer makes to th project campaign. The resource exchanges of rewards for financial contributions are the focus of this study.

Crowdfunding platforms are relatively new. Arguably, the most prominent reward-based crowdfunding platform is Kickstarter, which was launched in 2009.<sup>1</sup> To date, Kickstarter has facilitated over \$5.3 billion (USD) in transactions from more than 18.7 million backers across more than 190 000 successfully funded entrepreneurial projects since its inception.<sup>2</sup> As crowdfunding become increasingly popular, it is important to better understand what project creators can do to increase their chances of successfully obtaining the necessary funding for their projects. Many crowdfunding studies have focused on how project creators should craft their pitch to increase their chances of suc cessful funding outcomes (eg, Anglin et al., 2018; Bi et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2017) and explored the motives of th participants (eg, Bretschneider et al., 2014; Bretschneider & Leimeister, 2017; Gerber & Hui, 2013; Jian & Shin, 2015). However, less attention has been paid to the key element driving contributions – the rewards the project campaign offers in exchange for financial contributions. In fact, one recent study states the most distinctive feature of reward-based crowdfunding–rewards–has seldom been studied (Simons et al., 2017, p. 4346), which highlight the need to study the reciprocal nature of crowdfunding that is driven by the exchanges that occur between the pro ject creators and backers. Our study helps fill this gap by drawing from an extension of social exchange theory (SET called the resource theory of social exchange (RTSE; Mitchell et al., 2012). We use the RTSE as our theoretical foundation to examine how the types of resources exchanged as rewards on Kickstarter drive successful funding out comes. The RTSE defines types of resources (eg, love, status, goods, services) that can be exchanged and examines the characteristics of the resource types (ie, particularistic vs universalistic and concrete vs symbolic; Foa, 1971; Foa & Foa, 2012). For example, a good is a type of resource that is both concrete and universalistic. A good, fo example, a t-shirt, is tangible (concrete) and retains the same value regardless of the relationship between th exchanging parties (universalistic).

On reward-based crowdfunding platforms, the main mechanism through which funding is acquired is through defined resource exchanges or direct reciprocity. The first step of a resource exchange on Kickstarter occurs when the project creator defines a set of rewards and the contribution amount required to obtain each reward. This reward portfolio (ie, the rewards and the funding amounts attached to them) is displayed on the campaign website. The sec ond step occurs when a project backer chooses a reward from the reward portfolio on the campaign website and contributes the specified amount of money to obtain the selected reward. The final step occurs when the projec creator gives the project backer the chosen reward. An example resource exchange might be a project backer contributes \$10 USD to a project creator's campaign and in return, the project creator sends the project backer a t-shirt. In the parlance of SET, transactions in which money is exchanged quid pro quo for a defined reward are referred to as economic exchanges. The reward portfolio offered by the project creator is thus key to successfully attracting funding on reward-based crowdfunding platforms. Our study provides a comprehensive examination of resource exchange on Kickstarter, through which insights are provided regarding how to develop reward portfolios to more successfully attract funding.

Crowdfunding research has only begun to delve into the complexities of designing reward portfolios on crowdfunding platforms. To date, these efforts have largely focused on examining the number of rewards that pro ject creators offer in their reward portfolios. The results have been mixed, with some studies indicating that offering more rewards increases the chances of funding success (Kunz et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2016; Yin et al., 2019) and others finding offering more rewards decreases it (Chen et al., 2016; Xiao et al., 2014). A few studies have examine characteristics of the rewards, such as if backers prefer the middle options in a portfolio of rewards or if offering exclusive or limited rewards is advantageous (Adam et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2016; Simons et al., 2017; Thürridl & Kamleitner, 2016; Wessel et al., 2019). We extend this area of research by focusing on the types of resources exchanged for financial contributions through reward portfolios and how the choice of resources offered for exchange affects the funding outcomes of the campaigns. The design of the reward portfolio is a critical decision i the development of a project campaign and what constitutes an effective design is not yet well understood. B examining the content of rewards in detail, we are able to highlight the types of resources that project creators can offer to help successfully attract funding.

To summarize, we draw from SET (Mitchell et al., 2012) and the RTSE (Foa & Foa, 1980; Foa & Foa, 2012) to examine resource exchange on Kickstarter. Rewards, in which backers exchange resources (eg, a ‘thank you’, visibility in a ‘hall of fame’, a t-shirt, early access to the product) for financial contributions are a key component of reward-based crowdfunding platforms such as Kickstarter. However, little research has specifically focused on rewards (Simons et al., 2017), especially on how the specific types of resources offered as rewards may affect the success of crowdfunding campaigns. Filling this gap is of specific interest to project creators because such knowledge can provide insight into how to best construct a reward portfolio that maximizes the chances of successful funding Our study provides insight into the construction of reward portfolios by revealing (a) the types of resources, (b) th number of resource types and (c) the number of rewards that are most likely to result in successful funding out comes. The RTSE provides the basis to classify the resources offered as rewards and to develop the theoretical argu ments for what types of resources may be the most effective for project creators to offer as rewards

We examine the mechanisms of resource exchange on Kickstarter using a sample of 1001 project campaigns from the technology category. We selected the technology category for two reasons: (a) it includes projects that are of particular interest to information systems (IS) researchers and practitioners, and more importantly, (b) it provides mix of the main types of consumable goods (ie, search and experience goods). An experience good is defined as good that a person needs to experience (ie, use) to determine his or her opinion of it (eg, music, software, restau rants), whereas a search good does not require use to evaluate it (eg, consumer electronics such as cameras or iPads; Li & Wu, 2018; Mudambi & Schuff, 2010; Rönkkö et al., 2009). Hardware (eg, fitness trackers, cameras) is classifie as a search good, whereas software (eg, apps, desktop software, webpages) is classified as an experience good (Li & Wu, 2018; Mudambi & Schuff, 2010; Rönkkö et al., 2009). Because the technology category includes both search and experience goods (e.g., hardware and software), we can control for the type of product for which funding i being requested. Moreover, our data set is representative of projects on Kickstarter because other primary catego ries also contain both search and experience goods.

Our results reveal that offering universalistic and concrete resources as rewards is more likely to result in successfu funding outcomes. That is, project creators should populate their reward portfolios with tangible resources that have the same value no matter who gives them (e.g., money, goods). Project campaigns with reward portfolios that offer par ticularistic and symbolic resources as rewards are less likely to achieve funding success. Moreover, while offering larger number of rewards in the campaign's reward portfolio is more likely to result in successful funding, offering mor types of resources (e.g., information, goods, services) as rewards in the reward portfolio is less likely to result in funding success. We also perform a post hoc analysis to examine the interaction effects between the resources offered as rewards and the campaign goal (i.e., how much money the project campaign sought to raise). We find that the positiv effect of offering more rewards on funding success is diminished as the size of the campaign goal increases. Conversely, the negative effect of offering more types of resources as rewards on funding success is attenuated as the campaign goal increases. Moreover, the positive effect of offering universal and concrete rewards is diminished as the campaign goal increases. This indicates that project campaigns hoping to raise large amounts of money may be able to incorporate symbolic and particularistic resources into their reward portfolios with more success than smaller campaigns. Therefore the optimal design of the reward portfolio may not be the same for all campaigns, which reinforces the importance of thoughtfully designing it to maximize the chances of funding success. In our view, these findings, grounded in the RTSE offer scholars the means through which to take a critical view of emerging practices on crowdfunding platforms and provide more robust guidance for the effective design and development of reward-based project campaigns

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | SET and the RTSE

Research notes that ‘the term social exchange “theory” is a bit misleading’ because ‘social exchange does not involve a solitary conceptual model, but rather refers to a family of related theoretical frameworks (Mitchell et al., 2012 p. 99). In fact, SET has evolved through work in multiple disciplines, including sociology (e.g., Blau, 1964; Gouldner, 1960) and socia psychology (e.g., Clark & Mills, 1979; Mills & Clark, 1982), which has resulted in much overlap but some differences in ter minology and interpretation. Fundamentally, there is agreement that exchanges between individuals create a variety of social obligations, and that in some instances, these exchanges can help develop relationships over time (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). This general agreement led management scholars to organize the SET literature around three key con cepts, namely: the exchange, what is exchanged, and social exchange relationships (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). We focus on the second concept: the resources that are exchanged between project creators and backers. Specifically, we draw from an extension of SET, the RTSE, to examine the influence of what is exchanged on crowdfunding success.

The concept most integral to SET is the exchange, and different types of exchanges and the rules or norms that guide them have been the focus of much SET research (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). Blau (1964, p. 91), one of the preeminent scholars associated with SET, defines exchanges as voluntary actions of individuals that are motivated by the returns they are expected to bring and typically do in fact bring from others’. He differentiates between economic and social exchange by stating that ‘the basic and most crucial distinction is that social exchange entails unspecified obligations , whereas the prototype of an economic transaction rests on a formal contract that stipu lates the exact quantities to be exchanged’ (Blau, 1964, p. 93). Therefore, an economic exchange entails direct an defined reciprocity, in which an individual provides a resource (e.g., service or product) to another in expectation of receiving a defined resource in return. An economic exchange on a reward-based crowdfunding platform thus occurs when a project backer gives money to a project creator in expectation of receiving a defined reward in return. W rely on an extension of SET, the RTSE (Foa & Foa, 2012) to examine direct reciprocity through defined economi exchanges on Kickstarter.

In the RTSE, Foa and Foa (2012, 1974, 1980; Foa, 1971) extend research on exchanges by focusing on the types of resources that can be passed between two parties. A resource in the RTSE is defined as ‘anything that can b transmitted from one person to another’ and it is stated that a resource can be ‘things as different as a smile, a check, a haircut, a newspaper, a reproachful glance and a loaf of bread’ (Foa & Foa, 2012, p. 16). Foa (1971) devel oped a framework that integrates both economic and noneconomic resource exchanges, the former being the realm of economists and the latter being the domain of sociologists and social psychologists. Behaviour can be influenced by the resources offered and hence Foa (1971) reasoned a first step towards better understanding exchange rules (eg, what resource was substitutable for another) was to be able to classify resources and understand their similari ties and differences. The primary contribution of the RTSE is to group resources that can be exchanged into six types, which we list and provide definitions for in Table 1. The RTSE identified six distinct types of resources and defined each type. For example, love is one type of resource in the RTSE that Foa (1971, p. 346) defines as ‘an expression of affectionate regard, warmth, or comfort .

Once the six types of resources were identified and defined, the RTSE assessed characteristics that could cluste the resource types together into larger groupings, which is necessary in order to determine which resource type people consider similar and thus may prompt similar exchange behaviours or be substitutable (Foa, 1971; Foa & Foa 2012). The RTSE identified two characteristics that could be used to determine which resource types were similar in nature and thus people may treat and react to similarly during an exchange. Specifically, the RTSE proposed that th six resource types could be compared across two dimensions (ie, characteristics): (a) particularistic versus universalis tic and (b) symbolic versus concrete. That is, resource types may be characterised as concrete or symbolic and thus be more or less similar; for example, a person can hand a dollar to a cashier in exchange for a piece of candy and bot resources are tangible (ie, concrete) but one is money and the other is a good.

Foa and Foa (1974, 1980, 2012, 1971) cluster the resource types along a spectrum of particularism. The receipt of some resources means more (ie, is of more value) coming from particular parties (Foa, 1971). In other words, for certain resources, the perceived value of a resource depends on who gives it. Love is the most particularistic of the six resources because it matters a great deal from whom love is received. Status and services are also particularistic Status means more when it is granted from particular people, and the perceptions of the value of services are also tied to those who perform them. On the other end of the spectrum, money is universalistic; it does not change in value no matter who gives it. Information and goods are also universalistic because their perceived values are more independent than not from who provides them. Foa and Foa (1974, 1980, 2012) suggest that money is more universalistic than information and goods, and that love is more particularistic than status and services. However, because some of the resources are more similar with others in how they are evaluated, the six resource types can be clustered into two larger groupings: particularistic resource types (love, status, services) and universalistic resource types (money, information, goods). In the absence of additional context, the RTSE notes that a person who is given a particularistic resource is likely to reciprocate with a particularistic resource. In other words, similar resources (eg particularistic) are more likely to be substitutable (Foa & Foa, 1980). However, Foa (1971) states that the resourc types exchanged depend on the context. For example, in a gift giving context, a gift (eg, a good) given to a friend may be reciprocated by love due to the existing social relationship (Foa, 1971).

The RTSE also clusters the six resource types on a spectrum of concreteness. Foa and Foa (1974, 1980, 2012, 1971) propose that some resources are transmitted in tangible ways (e.g., handing an object to or performing an action for another) and thus are considered to be concrete. For example, a person can hand another person a t-shirt (good) or give another person a haircut (service). Therefore, goods and services are classified as concrete (Foa, 1971). Symbolic resources are transmitted via verbal or body language (e.g., posture, facial expressions, gestures). For example, a perso can tell another person the release date for a product (information) or add a person's name to a hall of fame (status) Therefore, information and status are classified as symbolic (Foa, 1971). The RTSE argues that love can be both con crete (e.g., a hug) and symbolic (e.g., a smile). On Kickstarter, love is expressed symbolically (eg, saying thank you because physical expressions of love are less likely online and less appropriate in our context. We thus consider love i its symbolic form and group it with information and status. The RTSE also argues that money in hard currency format is different from a credit card transaction, which was likely truer in the 1970s. Money in virtual form is very commo today and we would argue that it is considered similarly to hard currency. We thus consider money to be concrete and group it with goods and services. Therefore, the six resource types can be clustered into two groupings: symbolic resource types (love, status, information) and concrete resource types (money, goods, services). Experimentation reveals that people have similar preferences for resource types that are similar on a characteristic (eg, particularistic Foa & Foa, 2012). For example, if love is the preferred resource type, status and information will be chosen if love is not available. This implies that the groupings of resource types, where the groupings are determined by the RTSE char acteristics (e.g., concrete), are considered similarly. We test both groupings of the six resource types defined in th RTSE to determine if offering resource types that are (a) particularistic (love, status, services) or (b) universalistic (money, information, goods) in nature, or alternatively resource types that are (a) symbolic (love, status, information) or (b) concrete (money, goods, services) in nature, as rewards are more likely to result in successful funding outcomes.

Greenberg et al. (2013) examined several crowdfunding websites and found different exchange mechanisms: (a) direct: party-to-party, transferred by the platform, (b) indirect: party-to-party, screened, approved and transferred by the platform, and (c) inaccessible: no mechanism for exchange. Using the RTSE resource types, they also identified examples of exchanges on crowdfunding platforms in which project backers received information, status, love, services and goods. This work established the viability of using the RTSE to examine resource exchanges on reward-based crowdfunding platforms. We extend this initial study by coding the resource types (e.g., love, good) in the reward offered by project campaigns on Kickstarter and determining how the resource type groupings (e.g., symbolic and con crete) offered in the reward portfolios influence funding outcomes. To do so, we first coded the resource types offered in each reward for each Kickstarter campaign. In Figure 1, we show an example reward that contains each of the six resources: love, status, information, money, services and goods. For example, one of the campaigns in our data offered a \$10 coupon (money) as a reward. We provide the coding template used to code the resource types in each reward in Appendix A. Second, we grouped the resource types offered in the campaign's reward portfolio by each characteristi described in the RTSE. That is, we counted how many love, information and status resources were offered across the reward portfolio of a campaign and labelled this count ‘symbolic resources’. We use the groupings of similar resourc types to analyse the influence of reward portfolio design on funding outcomes

## 2.2 | Review of crowdfunding research

Research on crowdfunding can be grouped into three major categories that each focus on an essential element: (a) the project for which funding is being sought, (b) the motives of the participants (ie, project creators and backers) and (c) the rewards being offered to entice funding. First, research has often focused on the campaign pitch, which i the descriptive multimedia used to explain the project for which funding is being requested. The campaign pitch is critical element of project campaigns because it is intended to convince potential project backers of the merit of the proposed idea for which funding is being sought. Therefore, many researchers have concentrated their efforts on identifying elements of a campaign pitch that are critical to funding success or ascertaining the importance of mar keting the pitch to the success of campaigns (Anglin et al., 2018; Burtch et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2016; Davis et al., 2017; Gleasure et al., 2019; Gleasure & Feller, 2016a; Kim et al., 2017; Kunz et al., 2017; Majumdar & Bose, 2018; Manning & Bejarano, 2017; Mollick, 2014; Xiao et al., 2014; Yuan et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2015). Other studies have examined elements of the product for which funding is being requested such as backers' perceptions of its innovativeness or quality, or alternatively, backers' perceptions of the creators pitching the project such as their ability or prior experience (Allison et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2017; Liang et al., 2019; Lukkarinen et al., 2016; Wang & Yang, 2019). Quality perceptions have been inferred through how the pitch is developed (eg, the number of words used to describe the project or the inclusion of videos), and likes and counts of online reviews have been used to represent electronic word-of-mouth, both of which can be used to predict contribution decisions (Bi et al., 2017). A great deal of research has thus gone into determining factors that contribute to a successful campaign pitch.

Second, studies have investigated specific motives of project backers; that is, why people contribute to crowdfunding campaigns (Gerber & Hui, 2013; Ryu et al., 2016; Ryu & Kim, 2016; Steigenberger, 2017). For exam ple, studies have identified that prosocial and make-it-happen motives, which describe scenarios in which backers believe in the product and feel their participation is critical, can increase campaign success (Dai & Zhang, 2019; Zvilichovsky et al., 2018). Identified motives for contributors include, for example, fun, community, image, altruism, reciprocity, rewards and curiosity (Bretschneider et al., 2014; Bretschneider & Leimeister, 2017; Gerber & Hui, 2013; Jian & Shin, 2015). Researchers have also examined if localised altruism is magnified by social relations and socia norm compliance and identified the existence of a bias towards projects in a similar geographical area (Giudic et al., 2018; Lin & Viswanathan, 2015). How backers respond when their identities can be concealed has also been a topic of interest (Burtch et al., 2015, 2016). Social capital, including the effect of obligation and shared meaning, has been studied as a driver of funding outcomes (Foster, 2019; Kang et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2014; Zvilichovsky et al., 2015). Other studies have examined how community is developed and maintained around an innovative prod uct (Bitterl & Schreier, 2018; Gleasure & Feller, 2016b; Gleasure & Feller, 2016c). The importance of community can also be seen in studies that examine the effect of interaction (ie, comments and responses) between the project backers and creators on funding success (José Planells, 2017; Wang et al., 2018; Xiao et al., 2014). Studies have also begun to classify project creators by their motivations to participate in crowdfunding (Gerber & Hui, 2013; Ryu & Kim, 2018). This research contributes to explaining why people participate in crowdfunding, both why they use it to secure funding and why people contribute to the projects pitched.

Finally, while less attention has been paid to rewards, some studies have begun to examine their influence. Most studies have focused on the number of rewards or the value of the rewards offered. For example, one study found that the higher the price of the highest priced reward, the more likely the project was to achieve successful funding outcomes (Xiao et al., 2014). Research has also indicated a downside to offering too many rewards; that is, the more rewards offered the lower the chances of successful funding outcomes (Chen et al., 2016; Xiao et al., 2014). How ever, findings regarding the number of rewards offered have been inconsistent because offering more rewards has also been demonstrated to increase the chances of funding success (Kunz et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2016; Yin et al., 2019). Researchers have also discovered that limiting the number of a particular reward offered and adding rewards later in the funding period can increase funding success (Lin et al., 2016). Similarly, another study found that exclusive (i.e., limited) rewards were more common in successfully funded projects and that offering the product for pre-purchase as a reward was also an appealing option (Thürridl & Kamleitner, 2016). Arguments have also bee made for a moderate number of rewards (Zhang & Chen, 2019). Therefore, research has not agreed on how many rewards should be offered, nor has it provided insight into how the content of the offered rewards effects th chances of funding success.

Other studies have investigated more nuanced questions related to rewards. For example, Simons et al. (2017 found evidence of a middle-option bias, suggesting that if a menu of rewards is offered, project backers may have a tendency to favour the options in the middle, which on Kickstarter would mean a preference for the mid-priced options given that rewards are listed in order of price. This suggests how rewards are positioned in the reward portfolio may matter. Having limited availability rewards that have sold-out may also increase successful funding out comes (Adam et al., 2019; Wessel et al., 2019). Another study used a game-theoretic approach to show that offering a product line, that is, multiple versions of the product being produced at differing quality levels, is better than offering a single product version as a reward (Hu et al., 2015). Researchers have thus investigated the effect of rewards, but their investigations have been largely limited to exploring the number of rewards offered and with inconsistent results. Our study contributes to the reward category of crowdfunding research by establishing an approach to examine the effects of reward content grounded in the RTSE.

Reward-based crowdfunding provides a unique context in which to examine resource exchange through the lens of the RTSE because such exchanges are the fundamental mechanisms underlying the phenomenon of reward-based crowdfunding. That is, people provide money to support other's ideas on reward-based crowdfunding platforms, in large part, because they get a defined reward in return. Although our study provides an examination of resource exchange on a major crowdfunding platform drawing from an extension of SET, the RTSE, it is not the first to identify SET as a useful lens through which to investigate this context. Other researchers have broached the connection between SET and crowdfunding (Greenberg et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2017; Zvilichovsky et al., 2015). For example Zhao et al. (2017) examined how trust and commitment are generated on crowdfunding platforms to increase funding intentions, which helps establish the applicability of SET to the crowdfunding context. Notably, researchers have connected the RTSE with crowdfunding platforms by demonstrating that they support the exchange of all the resource types described in the RTSE (Greenberg et al., 2013). Although the researchers identified that the RTSE was applicable to the crowdfunding context, they did not explicitly test the influence of the resource types exchanged through rewards, rather the intent of the work was to introduce the RTSE as a useful lens through which crowdfunding platforms could be better understood. Finally, the importance of project creators also being project backers (ie, indirect reciprocity) on crowdfunding success has been identified (Davies & Giovannetti, 2018; Kunz et al., 2017; Zvilichovsky et al., 2015). These studies show that backing others' campaigns results in higher success rates for the prior backers' own campaigns. We extend these past efforts by empirically testing specific resource exchanges. Specifically, we delve deeply into the types of resources offered as rewards to determine how the choices project creators make when developing their reward portfolios ultimately influence the success or failure of their project campaigns.

## 3 | HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

On reward-based crowdfunding platforms, the main mechanism through which funding is obtained is the economic exchange of financial contributions for defined rewards. These resource exchanges are acknowledged to be of criti cal importance to crowdfunding (Gerber & Hui, 2013; Lin et al., 2016; Simons et al., 2017; Steigenberger, 2017), but how the reward portfolios should be constructed to improve funding outcomes remains an open question. To pro vide insight into the construction of reward portfolios, we hypothesize, in the following two subsections, how each resource type grouping (ie, particularistic, universalistic, concrete and symbolic) influences funding outcomes.

## 3.1 | Particularistic versus universalistic rewards

One characteristic that can be used to group the resource types is how particularistic people consider the resource type to be love, status and services resource types are considered to be more particularistic; and money, information and goods more universalistic in the RTSE (Foa, 1971; Foa & Foa, 1974, 1980, 2012). Sharing a characteristic like th degree of particularism with another type of resource is important, according to the logic of RTSE, because peoples exchange behaviours may be similar for resource types that they consider to be similar in nature. For example, a per son who wants a love-resource will be more likely substitute it with another resource that is more particularistic (eg status) if love is not available (Foa & Foa, 2012). Similarity and substitution were explored within the RTSE by conducting an experiment in which participants were given a type of resource and then asked to reciprocate from a selection of resource types that did not include the resource type they had been given (Foa, 1971; Foa & Foa, 1980) The result was that recipients were more likely to give a proximal resource type in return than a distal one; that is they reciprocated with a resource type in the same resource type grouping as the gift (Foa & Foa, 1980). This sug gests that a project backer, who is donating money to a project campaign, may be more likely to choose a universalis tic resource type as a reward because money is a universalistic resource type. Universalistic resource types proxima to money are information and goods. Money may also be exchanged for money on Kickstarter, but rather than cur rency, rewards may offer discounts or coupons. For example, one reward on Kickstarter offered a \$10 discount on the final product.

Marketing researchers extended Foa and Foa's work on the RTSE to examine the impact of resource exchanges in transactional settings (Brinberg & Wood, 1983). Their research generally confirms that transaction partners tend to exchange resource types that are similar (eg, universalistic resource types tend to be exchanged with universalistic resource types). In addition to finding that proximal resource types were more likely to be exchanged, they also found that when resources were perceived to be abundant, exchange partners were more likely to exchange univer salistic resource types (Brinberg & Wood, 1983). This indicates that without limits on rewards, project backers wil be more likely to choose rewards that offer universalistic resource types (ie, money, information and goods). Brinberg and Wood (1983) conclude that which resource types are exchanged depends on the initial resource type given, the grouping of that initial resource type, and external constraints (eg, resource scarcity). The RTSE has also been use to examine how customers respond to corporate service failures to find that customers expect organizations to match their repair attempts to the perceived magnitude of the service failing (Smith et al., 1999). That is, consumers expect companies to provide recompense that is similar, with regard to both type and amount of resource, to th experienced failure. These findings all indicate that because money is the resource type provided in the exchange b the project backer, a universalistic reward (money, information or good) will be most desirable as a reward from the project creator.

RTSE researchers have concluded that the size of the overall group, institutional settings and the mutual dependence among market actors shape the exchange of different types of resources (Dorsch et al., 2017; Foa & Foa, 1980; Griesinger, 1990). In general, there is a preference between exchange partners and exchange resource types that are the same or are considered similar in terms of their characteristics (eg, particularistic or universalistic Brinberg & Castell, 1982; Brinberg & Wood, 1983; Foa & Foa, 1980). Satisfaction with the exchange is also dependent upon the exchange of similar (or the same) types of resources (Teichman & Foa, 1975). People are more satis fied with exchanges of similar resource types than with dissimilar ones (eg, money for love; Dorsch et al., 2017; Teichman & Foa, 1975). Researchers have concluded that ‘perceived injustice of an inequitable exchange may occur when one or both exchange partners receive a smaller (larger) amount of a social resource than expected or receive resources that differ from their expectations’ (Dorsch et al., 2017, p. 17). Research thus indicates that people desire exchanges of resource types that are perceived to be of similar value

Many Kickstarter campaigns offer different versions of the product that funding is being raised to develop as a reward (Hu et al., 2015). Evidence of consumption motives have been uncovered among project backers, with on study stating that ‘even though supporters may delay gratification, they are motivated to increase their funding amount to get a desired reward, [which] suggests that supporters are aware of the value of the product, service o experience that they will receive in exchange for the financial support’ (Gerber & Hui, 2013, p. 14). Presented wit different options of the product being developed on a reward-based crowdfunding platform, contrary to a traditiona economic transaction, research has found that project backers with high product valuations ‘may choose the high price option to ensure the success of the crowdfunding project’ (Hu et al., 2015, p. 332). Such findings suggest a strong consumption motive on reward-based crowdfunding platforms. Moreover, the findings imply that project backers assign their own value to rewards and choose a reward they consider matches their valuation. Therefore, w expect that offering more universalistic resource types as rewards will generate a higher level of funding becaus these rewards are implicitly appealing to the economic motivations of backers and are easier for them to valuate because their value is not dependent upon who gives them.

Exchange relationships are impacted by the extent of prior social ties between actors (Frenzen & Davis, 1990) Recent developments in SET contend that social ties not only influence exchange relationships, but also are directl formed through exchange relationships (Kuwabara, 2011). Even during pure forms of economic exchange, social tie and cohesive relationships can form between market actors (Kuwabara, 2011). Both the frequency of exchange and the amount of resources exchanged can impact the evolution of the social relationship (Kuwabara & Sheldon, 2012). Prior social ties help determine the value people assign to a particularistic resource type, because by definition, it matters from whom a particularistic resource type is received (Foa, 1971). The exchange of financial backing for a reward is likely to be the initial exchange between the project creator and backer, and lacking prior social ties, universalistic resource types are likely to be preferred to particularistic resource types that are harder to valuate. Rewards that require project backers to assign a value to the giving of love, status or services from people they do not have a prio social relationship with may not be attractive. Furthermore, although the selection of particularistic rewards may signa social support, it is less likely to signal confidence in the product being developed. On Kickstarter, the number of pro ject backers who have claimed each reward is publicly visible. Project backers wanting to signal support for a campaign may thus more reliably do so by choosing universalistic rewards that others can easily attach a value to

Kickstarter campaigns offer both particularistic and universalistic resource types as rewards. For example, a pro ject creator who is raising money to create software may offer to put the project backer's name in the credits of the software as a reward for a financial contribution. Such a reward has little monetary value but provides the project backer with status because the project creator is publicly acknowledging his or her contribution to the project. How ever, the status reward may not mean the same from one software developer to another. From certain project creators, particularistic rewards may give project backers a sense of pride and create a sense of community involve ment or a collective identity around the project (Gleasure & Feller, 2016c). There is evidence that project backer desire to be part of a community, with one study finding that ‘supporters express the desire to see evidence of being part of a select group’ (Gerber & Hui, 2013, p. 15). Although there is some qualitative evidence that project backers may desire to be part of a community, there is little to support that beginning a relationship by taking a more particu laristic reward (ie, love, status or service) would help build community more than taking a more universalistic rewar (ie, information, money or goods). Moreover, research has revealed that the exchange of similar resource types is more likely to grow new friendships than the exchange of dissimilar ones (Törnblom et al., 1987). Therefore, we expect that offering more particularistic rewards will reduce the amount of funding raised by campaigns because these types of resources do not appeal to the consumption motives of the project backers and their valuation i dependent upon prior social ties between exchange partners that are less likely to exist at the start of a crowdfunding campaign.

H1. There is a positive relationship between the number of universalistic resource types offered in the reward portfolios of crowdfunding campaigns and the funding ratio of Kickstarter campaigns.

H2. There is a negative relationship between the number of particularistic resource types offered in the reward portfolios of crowdfunding campaigns and the funding ratio of Kickstarter campaigns.

## 3.2 | Concrete versus symbolic rewards

Another way to group the six types of resources is by how concrete or symbolic people consider the resource typ to be love, status and information are considered to be more symbolic and money, goods and services more concrete in the RTSE (Foa, 1971; Foa & Foa, 1980, 2012). Different groupings of the six resource types are obtained when grouped by the characteristic of concreteness rather than the characteristic of particularism. Concrete resource types are tangible, whereas symbolic resource types are intangible and expressed through verbal or body language Similar logic dictates the exchange behaviours associated with concrete and symbolic resource types; that is, th RTSE demonstrates that proximal resources are more likely to be considered substitutable, and thus be exchanged than distal resources (Foa, 1971; Foa & Foa, 1980, 2012). People are also more satisfied with exchanges of simila resources (Teichman & Foa, 1975). This suggests that financial contributions on Kickstarter (i.e., money) are more likely to be provided in exchange for concrete rewards (i.e., services, goods or money).

Organizational research generally focuses on differences in the characteristics of the resources exchanged, view ing them as economic or social in nature, where economic resources are more concrete and universalistic and socia resources are more symbolic and particularistic (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005; Guo et al., 2017). Cropanzano and Mitchell (2o05) describe how universalistic and concrete resource types are more likely to be short-term exchanges of a quid pro quo fashion, whereas particularistic and symbolic exchanges are often longer-term and may be used to build social relationships. For example, they argue that it is common to see money exchanged for a good, but les common to see money exchanged for love or status. Project backers are providing money to a project campaign and therefore the transactions on Kickstarter are more economic in nature. This logic suggests that project backers ar likely to be more satisfied when their financial contributions are exchanged for rewards offering resource types per ceived to be concrete (ie, services, goods or money).

Cropanzano and Mitchell (2005) describe how the exchange of concrete resource types, such as money, can facilitate trust. Trust is crucial in the formation of social ties through exchange processes (Haas & Deseran, 1981) Communities of individuals interested in seeing a product developed to fruition may arise around Kickstarter cam paigns (Gleasure & Feller, 2016c). Belleflamme et al. (2014, p. 586) note that crowdfunding is ‘often associated wit community-based experiences that generate “community benefits” for participants’. The possibility of building an active community around a product sets crowdfunding apart from traditional economic transactions. A project backe taking a purely utility-maximizing approach would back a project after its funding goal was reached at which point th crowd has signalled its quality and the receipt of rewards is more certain (Dai & Zhang, 2019). However, research ha revealed that funding is collected more rapidly before the funding goal is met, especially when a project's nature evoke a desire to help the project creator, which indicates prosocial motives on Kickstarter (Dai & Zhang, 2019). These results suggest that project backers may prefer concrete resource types as rewards in early exchanges to express a desire t help the project creator as a first step in building a trusting community around the product.

In economic exchanges on crowdfunding platforms, the project backer knows what reward he or she wil receive (ie, the exchange is explicitly defined). However, unlike a traditional economic transaction, the deliver of the reward is often delayed, especially when the reward is the product being developed. Prior research argue that ‘even though supporters may delay gratification, they are motivated to increase their funding amount to get a desired reward , but that the fact that they are willing to pay prior to reward creation and wait weeks o months sets this type of transaction apart from traditional consumer transactions’ (Gerber & Hui, 2013, p. 14) Research on crowdfunding has discovered that trust can reduce perceptions of risk and increase commitment both of which may increase funding intentions (Zhao et al., 2017). It has also been noted that project backer are willing to pay more for the community benefits associated with crowdfunding (Belleflamme et al., 2014). Paying more for delayed gratification and the benefit of participating in a community of like-minded others indi cates the intent to develop social ties that require trust. Project backers who are paying a premium to participat in a crowdfunding community may show a preference for rewards that offer concrete resources that researchers suggest help build trust between new exchange partners (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005).

Therefore, we posit that offering concrete resource types as rewards will increase the funding raised by project campaigns because their relative value is easier to ascertain during the initial stages of the relationship. Concret rewards motivate action when social ties are weak and social obligations are vague and undefined. Furthermore, we argue that the selection of concrete rewards belies the desire to trust and build community through the establish ment of trusting relationships. Symbolic rewards are likely less meaningful between new exchange partners who have not built trust. Project campaigns that can inspire trust and build community are likely to be more successful. Concrete resource types should be more attractive to project backers because they are proximal to the exchange resource type, money and can be exchanged to build trust and establish community that is a key feature of crowdfunding platforms.

H3. There is a positive relationship between the number of concrete resource types offered in the reward portfolios of crowdfunding campaigns and the funding ratio of Kickstarter campaigns

H4. There is a negative relationship between the number of symbolic resource types offered in the reward portfolios of crowdfunding campaigns and the funding ratio of Kickstarter campaigns.

## 4.2 | Operationalization of key variables

In what follows, we describe our dependent, independent and control variables. For convenience, a table of variable definitions is also provided in Appendix B.

## 4.2.1 | Dependent variable

We measured funding success by calculating the ratio of total funding received by the initial goal of the campaign We labelled this variable Funding Ratio. We elected to use this measure in our main models instead of a binary vari able to operationalize success to ensure that we could capture the relative amount of financial success over or under a campaign's fundraising goals (Frydrych et al., 2014). However, to ensure robustness of our findings, we also ran models using alternative dependent variables. Specifically, we used both Campaign Success, which is a binary variable that is 1 if the project reached its funding goal and 0 otherwise, and Number of Backers, which is an integer variabl representing the count of project backers for each campaign.

## 4.2.2 | Independent variables

Consistent with our theoretical arguments, we utilised the following approach for operationalizing our key indepen dent variables. To operationalize the campaign reward resource type groupings (ie, Universalism, Particularism, Con creteness and Symbolism), each of the rewards for each Kickstarter campaign in our sample was coded by hand b members of the research team or hired coders. All coders were trained in the logic of the RTSE and practiced codin campaigns' rewards prior to officially beginning the coding process to address any interpretation difference between how the logic of the RTSE was applied to Kickstarter rewards. We provided the coders with a coding tem plate that included definitions of each resource type (see Table 1), a definition of the resource type on Kickstarter, and example rewards for the resource type. An example for the status resource type from the final template i given below and the full template is provided in Appendix A

Status : ‘an evaluative judgment that conveys prestige, regard, or esteem’ (Foa & Foa, 2012, p. 16). In this pro ject, Status is often expressed through a public acknowledgment of support (eg, identifying contributors in a public forum) offered by the campaign attempting to raise funds in exchange for support.

We conducted several training rounds to ensure consistency between the coders. During each round of training, we had all coders code the rewards from the same campaigns, calculated a Cohen's Kappa, discussed any discrepan cies and updated the template as appropriate. The Cohen's Kappa is calculated to ensure an appropriate level of internal validity. At the last round of training, the calculated score was 0.93, which indicates a high level of interna consistency between coders. After the training period was finished, we had each coder independently code Kickstarter campaign rewards. For each reward, the coders identified which of the six resource types from the RTSE the reward contained.

For LOVE, we coded the love reward type as a ‘1’ if the reward offers a private ‘thank you’ (eg, written card, verbal acknowledgement, email, etc) in exchange for supporting the campaign, and a ‘0’ if the reward does not offer a private expression of love. The RTSE clusters the resource types into higher-level groupings (ie, particularism vs universalism and concrete ness vs symbolism). Importantly, one of the key arguments in the RTSE is that different resource types can be simul taneously linked with multiple groupings based on different characteristics at the same time (Foa & Foa, 1980 Griesinger, 1990). In Table 1, we show the mapping of individual resource types into resource type groupings based on the characteristics identified in the RTSE (ie, particularism and concreteness). Because our theoretical arguments focus on the relationships between these resource type groupings and funding success, we needed to create a set of second-order constructs by aggregating the different resource types into their corresponding resource type group ings. To operationalize these constructs, we counted the number of rewards in each campaign that correspond to each resource type grouping. That is, we counted the number of rewards that offered status, love and service resource types for the particularistic resources, and the number of rewards that offered information, money and goods resource types for the universalistic resources. For symbolic resources, we counted the rewards that offered love, status or information resource types, and for concrete resources, those that offered money, goods or service resource types. We then summed the total number of rewards offered by the campaign that contained any of th resource types corresponding to that resource type grouping (eg, universalistic resources = number of rewards offer ing information resource types + number of rewards offering money resource types + number of rewards offerin goods resource types). These second-order variables are labelled Particularistic Resources, Universalistic Resources Concrete Resources and Symbolic Resources.

## 4.2.3 | Control variables

We controlled for search versus experience goods using a dummy variable, which takes a 1 for search goods and a ‘0’ otherwise. We also developed dummy variables to control for the year in which the campaign was conducted, with 2014 as the omitted category. We coded all campaigns conducted outside of the United States (U.S.) (Foreign) with a 1 , and all U.S.-based campaigns were coded as a 0 . We controlled for the Text Length, which is the natura log of the number of words in the campaign description (‘pitch’). We controlled for the Campaign Goal that is the amount of money the campaign wanted to raise, which we represented in thousands to adjust for variable scale. We also controlled for Campaign Duration, which is the total number of days over which each campaign was conducted. We controlled for the number of previous campaigns created by the campaign creator to rule out the possibility that their Campaigns Created on Kickstarter influenced backer decisions. We also controlled for Prior Backer Behaviour by summing up the total number of other Kickstarter campaigns the project creator had backed. We controlled for project backers' altruistic behaviours using Altruistic Contributions and Altruistic Backers. We defined Altruistic Contributions as the difference between the total amount of donated funds a project campaign received and the sum of the amount of funds donated over all of the rewards offered by the project. This value was divided by the total funding received to obtain a ratio of altruism for each project campaign. In our data, we can determine how many project backers selected a particular reward and how much money the backer was required to contribute to obtain that reward. Altruistic contributions therefore account for the amount of donated funds a project has received about those contributed for specified rewards. Similarly, Altruistic Backers is the number of project backers who donated to the project campaign without selecting a reward.

Findings regarding the effect of the number of rewards offered in campaigns' reward portfolios has been tested in prior research, but the results have been mixed (Chen et al., 2016 Kunz et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2016; Xiao et al., 2014; Yin et al., 2019). Therefore, we begin our analysis by examining the influence of the number of rewards offered in campaigns' reward portfolios on the success of Kickstarter cam paigns (ie, Funding Ratio; Model 2 in Table 3). The number of rewards is the count of the rewards in the project campaign's reward portfolio. Notably, the more rewards offered, the more likely the campaign is to achieve fundin success. This result is in line with findings in other studies (Kunz et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2016; Yin et al., 2019)

Because our focus is on the types of resources offered as rewards, we examine the influence of the number of resource types offered across project campaigns' reward portfolios on the success of Kickstarter campaigns (i.e., Funding Ratio; Model 3 in Table 3). The number of resource types is the count of the resource types (i.e., love, status information, money, services, goods) represented in the project campaign's reward portfolio. Therefore, if the rewar portfolio contained information, status, good and services in their rewards, but not love or money, then the numbe of resource types would equal ‘4’. Somewhat surprisingly, the number of different resource types the campaigns offer decreases the likelihood of funding success. This result suggests that it is better to carefully consider the con tent offered in the rewards rather than offer a large number of rewards encompassing all the different resourc types. Following these general findings, we turn our focus to the content of the reward portfolios by examining th findings for our hypotheses.

Regarding the relationship between the particularism versus universalism of the resource types offered a rewards and the success of Kickstarter campaigns (i.e., Funding Ratio), Hypothesis 1 outlines the logic of the relation ship between the universalism of the resource types offered as rewards and the Funding Ratio of the Kickstarte campaigns. All project campaigns set a campaign goal, which can be interpreted as the project creator's estimation of the funding required to create the product they are pitching. It is possible that the elements of a successful reward portfolio are not uniform across smaller and larger project campaigns, which could indicate that some project campaigns may have more o less latitude regarding how they structure their reward portfolios. Therefore, we conduct a post-hoc analysis to investigate if the size of the campaign goal moderates the effects of the resource type groupings offered in the reward portfolios on funding success. The OLS estimation results for the models with the interaction effects are provided in Table 4.

We examine the interactions between number of rewards and number of resource types with the campaign goal. Our main effect revealed that the more rewards offered in the reward portfolio, the higher the chances of positive funding out comes. The interaction effect shows that this effect is diminished for campaigns with larger funding goals (Model 1 in Table 4). This result indicates that it is not as beneficial for project campaigns with large campaign goals as it is for cam paigns with small campaign goals to offer a lot of rewards. We also found that the more types of resources campaigns offered in their reward portfolios, the lower their chances of positive funding outcomes, but this effect is also diminished as the campaign goal increases (Model 2 in Table 4). The implication of this result is that larger campaigns do not suffe the same penalty for offering a lot of different types of resources in their reward portfolios that smaller campaigns do

Offering particularistic resource types as rewards decreases the chance of positive funding outcomes. However, the interaction between particularistic rewards and the campaign goal is positive (Model 3 in Table 4), which indicates that for larger campaigns offering particularistic rewards may not be as detrimental. Conversely, offering universalistic rewards increases the chance of positive funding outcomes. The interaction between universalistic rewards and th campaign goal is negative (Model 3 in Table 4), which indicates that as the campaign size increases, the positive effect of universalistic rewards is diminished. Similarly, offering symbolic rewards decreases the chance of positive fundin outcomes, but the interaction between symbolic rewards and the campaign goal is positive (Model 4 in Table 4). Thi suggests that the negative effect of symbolic rewards is mitigated for larger campaigns. Although offering concret rewards increases the chance of positive funding outcomes, this effect is diminished for large campaign goals (ie, th interaction between concrete rewards and the campaign goal is negative; Model 4 in Table 4).

These results indicate that proiect campaigns with larger funding goals can offer particularistic and symbolid resource types as rewards without suffering the same penalty as those with smaller funding goals. However, project campaigns with larger funding goals also may not benefit as much from offering universalistic and concrete resourc types as rewards as those with smaller funding goals. One explanation for these results may be that project cam paigns with larger campaign goals are likely to need to attract more project backers. Greater diversity in the rewards offered, with regard to resource types, may give a diverse set of project backers a diverse reward portfolio from which to choose. In other words, there is more likely to be a reward that appeals to more people if there are more resource types offered. The implication of these results is that project campaigns with larger funding goals have mor flexibility, with regard to the resource types they offer, to design their reward portfolios. Project campaigns with smaller funding goals should offer universalistic and concrete resource types as rewards.

The value of particularistic resource types is dependent on the giver, in other words, it matters from whom particularistic resource types are received. The value of love received from a project creator thus depends on the importance of the project creator to the project backer. Symbolic rewards are intangible and conveyed through verbal or body language. Our findings revealed that offering symbolic resource types as rewards (i.e., love, status and information) decreased chances of successful funding outcomes, wherea offering concrete resource types as rewards (i.e., money, goods and services) increased chances of successful fundin outcomes. In our context, where social actors lack prior relationships, exchanges of concrete resource types as rewards can be a more effective basis for establishing relationships because they help build trust. Exchanges of con crete resource types as rewards may lead to more successful crowdfunding campaign outcomes because they can signal trust in the product being developed and the project creator's ability to fulfil the promises made, as well as help build an involved and supportive community for the product post-funding. One implication of our findings is that project creators should construct their reward portfolios from universal and concrete resources. That is, they should offer as rewards resource types that are tangible and mean the same coming from anyone to best ensure funding success.

In general, our findings suggest that project creators are best served offering universalistic and concrete resource types (i.e., information, goods, services, money) as rewards. Moreover, in general, offering more rewards that contain less differentiation in terms of types of resources is also advisable. To add more depth to our analysis of resource exchanges, we conducted a post-hoc analysis to examine how a key component of project campaigns, the campaign goal, interacts with the reward portfolio design to influence funding success. We found that as the size of the campaign goal increases, the effect of the number of rewards on funding success is diminished. Moreover, as the size of the campaign goal increases, the negative impact of the number of resource types offered in the reward portfolios is attenuated. This means that campaigns requesting more money may be able to offer less rewards with more diversity in the resource types offered without suffering as much of a penalty as those asking for smaller funding amounts. Additionally, we examined the interaction effects of the campaign goal with the resource type groupings. We found that for campaigns requesting larger funding amounts, the positive effect of universal and concrete rewards was dampened. Conversely, the interaction effects for particularistic and symbolic rewards were positive indicating that offering these resources types are less detrimental to larger campaigns. Our results therefore demonstrate that crowdfunding campaigns requesting larger funding amounts may have more flexibility with regard to designing their reward portfolios. In summary, we find that the ideal construction of the reward portfolio varies with campaign size (ie, how much total funding the campaign is requesting). One implication of our results is that project creators who are seeking smaller amounts of funding may be most successful offering a reward portfolio composed mainly of concrete and universalistic resource types. Project creators who need to attract a lot of project backers to raise substantial capital may consider making limited use of symbolic and particularistic resource types to add diver sity to their reward portfolios

## 6.1 | Implications for research on the crowdfunding and the RTSE

Our findings regarding how resource exchanges influence funding success indicate that SET, and its extension the RTSE, provide an elegant and effective framework for explaining and advising reward portfolio develop ment on crowdfunding platforms. Due in large part to the relative newness of the phenomenon, research has not yet adequately developed a comprehensive set of theoretical arguments to explore patterns of exchange on crowdfunding platforms. Examinations of rewards on crowdfunding platforms have been lacking from th literature, with one study stating the most distinctive feature of reward-based crowdfunding – rewards – has seldom been studied’ (Simons et al., 2017, p. 4346). To date, research on crowdfunding rewards has emphasised basic characteristics of rewards offered in reward portfolios, such as the number of rewards or limited availability rewards (eg, Kunz et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2016; Yin et al., 2019; Zhang & Chen, 2019). Our study extends this research through an in-depth examination of the content of the rewards project campaigns offer and the effects of the reward portfolio design on funding success. In doing so, we offer theory-grounded insights into how reward portfolios should be constructed to best position the project campaign for successfu funding outcomes. We add nuance to the knowledge about reward portfolio construction by going beyond the ideal number of rewards offered to examine the ideal resource content of offered rewards for different size crowdfunding campaigns. Therefore, our findings help extend the knowledge about an under-researched but key component of crowdfunding – rewards

Drawing from the RTSE, we provide a strong theoretical rationale to explain the nature and structure of exchange on reward-based crowdfunding platforms. We extend research that identified the RTSE as an appropriate lens through which to examine reward-based crowdfunding (Greenberg et al., 2013) by applying it to archival data from a major crowdfunding platform. We thus confirm the appropriateness of the RTSE for this context and provide useful insights into the design of reward portfolios from its application. We also contributed to the broader literature on the RTSE. Although the RTSE is well established as a theory of resource exchange (Dorsch et al., 2017; Foa & Foa, 2012), it is not a theory that has been widely applied in different contexts. Some of the original work on the RTSE suggested a set of exchange rules and thus explored what resources may be satisfactorily substituted for others under particular conditions (Dorsch et al., 2017; Foa, 1971; Foa & Foa, 1980, 2012). The applicability of the RTSE to consumer behaviour has been noted (Dorsch et al., 2017) and marketing researchers have examined exchanges under the RTSE (Brinberg & Castell, 1982; Brinberg & Wood, 1983). We extend the use of the RTSE to a unique IS context in which peopl exchange money for defined rewards to enable entrepreneurial efforts. We provide evidence that all six types of resources are offered as rewards on Kickstarter and demonstrate that project campaigns that experience successful funding outcomes tend to exchange money for resources that are in the same resource type grouping, where the resource type groupings are defined by the particularism and concreteness characteristics described in the RTSE. Our results show that the RTSE is an appropriate lens through which to study resource exchanges on reward-based crowdfunding platforms and applies the theory in a novel context. The economic exchanges in reward-based crowdfunding, in which project backers contribute funding for a defined reward occur early in the project's lifecycle and may be among the earliest exchanges between the parties. Our research shows that rewards offering concrete and universalistic resources may be better suited for these early exchanges in which trust and relationships are being built. Future work could extend our study to examine how these resource exchanges contribute to outcomes after funding is obtained (eg, through the produc development and commercialization phases).

## 6.2 | Implications for crowdfunding practice

Our study also provides important contributions to practice by highlighting the importance of resource exchanges t successfully obtaining funding for crowdfunding project campaigns. In particular, our results contribute to a conversa tion about the importance of designing the reward portfolio of Kickstarter campaigns with an awareness of how it may influence funding success. Specifically, project campaigns with smaller funding goals may want to offer large number of rewards but limit the number of different resource types offered in their reward portfolios. Moreover, they should offer more universal and concrete resources as rewards. Project campaigns with larger funding goals may be able to consider offering a smaller number of rewards with more variation in resource types offered. Project cam paigns with larger campaign goals may also be able to offer more symbolic and particularistic resources as rewards. In summary, our results illustrate that project creators should carefully consider the design of their reward portfolios because it is key to successful funding outcomes. Moreover, one uniform design is not sufficient and the most effective design of the reward portfolio may depend on the campaign goal.

## 6.3 | Limitations

It is important to note that while our study offers several important contributions to both theory and practice, there are limitations to our study. The results of our study are clearly limited to Kickstarter and perhaps other reward based platforms. Although we believe our use of the RTSE offers a compelling set of theoretical arguments for inves tigating resource exchange on crowdfunding sites, our results are not generalizable to all types of crowdfunding plat forms such as equity crowdfunding or other forms of crowdsourcing. Due to the extensive work required to cod each reward for each Kickstarter campaign, it was necessary to use a sample of project campaigns for our analysis Therefore, we were only able to examine projects from one category of Kickstarter and had to create a subsample of projects from that category. Although we focus on technology projects, a category that includes both search and experience goods, future research may want to confirm our results on other project categories such as Arts or Music, as well as on other types of crowdfunding platforms. Moreover, our study used archival data, so we were limited by the exchange mechanisms implemented by Kickstarter. Crowdfunding platforms that implement different technolog ical mechanisms to encourage exchange should be studied.

Our study constrained its purview to the study of exchanges during the funding period, which limits the scope of the social exchange processes studied. We focused only on the campaign stage (ie, the exchanges that lead to successful funding outcomes). The websites for successfully funded Kickstarter projects do not disappear after funding is obtained, but rather become hubs for the project creators and backers to post updates and questions as the project proceeds through the development and commercialization phases. Research has suggested that there are mor than simple transactional motives (eg, donating funding in exchange for the proposed product) at play on reward based crowdfunding platforms (Belleflamme et al., 2014). Our findings suggest that carefully constructing the reward portfolio is critical to funding success and SET research suggests initial economic exchanges can help develop socia relationships (Cropanzano & Mitchell, 2005). Project creators may want to consider how to develop reward portfo lios that encourage community building that may be useful when their projects move past the funding stage. Longitu dinal studies that follow the campaigns past the funding stage are necessary to examine the development of long term supportive project communities

## 6.4 | Directions for future research

While reward-based crowdfunding is an important phenomenon, other types of crowdfunding exist. For example some research has already explored crowdfunding platforms dedicated to charitable giving (Gleasure &

Feller, 2016a). We found evidence of exchange behaviour on Kickstarter, which is a reward-based crowdfundin platform. Future research could extend work on SET and crowdfunding like ours to explore social and economi exchanges on crowdfunding and crowdsourcing platforms dedicated to different types of fundraising or project (e.g., Kiva, GoFundMe). Furthermore, different crowdfunding platforms may have different technical designs (eg website characteristics, privacy rules) that may impact exchange processes and should be investigated in futur studies.

Interesting insight could be derived from survey or interview methods. Our study was able to analyse the actua project backer funding decisions (ie, exchanges) from the historical record, but was unable to tease out the specifi motives behind the project backer choices. Researchers should consider extending work on project backer motive and characteristics. Some early work in this area has appeared (e.g., Bretschneider et al., 2014; Bretschneider & Leimeister, 2017), and there is much promise in this approach. There are many reasons why individuals may decide to participate (or not) in crowdfunding communities (Gerber & Hui, 2013), either as a project creator or backer, and future research should continue to investigate these motives.

Future research should also explore how campaign creators can develop the elements of their project campaigns in ways that engender community development. The design of the reward portfolio, which determines the initia exchanges between community members, is an important element, but past the selection of a reward, project creators need to deliver the reward and maintain community around an entrepreneurial product that will face challenges as it is developed and commercialised. As reward-based crowdfunding platforms continue to mature, we argue that community building will become increasingly important to the long-term success of projects over the various stages of their life cycles. In fact, research has described the importance of community in crowdfunding (Belleflamm et al., 2014). Therefore, researchers should investigate this phenomenon in more depth, and platforms such as Kickstarter should continue their efforts to facilitate community building around successful campaigns.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

Reward-based crowdfunding platforms are one of the largest and fastest-growing segments of the crowdfunding industry (Mollick, 2014). Although rewards are integral to these platforms, they have been overlooked in the crowdfunding literature (Simons et al., 2017). Our study applied SET and its extension, the RTSE, to the crowdfunding context to explore how resource exchange on Kickstarter affects the project campaigns' funding out comes. We found that offering universal and concrete resource types as rewards increase the chance of funding suc cess, but that this effect is diminished for larger campaigns. We also found that whereas particularistic and symbolic rewards decrease the chances of funding success, these reward types are not as detrimental for campaigns with larger funding goals. Overall, we find that resource exchange is key to funding success in reward-based crowdfunding, but that the optimal design of the reward portfolio may not be the same for all campaigns because it may vary depending on the amount of funding requested. Our results provide important insights into reward portfo lio design.
