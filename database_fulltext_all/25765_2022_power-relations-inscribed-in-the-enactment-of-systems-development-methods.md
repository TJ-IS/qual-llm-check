# Power relations inscribed in the enactment of systems development methods

Bruce Rowlands<sup>1</sup> | Karlheinz Kautz<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Issues of power are often neglected in information systems (IS) studies and under-theorised in IS research. Systems development methods (SDMs) are commonly used in the IT industry to coordinate the activities between developers and clients. The role of power in the relationship between clients and systems developers remains an important topic of research in information systems development (ISD). Yet, despite the importance of understanding this relationship better, there has only been a limited number of studies exploring the role that an SDM can play in influencing this relationship. What is not widely acknowledged or researched is how different forms of power are inscribed in and enacted through an SDM. The aim of this paper is to advance our understanding of different forms of power— here, obtrusive and unobtrusive power—to show how ISD concepts provide structures during the enactment of an SDM and thereby influence relationships between developers and clients. We present qualitative results from an exploratory case study within an IT division of a large international bank and interpret the results using Clegg's (1989) circuits of power (CoP). Our analysis shows that developers feel disempowered in relation to the client, with developers playing a cooperative but submissive role. Prior SDM enactment studies have either not encountered or not recognised cases where obtrusive and unobtrusive forms of power inscribed within the SDM directly determine the relationship between developers and the client. Our results are presented as a set of propositions explaining how obtrusive and unobtrusive power is inscribed in the SDM and the effect such inscription has on the enactment of the SDM.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Issues of power are often neglected in information systems (IS) studies and under-theorised in IS researc (Simeonova, Karanasios, Galliers, Kelly, & Mishra, 2018). In this context, the role of power in the relationship between clients and systems developers remains an important topic in information systems development (ISD) research (Bano, Zowghi, & Ramini, 2018; Dhillon, Caldeira, & Wenger, 2011; Hekkala & Urquhart, 2013; Milne & Maiden, 2012; Pozzebon & Pinsonneault, 2012; Siadati, Wernick, & Veneziano, 2019). This paper reports on inter pretive research into how obtrusive and unobtrusive power is inscribed within a systems development method (SDM) and what the effect of this power has on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of an SDM.

First, we use the term unobtrusive power in a general sense in contrast to any obtrusive hierarchy-based or economic-based form of power. We also use unobtrusive power in terms of the ability to give meaning to events and actions, and to influence the perceptions of others (Hardy, 1985). Obtrusive and unobtrusive power are generic forms of power. Additionally, we follow Clegg's (1989) more specific terminology of causal, dispositional and facilita tive power, where causal power is a form of obtrusive power, while dispositional and facilitative power are forms of unobtrusive power.

Following Markus and Bjørn-Andersen (1987), we understand systems developers to include all those individual and groups who interact with the client; in assessing their needs, proposing solutions and developing software systems. In this study, our definition of the client does not include operative end users, rather it includes managers and their representatives of business departments who devise and sanction software systems to achieve organisationa goals. The client generally initiates the development or enhancement of systems but, most importantly, funds th development of these systems. Here, clients are in-house clients , often referred to by our informants as the busi ness’ or ‘business client’. We use the terms ‘client’ and ‘business client’ synonymously. We use the term SDM very broadly (cf., Fitzgerald, Russo, & Stolterman, 2002), as any formally documented in-house or commercially availabl ISD approach. SDMs, either employing a traditional, plan-driven or agile, change-driven approach to ISD, are com monplace in the IT industry and are often used to coordinate and control the activities of developers and the busi ness client (Almeida & Simoes, 2019).

We focus on the developers' perception of their relationship with the client and the SDM's possible impact on this relationship. In our interviews in the IT division within a major international bank, we asked systems developer across all levels to describe how they used the SDM during the ISD process in their dealings with the client. The developers perceived the ISD process as unequal and often framed their answers in terms of a subordinate relation ship with the client, and portrayed themselves in a cooperative, but submissive role. This situation intrigued us because developers have historically been depicted in a dominant position (Hussain & Cornelius, 2009; Markus & Bjørn-Andersen, 1987; Robey & Markus, 1984). In addition to understanding and explaining this case, our aim was to theorise how obtrusive and unobtrusive power is inscribed in methods of systems development and what the effect of this power is on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of SDMs.

An underlying issue in relation to the development of IS has been the acknowledged but elusive political natur of such activity (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Howcroft & Wilson, 2003), of which power relationships between the cli ent and developers are an implicit part. SDMs significantly inform and shape the cognitions and actions of organisational members engaged in ISD (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989), and technical artefacts such as an SDM can act as a carrier and an important institutional embodiment serving to preserve rules by constraining the actions of human actors (Gosain, 2004). SDMs are often not used in the manner intended by method developers; in practice, SDMs are often adapted and modified to meet organisational and project exigencies (Päivärinta, Sein, & Peltola, 2010); but Thummadi and Lyytinen (2020) reinforce that research on SDMs remains current. Despite general agreement concerning the importance of understanding the systems developer-client relationship (Majchrzak, Beath, Lim, & Chin, 2005; Markus & Bjørn-Andersen, 1987; Milne & Maiden, 2012; Siadati et al., 2019), there has only been limited research exploring the role that SDMs can play in influencing this relationship. Relatively few IS researchers hav used concepts from theories of power to investigate SDM enactment (Fitzgerald et al., 2002; Lang & Fitzgerald, 2007; Mackay, Carne, Beynon-Davies, & Tudhope, 2000).

Our research thus builds on recent work concerning developers and clients as political actors (Dhillon et al., 2011; Hekkala, Stein, & Rossi, 2014; Hekkala, Urquhart, & Iivari, 2009). Specifically, what makes this study significant and a distinct contribution to the literature is a focus on: (a) an explanation of how a technology—the SDM— reinforces and transmits power structures in a social setting and (b) an application of Clegg's (1989) circuits of powe (CoP) framework as a research instrument to make sense of power and its relation to the enactment of the SDM. As such, our research complements existing studies by adopting a view that SDM enactment is an expression of power within the systems development process. It does so by providing an understanding of how power is shaped in the IT division of our case organisation through a detailed description of a point of convergence—the enactment of th SDM, where developers' and clients' interests meet and possibly diverge. We provide descriptions and analysis of the developer-client relationship from a power perspective in response to the following research questions: (a) How is obtrusive and unobtrusive power inscribed in an SDM? and (b) What is the effect of obtrusive and unobtrusive power on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of an SDM?

Insight with respect to these questions is derived from a case study of the enactment of a mandated, in-house developed, traditional waterfall type SDM where systems developers were required to interact and work with th in-house client. Although existing theory acknowledges the political nature of ISD (Jasperson et al., 2002), ou research is the first in the IS field to adopt a specific theory of power—in this case, in the form of Clegg's (1989) CoP—to investigate and theorise how obtrusive and unobtrusive forms of power are inscribed in SDMs, and what the effect of such inscription is on the developer-client relationship during and through the enactment of an SDM Theorising from the results of our case analysis, we present a set of propositions to be validated in future research.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. In the next section, we turn to the literature on power in ISD, and power inscribed in SDMs. In Section 3, we discuss the paper's conceptualisation of power, and present Clegg's (1989) relational approach to power, particularly with respect to his integrative CoP framework. This approach, we apply in analysing our case data. In Section 4, we discuss our research approach, data collection and analysis. In Section 5, we provide context and background to the case and the different types of power that Clegg's (1989) framework introduces. In Section 6, we present our analysis and findings, identifying instances of power inequality between the developers and the client and applying the CoP framework to understand how these relationships were shaped by SDM enactment involving various power sources. In Section 7, we discuss our findings and contributions and present the results of our theorising as a set of propositions, explaining the inscription of obtrusive and unobtrusive power and the effect of such inscriptions on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of an SDM. In the eighth and final section, we conclude by discussing limitations to the study and provid ing directions for future research.

## 2 | A REVIEW OF POWER IN THE ISD LITERATURE

The relationship between power and ISD has been studied over nearly four decades (Jasperson et al., 2002; Sab herwal & Grover, 2009; Sillince & Mouakket, 1997; Silva, 2007). Robey and Markus (1984) were among the first to recognise that ISD is a political process in which different actors pursue their individual interests. ISD projects hav been described as a process of social contention and political, as well as technical, determinism (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989).

Conceptual research on the role of power and politics in ISD projects has been growing since the 1980s (Franz & Robey, 1984; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Milne & Maiden, 2012; Sabherwal & Grover, 2009; Silva, 2007) and empirical studies—particularly field and case studies that focus on power play among project stakeholders—ar increasing in number and have been helping to unpack how power is inscribed in ISD (Dhillon et al., 2011; Doolin, 2004; Heiskanen, Newman, & Eklin, 2008; Hekkala & Urquhart, 2013; Hirschheim & Newman, 1991 Howcroft & Light, 2006; Kolkowska & Dhillon, 2013; Kromidha, 2017; Valenca & Alves, 2017).

While Simeonova et al. (2018) argue that issues of power are still often neglected in IS studies and undertheorised in IS research, they refer to IS research in general, particularly work on IS use. Notwithstanding, their view remains valid in the area of ISD research as the conventional view within studies of power in ISD projects is that information and power are synonymous, and hence those who build systems are viewed as instrumental in influenc ing power relationships (Jasperson et al., 2002). Early reviews of the literature portray developers as being in contro (Markus & Bjørn-Andersen, 1987). They provide an early framework to identify different forms of exercised powe to make clients and systems developers more aware of the influence of power. Smith (1990) found that a significant feature of the relationship between clients and developers was that any antagonism appeared to be one-sided—from clients towards IS professionals. Beath and Orlikowski (1994) identified a contradictory dichotomy between clients and developers, with developers in command and clients submissive, while clients were expected to be responsible for the outcomes of the development process. Mackay et al. (2000) found from a case study of developer-user relationships in a UK bank, clear indications that developers feel disempowered in relation to the client. If the client is of very high status, disempowerment is exacerbated. Hussain and Cornelius (2009), in a study of the implementation stage of an ISD, found the opposite by reporting on how dominant structures favouring IT management were pro duced and authority over clients was secured.

Only a few studies focus on the deployment of SDMs in their social and organisational contexts and on th effect that power has on relationships between developers and the client in this context. One example is by Sauer and Lau (1997), who reported on a case where the role of the SDM was highly influential in distributing power between client and developers and where the client was able to impose their priorities, thereby demonstrating power in the ISD process. These findings recognised that clients are a source of influence on SDM enactment because they control the resources. In another study of the impact of organisational context involving SDM enact ment, Nandhakumar and Avison (1999) highlighted various influences such as developers' knowledge abou methods, implicit social norms, organisational form and culture

Our research presents one of the few empirical studies that explore the concepts of power and SDM enactment We identified three studies related to ours: Mackay et al. (2000), Fitzgerald et al. (2002), and Lang and Fitzgerald (2007).

In Mackay et al.'s (2000) study of rapid application development within a UK bank, while developers were expected to follow organisational methods and procedures, they were allowed considerable freedom from organisational constraints. However, when identifying sources of power, the client was identified as exerting consid erable power over projects, with developers having ‘no real power’ because, at the end of the day, the client had no obligation to come to IT for the development of their systems, and developers were accountable to their client because they paid for the project.

Fitzgerald et al. (2002) proposed a ‘method-in-action’ framework to understand how SDMs are enacted in prac tice. Their framework provided a contextual overview of the main actors and issues in ISD, and how they are inter related. Their framework included a set of political roles supplementing a set of rational roles that methods may pla but place little emphasis on the relations between political roles and the client.

Lang and Fitzgerald (2007) presented a variant of the ‘method-in-action’ framework of ISD by including empiri cally grounded ‘mediating factors’, such as the nature of client-developer relationship and the locus of power

Included in these factors was an acknowledgement that design methods can fulfil political roles, such as in esta blishing a power base for method champions, or in maintaining an audit trail of design process decisions as a protec tive fall-back for developers when negotiating responsibility for change requests involving the client. These are al political roles afforded to the developer, yet the discussion about the potential locus of power involving the client and how unobtrusive mechanisms can be embedded within the SDM received little attention.

Although illuminating, Fitzgerald et al.'s (2002) and Lang and Fitzgerald's (2007) studies do not delve deeply into the organisational and power issues concerning the enactment process but focus instead on providing a macro-leve overview by identifying the elements of the SDM framework. Yet, these studies are most valuable as they pioneered and highlighted the importance of identifying the main elements concerning the use of SDMs.

While not specifically about power, further studies advanced a growing argument that IT practice should not be seen as a mere technical activity and, as argued by Goulielmos (2004), SDM enactment can and should be under stood as a complex social activity influenced by the organisational and institutional context in which it takes place. A central argument of this research is that power and SDM enactment is best understood as a socio-political process that is inextricably embedded within an organisational context (Milne & Maiden, 2012). As noted in Aydin, Harmsen van Slooten, and Stegwee (2005), context plays an important role in the adaptation of an agile SDM.

Extending the method-in-action model of Fitzgerald et al. (2002), Madsen, Kautz, and Vidgen (2006) focused on the unfolding development process, activities and method elements that comprise this process. They describe th emergent method as a process of social moderation of method use, covering both the change of method, as for mally prescribed through its use, and the change of its clients' actions that result from their interactions with the method. Madsen et al.'s (2006) conceptualisation portrayed the role and usefulness of SDMs as a means for communication, coordination and (re)direction, rather than as a rigorous or rigid means for control. They identified the distri bution of power in the ISD project, which they investigated without explicitly relating it to the method in use.

In contrast, Huisman and Iivari (2006) considered the difference in perception between IS managers and devel opers regarding the deployment of SDMs. They found that both groups saw SDMs as a control technology in term of keeping to deadlines and budget, yet they offered no discussion of how control was achieved or how power was inscribed in SDMs.

Milne and Maiden (2012) considered the role of power and politics in requirements engineering. Relating their findings to the structure of power relationships, they presented a theory for mapping power relationships. The found that power operates through both formal and informal channels, that power relationships are pervasive an complex, and that power relationships are dynamic and contingent. However, their underlying theoretical model di not study SDM enactment or emphasise macro societal forms of power in these relationships, as we seek to do.

In summary, prior literature has not focused on how unobtrusive power can be inscribed into and enacted through SDM practice. Our review suggests that few have studied a situation where developers feel disempowered in the ISD process. In the context of understanding our case scenario, the literature is limited. However, our focus is not about identifying who has or does not have power. Our focus is more concerned with empirically examining how both forms of generic power—obtrusive and unobtrusive—are inscribed in an SDM, and what the effect of such inscription is on the developer-client relationship during its enactment. To do so, we need an appropriat conceptualisation of power.

## 3 | THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

## 3.1 | The concept of power

Power is a complex concept where any consideration of its role depends on which perspective one chooses (Sillince & Mouakket, 1997). In much of the Organisational Studies literature, power has been conceptualised as eco nomic power—the mobilisation of resources such as money, funding, the ability to hire and fire, and to influenc problem-solving (Hardy, 1985). Power, in this case, involves the possession of strategic resources on which others depend, and enables actors to produce the substantial outcomes they desire. This type of power has been referred to as causal power. Causal power is an obtrusive form of power enacted in relatively discrete, strategic events or epi sodes that are initiated by self-interested actors (Clegg, 1989). This mode of power has often dominated the stud of power in organisations and has been approached from a wide variety of perspectives (Pfeffer, 1981). Causa power can take the form of economic power, power to reward or punish actors for outcomes based on funding, o legitimate power, power grounded in the hierarchical position of individuals. This view of power is also person-based in that power is located within certain groups or individuals

Additionally, however, there are views that both challenge and go beyond this causal view of power exerted b an individual or group. According to Foucault (1980), power is understood as not being in any one place or as something leaders or persons in authority possess. Rather, power is exercised in relationships between people and becomes apparent when it is enacted through a web of social relationships and structures existing in organisation and society (Clegg, 1989; Townley, 1993). In other words, power is exercised rather than possessed, and its exercis ing is not a one-way process (Hekkala et al., 2014).

Relational forms of power work through the routine, on-going practices of organisations to advantage groups without those groups being obviously or clearly connected to the establishment or maintenance of those practice (Lawrence, Winn, & Jennings, 2001). Research on relational forms of power has emphasised that power is not always obtrusive, but sometimes much more subtle, silent and often invisible. This includes processes of meaning creation and persuasion—a form of power Hardy (1985) termed unobtrusive, as it creates legitimacy and justification for cer tain arrangements or actions in such a way that they remain unquestioned. Unobtrusive power is not predetermined by the control of resources; rather, it emerges during actors' interactions and relationships and is the outcome of th translation of meaning—as noted by Azad and Faraj (2010) in their work on organisational IT implementation—and a a socialisation process occurring, for example, at the workplace and within the IT industry.

## 3.2 | The circuits of power framework

Clegg (1989) presents a relational approach to power with his integrative, three circuits of power framework that has been applied effectively in IS research, studying the institutionalisation of IS (Silva & Backhouse, 2003) as well as the shaping of and compliance with security standards (Backhouse, Hsu, & Silva, 2006; Smith, Winchester, Bunker, & Jamieson, 2010). The CoP framework is an integrative model of power that depicts power as a circular process flowing in three distinct channels, operating at three different levels. At a surface or micro level between co-worker relationships, an episodic circuit of power exists, in which casual power is exercised. This circuit of power has bee explained by Vad Baunsgaard and Clegg (2012, p. 204) as being, ‘… premised on the idea that some subjects are able to exercise sovereign power, much as Hobbes and subsequent political scientists have conceptualised power as an essentially causal relation in which an agency achieves some (intended) effects on another (Saxonhouse & Reynolds, 1995). A fundamental prescription of these approaches to power and thus of the episodic circuit is that an agency A, makes another agency, a B, do something that B would otherwise not do (Dahl, 1957)’. Vad Baunsgaard and Clegg (2012) characterised this circuit as a uni-dimensional circuit. Due to the obtrusive nature of causal power, this form of power is directly observable.

At a more macro, socio-cultural level, power is entrenched in the shared meanings that shape organisationa practices using unobtrusive means such as language, symbols and rituals, termed as the circuit of social integratio (Clegg, 1989)—or the social circuit (Backhouse et al., 2006). The concept of social circuit, used in the following focuses on symbolic sources of power and relates to the ability to impose meaning, the shaping of perceptions, cog nitions and preferences in such a way that people accept their role in the existing order of things, either because they can see or imagine no alternative to it, or because they view it as natural or unchangeable. This circuit repre sents dispositional power (Clegg, 1989), corresponding to the case where entity A, imperceptibly drawing on these intangible and often invisible resources, exercises power over entity B. Dispositional power therefore takes the form of unobtrusive power.

At an even more pervasive, macro, societal level, power is embedded in the historically constituted knowledg of a discipline flowing through a systemic circuit of system integration, which Backhouse et al. (2006) abbreviated as the systemic circuit. In this circuit, power is viewed as a facilitator for producing and achieving collective or individua goals. Facilitative power in the systemic circuit is disciplinary in nature and helps to shape organisational members beliefs about their roles (Dhillon et al., 2011). Like dispositional power, facilitative power is a subtle form of power; it is invisible and likewise is a form of unobtrusive power.

Discipline is understood in two ways: as a body of knowledge, and as a synonym for control. Knowledge or disci pline know-how provides a framework that guides, directs and restricts the activities within a disciplin (Foucault, 1980). It both constrains and enables how involved social actors see, how they think and how they do things. Therefore, the discipline or the recurring practice of social relationships represented as knowledge that any entity has tacitly acquired over time is not recognisable remains unquestioned, and is simply the unconscious accep tance of the way things are and get done in an organisation. According to Clegg (1989), all social actors are subject to the ‘disciplinary power’ embedded in socio-cultural meaning systems that lead to a judgment by actors of what i acceptable knowledge, and therefore represents a form of control.

The CoP framework emphasises context by considering institutional and environmental factors as well as tech nological artefacts. Each of the three circuits describes a different type of power and it is the integration of these three conceptions that gives the theory its explanatory power. The three circuits are integrated into an obligatory passage point (OPP), representing the enactment of power, as a seamlessly integrated artefact involving routine processes and practice. OPPs can be thought of as devices, either rhetorical or material, that channel and frame the conduct of behaviour of actors in specific situational contexts (Callon, 1986). The OPP thereby becomes a conduit o the juncture where the three CoP interact that forces actors to converge on a certain purpose or work activity.

Recent applications of the CoP framework in IS (Backhouse et al., 2006; Silva & Backhouse, 2003; Smith et al., 2010) do not present a dynamic understanding of the OPP concept in its relationship to power (Kromidha, 2017). What is missing is a clear representation of the forces that give momentum to the flows of powe within the three circuits. Understanding how power is shaped in a case such as ours requires a detailed description of the converging point, the enactment of the SDM as the OPP, where developers' and clients' interests meet and possibly diverge.

External forces or exogenous environmental contingencies (Clegg, 1989)—exogenous contingencies (Backhous et al., 2006)—introduce changes into the circuits by affecting the rules of meaning and membership in the social cir cuit and/or the techniques of discipline and production in the systemic circuit. These contingencies include laws, reg ulations or innovations from competitors. These forces can, in some situations, introduce a new OPP.

The relationships among the different circuits and their integration in the OPP are depicted in Figure 1, which is adapted from Backhouse et al. (2006). To illustrate the correspondence of obtrusive and unobtrusive power in our study with Clegg's three forms of power (causal, dispositional and facilitative), we explicitly added these two generi forms of power to Figure 1. The strength of the CoP framework is that it integrates different views, and perspectives, as well as the micro and macro levels of power. The circuits metaphor emphasises that power has a relationa nature in contrast to the idea that power can be owned. The framework's central thesis is that power ‘circulates through social relations, working practices and discipline techniques (Backhouse et al., 2006), providing stability to social systems (Silva & Backhouse, 2003). However, according to Clegg (1989), nothing circulates or moves: he uses the metaphor of circuits primarily to emphasise the relational nature of power. According to Silva and Back house (2003, p. 297), what happens is that ‘rules, procedures and techniques of discipline shape both interpersona relationships and work tasks, thus circumscribing the scope for action of the individuals in organisations. So strong are these social and disciplinary forces that the electro-physical circuit metaphor renders the idea well’

Real, visible acts of power in co-worker relationships involving discrete acts of mobilisation initiated by self interested actors appear in the episodic circuit. However, the routine on-going work practices that can advantag groups—without these groups necessarily maintaining these practices—appear in the social and systemic circuits which are complex and contextual. Hence, according to Clegg (1989), a one-dimensional, episodic perception of power relationships can explain something about the character of the relationships between A and B, but nothing about how A's objectives are legitimised, or about how a discipline influences their access to resources of power an their ability to use them. These relationships are described in the social and the systemic CoP. According to Clegg (1989), it is important to understand that what happens in an exercise of power is not dependent only on what happens in one of the circuits, as all three circuits conceptually are inter-connected. Each of the five components of the CoP framework as described above has a dynamic form of its own.

As stated earlier, the usefulness of the CoP framework in the context of the IS field has been illustrated through several prior studies (Backhouse et al., 2006; Fragos, Karyda, & Kiountouzis, 2007; Lawrence, Malhotra, & Morris, 2012; Oliveira & Clegg, 2015; Silva & Backhouse, 2003; Simeonova, Galliers, & Karanasios, 2020; Smit et al., 2010), but none of these examples relate specifically to ISD projects or SDM enactment. The CoP framewor examines both obtrusive and unobtrusive uses of power. It is, therefore, appropriate and useful to our case in explaining developer perceptions of their relationship with the client and offers a plausible explanation as to wh developers may be compliant in a situation of perceived power inequality.

## 4 | RESEARCH APPROACH AND METHOD

Our fundamental ontological assumption is that the technological artefact—the SDM—shapes human social context and reciprocally, the SDM is shaped by human social context. That is, developers' ability to shape their working prac tice is restricted by social norms and organisational constraints. Nor is the developer an abstraction that enacts th SDM in a social vacuum. Rather. they are best conceptualised as a social actor (Lamb & Kling. 2003) and not a techni cally focused tool user.

In terms of epistemology, Silva (2007) argues that the study of power poses epistemological challenges and that given the hidden nature of power and politics in organisational settings, an approach that emphasises the interpreta tion of meanings, intentions and actions is most suitable for making sense of such a complex phenomenon. Similarly denying the concept of power as a commodity possessed by an authority or institution has implications for the wa it is studied. Thus, questions such as who has power are changed to what Foucault would term the ‘how of power’: how do practices, techniques and procedures of ISD processes create order, knowledge and ultimately powe effects?

When advocating for an interpretive approach, Hardy and Leiba-O'Sullivan (1998) consider that researching issues of power may be problematic within the context of positivistic science, and as the focus of Clegg's frame work is on more unobtrusive forms of power embedded in organisational social systems, studies of these phenom ena do not lend themselves to quantitative approaches. Consequently, our research draws on interpretive methods, instruments and protocols that facilitate the detailed recording of what happened in a local socia system.

Accordingly, the research approach adopted in this study is that of an interpretive case study, with an emphasi on the language of developers and the meaning of their actions. Therefore, a single case study was chosen to pro duce a subjective, albeit shared between the researcher and the interviewee, understanding of phenomena Walsham (1995) and Stake (2000) advocate the use of a single site case as being appropriate because it allows a more precise understanding of the circumstances in which particular phenomena occurred.

We do not, however, present a longitudinal description of an implementation of an SDM to see how the powe relationships evolved, to see how the meaning was negotiated and how the various social actors struggled over resources. Rather, we report snapshots in time, based on developers' opinions about the SDM in response to inter view questions, and our interpretation of these opinions. For that reason, we present a CoP perspective with th SDM as a source of obtrusive and unobtrusive power.

We accept that scholars engaged in subjective research should provide their readers with some assuranc regarding the validity of their investigations. Klein and Myers (1999) offer a set of criteria that can be applied to gauge the validity of interpretive research. Table 1 provides a summary of our reflections and evaluation of the inter pretive method used in this study, and how our research stands against such criteria.

## 4.1 | Field site and data collection

The banking and financial services sector was chosen because of the central and long-standing roles that IT plays in the success of companies in this industry. The Bank selected has extensive experience and in-practice use of an inhouse developed SDM. The selection of the case site was based on a combination of accessibility to the company's IT managers and project members, and interest—in the sense that its IT organisation is considered a leading player in providing state-of-the-art IS solutions to customers. Given that our focus was solely on developers' perceptions and views, business clients were not interviewed: where this article reports on claims related to the business client, it i the developers' assumptions about clients that are presented.

The sampling strategy for the interviews included a combination of purposeful and theoretical sampling (Schwandt, 2001). Three occupational functions were selected for their similarities as well as their differences Informal interviews and conversations were conducted with developers—comprising the CIO, project managers, senior consultants and consultants within the systems support, new development and method support sub-divisions of the IT division. A total of 30 interviews were conducted approximately 2 months apart wit 28 informants from different projects and at varying levels within the organisation (cf. Table 2). Two method support personnel were interviewed twice. In most cases, each face-to-face interview of approximately 1 hour duration was complemented by a follow-up email to clarify issues, verify the accuracy of the transcription and to obtain supplementary information

## 4.2 | Data analysis

A recurring theme in the interviews was the developers' description of their subordinate relationship to the business client. This turned our attention to the role of SDMs in such relationships and how obtrusive and unobtrusive power is inscribed in an SDM and what the effect of this power is on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of the SDM.

Clegg's (1989) CoP framework was used to organise responses under three themes: was the power relationa and largely obtrusive and episodic (conceptualised in terms of interpersonal co-worker relations), unobtrusive and dispositional (conceptualised in terms of organisational norms, routines, policies, and practices) or unobtrusive and facili tative (conceptualised in terms of socio-regulatory structures, or context)? Analytic attention then focused on Clegg's OPP—the SDM—and its enactment to observe and document power as the three circuits interacted. The outcome of our findings is theorised as a set of propositions (Valenca & Alves, 2017), based on our analysis of articulating SDM enactment within the Bank.

Grounded in exploratory case studies and an inductive research method, Valenca and Alves (2017) developed theory on the occurrence of power and dependence in software ecosystem partnerships based on the obtrusive and unobtrusive forms of power they found. They presented their findings as propositions describing the power issues in these partnerships.<sup>1</sup> This approach is in line with Walsham (1995), Klein and Myers (1999) and Stake's (2000) postulate that findings from case studies can be transferred to theoretical propositions, as in Mueller, Mathiassen, and Saunders (2017), for example.

The process of analysis was assisted by and recorded in a database through procedures such as importing chunks of transcribed interviews, adding comments and reflections, sorting the interpretations by themes and text retrieval of selected instances into the body of the research report. Following data collection and preliminary analysis, the first author developed and shared an initial case report, including a case summary and preliminary element of analysis, with a project manager involved in the use of the SDM. This manager commented on the report and gav confirmation of many points and qualifications of others. Drawing on this feedback, the scrutiny of informants on the accuracy of their transcribed interviews, the clarification of discussed issues, our relativistic and subjective understanding of the case phenomena, the interpretation of power concepts based on the CoP framework, led to our ana lytical findings being transferred in a process of theorising into a set of generalised propositions that can usefully b validated in further research

We now present our analysis in the following two sections. First, we introduce the case in terms of the organisa tion and the SDM, its context and practice in identifying the three types of power offered by Clegg (1989) in The Bank's IT division. We then present our detailed analysis and findings applying the CoP framework

## 5 | THE CASE: ORGANISATION, CONTEXT, SDM AND PRACTICE

The site selected for study was the large-scale IT division of The Bank, employing approximately 700 personnel, con sisting of an architecture of centralised mainframes, primarily custom IS and software developed in-house, with th deployment of a mandated SDM requiring the services of professional systems developers. Each new development or support team had a project manager who reported to a business unit manager, the (representative of the business) client, who had overall control of the project through budget and a stage-gate funding approval process. The siz and composition of project teams usually consisted of core people on the project (eg, project manager, business ana lyst, solution designer, developers and sub-contractors); those that interface with the system (eg, business clients hardware and telecommunications providers), and other partners who may have been involved in outsourced busi ness processes.

The types of systems built ranged from consumer banking (internet banking, personal loans, credit cards, etc.) to corporate banking (trading in the futures exchange, commodities, currencies, trade finance, etc.), investment banking (shares, funds, fund management, etc.) and internal accounting and management applications.

Projects are initiated by the client in most instances. This initiation happens within a team in a business unit. Th business team engages with their management to get approval and funding, and if approved, a project manager is appointed. The project commences with the client securing IT services from a supplier, in most cases, the in-house IT division. However, the business client has the option to procure software services from external third parties, rep resenting in Clegg's (1989) terms, a form of resource or causal power.

To develop, customise or maintain these systems, the IT division, in conjunction with the business client, had developed and documented their own in-house SDM applicable for all development and maintenance tasks. Th SDM was based on traditional ‘waterfall’ lifecycle phases,<sup>2</sup> and its use in practice for developers was mandatory. An intranet site including the manual and templates of all the documents required at different stages of the lifecycle was available to all systems developers. For most developers, knowledge of the SDM was acquired ‘on the job’ and inter nalised over time. In reality, SDM enactment involved tacit knowledge being inter-twined with practical experienc over time, which, according to Clegg (1989), is a form of facilitative power.

The SDM emphasised the production of documentation within each phase and sub-phase of the development lifecycle. Once a document was produced, it was sent for review to a distribution list identified by the project manager. Key project members, notably the business client, were needed to sign-off or review the documents; others just received the document for information purposes. Often there were formal document review sessions or walk throughs where key project members from the business client, the hardware vendor and developers gathered wit the author of the document from the development team to ‘walk through the document’ before formal sign-off. Thi indicated the transition from one specialist group to the next, when work on one step was completed. These walk through and sign-off meetings involved developers describing the work they had undertaken and then the client, if satisfied, signed off, thereby exhibiting traits of a ceremony or ritual (Robey & Markus, 1984) and according to Clegg (1989), constituting a form of symbolic or dispositional power.

The ISD procedures and developer acquiescence could be explained simply along conventional lines as something representing a ‘rational business process’: the SDM was mandated and developers were required to cooperate with the client otherwise they would not get their work done, and more importantly, were not permitted to infringe organisational rules. However, such managerialist descriptions considerably under-play the role of how power wa exercised in The Bank. It is this process, involving work routines, institutions, meanings, the discipline of ISD and external influences that we identified and analysed using Clegg's (1989) CoP framework including his specific term of causal, dispositional and facilitative power. We return to the generic terms of obtrusive and unobtrusive powe when discussing the research questions and our contributions.

## 6 | ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS—THE CIRCUITS

Having identified the three types of power in The Bank's IT division—causal, dispositional and facilitative—we pre sent an explanation of how these forms of power are inscribed within the SDM. We also illustrate how each circuit of Clegg's framework manifests itself in the day-to-day practices of ISD, helping to understand what the effect of obtrusive and unobtrusive power is on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of the SDM.

## 6.1 | Obtrusive, causal power in the episodic circuit

Causal power involves the classic notion of actor A getting actor B to do something that B otherwise would not have done (Dahl, 1957). Although some scholars have adopted the notion of power as a capacity of A (Lawrence et al., 2012; Pfeffer, 1981), we follow Clegg's (1989) line of argument that causal power only exists in its exercis and is expressed in relationships. Recognising instances of causal power in the context of SDM enactment requires evidence of a shift in an actor's behaviour or beliefs that stems from the actions of another. In this section, we revea examples of developers' behaviours as they complied with the client's insistence to follow the SDM's prescriptions Examples include accepting the client's rejection of modern development methods, agreeing to unreasonable schedules, having to produce volumes of documentation in advance and having to chase the client's signature before th next phase of development could commence.

## 6.1.1 | Rejection of modern development methods

In terms of work practices migrating within The Bank, developers were required to work with external organisation when aspects of projects had been outsourced, or when dealing with contractors brought in on an as-needed basis These interactions bring about potential change to work practices and shape how the method was used and how the SDM evolved over time. In terms of making changes to the method, a developer when describing how his project team was trying to update the SDM to incorporate object-oriented techniques found resistance from the client:

We attempted to modify the requirement specification to make them more like RUP. We wanted to include some of the RUP in the templates. But we had to check it with the business. And then business came back and said they didn't like some of the concepts, like use case diagrams. So, we had to remove some of those key parts of the templates because business found them too confusing which is crazy, as it's a pretty simple concept.

In this case, the developers wanted to update the SDM to conform to aspects of current industry practice—using object orientation and the Rational Unified Process (RUP)—but the alien nature of use case diagrams and the rejec tion of them by the client added a layer of frustration on the part of the developer. Perhaps, use cases were not nec essarily simple from the client's perspective, but there was also no suggestion that developers wanted to control or bamboozle clients with this issue either. But the client had the power to say ‘no’ to proposed changes involving us cases. This is a clear indication of causal power in the form of the power of decision-making.

The transcripts also identified a feeling among developers that the existing SDM was due for an overhaul. How ever, when asked what would be required to introduce an entirely new SDM with the existing SDM phased out, th method support manager replied:

… it'd be a big effort. However, when I talk to developers, they're quite happy to use other processes. And even some of the project managers would be quite happy to move away from the existing methodology…. There seems to be a desire [among developers] to look at something else, rather than what we currently use as our methodology.<sup>3</sup>

However, to introduce a new SDM would involve gaining business agreement. Business would have to be convinced that there were alternate forms of control as the method support manager commented:

This is a business and the methodology is part of a control mechanism … we need to show people [business] that there are better things out there, if there are, and will enable them [business] to not only get the controls that they do currently have but be more productive and more appropriate for the way that they do their work.

Another observation was how the systems developers referred to the business client. The dichotomy between the developers and client is evident in the interviewee's reference to the business client as business rather than as, for example, clients or domain experts . We interpreted the term business as used by many developers as an indica tion of superiority—one who consumes, controls, prescribes and manages. The term ‘business’ piqued our attention as it is unusual in the IS and software development industry, as most developers acknowledge that business value is a key driver. Developers referred to business in terms of ‘them and us’ and from their perspective, power was seen to be vested with the client. Asked specifically, who was driving ISD, a senior analyst responded in a way that was representative of many similar comments about the developer-client relationship, providing a further indication of causal power in the form of control over financial resources:

It's the business definitely. Sometimes the IT areas will, once they get a project, try to drive what they think. But on the whole, the business are paying [for services and products] and whatever they want gets done.

## 6.1.2 | Unreasonable schedules

Being perceived as controlling resources, it is, therefore, not surprising that the business client was proficient at hav ing causal power over developers. The same senior analyst even admitted that some project managers were intimidated by the power that ‘business’ wields in terms of agreeing to their unreasonable schedule demands:

I have worked for managers where they have agreed to deadlines that are too close and not reasonable. Sometimes too, I think they get a little bit scared of business. Like if the business wants something and they're demanding it, they're scared to tell them that their request isn't reasonable.

Another senior analyst believed that the client used the SDM to lock development teams into unreasonable schedules, making life very difficult for the developers and, in the end, producing poor quality systems, because:

… it means that your development time is locked in. Developers don't have a lot of say. Usually, your technical needs are bolted. You're told that you've got to develop a system, so there always has to be an estimate. So what it means to me is that once you've signed-off on a certain amount of money those requirements will be delivered by the end of the project. We're sort of locked in. That's what don't like about it.

Relationships between developers and the client and their differing interests are further demonstrated in the follow ing, where the method support manager was commenting on the power of resources between the client (‘business’) and developers:

Business sometimes do hold developers to ransom, so to speak. Really, they should be working together to try and deliver solutions rather than using contracts as ransom to force them to do something.

A relative new developer also commented about how he saw causal power in the relationships between business and IT being based around financial rewards for delivering projects on time:

… a lot of the management is structured such that project managers and the CIO are rewarded or punished based on their ability to deliver. So, they apply that pressure downward. Management and business are probably the most in-flexible areas because they're very much used to having a lot of control. They have a lot of power, because they hold the money.

The same developer described the relationship between the client and developers as imbalanced based on the control of resources:

This organisation has got a really lean cost model. We [developers] all hate it because it's a means where we can get shafted. The business likes it though.

## 6.1.3 | Excessive documentation

A further example of differing interests is how the SDM mandated documentation throughout all phases of th development lifecycle and required project managers to call for meetings, both formal and informal, among affiliate to review project status, and to sign-off on design specifications. Many developers commented that the amount of documentation required was excessive and often overwhelmed the client, even though the client insisted on having it produced, and having the ultimate say by signing off on it:

Let's say you need a few alterations. You need to send them out to review, to get them signed off … sometimes business didn't even look at it because they think we produce it in more technical jargon than they understand. So, they just sign off on the front page saying “that's fine”.

However, the main reason cited for producing documenting was to gain approval from the client to receive funding to com mence the next stage. To do so, there needed to be visibility of development progress as a project manager commented:

… producing a document is one way of providing visibility of what's actually happening and with al the formal documents that are required to be signed … its approval [by the client] to go to the next stage and that the work can begin. Also, project funding is dependent on these documents having to be produced.

Another experienced consultant agreed that the advantage of documentation was a safety mechanism, especially if it had been signed and agreed on. In this way, documentation can shield developers from unreasonable demand from business and as an audit trail of decisions if requirements change or if the client was not happy.

… once the thing [requirements specification] is documented, we are safe. Yeah it means that they cannot blame you. They can't claim it's not what they want, because it's what they agreed on and what we deliver to them

In this situation, an attempt was being made by developers to arrest some power from the client and to protect their interests. Adherence to contracts was an attempt by developers to regain some power but based on what developer told us there were no other examples of resistance. What the interviews are saying is that in the end it was the cli ent, who had control over resource and decision-making, which was causal power, and that while some senior devel opers did have a contractual mechanism to redress the power imbalance, an overall subordinate relationship whe dealing with the client existed.

## 6.1.4 | Having to chase the client

A client controlling resources and decision-making is not a revelatory finding. However, what is of importance is that the SDM mandated documentation, documentation led to sign-off and sign-off led to resourcing for the next stage of development. This resembled an episodic circuit where the enactment of the SDM represented an OPP for th developers and provided further evidence of causal power vested in the client through sign-off. From the perspective of the developers, sign-off was viewed in two ways: positively—to gain approval and resourcing so that work commenced on the next stage, and negatively—as a way whereby clients overtly maintained power and control ove the development process. A project manager commented that the process of gaining sign-off was an impediment to delivering systems on time because they had to chase the client. He admitted that having to wait for sign-off wa often frustrating:

… [one of the] areas where things can be a real barrier is the sign-off process. So, let's say we produce a design document and we send it out for review and sign off. Well, you might then have to wait a week or two weeks for other areas to review it and sign off and chase them up because they've al got busy lives as well.

As demonstrated by the above excerpts from the interviews, a recurring theme among the responses were devel opers describing their relationship as subordinate and inferior when dealing with the client. This perception of a lack of power, a feeling that the IT division does not have the authority to affect its relationship with business and having differing interests, was a theme expressed on many occasions by those in the IT division. Our analysis also indicate that causal power structurally embedded within the SDM influenced the relationships between developers and cli ents. The work practices embedded in the SDM, through sign-off and stage-gate funding demonstrated a form of developer dependence on the client for important resources, which constituted causal power in the episodic circuit The conventions of the client embedded within the SDM held legitimacy in The Bank and, accordingly, the advan tages in terms of whose interests were met in the systems development process were in favour of the client.

While our analysis so far enabled us to identify sources of authority and power afforded to the client and to identify concepts of unmistakable power. Given that some senior developers had the opportunity to exercise causa power through the adherence of signed documents shielding them from unreasonable demands, it does not provid an explanation as to why the developers did not exercise this form of causal power more often. At this level of analysis, the episodic circuit of power does not explain why developers were compliant and accepting of their situation in relation to an unequal relationship with the client, given that they had other forms of causal power in their favour.

## 6.2 | Unobtrusive power in the social and the systemic circuits

In the following sub-sections, we turn to the two other CoP to analyse the case further. Clegg's social and systemic CoP propose that acquiescence on the part of the developers may be the result of an exercise of unobtrusive power Power can be used to prevent people from having grievances by shaping their perceptions and preferences in such a way that they accept their role in the existing order of things, see it as natural, or see no alternative to it (Clegg, 1989; Lukes, 1974). This acceptance on the part of the developers explains why there were few direct references to dispositional power or to facilitative power made during the interviews. Clegg's social and systemic circuit drew our attention to the effects of these types of power and to any exogenous contingencies in these circuits

## 6.2.1 | Dispositional power in the social circuit

Our analysis shows that the developers' perception of the SDM, their work and their relationships with the client were influenced by symbolic aspects of power—the use of language, symbols and rituals in the workforce. In terms of language, (a) developers valued common terms that enabled communication of ideas between themselves, th business client and consultants external to the organisation; (b) they habitually used the SDM in producing lifecycl documentation as symbols and evidence of work performance; (c) they made use of walk-through meetings as rituals (Robey & Markus, 1984) with clients to validate the ‘accuracy’ of their requirements and subsequent design deci sions to gain their signature of approval. Thus, in terms of rules of meaning and membership, the SDM stands for something more than just a way to coordinate projects. The meaning of the SDM came from its context and it enactment within the IT division. The language, symbols and rituals in the enactment and deployment of the SDM in the IT division and in the developers' interactions with the client were based on the disciplines associated with ISD and established work practices outside the organisation. They represent a form of dispositional power and thes work practices are also the outcome of exogenous contingencies (see Section 6.3)

In terms of language, the SDM provided developers with a checklist of deliverables that had to be produced in different stages of the project and provided a terminology, so when developers talked with clients and among them selves, they knew the language of the business. In other words, the SDM provided a consistent common language and many developers spoke positively in terms of how the SDM facilitated communication, as the following illustrates:

Well, you need to have some methodology … you need a vehicle for communicating particularly between the teams. So, if we were working with another group within the organisation or even external to the organisation you have to have a common language. So, it's not bad in that respect.

Documentation was used to share ideas between developers and business clients. The documents, as a communica tion tool, facilitated the process of requirements gathering, project costing and project design.

Business generally are very vague about systems—they know what they want but they don't exactly know what is involved or know if it's feasible. Because they don't know the internals like how the programs are going to work or any limitations and those kinds of things … they're not that clear with their requirements. That's OK, so we want to communicate our work in a certain way. So we take it from there and try to understand what they really need. We document the implications. Then they check our documents and go “OK”. We say, this is going to take this long, and that is going to take that long. It's complex but it gives the business the option to review what they're asking for and also so they can see the costs, what is involved, and how big the task is

In terms of symbolism, using the SDM in The Bank, created an image for developers that they were professiona and producing documentation came to represent competent work performance. Many newer staff had littl or no training in the SDM but were happy to use it. A junior developer remarked that using the SDM helped him to appear as performing appropriately in terms of his relationships with others in the workplace. H admitted:

I've been using the same documents since I started. I guess [the SDM] has been around for a lot longer than that. Where those documents originated from, I have no idea. So, well, I guess what I'm trying to say is that people are happy with what I deliver.

Competent use of the SDM helped construct developers' identity, legitimised their role and symbolically constructed perceptions that they were professional. In short, identities were constructed through the process of interaction wit the clients' preferred SDM. A project leader stated it emphatically when she said

If I didn't do it [follow the steps and produce the documentation] then I wouldn't be doing my job properly.

In terms of rituals, in its most visible form, the SDM in The Bank mandated documentation and required project leaders to call for walk-through meetings to review project status and sign-off on deliverables to indicate work at that stage was completed satisfactorily. According to Hirschheim and Newman (1991), these sign-off meetings ar an example of a ritual or ceremony that can be very important to developers because they provide order and struc ture to their profession. The SDM in this sense was seen in a functional way beyond facilitating project coordinatio as a mechanism to review progress, as expressed by a project leader:

… when it comes to [walk-through] meetings with various participants they know that what they'll get from the meeting will be documented and they know that the document is a part of method deliverables.

However, rituals such as walk-through meetings in ISD can also function to maintain the appearance of rationalit (Robey & Markus, 1984), and signify that the actions taken are not arbitrary but acceptable, and provide meaning to the actions taken, even though often the reality of ISD tends to defy rational explanations (Hirschheim & Newman, 1991). The ritual of attending these walk-through meetings was understood by developers as legitimat because they saw it as natural, and contextually and culturally grounded

The walk-through meetings took on a ritualistic character that conveyed a powerful message to the developers: ISD is meaningful work practice, sign-off with the client involves rational decision-making and the IT division you work for is a professional entity providing valuable services to the client. Consequently, as a result of this message the developers complied with these work arrangements because they met their sense of professional reality and the felt valued. In terms of power, when the developers participated in the walk-through process, they were acting in self-interest, while the client who unwittingly benefited from the developers' perception of the walk-through ritua as rational, imperceptibly exercised dispositional power in an unobtrusive way.

## 6.2.2 | Facilitative power in the systemic circuit

Power is also exercised through work practices and techniques of a discipline, in our case, the discipline associate with ISD. Discipline can be understood as a body of knowledge, and as a synonym for control. As such a discipline presents a social structure, and power is embedded in the very fabric of that structure. It constrains what developer see, how developers see, and limits their capacity for resistance. Knowledge or know-how of the discipline of ISD and its language guide, direct and facilitate the activities of developers. This knowledge both constrains and enables how developers think, and how they do things.

In The Bank, the developers took the role conferred on the SDM for granted as the accepted way of doing work—a role that constituted an element of facilitative power. When they joined The Bank, developers accepted their role in the existing order of things because they saw it as natural, and the use of the SDM mostly went unnoticed b them. The SDM had become habitual and part of the work culture of the organisation. The control structures embed ded in the SDM, while not entirely undetected by developers, remained largely un-discussed. Many interviewees when asked if they discussed the relative merits of the SDM with other colleagues said they did not as the follow ing excerpt from a developer illustrates:

It's one of those things that you discuss when you're relatively new to the organisation but after that it's just accepted. You do it because it's part of the culture.

The work practices such as the acceptance of the mandated lifecycle, obtaining sign-off on documentation, routine patterns of development and conforming to external industry work practices all formed part of the discipline of ISD

By focusing outside the organisation on its disciplinary environment, the techniques of the discipline of ISD also enable us to understand the stabilised and institutionalised practices that took place within The Bank. These tech niques are based on the ISD discipline and its recognised work practices and are thus the result of exogenous contin gencies that influence the enactment of the SDM. Disciplinary techniques and their impact on enactmen demonstrate that it is not just the client who is the only or main source of power.

The following interview excerpts illustrate how disciplinary techniques exert power in the form of adherence to industry-wide work practices, and technical standards. As a developer expressed, the SDM played a significant guid ing role with frequent use of contractors and employment mobility from outside The Bank, using a standard practic made sense:

… because you're working with people who move around a lot, obviously it's going to help a lot if everyone's using the same style. But I think, like anything, it's the role of a methodology anywhere: it's a recipe, it's a rule book.

Another example of industry-wide work practices, as an external force, was the gaining of quality certification. Th Bank needed to demonstrate that standard processes were in place, and that they followed a method for both pro ject management and software development. A project manager agreed that with a mixture of skill sets a commo standard built into the SDM has advantages in terms of indicators of development process capability:

I believe the bank wants to reach some kind of maturity where there is a format and standards. And it just provides some visibility as far as ensuring that the outcomes are doable. With a mixture of skil sets it's important that we all have the same standards and the outcomes are the same.

These technical and institutional work practices illustrate a source of power emanating through the systemic circui that presents an implicit requirement to comply with industry and global work practices embedded within the SDM. As such, this is an exemplar of facilitative power.

## 6.3 | Exogenous contingencies

As illustrated above, exogenous contingencies impact and introduce change to the techniques of a discipline in th systemic circuit of power; in our case, the discipline of ISD and the rules of meaning in the social circuit in The Bank. In terms of techniques of the discipline, The Bank was influenced to follow industry standards because of the mobile nature of workers in the IT profession, and to meet external standards of development practice

Concerning rules of meaning and membership in the social circuit, the language of the SDM as expressed in doc umentation developers' produced facilitated communication between full-time employees and contract developers The habitual use of documentation also served as a symbol and evidence of developers' work performance, whil rituals involving walk-through meetings demonstrated and validated developers' professional membership in the IT division.

## 6.4 | Obligatory passage point

The three CoP are connected into a network through an OPP, which the immediate actors in the episodic circuit accept and adopt as the mediator for all interactions (Clegg, 1989). Once adopted, the OPP influences techniques of the discipline in the systemic circuit, while empowering or disempowering the actors in the episodic circuit. The OPP also influences the rules of meaning in the social circuit, providing access to resources for the actors in the episodic circuit.

In the case of The Bank, the enactment of the SDM was the OPP for the developers and the client. ## 6.5 | Summary of the findings

We have applied Clegg's (1989) CoP framework of causal, dispositional, as well as facilitative power and hav shown that developers were exposed to all three types of power inscribed within the enactment of the SDM ultimately impacting on their relationships with the client, leading to their acquiescence and acceptance of a power relationship they perceived as unequal. In terms of the first part of the research question, ‘how is obtru sive and unobtrusive power inscribed in SDMs?’ our analysis found that the client controlled the critica resources and funding through stage-gate approval and sign-off on documentation, an instance of obtrusive causal power. Second, more subtle but equally important, the developers in this relationship were exposed to unobtrusive, dispositional power of the SDM: they were unaware that the work context imperceptibly and uncon sciously induced the meaning and perception of the SDM using symbols and rituals in the workplace that met their sense of professional reality. Third, as an instance of unobtrusive, facilitative power, in their relationshi with the client, the developers complied with the status quo because power was embedded in the SDM in the form of habitual work practices and discipline of ISD that they took for granted. Thus, in answer to the secon part of the research question, ‘what is the effect of obtrusive and unobtrusive power on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of SDM?’, it appeared that during the enactment of the SDM the developers felt disempowered and were compliant, while the client appeared to benefit from a situation that seemingl favoured them.

In the next section, we discuss our findings in relation to the research questions in more detail, leading us to transfer and formulate our findings in the form of a set of propositions theorising how obtrusive and unobtrusiv power is inscribed in SDMs, and what the effect of this power is on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of an SDM.

## 7 | DISCUSSION AND CONTRIBUTIONS

Our research adds to the understanding of how obtrusive and unobtrusive power is inscribed in SDMs and what the effect of this power is on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of an SDM. By examining how one SDM shaped the developer-client relationship, how it conveyed meaning and how systems developers responded to its prescriptions, we gain insight into how power was practised and how authority structures embedded within th SDM constrained the actions of the developers.

The findings provide grounded evidence of how enacting the SDM constrained the routines of the developers We found that the influence of the SDM occurred through the constraints and prescriptions partially mandated b the client (obtrusive power), but more so through the habitual behaviour or routines that shaped developers approaches to using the institutionalised SDM in their workplace (unobtrusive power)

Using Clegg's (1989) CoP framework facilitates deep understanding of how obtrusive and unobtrusive power is inscribed in an SDM. As the effect of such inscription with the SDM as an institutionalised source of power and through its enactment, developers were compliant with an unequal power scenario. The essence of unobtrusiv power was the perceived legitimacy of the SDM in the social and systemic circuits within The Bank and its IT division that conveyed its meanings and taken for granted work practices. These meanings and work practices influenced the perceptions of the developers so that they viewed the ISD process in an unbiased way unaware of the dispositiona and facilitative power embedded within the SDM

These findings build on the research of Hirschheim and Klein (1989) who suggested that SDMs can significantly inform and shape the cognitions and actions of organisational members engaged in ISD. Using the CoP framework as a base, we can theorise about the effect of obtrusive and unobtrusive power on the developer-client relationshi during the enactment of an SDM in ISD.

## 7.1 | Theorising power in SDM enactment

In this section, we move from empirical description and existing theory towards theorising how power is inscribed in SDMs, and the effect such inscription has on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of an SDM. We present a set of propositions grounded in our case findings in response to our research questions. The proposition theorise what underlies the relationship between developers and clients based on both obtrusive and unobtrusiv power inscribed in an SDM in terms of Clegg's integrated CoP framework. We also explain the relationships between the framework's concepts and elaborate each proposition with evidence from both the case and prior literature

The role of an SDM is not to obtain funding for IT projects. However, since causal power is directly related to the use of financial resources, it is not surprising that the developers perceived the client to exercise control through a budget and stage-gate funding approval process inscribed within the SDM. In terms of the developer-client rela tionship, it is reasonable to expect developers would perceive the business client—during the enactment of the SDM and its mandate to adhere to stage-gate funding approval—to exercise causal power in its obtrusive form over them In this regard, as an effect for the developer-client relationship, and with a focus on causal power inscribed and enacted through an SDM, we propose the following.

Proposition 1a. Dependence on a stage-gate funding approval process inscribed within an SDM, generates causa power in the enactment of an SDM

Valenca and Alves' (2017) empirical findings suggest that an increase in an actor's dependence leverages another actor's power. Considering the situations observed in The Bank, we propose that dependence on financial resource through stage-gate funding has a significant impact on the exercise of causal power in the enactment of the SDM

Our findings in relation to gaining sign-off in walk-through meetings further exposed the developers' perception that their relationship with the business client was unequal. This contrasts with a body of literature that views signoff as a way to oppress the business client instead of empowering them. For instance, Robey and Markus (1984) and Hirschheim and Newman (1991) interpret sign-off as giving power to developers over the client. They argue that sign-off does not empower the client but the opposite. This is so since the client is required to make decisions and bear responsibility for those, without being properly informed and without necessarily having any understanding of what the technical result will be like. Likewise, Kirsch and Beath (1996) viewed sign-off for clients as purely symbolic rather than as a mechanism for clients to accept ownership of project outcomes. This argument is not supported b findings from this study. Rather, there is evidence in this case that the client exercised causal power over the devel opers through their mandate requiring documentation as inscribed in the SDM as a prerequisite to sign-off, with sign-off leading to resources for the next stage of development. Based on our findings, we thus propose:

Proposition 1b. Sign-off on documentation leading to resources inscribed in an SDM generates causal power of the client over developers in the enactment of the SDM

Figure 2 summarises our theorising and depicts the generic forms of obtrusive and unobtrusive power in the three CoP that were all at play between the developers in the IT division and their business clients within The Bank Propositions 1a and 1b are both placed on the arrow, denoting causal power circulating between the client and the developers.

Dispositional power is derived from symbolic sources represented by the rules of meaning brought into play to legitimise objectives and outcomes within the social circuit of power.

The meaning of the SDM in The Bank, represented through its local language, habits and rituals, played an important role in mobilising the norms and values of development practice for developers. Based around its accepted an everyday use, the SDM defined their identity, met their sense of professional reality and facilitated a professiona relationship with the client, thereby obviating any direct or indirect resistance by the developers. This perception of the legitimacy of SDMs accords with Orlikowski and Robey (1991, p. 159) who, without a particular focus on the rol of power, argue that, ‘developers draw on the values and conventions of their organisation, occupation, and training to build information systems … they are informed by information systems development methodologies and knowledge about their organisation to build information systems’. This leads us to the following proposition about th effect of this unobtrusive form of dispositional power on the developer-client relationship:

Proposition 2a. The socially constructed sense of professionalism provides social meaning and social integratio obviating resistance by developers towards the client in the enactment of the SDM.

The interview excerpts show how enactment of the SDM over time led to the development of change-resistant norms and values that were perceived as natural and legitimate by developers. Through uncovering the rules of meaning and formal structures inscribed in the SDM within the social circuit of power, an application of Clegg's (1989) framework offers a partial explanation of how dispositional power in The Bank was established and maintained.

Our application of the social CoP provides explanations to why and how SDMs are accepted. The essence of facilitative power in this case was the unconscious acceptance of the techniques and traditions of the discipline of ISD by the developers. This discipline provided a way of thinking and acting for the developers who took for granted the role conferred on the SDM as the accepted way of doing work. The developers drew upon thei knowledge of the SDM that was deemed both plausible and legitimate, thereby securing meaning and identit through the practices it invoked. The facilitative power of the discipline generates consent because the dominant discourse and knowledge of established work practices embedded within the disciplinary technology—the SDM, becomes reified and taken for granted (Doolin, 2004). Facilitative power was intertwined into the fabric of The Bank' IT division and inscribed in the SDM as a disciplinary technology, and its effect during enactment ensured that demands and challenges were never made by developers. This leads us to our next proposition:

Proposition 3a. The discipline of ISD generates consent as the dominant discourse and knowledge of established work practices become inscribed in the SDM with the effect that the status quo of work practice will not b challenged during enactment.

Our findings relating to the systemic circuit of power explain the developers' acquiescence further. The materia conditions such as the generation of documentation and the developers' obligation to report to the business client for signatures were systemically inscribed in the enactment of the SDM and remained unquestioned. Furthermore, the discipline of ISD and the knowledge of how things should be done is more than just an unconscious acceptance of values, traditions and culture as manifested in the social circuit of power. Rather, it is something that was continuously reproduced and reinforced by work practice and manifested itself in material form in the SDM. We illustrated that the SDM as a material artefact—a technology possessing disciplinary agency—reinforced and transmitted power structures. This finding is also consistent with previous studies, by Gosain (2004) for example. He postulated that technical artefacts such as an SDM can act as a carrier and an important institutional embodiment serving to pre serve rules by constraining the actions of human agents, on the one hand, and that the technical artefact is subject to institutional forces and institutional processes that set the rules of rationality, on the other. This leads us to our next proposition:

Proposition 3b. Facilitative power is continuously reproduced and reinforced by work practice and manifests itself in material form in the SDM.

Moreover, our application of Clegg's (1989) notion of facilitative power encompasses the concept of institution (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Scott, 2001) and offers further explanation of how the power of the discipline operates as an institutional source of power. According to new institutional theorists (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Scott, 2001) this form of disciplinary power works through the on-going routines and day-to-day work practices in organisations In this way, power is built into and diffused through the system in which it is exercised. The discipline of ISD ensured that the imposed SDM-enabled functions were translated into the unquestioned normative structures of the IT divi sion in The Bank (eg, a common terminology, sign-off, walk-through meetings and acting professionally). With com plete acceptance and use within the IT division, the enacted SDM as the OPP in the systemic circuit played an important role in mobilising the norms and values of development practice, and provided a sense of legitimacy whe dealing with the business client—with the effect that the developers complied with the status quo

The original CoP framework depicted only social relationships occurring among agencies in the episodic circuit In line with Pickering (1995) and Oliveira and Clegg (2015), we propose that both human actors (in our case, the developers and the business client) and the technology (in our case, the SDM) have agency and thus socio-technica relationships can potentially replace social relationships. Through the developers' acceptance of work practices involving the SDM as the accepted way of doing work in the systemic circuit, episodic exercises of causal powe involving stage-gate funding processes are not always required for developer compliance. Instead, causal power in the episodic circuit can be replaced by facilitators emerging from relationships involving work practices and tech niques of the ISD discipline in the systemic circuit. In this way, an OPP is established through the systemic circui and works just as effectively as episodic exercises of power. This leads us to our next two propositions:

Proposition 3c. The established routines of ISD practice become part of the socialisation process in the form o facilitative power that influences not only what system developers do but also how they feel about what the do during the enactment of an SDM during ISD practice.

Proposition 3d. Episodic exercises of power in ISD practice are not always required and an OPP in the form of an SDM from the systemic circuit replaces episodic episodes of causal power during the enactment of an SDM.

Consequently, Propositions 3a, 3b and 3c are placed on the arrow depicting how facilitative power circulates towards the episodic circuit where it disempowers the developers and replaces causal power in that circuit Proposition 3d is placed on the arrow depicting the circulation of the resulting power through the OPP labelled Enact to signify (as described in Figure 2 and above) that the enacted SDM becomes an OPP for the developers Once adopted as the OPP, the SDM then influences the development organisation through its inscribed rules of meaning and membership as well as through the techniques of the IT discipline and existing work practices.

The exogenous contingencies of the ISD discipline that are imposed on project managers and developers in an IT division, and on the client are a requirement to comply with industry, national and global work practices. Global work practice and the mobile nature of system developers subtly introduce change in the form of new ISD practices, and thereby exert facilitative power over internal development practice. We therefore make the following proposition:

Proposition 4. External forces such as new industry practices outside of the IT division exert facilitative power over internal ISD practices during the enactment of an SDM.

In Figure 2, Proposition 4 is placed next to the arrows representing the circulation of power towards both th social and systemic circuits and depicts how exogenous contingencies introduce changes to both these circuits and their respective types of power.

For the most part, the SDM played the role of an OPP mediating the relationship and interactions between th developers and the client in a network of work activities, fixing the rules guiding developers' actions and constraining available possibilities. Together, in the episodic circuit, through a stage-gate funding approval process linked to bud get and financial resources, the developers are exposed to causal power. Through rules of meaning and membership in the social circuit and techniques of production and discipline in the systemic circuit, power and its effect on the developer-client relationships are an expression of, and inscribed in, the SDM. Thus, the effect of such inscription is that the enactment of the SDM as the OPP framed the institutional field as both an obtrusive source of power in th episodic circuit, and an unobtrusive, invisible source of power in the systemic circuit. This leads us to our fina proposition:

Proposition 5. Developers have no choice but to accept the enacted SDM as the OPP, as created through the three CoP.

We therefore placed Proposition 5 next to the box representing the enacted SDM as the OPP in the analysed CoP.

This research contributes to the literature on SDM enactment (Fitzgerald et al., 2002; Lang & Fitzgerald, 2007; Mackay et al., 2000) by providing a novel perspective of obtrusive and unobtrusive power, uncovering the invisible structures of power impacting on relationships in ISD between client and developer. Our contribution is twofold: first, we explain how obtrusive and unobtrusive power is inscribed in an SDM as an institutionalised source of power. For this purpose, we provide an adaptation of the original CoP framework based on Clegg (1989) and Oliveira and Clegg (2015) (see Figure 2) by highlighting socio-technical relations, and facilitators such as work practices and tech niques of the ISD discipline in the systemic circuit. Second, we answer the question: What is the effect of obtrusiv and unobtrusive power on the developer-client relationship during the enactment of an SDM? By doing so, we exten existing research programmes on the enactment of SDMs by adding concepts and themes of power as summarised in Table 3, as well as five propositions theorising the enactment of an SDM as an instance of obtrusive and unobtru sive power.

## 8 | CONCLUSIONS, LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

The theoretical discussion provided in this paper contributes to research on the topic of SDM enactment and in par ticular the effect of obtrusive and unobtrusive power on the developer-client relationship. Further studies that extend or evaluate our theoretical framework in the context of SDM enactment are warranted. These studies coul take the form of interpretive or in-depth case studies where further theorisation of the enactment process is built These studies could concentrate on a different type of method such as agile methods. Such case studies can draw on the theoretical propositions presented in this paper to elicit meanings of current practices in agile IS and software development processes. The results could develop the propositions further by qualifying them according to different types of development environment, which in turn would enrich our understanding of the social aspects that are in play during the enactment of SDMs.

For example, a specific avenue for future research would be to study the other half in the client-developer pair Clients' impression of SDMs and how they view their relationship with systems developers would be insightful. Apart from Sauer and Lau (1997) who argue that previous research has obscured the legitimate concern of business with SDMs, and Kirsch, Sambamurthy, Ko, and Purvis (2002) who examined client liaisons between developers and th business unit, there is a paucity of research about the role of SDMs in the control of ISD projects from the client' perspective. Based on an understanding that power is relational (Clegg, 1989), and this study's findings, a natura follow-up question would be: To what extent is power reflected in the enactment of an SDM from the client's per spective? This research can build on the results presented here, together with the methods-in-action framewor (Fitzgerald et al., 2002; Lang & Fitzgerald, 2007) by providing additional theoretical content

Our study has some limitations. We identified in Section 3 the strengths of the CoP framework—it offers a rela tional view of power, integrates three levels of power, emphasises context and external factors but most importantl considers the technological artefact—the SDM. These concepts give the theory its explanatory power. Yet, as w found, deploying the CoP elements—the circuits with their own nomenclature, the OPPs, the exogenous factors— can leave the researcher and readers puzzled as to their meaning and this complexity represents a limitation. A Backhouse et al. (2006, p. 429) clearly put it: ‘the framework is not exactly intuitive’.

In this study, we could have employed other models of power. One prominent alternative is Hardy (1985) an Hardy and Leiba-O'Sullivan's (1998) four-dimensional model of power based on the work of Lukes (1974) and Fou cault (1980). Clegg and Hardy's models share many concepts in common. Both view power as a property of relationships and both agree that power is ‘systemic’, invisible or not apparent although they use vastly different terms to denote the same concepts. Both models illustrate the complex relationships involved in the various forms of powe with figures. Yet, the main reason why the CoP framework was chosen is its integration of the different types of power within the OPP represented by the SDM, thereby providing an explanation of how power is inscribed within its enactment.

From a methodological perspective, adopting the CoP framework as a theoretical background and analytical too for investigating the workings of power is not without challenges. It has also been argued that such studies require a large amount of data, preferably gathered in longitudinal case studies (Silva & Backhouse, 2003). The Bank was stud ied within a relatively short period, with two rounds of data gathering over 4 months, thus being somewhat limited in this regard. However, the number of the personal interviews undertaken, and the quantity of data gathered, provided us with insight into power relationship issues of the IT division, together with the developers' impression when enacting the SDM.

A further limitation relates to the scope of the study. Additional case studies must be conducted to increase th credibility and reliability of the theoretical propositions developed in this research. Future efforts should be pursued and directed towards providing more research explaining how power issues and outcomes are associated. The prop ositions would benefit not only from being refined or tested in newer organisational forms (ie, outsourcing of IT ser vices), but also from being tested in other development scenarios (ie, Agile, DevOps) incorporating different methods based on best practices for systems development where the levels of interaction between developers and client differ.

In conclusion, few studies in the ISD literature have explicitly examined how power is inscribed in SDMs and how the effect of unobtrusive power can be exerted both as dispositional and facilitative power on developer-client relations. To extend the understanding and to further explain SDM enactment, we recommend Clegg's (1989) inte grative CoP framework as a means of addressing how obtrusive and unobtrusive power is inscribed in an SDM, and we present a case where the developers did not challenge, and by-and-large, complied with, the perceived distribu tion of power in their relationship with the client. IS researchers still have an incomplete understanding of how developers and the client collectively use SDMs in their day-to-day work, and the forces that impact on the situated use of these devices for ISD. A lack of field research on the impact of unobtrusive power between these two groups precludes a full understanding of how SDMs are enacted. Our analysis and findings are a contribution in this regard and provide a foundation for further studies.

## AUTHOR BIOGRAPHIES

Bruce Rowlands is an adjunct senior lecturer within the School of Information & Communication Technology, Griffith University. His research is informed by the social informatics literature as a means for conceptualising the social context of information systems development. His research has been published in ranked IS journals including European Journal of Information Systems, Information & Management, Information Technology & People, Scandinavian Journal of Information Systems, Australasian Journal of Information Systems, and has presented his research at the major IS conferences.

Karlheinz Kautz holds the position of Professor of Digital Business at the Royal Melbourne Institute of Technology (RMIT) University, in Melbourne, Australia. His research interests are in information systems development, digitalization and digital transformation, the diffusion and adoption of digital innovations, and the impact of IT on organizations and society. He has published in these areas in journals such as Management Information Systems Quarterly, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology, Information Technology & People, the Scandinavian Journal of Information Systems and the Australasian Journal of Information Systems.
