## Abstract

China is widely considered a world leader in e-commerce. In recent years, e-commerce in China has made significant progress and gone through rounds after rounds of innovations. Technological advancements have enabled the integration of online and offline channels, allowing consumers to choose their preferred shopping channel. Thus, competition and cooperation between online and offline channels have become important issues. Drawing on confirmation bias theory and the unique cultural lens (low uncertainty avoidance and a high level of cynicism) that exists in China, we conceptualize online prejudice and propose a model to analyse how it affects channel selection. A scenario-based survey, along with an explorative pre-study, was conducted to test our hypotheses. The results showed that prejudice toward the online channel does exist in China. Further, this online prejudice mediates the relationship between perceived uncertainty and channel selection. That is, uncertainty can induce consumers' online prejudice, which in turn predicts their channel selection behaviour. Furthermore, the mediating effect of online prejudice is contingent upon product type (i.e., experience products vs. search products).

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

E-commerce has developed rapidly over the past few decades and continues to grow. Globally, increasing numbers of business models and innovations are being introduced into this field. This is particularly the case for e-commerce businesses in China, where new e-commerce modes, for example, live streaming e-commerce, social e-commerce and online to offline (O2O) e-commerce, are growing dramatically in recent years (CNNIC, 2020). One fundamenta challenge behind the above-mentioned development is the competition between online and offline channels. That is, only when online channels win the competition against their rival offline channels can an e-commerce business pros per. It is argued that this challenge is becoming especially critical nowadays when e-commerce is embracing mor and more new business models and innovations (e.g., social e-commerce, rural e-commerce, and O2O business), which may involve more competition against offline channels. While existing literature mainly focuses on what may drive customers to adopt online channels (e.g., Gefen et al., 2003; Pavlou et al., 2007), seldom is known regardin what inhibits them from choosing some online channel when compared with offline channels. Particularly considering the co-existence of online and offline channels, it is believed that the perception (toward the two competing channels) rooted in a customer's mind will determine her/his final channel selection.

Although the development of e-commerce enhances consumers' ability to search for and purchase products an services via either online or offline channels (CNNIC, 2018; Verhoef et al., 2015), some consumers are still more likely to choose offline channels even though they are not as beneficial as e-commerce channels. For instance, whil prices on fresh food e-commerce platforms are relatively low as a result of their lower operating costs, most Chinese consumers still prefer to purchase food from offline markets (iResearch, 2020). Furthermore, interviews with cus tomers revealed that many questioned the quality of the products offered by the fresh food e-commerce platforms which confirms their previous negative experiences with other e-commerce businesses. Thus, despite the number of transactions continues to rise, most Chinese e-commerce firms are involved in price wars, pursuing impractical busi ness models, and running at a loss (Huang et al., 2015; Zhao, 2017).

Upon the above-mentioned phenomenon, we conjecture that a consumer may unconsciously compare the com peting online and offline channels, and accordingly form a biased perception which is rooted in the consumer's mind that is, simply prefers offline channel while questions online channel. That is, consumers are more likely to believ that online channels tend to be fraudulent, counterfeit, or inferior, that is, they to some extent display prejudice against online channels (hereafter referred to as online prejudice). It is argued that online prejudice is particularly sig nificant in China. On the one hand, different from those in many other developed economies, China's online market place is characterized by a high level of uncertainty, with both good and bad sellers and products (Bai et al., 2015 Chang et al., 2019; Taormina & Chong, 2010). It is argued that e-commerce may grow faster in China because of its low level of uncertainty avoidance (Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Stojcic et al., 2016; Zeng & Hao, 2016), whereby con sumers are relatively more tolerant of ambiguous businesses and products. That is, due to low uncertainty avoidance culture, Chinese consumers are more likely to suffer from negative experiences in the online channel, which in tur may lead customers to form a biased perception.

On the other hand, compared with an offline channel, an online channel always involves some ‘hidden cues’ that may potentially result in consumer misunderstanding (Ghose, 2009; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2008), and this is particularly significant in China because of its unique culture and business environment. For instance, the lower prices offered for online transactions are often based on lower inventory and property rental costs. However, as they gain more experience in online marketplaces, consumers will be exposed to more negative experiences as a result of either product or seller uncertainty, which may lead them to question the underlying ethics of online businesses. These biased beliefs or attitudes, which can potentially distort people's behaviour in online marketplaces, might be particularly relevant in China, which is often seen as a highly cynical culture in which people are more likely to be suspiciou of the motives of other social actors (Malham & Saucier, 2014; Wang & Laufer, 2020). In this cultural context, Chinese consumers are more likely to suspect that sellers are self-interested and thus perceive low prices as reflecting low product quality.

A large survey revealed that most Chinese consumers assume that the prices charged by online channels shoul be cheaper than those charged by offline channels (CCTION, 2020). This finding echoes those of previous studies which found that the rapid growth of online businesses in China was largely attributable to low prices and efficien logistics (Kwon & Lennon, 2009; Yang et al., 2013). If consumers always expect lower prices from online channels the long-term growth of Chinese e-commerce firms will be threatened. Therefore, it is important to investigate: (a) how does online prejudice affect online channel selection in China, and (b) which are the critical antecedents fo online prejudice?

Drawing upon confirmation bias theory (CBT), we propose that Chinese consumers' selection of online channels is influenced by two particular types of online prejudice (i.e., product prejudice and seller prejudice), which in turn are affected by perceived uncertainty. Further, these relationships are contingent upon the product type (i.e., searc products vs. experience products). To test our hypotheses, an explorative study and a scenario-based survey were conducted.

This study makes several significant contributions to the growing body of literature on e-commerce develop ment in China. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to theoretically analyse the critical yet understudied concept of online prejudice and empirically confirm its effect on channel selection in the e-commerce marketplace in China. Our study establishes a solid theoretical foundation for future research aimed at exploring the impact of online prejudice on numerous other outcomes. Second, the results of this study extend our understandin of CBT by expanding its application to the investigation of online prejudice. This opens a new window for future research on the application of this theory to consumer bias. Besides, the inclusion of product types further enriches our understanding of the application of CBT concerning e-commerce. While online prejudice is pervasive, its rela tionship with relevant outcomes and antecedents is not static; rather, it is contingent on certain external factors. This contingency perspective should provide scholars with the incentive to seek a more comprehensive understanding of online prejudice. Furthermore, the current study opens a ‘black box’ by demonstrating the mechanism underlying the effect of uncertainty perception on shopping channel selection. Future research should investigate channe selection in other areas.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND AND RESEARCH MODEL

## 2.1 | Prejudice and purchase channel selection in China

Today, products can be purchased via either online or offline channels, and consumers need to make a choic between these two purchase channels based on a proper evaluation and comparison (Frasquet et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2011, 2013; Yi et al., 2018). Although a detailed cost–benefit analysis can be used to explain channel selection (Falk et al., 2007; Jian et al., 2017; Neslin et al., 2006), an examination of differences among vari ous cultures may provide us with a greater understanding because consumers' beliefs can be shaped by the local cul ture (Aaker, 2000; Lalwani & Shavitt, 2013). As one of the leading nations in the field of online markets, China' practices in general and consumer behaviour in particular, can provide significant contributions to the literature Thus, in this study, we investigate Chinese consumers' purchase channel selection.

In general, two Chinese cultural features confirm the importance of understanding channel selection from a prej udice perspective. First, China's low uncertainty avoidance culture underlines Chinese consumers' biased understanding of online channels. The level of uncertainty avoidance refers to the extent to which people feel threatene by unknown or ambiguous situations (Hofstede, 2001). It reflects how society deals with future uncertainty and th level of anxiety brought about by this ambiguity (Stojcic et al., 2016). China displays a relatively low level of uncer tainty avoidance (Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Stojcic et al., 2016; Zeng & Hao, 2016), and consumers are likely more toler ant of uncertainties associated with online businesses (Dimoka et al., 2012; Pavlou et al., 2007). Because of thi particular culture, e-commerce has expanded rapidly in China over the last two decades (Gefen et al., 2003)

However, a low level of uncertainty avoidance also encourages sellers to flood online markets with low-quality prod ucts, which may outperform high-quality products as a result of their lower prices. Faced with more and more nega tive experiences involving low-quality products, Chinese consumers may form the belief that products offered in online markets are more likely to be low quality. Obviously, this biased belief, that is, prejudice against online chan nels, is harmful to China's online markets and may lead to a predicament whereby consumers will be less likely to pay more for products sold online

Second, cynicism can reinforce the significance of interpreting channel selection from a prejudice perspective Cynicism refers to a generalized belief that others' actions are deceitful. It is associated with unrealistic expectations a sense of being let down, feelings of betrayal, and negative affect (Hochwarter et al., 2004). As an essential dimen sion of social axioms, cynicism has been used to explain variances in individual behaviours (Iliescu et al., 2017 Leung & Bond, 2004; Leung et al., 2002). China is seen as a society with a high level of cynicism (Leung & Bond, 2004; Malham & Saucier, 2014), and the online atmosphere in China is also highly cynical (Malham & Saucier, 2014 Wang & Laufer, 2020).

In general, a highly cynical culture is likely to distort Chinese consumers' judgement. When people believe that others' actions are motivated primarily by self-interest, they are less likely to value or engage in collaboration and compromise. Highly cynical customers, because of their enduring mistrust and negative attitudes, are more likely to question sellers (Skarmeas & Leonidou, 2013). An increasing body of literature on cynicism indicates that cynical con sumers in developing and emerging countries may become sufficiently aggravated to display deviant behaviour, suc as being less inclined to adopt new products/services (Efendioglu & Yip, 2004; Lee, 2008). Cynical consumers are unwilling to adopt digital innovations such as mobile banking (Chaouali et al., 2017) and online shopping (van Dolen et al., 2012) because cynics tend to be suspicious of new products/services (Alnuaimi et al., 2011; Chylinski & Chu, 2010; Darke & Ritchie, 2007). In the Chinese context, Gao et al. (2014) found that consumers are more cynica regarding advertising in China than in the USA, while Yin et al. (2019) found that Chinese consumers were sceptica regarding green practices by hotels, especially those advertising low prices. Since cynical individuals usually display an attitude of suspicion toward products (Alnuaimi et al., 2011), Chinese consumers may be more likely to suspect the motives of sellers, that is, to assume that sellers are self-interested and often deliberately deliver low-qualit products. Thus, it is believed that prejudice toward both sellers and products may affect customers' channe selection.

Although online channels have been prevalent in China for some time, the low level of uncertainty avoidance and high level of cynicism in Chinese culture demands an interpretation of the channel selection process from a prej udice perspective because consumer bias can inhibit the development of online markets in China. Thus, we are moti vated to develop a deep understanding of the mechanism underlying consumers' biased shopping channel selection As shown below, prejudice can distort consumers' judgments toward a focal object, and it is particularly meaningfu for explaining consumers' channel selection behaviours in our study.

## 2.2 | Theoretical framework

Consumers' multi-channel selection behaviour is based on their cognitive evaluation of different channels (Frasquet et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2011, 2013; Yi et al., 2018). However, during the cognition process, people tend to simplify things and quickly assimilate them into a stereotype (Fiske, 2000) based on the traits that imme diately come to people's minds when thinking about a specific group (Nelson, 2015) without examining the detail (Hippel, 1994). Although such stereotypes may be efficient, they are often inaccurate, negative, and overgeneralized (Allport, 1954). In the literature, those negative stereotypes are conceptualized as prejudice, which refers to a nega tive attitude toward a group or members of a group (Nelson, 2015). Various types of prejudice pervade society, such as racial, gender, and regional prejudice. As discussed above, Pavlou et al. (2007) pointed out that uncertainty in rela tion to online shopping includes both seller uncertainty and product uncertainty. Similarly, the object of consumers prejudices can be both sellers and products. Thus, in this study, we identified two types of prejudice against onlin shopping: seller prejudice and product prejudice. While seller prejudice refers to consumers' negative attitude regarding online sellers compared with offline sellers, product prejudice refers to consumers' belief that product sold online are not as good as those sold offline.

To enable us to examine the significance of online prejudice, we use CBT to develop our theoretical framework Confirmation bias refers to the tendency of humans to allocate excessive weight to information that confirms their initial beliefs (Forde, 2017; Nickerson, 1998). Specifically, CBT posits that individuals prefer to make decisions by relying on information that confirms their initial beliefs, hypotheses and conjectures (Klayman & Ha, 1987; Trope & Bassok, 1982; Yin et al., 2016). For instance, CBT has been used to investigate how consumers evaluate positive and negative reviews, and consumers have a tendency to perceive the reviews that confirm (vs. disconfirm) their initia beliefs as more helpful (Yin et al., 2016). Similarly, CBT can explain consumers' online information search and infor mation avoidance behaviours (Lallement et al., 2019).

It is argued that CBT is useful for understanding the role of online prejudice in our context. First, the rapid, initia stages of growth of e-commerce in China (i.e., numerous low-quality products spread over various e-commerce platforms) created an initial bias against online transactions, and China's low-level of uncertainty avoidance and highly cynical culture added to this bias. Although e-commerce in China has changed significantly, with greatly improved regulations and business environments, the initial bias remains because it is transferred to every new customer vi the social learning process. Negative events, such as counterfeit transaction records or consumer comment manipulation, continue to occur on many e-commerce platforms, particularly small platforms, confirming some consumers initial bias. In addition, most customers hear some negative comments regarding online transactions when they first explore online channels (Dimoka et al., 2012; Liang & Huang, 1998; Pavlou et al., 2007). Thus, most participants in our study displayed some degree of bias. When their bias is repeatedly reinforced, consumers may develop a negative stereotype regarding online transactions and thus develop online prejudice (Fiske, 2000; Tsao, 2014; Yin et al., 2016). When an individual displays prejudice against a particular social actor, she/he pays more attentio to their negative features and is less aware of any positive behaviour that they exhibit (Nelson, 2015). Thus, in th context of our study, a consumer may focus on negative experiences or information (e.g., other consumers' negativ online reviews), thereby confirming their initial bias, that is, online prejudice. If consumers hold prejudice against online transactions, they are more likely to focus on the negative aspects (Nelson, 2015), reducing the likelihood of them purchasing products/services via the online channel.

Online prejudice, in turn, might be affected by the uncertainty surrounding online purchases. CBT posits that confirmative cues or information can trigger a biased belief (Pyszczynski & Greenberg, 1987), and while uncertaint is mainly caused by incomplete information (Garner, 1962; Knight, 1921), it is argued that online purchase uncertainty can reinforce the confirmative cues or information on which the initial biased belief is based (Watts & Zhang, 2008; Yin et al., 2016). Specifically, online purchase uncertainty reinforces cues indicating that the seller is not honest and trustworthy and that information regarding product quality is questionable (Pavlou et al. 2007). Obviously, these cues reinforce existing biased beliefs and thus reinforce consumers' prejudice against online purchases That is, online purchase uncertainty may reinforce consumers' biased evaluations by overweighting confirmativ information and exaggerating deviations from the desired situation, and thus shape a biased belief, that is, prejudic against online transactions.

This cognition process is similar to the bridging role of stereotype matching in CBT, whereby individuals tend to simplify the cognitive process by assimilating relevant cues into stereotypes in response to which they behav accordingly (Nelson, 2015). That is, stereotypes bridge relevant cues and behaviour, and thus it is believed that prejudice can mediate the relationship between uncertainty and channel selection. Further, considering that prejudice is stronger when individuals can only access limited information (Nelson, 2015), it is proposed that transaction feature related to information availability may further explain the bridging role of online prejudice. In this study, we explor different product types in an attempt to further explain the role of online prejudice because different product type can reflect different levels of information availability (Weathers et al., 2007).

## 2.3 | Research hypotheses

It is proposed that online prejudice (including both seller prejudice and product prejudice) can prevent consumers from purchasing via online channels. CBT (Nelson, 2015) posits that when consumers display prejudice against online channels, they are more likely to label them with a negative stereotype, that is, bad sellers. More specifically, online seller prejudice refers to consumers' negative attitudes regarding online sellers compared with offline sellers, and it indicates the biased belief that online sellers are dubious regarding their marketing attempts, promise-keeping, and other relevant actions compared to offline context. These suspicions can lead consumers to believe that online sellers are self-interested, and will take advantage of online transactions. Further, online product prejudice, whic refers to consumers' belief that products sold online are not as good as those sold offline, may lead consumers to believe that online channels are gray markets selling inferior products and that the quality of products sold via onlin channels is likely to be lower than that of products sold through offline channels. This low-quality product stereo type may lead consumers to devalue online products, thereby increasing the perceived risks related to online trans actions, which in turn can prevent them from purchasing via online channels. Thus, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1a. Consumers' online seller prejudice is negatively related to online channel selection.

Hypothesis 1b. Consumers' online product prejudice is negatively related to online channel selection.

CBT states that prejudice is mainly derived from individuals' perceived level of uncertainty as a result of insuffi cient information about the target product/service (Nelson, 2015). This is particularly true in the case of online mar kets, which involve a relatively high level of uncertainty for buyers, who only have access to limited information in relation to products and sellers (Dimoka et al., 2012; Pavlou et al., 2007). In online channels, the level of perceived uncertainty depicts the extent to which a buyer believes that the online transaction is uncertain (Pavlou et al. 2007) When consumers perceive a high level of uncertainty, they may assimilate relevant cues into a negative stereotype and thus develop a prejudice against either sellers or products. This prejudice formation process is particularly salient in China. As noted above, Chinese consumers often have an initial bias against online purchases. When they perceive that an online transaction is uncertain, they believe that they are unable to obtain sufficient information regarding the transaction. To confirm their initial bias, they may choose to believe that sellers intentionally hide relevant infor mation to take advantage of them (Pavlou et al., 2007). Further, given their cynical culture, when they are unable to control the transaction process as a result of a high level of uncertainty, Chinese consumers are more likely to ques tion sellers, such as their product quality and purpose (Malham & Saucier, 2014; Wang & Laufer, 2020), whic strengthens their biased evaluation of products and sellers. Thus, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2a. Perceived uncertainty is positively related to seller prejudice.

Hypothesis 2b. Perceived uncertainty is positively related to product prejudice.

In addition to explaining the outcome (i.e., channel selection) and antecedent (i.e., uncertainty), it is proposed that online prejudice mediates the relationship between uncertainty and channel selection. CBT states that in th cognition process, people tend to simplify things by stereotype matching, which in turn drives behaviou (Nelson, 2015). That is, a given behaviour is more likely to be exhibited if some relevant external or internal cues can be matched to some stereotype. In our study, online prejudice is a form of stereotype representing consumers biased beliefs against online sellers and products, while uncertainty and channel selection can be viewed as an exter nal cue and behaviour, respectively. Following CBT, we argued that perception of uncertainty could induce and intensify online prejudice, which in turn can create bias in individuals' channel evaluation and selection. That is, the relationship between uncertainty and channel selection is mediated by online prejudice

It is further proposed that this mediation effect is contingent upon the product type. CBT implies that the bridging power of stereotype matching is determined by whether the individual can obtain sufficient information about the target product/service. That is, individuals who are unable to gain access to sufficient information are more likely to make decisions based on prejudice (Nelson, 2015). Thus, we use product type (i.e., search products vs. experience products) to further examine the mediating effect of online prejudice because product type can explain the variance in consumers' access to relevant information. Theoretically, products can be categorized as either search products or experience products based on whether consumers can evaluate the product prior to purchase (Nelson, 1970) and obtain product information without sensing or experiencing the product (Weathers et al., 2007). The greater the need to rely on one's senses to assess a product, the more experience characteristics the product possesses. By con trast, the more one believes that product information is adequate to enable evaluation of the product, the more sea rch characteristics the product possesses (Luan et al., 2016). Therefore, when shopping online, the informatio asymmetry problem may be more severe in relation to experience products than to search products. Consumers need to conjecture the quality of an experience product, which introduces channel prejudice. Although prejudic may also affect consumers' judgement regarding search products, the attributes of search products are relativel objective, which is conducive to alleviating the role of prejudice. Thus, we propose the following hypotheses

Hypothesis 3. Online prejudice (both seller prejudice and product prejudice) mediates the relationship between perceived uncertainty and channel selection.

Hypothesis 4. The mediation effect of online prejudice (both seller prejudice and product prejudice) is stronger in relatio to experience products than to search products.

## 3 | METHODOLOGY

To empirically verify the existence of online prejudice and validate the proposed hypotheses, two studies were conducted in China. First, an explorative study using convenient subjects (i.e., undergraduate students) was conducted to confirm the existence of online prejudice. Participants were asked to complete a survey questionnaire and th results were analysed to obtain empirical evidence of how online prejudice and purchase uncertainty influenced their channel selection. Second, a scenario-based survey was conducted to verify the findings of the first study and further test the mediating effect of the product type (i.e., search products vs. experience products) on onlin prejudice.

## 3.1 | Pre-study

## 3.1.1 | Research design

An exploratory study was conducted to confirm the existence of online prejudice among potential consumers and preliminarily verify the first three hypotheses (i.e., Hypotheses 1a–3). After considering the research context and objectives, chocolate was selected as the product used to test the various hypotheses for the following reasons First, the taste is the main attribute of chocolate and easy to evaluate. Second, people are familiar with chocolate especially young people, who are the primary users of online markets. Third, chocolate is widely sold, both online and offline. Finally, chocolate is reasonably priced, in that it is not prohibitively expensive. Thus, participants could commit to the study while remaining relatively relaxed (Astous & Mathieu, 2008; Wang & Chang, 2013).

All of the chocolate used in the study was purchased from an online vendor and had the same packaging style price and production date. Specifically, each package included numerous small, individually wrapped pieces of choco late that did not have any detailed product information, such as the production date or price. In the explorator study, we showed participants two packages of chocolate and told them that one package was purchased online while the other package was purchased from an offline store. They were told that the packages were identical in terms of price, production date and production location.

Then, we distributed two small pieces of chocolate to each participant, one labelled ‘online’ and the other labelled ‘offline’. Each participant was invited to taste the two pieces of chocolate in random order. Then, they were asked to complete a brief paper-based questionnaire designed to measure their subjective feelings regarding the two pieces of chocolate. Finally, each participant was told that they could select one more piece of chocolate from eithe the online or the offline package as a gift, reflecting their channel selection.

The questionnaire consisted of three sections. The first section was designed to confirm the existence of preju dice. Participants were asked to compare the two pieces of chocolate and answer three questions using five-point Likert scales ranging from 1: ‘strongly disagree’ to 5: ‘strongly agree’. The three questions were as follows: (1) ‘In my opinion, the piece of chocolate bought online is tastier than the one bought offline. ; (2) The piece of chocolat bought online has a longer quality guarantee period than the one bought offline.’; and (3) ‘The piece of chocolat bought online tastes more likely it is wrapped in the original packaging, rather than fake packaging, than the one bought offline.’

The second section was designed to measure our key constructs, including product prejudice, seller prejudice and perceived uncertainty, which were used to test the first three hypotheses. Our dependent variable, channe selection, was measured using the participants' choice of either the ‘online’ or the ‘offline’ piece of chocolate. The measurement of product prejudice was based on the method proposed by Atuahene-Gima and Li (2004). Seller prej udice was measured using a scale adapted from Ghosh and John (2013). Perceived uncertainty was measured using the instrument developed by Pavlou et al. (2007). The third section collected demographic information, such as the participants' age and gender (see Table 1). All of the measurements were contextualized to the current study by a group of experts, including three researchers and two language experts. The contextualized scales were translated from English to Chinese and then back translated to verify reliability and face validity. The measurements are show in Appendix B. A total of 124 subjects participated in the explorative study, and after eliminating responses wit missing data, 120 valid responses were available for analysis

## 3.2 | Scenario-based survey

## 3.2.1 | Research design

To further confirm the direct and mediating effects and verify the boundary condition of our hypotheses, we conducted an online survey in China based on a simple shopping scenario:

Assume that you are going to purchase a product. Both online and offline channels offer the same brand, packaging, consumption time, and price. Which channel do you prefer?

To test for potential differences in relation to product type, we adopted the classic taxonomy using the terms ‘search product’ and ‘experience product’. These categories are determined based on whether consumers can evalu ate a product prior to purchase (Nelson, 1970) using the information on the product without sensing or experiencing the product (Weathers et al., 2007). Printed books and cell phones were selected as the search products, and clothe and cosmetics were selected as the experience products (Liu et al., 2016; Luan et al., 2016)

Two versions of the questionnaire (differing in terms of product type) were created on wenjuanxing.com, which is one of the largest online survey websites in China. Each questionnaire provided a brief greeting and research pur pose statement, that is, to investigate consumers' channel selection behaviour, and then requested the participants demographic information such as their age and gender. A screening question was used to determine whether partici pants had previously purchased a similar product through either an online or offline channel. Participants who had done so were able to continue the survey. The product type that they had purchased was identified, and th corresponding version of the questionnaire was presented. The snowball method was used to distribute invitations to participate in the survey. Initially, 12 assistants were invited to spread the invitations via social media such as WeChat Circle, and the people who were reached were encouraged to diffuse the invitations again. It went round and round, and we finally accumulated enough participants. The IP addresses of respondents were recorded an screened to prevent repeated responses. A total of 438 valid responses were received, 241 in the experience prod uct group, and 197 in the search product group. The survey questionnaire collected the participants' demographi details (see Table 3) and measured their perceived uncertainty, product and seller prejudice, and channel selection intentions. The instruments were identical to those used in the Pre-Study, with the exception of the dependent vari able used to measure intention to purchase via the online channel.

## 4 | DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

## 4.1 | Discussion of findings

In this study, we investigated consumers' multi-channel selection behaviour (i.e., online vs. offline shopping chan nels). The results collectively indicated that prejudice toward the online channel exists in China. Ceteris paribu (i.e., same price, same brand, same packaging), the cue of online purchases creates a bias in consumers' cognitiv evaluation and consequently affects their channel choices. The results imply that although the online shopping chan nel has grown in popularity, Chinese consumers still treat online and offline channels unequally

While previous studies have mainly examined the impact of various channel attributes on consumers' channe selection (Falk et al., 2007; Neslin et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2011, 2013), we go further by examining the role of online prejudice as a biased belief by consumers. Previous studies on channel adoption have focused on the evaluation of either the online or the offline channel (Frasquet et al., 2015; Neslin et al., 2006; Shin et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2013) However, consumers are often confronted with a situation where both online and offline channels are available and have developed a preference for a particular channel. To replicate this choice, we identified and empirically analysed two types of online prejudice, that is, seller prejudice and product prejudice, and empirically analysed their effects on channel selection. The results indicated that consumers discriminate against not only online sellers but also product that are sold online. All other conditions being equal, if a consumer displays prejudice against online shopping, she/h is more likely to purchase products via an offline channel.

In addition, prejudice was found to be reinforced by consumers' perceived uncertainty regarding online shop ping. This finding deepens our understanding of the perceived uncertainty of online purchases. Prior studies hav mainly linked uncertainty to consumers' online purchase intentions (e.g., Dimoka et al., 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2010 Pavlou & Dimoka, 2008; Pavlou et al., 2007). However, this study revealed that uncertainty might induce biased evaluation of online sellers and products, which in turn can predict consumers' purchasing behaviour. Thus, this stud reveals that a prejudice perspective is an insightful theoretical lens for reinterpreting the role of online uncertainty.

Finally, the mediating effects of prejudice are contingent upon product type (experience products vs. searc products). Specifically, the mediating effects of seller and product prejudice are stronger in relation to experience products than to search products. While prior studies on prejudice have mainly focused on its significance regarding individuals' decision-making (e.g., Nelson, 2015), our findings show that the significance of prejudice in the context of online markets needs to be re-examined by including various critical contingencies

## 4.2 | Theoretical implications

Resonating to the call for contextualized information systems research in China (Davison & Martinsons, 2016; L et al., 2014), this study particularly focuses on the critical phenomenon of consumers' channel selection in China. In essence, China provides us with a quite appropriate context to interpret the focal phenomenon from a new perspective of prejudice because of its e-commerce status quo (i.e., fierce competition between online and offline channels and its relevant cultural cues (i.e., low uncertainty avoidance and high cynicism). Upon scrutinizing the particular con textual and cultural characteristics of China and empirically validating the research model (Hong et al., 2014), severa interesting findings have been obtained. It is believed that this study makes several important contributions to the lit erature. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to explore the important concept of online prejudice. Drawing on CBT, we theoretically conceptualize online prejudice and identify its two critical components, that is, seller prejudice and product prejudice. More importantly, we empirically verify the existence of online prejudice in China. Considering that prejudice is often pervasive among human beings (Nelson, 2015), online prejudice should also exist in other countries, implying that consumers' evaluation of online markets is likely to be biased. Thus we believe that confirming the existence of bias will motivate more scholars to investigate the antecedents and out comes of online prejudice, or to re-examine previous findings by considering the impacts of online prejudice. The results of our study can provide them with a solid theoretical foundation.

Second, this study can extend our understanding of CBT by expanding it to the investigation of online prejudice First, the existence of online prejudice indicates that CBT can be used to explain online phenomena such as channe selection, which extends the theoretical boundaries of CBT. Second, the contingent effects of product type furthe enrich our understanding of CBT. Although online prejudice is pervasive, its significance is not static, but rather is contextual. The findings of this study should encourage scholars to search for other contingent factors related to CBT, thereby further increasing our understanding of CBT.

Third, the results of this study open the ‘black box’ of the effect of uncertainty by highlighting the mediating effect of online prejudice based on CBT. Previous studies have mainly highlighted the direct effect of online uncer tainty on consumers' online purchase intentions based on cost–benefit analyses (e.g., Dimoka et al., 2012; Hong & Pavlou, 2010; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2008; Pavlou et al., 2007). In contrast, we examine the effect of uncertainty via a simpler or more automatic mechanism (Nelson, 2015), that is, online prejudice. While we agree that cost–benefit ana lyses are critical for understanding uncertainty and consequent behaviour, we believe that online prejudice can also serve as an insightful theoretical lens to increase our understanding of uncertainty and online purchase behaviour.

## 4.3 | Practical implications

The results of our study also have several practical implications. First, our confirmation of the existence of onlin prejudice is a significant finding that can potentially contribute to the further development of e-commerce. Over th past few decades, we have witnessed the rapid growth of e-commerce throughout the world in general, and in Chin in particular. In practice, there are both enablers (e.g., good IT infrastructure, better logistics and supportive govern ment policies) and inhibitors (e.g., online prejudice, as we have confirmed) in relation to the development of e-com merce. The rapid growth we have seen is derived from the fact that the effects of the enablers are much stronge than the effects of the inhibitors. Thus, it is argued that mitigating the negative effects of the inhibitors will contrib ute to the further development of e-commerce. To this end, the results of this study draw e-commerce firms' atten tion to one critical inhibitor, that is, online prejudice is hindering the potential development of their onlin businesses.

It is argued that understanding online prejudice is particularly significant in relation to businesses attempting to engage in O2O e-commerce. Because O2O businesses make the focal firms more involved in the competition with those offline competitors and often demand more investments that are difficult to be balanced until enough con sumers do not hold prejudice toward, and accordingly transact with, these focal firms. Thus, understanding how to deal with the issue of consumers' prejudice against online transactions will enhance the growth of O2O e-commerce This would be particularly meaningful for those Chinese O2O e-commerce firms that are suffering from consumer distrust and suspicion.

Second, several managerial actions can be taken to reduce uncertainty in various e-commerce contexts to mitigate the effects of online prejudice. For instance, managers of online markets can include more insuranc mechanisms (e.g., third-party authorities) to reduce potential transaction risk and enhance sellers' reputations thereby increasing consumers' level of trust (Pavlou et al., 2007). Further, new technologies such as virtual realit and blockchain could be utilized to increase information transparency and mitigate the effects of onlin uncertainty.

Third, considering that online prejudice is more significant in relation to experience goods, we suggest that online sellers of experience goods need to be particularly aware of the negative effects of online prejudice. The need to take appropriate measures to further reduce uncertainty, such as providing consumers with an offline expe rience store and introducing a free-trial-before-purchase marketing strategy.

## 5 | LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

There are some limitations to this study that offer possible directions for future research. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to empirically investigate online prejudice. Although we attempted to investigate the mechanism underlying online prejudice, much remains to be investigated if we are to increase our understanding of the mechanism. For instance, we considered the roles played by uncertainty avoidance and a cynical national cul ture. However, we did not directly test the effects of these factors on prejudice and channel selection. Thus, it woul be useful in the future to conduct a cross-cultural study to empirically verify the effects of relevant cultural anteced ents. In addition, we only focused on one-dependent variable, that is, channel selection. Future research shoul explore the effects of online prejudice on other critical outcomes (e.g., the performance of particular shopping channels).

Second, the generalizability of this study can be further improved. As discussed, the online market in China is unique. We accordingly focused on the contextualization in terms of ‘incorporating the context in describing, understanding, and theorizing about phenomena within it’ (Tsui, 2006, p. 2) and developed a contextualized theory to explain the online prejudice in China (Li et al., 2014; Tsui, 2006). However, the constructs involved are universal, and thus we believe that consumer prejudice against online channels exists in other contexts. More importantly, th effects of online prejudice on relevant outcomes may vary among different cultures (Martinsons, 2016). Thus, it is important to repeat our study in other contexts to further investigate the impact of online prejudice on other outcomes in different cultural settings (Hong et al., 2014). We believe that cross-cultural comparisons can enhance ou understanding of the critical phenomenon of online prejudice.

Third, although the results of our scenario-based survey provide some evidence of the generalizability of the findings of our study, the products we used were physical goods that are equally accessible through both online and offline channels. Therefore, the results may not be applicable to products that are more accessible through a particu lar channel, such as digital music and movies. In addition, we included an explorative study to confirm the existenc of online prejudice. Future studies should develop a more rigorous experimental design to further explore the spe cific psychological processes leading to the formation of online prejudice.
