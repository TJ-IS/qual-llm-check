## Abstract

This article responds to increasing discourses on digital social innovation (DSI) from the perspectives of women entrepreneurs. Using the individual differences theory of gender and information technology (IDTGIT), this research explores how digital technology is used by women entrepreneurs to create opportunities in response to the challenges associated with individual identity, individual influences, social influences and structural influences. We also extend the IDTGIT by exploring how technology is used by women entrepreneurs in their DSI ventures and how technology facilitates the social impact of such ventures. This paper draws on a qualitative study using interviews with 17 women entrepreneurs in Australia, and our findings indicate that individual identity, individual influences and social and structural influences play a significant role in inhibiting women entrepreneurs' business ventures but technology helps to create opportunities for women entrepreneurs to address these factors. We also found that technology plays a role in helping women entrepreneurs to pursue social innovation in two different ways: through social innovation that is embodied by technology and social innovation that is enabled by technology. Our findings further indicate the social impact of DSI in the areas of education, employment, environment and climate, community development and progress and healthcare. The theoretical and practical implications of DSI for women entrepreneurs are provided.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Social innovation, innovative activities and services that are motivated by the goal of meeting a social need (Phillips, Lee, Ghobadian, O'Regan, & James, 2015, p. 430), is an emerging research area that has attracted a growing amount of scholarly attention in recent years (van der Have & Rubalcaba, 2016). One way to under stand social innovation is through linking it to entrepreneurship (Bacq & Eddleston, 2018; Bhatt, Qureshi, & Riaz, 2019; El-Haddadeh, Irani, Millard, & Schröder, 2014; Jaeger-Erben, Rückert-John, & Schäfer, 2015). Indeed, there is an increasing number of entrepreneurs who engage in social innovation as they “find innovative solutions to [the] social problems of his/her community that are not adequately met by the local system” (Bacq & Janssen, 2011, p. 382).

However, research efforts combining social innovation and entrepreneurship are particularly limited in the information systems (IS) field, mainly because studies that examine the nexus of Information Technology (IT), entrepre neurship and social innovation in the IS field are not extensive (Charalabidis, Loukis, & Androutsopoulou, 2014; Fang, Henfridsson, & Jarvenpaa, 2018; Steininger, 2019). We consider the IS field as a social system with a variety of IT (or technologies) such as computers, software, mobile applications (Bulchand-Gidumal & Melián-González, 2011) embedded within it. Charalabidis et al. (2014), for example, noted that only a few studies have explained the role an impact of technologies on social innovation. Fang et al. (2018) further argued that we are still in the early stages of understanding the impact of digital technologies in entrepreneurship. This unveils a significant avenue for research to explore the use of digital technology by entrepreneurs in facilitating and understanding social innovation and the impact of such action.

In this study, we thus take the approach of combining the three areas of interest—IT, entrepreneurship and social innovation. To explore these areas of interest, our focus is on examining digital social innovation (DSI) by entrepreneurs (Tracey & Stott, 2017). We define DSI as the use of digital technologies to create, implement and pro vide novel ideas, products, services or models to address social issues (Edwards-Schachter & Wallace, 2017). In exploring the concept of DSI, we further extend the work of Lindberg, Forsberg, and Karlberg (2016) by considering digital entrepreneurship and social innovation through a gendered lens. In essence, our knowledge is still limite when it comes to women's underrepresentation in entrepreneurship (Jennings & Brush, 2013; McAdam, Harrison, & Leitch, 2019) and social innovation (Lindberg, Forsberg, & Karlberg, 2015; Maestripieri, 2017), resulting in a major gap on this topic in the IS literature. Lindberg et al. (2016), for instance, noted, “[few] published studies address gendered aspects of social innovation… Only five publications can be identified via the academic search engine Scopu where gendered aspects of social innovation are highlighted” (p. 412). Recognising this, we answer recent calls to better understand the enabling effect of digital technologies for women entrepreneurs, particularly those who are engaged in social innovation (Crittenden, Crittenden, & Ajjan, 2019; Warnecke, 2017).

The topic of DSI is particularly critical for women entrepreneurs given that there are arguably invisible factors that inhibit their entrepreneurial ventures. In this study, we draw upon the individual differences theory of gender and IT (IDTGIT) (Trauth, 2002; Trauth, Quesenberry, & Huang, 2009) to examine the experiences of women entrepreneurs who use technologies in their social innovation ventures. The IDTGIT examines both individual and societa factors that affect women's participation in the IT field (Trauth, 2017). Although the IDTGIT has been applied to examine women professionals' employment and retention in the IT field (Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018; Trauth et al., 2009), the application of the theory is relevant for addressing the study of women entrepreneurs, particularly those who use technology in pursuing social entrepreneurship and social innovation. This is because the theory high lights the issues and barriers that women professionals face—either as IT professionals or entrepreneurs involved in digital technology. Given the limitations of existing entrepreneurship research that is often gendered and the fact that gender is largely neglected and under-theorised in the IS literature (Adam, Howcroft, & Richardson, 2004 p. 223), exploring the various influences that create barriers and opportunities for women entrepreneurship in DSI i important to address the challenges and better promote social inclusion for women entrepreneurs in the IS field. A noted by Trauth, Joshi, and Yarger (2018), we “have a moral obligation to proactively remedy the biases … before they become an instrument of injustice (p. 990)

Our study is essentially driven by three research questions: (1) How have women entrepreneurs used technolog to address the individual, social, and structural factors? (2) How is technology used by women entrepreneurs in their DSI? and (3) How has technology affected the social impact of women entrepreneurs' DSI? In addressing thes research questions, we make contributions to scholarly research in entrepreneurship, innovation and IS in severa ways. First, we leverage the fact that gender issues are still under-explored in the context of digital entrepreneur ship (Dy, Marlow, & Martin, 2017) and social innovation (Lindberg et al., 2015; Lindberg et al., 2016). Our focus on women entrepreneurs' DSI is important as research on social innovation and social entrepreneurship is not simplis tic; it involves gendered identities, gendered experiences and socially situated practises (Cajaiba-Santana, 2014; Datta & Gailey, 2012).

Second, we extend the IDTGIT and the application of this theory by specifically highlighting how women entrepreneurs use technology in their DSI to address the challenges imposed by the individual, social and struc tural influences. The IDTGIT specifically highlights the challenges women face in progressing in their chosen fields, but the use of digital technologies can have an impact on enabling opportunities for women in thei careers and business ventures. It is no longer simply about, for example, women's individual identities that pre sent barriers to progress, but it is now about exploring the different ways women entrepreneurs can use digita technology to overcome the barriers presented by individual identity, individual influences, social influences and structural influences

Third, we also extend the IDTGIT through not only highlighting the opportunities and challenges that women entrepreneurs face through the lens of the IDTGIT but also in exploring how digital technology can be used to creat social impact. As Ramani, SadreGhazi, and Gupta (2017) noted, the impact of innovation needs to be examined not only just in terms of economic outcomes, but also its social consequences. We argue that technology is an enabling factor in facilitating social innovation by creating different types of social impact

The context of our study is Australia; the country is noted as second in the world after the United States a the best place for female entrepreneurship (GEDI, 2015). We conducted a qualitative study by interviewing 17 women entrepreneurs in Australia. Australia's female participation in entrepreneurship is ranked fourth among the developed economies in terms of Total Early-stage Entrepreneurial Activity (Steffens & Omarova, 2019), wit about 41% of early-stage entrepreneurs being female. Despite this, empirical studies on women's entrepreneur ship have not been extensively conducted in this region. Recognising the significance of women's entrepreneur ship for the country's economic growth, we examine women entrepreneurs' DSI in Australia, prompted b Australia's growing digital economy (Deloitte Access Economics, 2019) and the pressing need for a social innova tion ecosystem (Mason, 2017).

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In the following section, we highlight the concept of DSI in the context of women's entrepreneurship. Following this, we elaborate on the IDTGIT and the application of thi theory in our context. Our methods and findings are then elaborated. This is followed by a discussion and the theo retical and practical implications of our study before we conclude with the study's limitations and the directions fo future research.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Women's entrepreneurship and DSI

Existing studies on social innovation highlight the “social” outcomes encompassing aspects such as the empowerment of individuals and communities, the improvement of quality of life including justice and poverty eradication the promotion of social inclusion and progress for social impact (Edwards-Schachter & Wallace, 2017). Studies exam ining social innovation have also considered this from the angle of the creation of social practises and social chang (Mulgan, Tucker, Ali, & Sanders, 2007; Mumford, 2002). Social innovation is essentially undertaken as a response to meet social challenges (Cajaiba-Santana, 2014; Turker & Vural, 2017).

Recent years have witnessed a growing interest in examining social innovation in the context of entrepreneurship. The majority of work on social innovation is primarily focused on the implementation/impact of social innova tion at the national/international level (eg, Charalabidis et al., 2014; Rao-Nicholson, Vorley, & Khan, 2017), examining the antecedents including organisational routines/processes to facilitate social innovation (eg, Hsu, Liu Tsou, & Chen, 2019; McLoughlin, McNicoll, Beecher Kelk, Cornford, & Hutchinson, 2019). For instance, the role of innovative entrepreneurial ventures to address social issues has been highlighted (Zahra & Wright, 2016). Sandeep and Ravishankar (2015) noted how social innovation can lead to jobs being outsourced to marginalised individuals in villages and small towns in India. Cui, Pan, Newell, and Cui (2017) considered the strategies and resource orchestra tion used by social entrepreneurs to facilitate social innovation in rural China, such as by developing online shops in the village or showing villagers how to sell their items online. Ramani et al. (2017) highlighted the role of social entre preneurs in the distribution of toilets as an innovation in the Indian sanitation sector.

In reviewing the literature, we found that there is still a limited number of studies focusing on the role of digita technology in the context of social innovation (Tracey & Stott, 2017), social entrepreneurship (Bacq & Janssen, 2011) and women's entrepreneurship (Dy et al., 2017). We found overlapping research opportunities exam ining women's entrepreneurship and social entrepreneurship (eg, Bernardino, Freitas Santos, & Cadima Ribeiro, 2018; Datta & Gailey, 2012; Lortie, Castrogiovanni, & Cox, 2017). For instance, Bernardino et al. (2018) examined the personality traits of women entrepreneurs and found that the agreeableness trait was a factor in the creation of new social entrepreneurial ventures. There is also scholarly work examining women's entrepreneurshi and social innovation (Lindberg et al., 2016). Additionally, there are studies examining women's entrepreneurship and the use of digital technologies (eg, Crittenden et al., 2019; Pergelova, Manolova, Simeonova-Ganeva, & Yordanova, 2019), although they have not been as extensive.

Figure 1 illustrates the overlapping areas of this scholarly work, with our focus of study being the intersection of the four constructs. This intersection highlights women entrepreneurs who use digital technologies in their pursuit of social innovation. The lack of studies focusing on the use of technology in social innovation and social entrepre neurship for women entrepreneurs and the limited theory underpinning gender and technology in the IS literature (Adam et al., 2004) are significant prompts for an important area of research that is much needed as a contribution to theoretical debate and practise.

## 2.2 | The IDTGIT

In this study, we apply the unifying framework of the IDTGIT as a theoretical underpinning for examining how women entrepreneurs use digital technology in their social innovation and how technology creates social impact The IDTGIT is used to explain nuanced similarities and differences in the experiences and responses of women in IT (Quesenberry & Trauth, 2012). With the number of women working in the IT industry continuing to declin (Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018), the theory is applied to examine the social inclusion/exclusion of women in the field. The IDTGIT bridges feminist and IS/IT gender literature to remove barriers that prevent equal opportunity and access fo women in fields that are primarily viewed as male domains (Trauth et al., 2009). Given that research needs to pro gress further to include the issues and barriers that women face and their social acceptance and support (Trauth, 2017), an exploration of the IDTGIT beyond women in IT is relevant. In our study, we not only broaden th application of the IDTGIT in the context of women's entrepreneurship, but also contribute to expand the IDTGIT through examining how digital technology influences the factors highlighted in the IDTGIT

The IDTGIT conceptualises the gender effect—women's participation—in the IT field based on individual varia tions, including intra-gender variation, that result from a combination of individual identity differences and individua and environmental influences (Trauth et al., 2009; Trauth, Quesenberry, & Morgan, 2004). The individual identit construct consists of the differences in individual factors, such as demographics and career choices, that make up one's identity (Trauth, 2017). Individual influences are concerned with the availability or the lack of mentors/rol models, as well as women's personal characteristics, such as education, personality and technical aptitude. Both indi vidual identity and individual influences highlight women's varied backgrounds and influences that could determin their underrepresentation in a particular field.

The third construct, environmental influences, is related to social/societal norms, attitudes and behaviours, as well as the structural factors that may influence one's identity (Trauth, 2017). The social norms reflect cultural influ ences, such as societal attitudes about women and/or women working in the IS/IT profession. The structural factor examine economic aspects, such as the availability of employment in the IS field, policy influences, for example th laws about gender discrimination, and infrastructure influences, for instance in relation to the availability of childcar services. Aligned with previous approaches to ensure clarity, we separate the environmental influences into socia influences and structural influences (Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018; Clayton, Beekhuyzen, & Nielsen, 2012). All of the con structs, when considered together, can explain within-gender variation in women's participation and progress in th IS field (Trauth, Cain, Joshi, Kvasny, & Booth, 2016).

Several studies have used the IDTGIT to illustrate the differences in women's actions and participation in the IS IT field. For example, Adya (2008) compared the career experiences and perceptions of South Asian and America women IT professionals by looking at their relevant social, cultural and individual factors. The study highlighted that career genderisation did not occur as much in South Asian workplaces, but American women IT professionals noted that they experienced discrimination and stereotyping. Trauth, Quesenberry, and Huang (2008), when analysing female IT professionals, found that economic and cultural factors influenced women's participation in the IT field They noted that various socio-cultural factors and cultural attitudes, including maternity, childcare, gendered caree norms and social class, influenced women's career choices in IT. Similarly, Annabi and Lebovitz (2018) considered th barriers present in IT workplaces, such as isolation, legitimacy/discrimination and IT work-life conflict, that can affect individual women's experiences.

All these studies illustrate that individual identity, individual influences and environmental factors influence women's social inclusion in the IS/IT field (Trauth, 2017). These studies contributed to our understanding of women's experiences and the barriers they face in the IS/IT industry but these factors have not been examined beyond this context. There is a need to explore detailed accounts of the nature of the barriers and opportunities for women's entrepreneurship as reflected in the IDTGIT, beyond simply considering the application of the theory in the context of women professionals in a male-dominated IS/IT industry only. Exploring the context of women's entrepreneurship is timely and important—on the one hand, there is an increase in entrepreneurial intentions and activities by wome (Dawson & Henley, 2015); but on the other hand, the gender gap still persists, including inequity and inequality of women's entrepreneurship in terms of the overall participation rates, growth expectations, and the extent of women-led technology-related businesses in comparison to ventures started by male founders (Global Entrepreneur ship Monitor, 2019). Studies have indicated that women are less likely than men to be entrepreneurs, and this trend is even more obvious in the high-technology sector (Marlow & McAdam, 2012; Ozkazanc-Pan & Clark Muntean, 2018). Studies have also indicated that women entrepreneurs are more likely to have a natural inclination to start ventures with social goals (Huysentruyt, 2014; Lortie et al., 2017), yet the focus on women's entrepreneurship in social innovation, particularly DSI, is little studied—with a few exceptions (Ford & Cooper, 2016; Uygur, Kahraman, & Günay, 2016)—which offers promise for future work.

We extend the IDTGIT in this study through highlighting the role of digital technologies in facilitating socia innovation (Fang et al., 2018; Rahman, Taghizadeh, Ramayah, & Alam, 2017; Ryu & Kim, 2018). Recent social innova tion research calls for connecting agentic actions with social structures, proposing that “an agent is at the same time constrained and enabled by structures (or institutions) in order to (re)create social systems (Cajaiba-Santana, 2014 p.47). Following this line of argument, women entrepreneurs' DSI activities are arguably constrained and enabled b the different individual variations and environmental factors as illustrated in the IDTGIT. The application of the IDTGIT is relevant in our context given that the IDTGIT is focused on the sources of within-gender variation. W can thus draw on this theory to illustrate within-gender variations in terms of how individual identity, the individua influences, and the environmental influences (social and structural) play a role in women-led ventures. The applica tion of the IDTGIT is essentially to answer the call for research to incorporate broader theoretical perspectives to examine the factors contributing to women entrepreneurs' challenges in business (Wheadon & Duval-Couetil, 2019)

## 3 | METHOD

We adopted a qualitative method in the interpretivist paradigm to gain an in-depth understanding of the perspectives of women entrepreneurs and their interpretations of the factors influencing their pursuit of DSI and their social impact. Given our research questions, a qualitative research design is particularly appropriate to our study as it is underpinned by an assumption that “access to reality (given or socially constructed) is only through social construc tions such as language, consciousness and shared meanings (Ravishankar, Pan, & Myers, 2013, p. 392). Interpretiv research attempts to understand the phenomenon from the perspectives of those who live it, with its focus on th subjective experiences and beliefs of the individuals involved (Trauth, 2001; Walsham, 2006). When applied to analyse gender issues, an interpretivist paradigm not only enables more nuanced understanding of gender relations but also challenges existing assumptions about these relations (Trauth, 2013; Trauth & Howcroft, 2006)

Although an interpretivist approach to gender research is increasingly common in the IS field (Adam et al., 2004; Walsham, 2006), such an approach has been neglected in entrepreneurship research (Packard, 2017), and yet it is powerful in enabling a more nuanced understanding of the lived experiences of women entrepreneurs (eg, Dy et al., 2017; Khan & Rowlands, 2018; Tlaiss, 2015). For example, Dy et al. (2017) noted that when examining th lived experiences of digital entrepreneurs, a qualitative, interpretivist approach is axiomatic” (p. 291). In applying and extending the IDTGIT, we further follow the principles for conducting interpretive studies outlined by Klein and Myers (1999). In our case, we sought to explicate and subjectively interpret the socially constructed personal storie and experiences of women entrepreneurs in relation to the individual and environmental factors that influence them not only in their ventures, but also in their creation of social impact through their social innovation.

## 3.1 | Data collection

In this study, we conducted in-depth semi-structured interviews with 17 women entrepreneurs in Perth, Wester Australia (WA), as part of a larger research project. As both researchers were previously based in Perth, the choice of location allowed for a strategic and convenience sampling method, a similar approach adopted in other studies (Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018; Armstrong, Riemenschneider, & Giddens, 2018; Trauth et al., 2008). Additionally, althoug WA has traditionally been dependent on the mining and resources sector, it has become a vibrant emerging entre preneurial hub. There are many networking events, meetings and associations for female entrepreneurs in WA, wit a lot of exciting development and progress in the innovation and entrepreneurship space for women entrepreneurs By focusing on women entrepreneurs in WA, we could better understand their socially constructed experiences in state that is pushing for innovation and where women are increasingly becoming role models and empowering themselves to be entrepreneurs involved in DSI

We used theoretical sampling to identify interview respondents from women entrepreneurs' networks in WA. Respondents can offer rich information about issues related to the research purpose (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007) and were selected based on the following criteria: (1) they are female founders or co-founders (2) they use digital technologies in their businesses and (3) their business creates social impact (such as through con tributing to communities or involving marginalised communities). We made efforts to maximise sample diversity to not only mitigate data collection bias, but also ensure the breadth and depth of our understanding of the phenome non, thereby producing more valid findings (Bouncken, Laudien, Fredrich, & Görmar, 2018; Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). As noted by Dy et al. (2017), “broad heterogeneity exists among digital businesses” (p.288)—when explaining the utilisation of technology, Dy et al. (2017) noted that there are digital entrepreneurs who rely heavil on technology but there are others who simply focus on online marketing and communication. Our sample wa mostly comprised of women entrepreneurs involved in technology start-ups in different areas, including virtual real ity (VR), education and training, cybersecurity, healthcare, disability care, construction and engineering

All of the 17 respondents were categorised as social entrepreneurs given that they exploit business opportuni ties by innovative means to satisfy social needs. These women entrepreneurs all explicitly demonstrated pro-socia motivation—a central characteristic of social entrepreneurs (Saebi, Foss, & Linder, 2019), in their companies' vision mission statements as well as in their business conduct and daily social conversations. Not only do these businesse address unmet social needs, but some of them distribute part or all their profits in the pursuit of social goals

Among the 17 interview respondents, two respondents were recruited at a business event celebrating Women Entrepreneurship Week and one was the researchers' acquaintance. We also successfully invited five respondent through cold emailing; the response rate of cold emailing was about 50%. We then used snowball sampling, where the other nine respondents were referred to us by prior respondents; we checked their business profiles to ensur that our three sampling criteria were met before contacting them.

Table A1 shows the profiles of the respondents. Their ages range from mid-20s to late 50s, with the majority of them (13) being under the age of 45. This is consistent with the typical profile of entrepreneurs in the technology sector being younger (Fang et al., 2018). With regard to the size of their businesses, 10 of them are small business owners employing between one and 19 people, 6 operate as sole proprietors, and one business has more tha 20 employees. The entrepreneurial experience of the respondents varies from 1-5 years, to more than 15 years, with only one entrepreneur having less than 1 year of entrepreneurial experience. Almost all of them (16) were in full-tim corporate employment before setting up their own business

Our semi-structured interviews consisted of open-ended questions guided by a formal interview protoco (Franklin & Ballan, 2001; Gibbert, Ruigrok, & Wicki, 2008). The use of semi-structured interviews allows for the flexibility needed, while a formal protocol ensures the consistency of questions across all interviews (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Spieth, Schneider, Clauß, & Eichenberg, 2019). Our interview protocol was comprised of three main parts. Part 1 focused on an overview of the participating women entrepreneurs' businesses and their motivation (eg, What motivated you to start your own business?), part 2 centred on the use of technology in their businesses (eg How have technological changes influenced your business decisions?) and part 3 focused on the social impacts of thei businesses (eg, What are you achieving or aiming to achieve?). We encouraged respondents' free expression bu followed up with additional questions to clarify or elaborate on any relevant aspects in more detail

We also conducted archival research about the respondents, studying their LinkedIn profiles and their compa nies' websites prior to the interviews, so that we were able to raise questions that were relevant to their specifi business context. Additionally, we used online news or articles written about or by the respondents, to familiaris ourselves with our interview subjects. The interviews with 16 respondents were conducted in person and one inter view was conducted via telephone. The average interview time was about 1 hour. All interviews were audio recorded with the respondents' permission and transcribed verbatim.

## 3.2 | Data analysis

The interview data were thematically analysed using the NVivo version 11 software. The software allows efficient and effective organisation of the data, which enabled us to clearly analyse the code structure (Atherton & Elsmore, 2007). The analysis followed Boyatzis' (1998) guidelines for the development of thematic codes and Brau and Clarke's (2006) thematic analysis procedures. After familiarising ourselves with the data by reading all the interview transcripts in detail, we generated initial codes across the entire data set (first-order concepts). We then col lated the initial codes and coded these into potential themes that were theoretically related to the first-order concepts.

Specifically, the first-order concepts were derived through the analysis of informant-centric terms and code (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013). That is, we coded phrases or sentences in the transcripts that were related to simi lar topics or meanings to develop first-order concepts. For example, the educational, occupational and technologica backgrounds and previous entrepreneurial and/or managerial experiences were grouped and labelled as human cap ital.” The desire to help others, the motive to make positive change, the trait of empathy, a strong sense of justice and/or community and willingness to compromise on self-interest were grouped and labelled as “prosocial motiva tion.” During the coding process, we tried to adopt the interviewees' language to label concepts. This inductive cod ing process eventually generated a long list of first-order concepts, as shown in the first column in Figure 2

The analysis of the second-order themes was based on the use of researcher-centric concepts and themes, that is, the coding was guided by a relevant theory or theories (Gioia et al., 2013). Guided by the IDTGIT, we coded a large part of the first-order concepts into the four constructs: individual identity, individual influences, social factors and structural factors. As an example, “human capital” and “prosocial motivation” as first-order constructs wer coded under the second-order theme of individual identity because both human capital and prosocial motivation represented women entrepreneurs' capabilities and motivations to pursue DSI.

Lastly, we analysed the second-order themes and sorted them into aggregate dimensions (ie, higher orde themes) based on the conceptual relationships between the themes. For instance, the four IDTGIT constructs were grouped into one aggregate dimension of “factors influencing women's entrepreneurship.” All the themes that wer related to the different ways digital technology is being utilised for social innovation were categorised under the aggregate dimension of “how technology is used in DSI”. The themes about various domains of social impact wer aggregated into the higher order theme “social impact.”

Using the format suggested by Gioia et al. (2013), we summarised our analysis and the coding results in Figure 2. This coding method has been used in a number of studies and has shown a high level of reliability (eg, Dy et al., 2017; Pecis, 2016; Spieth et al., 2019; Vough, Bindl, & Parker, 2017). The coding procedure highlights a sys tematic presentation of both first-order and second-order analysis, and together, the “tandem reporting of both voices – informant and researcher – allowed not only a qualitatively rigorous demonstration of the links between the data … but also allowed for the kind of insight that is the defining hallmark of high-quality qualitative research” (Gioia et al., 2013, p.18).

## 4 | FINDINGS

Our findings indicate the factors that influenced women entrepreneurs' DSI endeavours, and how technology can b used as a source of opportunities for women entrepreneurs to overcome these challenges. Our findings further indi cate how technology is used by women entrepreneurs in their DSI and finally, how technology affects the socia impact of women entrepreneurs' DSI.

## 4.1 | Factors influencing women's entrepreneurship

Our data analysis indicated the different influences of women's entrepreneurship. We categorised these influence into four groups that are reflective of the IDTGIT: (1) individual identity, (2) individual influences, (3) social influence and (4) structural influences.

## 4.1.1 | Individual identity

The major factor indicated by all our interviewees in their pursuit of social innovation is their prosocial motivations All of them commented that they are driven to address particular social issues by means of their entrepreneuria endeavours, even though their businesses can be for-profit organisations. For example, some women founders con tribute part of their business profits to social causes (No.04, No.14), while others founded non-profit (No.06) or for profit social enterprises (No.08, No.09, No.15). The desire to influence social change enabled them to identify entrepreneurial opportunities to fill unmet social needs, either as a result of market failure or due to institutional void, a highlighted by the following quote:

I definitely started my business in response to the challenges I had returning to work with both children … I experienced a lot of challenges in terms of negotiating flexible work, negotiating things like school hours and that kind of thing. I realised if I was experiencing those challenges, there must be challenges for a lot of other women who don't necessarily speak up about it … [I want] … to be sort of a social change maker … seeing what the gaps are socially and how to change it and leading those dialogues. So my business is more of a social enterprise. (No. 08)

As many of them are driven to influence social change, new opportunities can be further identified and exploited one of which is to use technologies to balance the focus of making money and solving societal problems. For exam ple, respondent No.03, with her quote illustrated below, provided a digital learning platform in cybersecurity and innovated in her product/service delivery to meet the demands of individuals, organisations and communities Respondent No.06 was innovative in the way she provided online and offline consultancy services to encourage women's participation in technology, science and innovation. The illustrations of their quotes are provided below:

Technology and commerce have collided and made data much more valuable compared to other objects or tangible physical objects … I needed to look into a scalable business, and technology could provide that, provided you have an innovation. (No.03)

My catchphrase is that I believe technology takes gender out of the equation in many ways because technology has enabled us to come up with new business models, new systems, new opportunities that are no longer dependent on physical attributes. So, I do think technology has given us a unique opportunity to participate to a greater extent in the entrepreneurial space. That allows women to a lot more opportunities. (No.06)

Our findings also show that human capital is an important individual characteristic of women entrepreneurs that can impact their DSI. It is evident that our respondents have a high education level, with all having tertiary qualifications and more than half of them having completed a postgraduate degree. Furthermore, 16 of 17 respondents have employment history as either professionals or senior managers, and one (No.11) had her own professional practis prior to starting her technology venture. Interestingly, although most of our respondents have relevant industry and occupational backgrounds, only four of them have a technological background (No.03, No.04, No.13, No.16). This lack of technology-specific human capital might disadvantage women entrepreneurs. For example, they may need to rely on their co-founders, who are sometimes younger and often male, to gain legitimacy for their entrepreneuria plans and social objectives:

Back then I was pitching this new technology which some people were sceptical of. I was also pitching a need to progress diversity and inclusion and pitching the problem to a lot of white middleaged men … It was very, very challenging, and I got some feedback that I don't think was delivered very nicely … It was in front of a group of about 20 other founders. I felt attacked. I was trying very hard not to take it personally and emotionally. But there were a couple of instances like that where I felt if I had been male or if I even had a male co-founder standing next to me, it wouldn't have been the same. (No.02)

I've noticed that when it comes to talking about the product or the technology, they'll always talk straight to David and I'm almost there as the side person. Though he does know a lot more about the technology than I do because I'm more business-focused, the consequence of that is that when they start talking about strategy or finances or anything that's business-related, they continue talking to David as opposed to talking to me … They just assume that David must be all over those as well. (No.10)

Despite the (perceived) lack of technology-specific human capital, our findings suggest that there are several women entrepreneurs who were proactive in complementing their lack of technology-specific human capital with external resources or quickly building their capability in technology, as illustrated by the followin quotes:

My website's a great example. I just outsourced everything to this wonderful person who does a great job with WordPress. I don't stress about a thing. I know it'll take her two hours but it will take me ten. Just accepting that I can write content and let her to make it look good [on the website] … it's completely unreasonable to be good at everything in a business. (No.07)

I built the website myself. But I do not write code … And to be fair what we have built is not completely purpose-built for what we want, but it is good for the product and to test the market. YouTube has been my teacher … I think people who are into entrepreneurship do use a lot YouTube because it is like their second school. (No.15)

## 4.1.2 | Individual influences

All of the 17 women respondents noted the importance of their individual influences, such as support from thei family members, mentors and/or business partners in helping them start and sustain their business and DSI. In partic ular, they noted the importance of having role models, particularly if their business is in the high-technology field Interestingly, our interviewees noted the lack of female role models for other women to identify with and emulate While there are always plentiful male entrepreneurs and their technology success stories, similar stories about women entrepreneurs in digital businesses are not that well publicised. Positive role models play an important role as they can counteract existing gender stereotypes and raise the confidence of women that they can also be success ful in male-dominated careers, either in technology or entrepreneurship, or both (Ahuja, 2002; Byrne, Fattoum, & Díaz García, 2019; Trauth, 2017). Below is a quote reflecting our finding:

In start-up land, it's about role models – really having those female role models and seeing a variety of them. It's actually just having someone that you can see that they have actually done it and how they've done it … Currently, there are a lot of start-ups run by men. So there's a lack of diversity for start-ups. There's a lack of championing for set up that is run by diverse cultural backgrounds or different gender or mixes of life experience or anything. (No.11)

The absence of role models, on the other hand, may present opportunities for women entrepreneurs to be inspire to become role models themselves, and to inspire other women to engage in DSI through entrepreneurial ventures Successful role models in technology enable visibility, letting others know that there are strong, innovative women out there that they can identify with. These role models can also help provide access to advice and feedback as wel as network resources. In this sense, the role models address the individual influence factor—they can have a motiva tional effect on others. Our findings indicate that this may facilitate a chain reaction, encouraging other women to consider and build an entrepreneurial career in technology. Given that the proportion of women as entrepreneurs particularly in the technology field, is not as high, female role models indeed lead the change by empowering an inspiring others, as indicated in the illustrative quote below.

I'm involved with X … they do some really good work in showing girls and women what's possible for their future careers, whether it's STEM-based or whether it's innovation-based; they are role modelling what's possible. (No.02)

## 4.1.3 | Social influences

The third factor concerns social influences, such as the social expectations of certain gender roles, particularly in male-dominated industries like technology and construction. A number of respondents noted that gender stereo types are still very common despite the push for gender equality and women's emancipation. Gender stereotypes create barriers for women starting and sustaining their business and make it more difficult for them carrying out social innovation initiatives. Women entrepreneurs are sometimes not taken seriously by other business stake holders, and their businesses are frequently seen as micro- or hobby businesses with little value and prospects. Furthermore, socially innovative business practises were not well supported by commercial investors, including banks, which created additional barriers for women entrepreneurs to overcome. For example, one respondent's (No.08 technology start-up is to promote gender equality in organisations, but she found this socially innovative busines was devalued by investors despite its social value, as illustrated below:

It is a social network, which is around efficacy and information sharing, as well as online consulting and coaching … I presented my business to a group of investors and one of the people there, a male investor asked quite directly why they would take my business seriously … I think for start-ups focused on technology there's still probably an expectation that technology's a male area. (No.08)

Some women entrepreneurs, however, highlighted the opportunities brought by the utilisation of technology. Fo instance, some critically commented that their entrepreneurial ventures are technology-based innovations that ar more than just offering a new app or being a hobby business. Indeed, they viewed that hobby-related entrepreneur ship is not sustainable and scalable, and more women entrepreneurs should be involved in high-technology area such as in health and cybersecurity, where there are opportunities to generate not only economic outcomes but also promote social inclusion and gender equity:

Let's use technology to fix real problems, not like, “I'm going to make a million dollars by inventing an app”. (No.01)

I didn't see any problem being a woman entrepreneur, especially in this field because everywhere you go, you talk about diversity, you talk about the need to have women in this field … Cybersecurity is predominantly a male field, but it is also a huge area … Cybersecurity is not a mature area; it's still at a very infant stage. So, we do need people from various backgrounds [including women] to provide input and way forward, and the best way forward is to have expertise from everyone. (No.03)

Many women entrepreneurs in our study also indicated that they often faced difficulty in obtaining funding given that their industries are still very much male-dominated. The advancement of technology and the digital infrastruc: ture, however, serves as an opportunity for women entrepreneurs' DSI to find alternative funding and not rely on traditional venture capitalists, which are often perceived as being reluctant to invest in women-led businesse (Brush, Greene, Balachandra, & Davis, 2018) and social ventures (Calic & Mosakowski, 2016; Renko, 2013). A few women entrepreneurs, for instance, used crowd-funding and crowd-sourcing platforms as an alternative source of finance for their start-ups. This suggests that conventional funding sources can be disrupted through the utilisation of technology, which can consequently remove the barriers that women entrepreneurs face, giving them as equal chance of success as their male counterparts (Groza, Groza, & Barral, 2020; Mollick & Robb, 2016).

## 4.1.4 | Structural influences

Structural influences reflect the economic and political influences and the institutional stereotypical gender roles in occupations, organisations and societies. In the digital space, the field is male-dominated and, as such, the environment of technology start-ups is not particularly conducive to women entrepreneurs, particularly those with young children or older female founders. This structural factor highlights gendered social networks, where females still fac barriers and challenges in many industries including the technology field. For example, business culture in Australia is known for drinking and doing sports with the “good old boys,” especially in WA, where the state economy is mainl driven by mining and resources. This structural influence is also apparent in co-working spaces where many of th technology start-ups are located:

I have found I don't feel like I fit in many of the co-working spaces and the accelerators. I joined [a coworking space] and when I asked them if I could bring in my second child who was under four weeks old, they were like, “Only if you can make sure they don't make any noise.” It's a baby … There're probably no bathrooms for breastfeeding. I was like, “I'm not breastfeeding my child in the toilet.

That's ridiculous.” I think that's because they've got a lot of young tech blokes. They have some old tech blokes too but not a lot of young women, not a lot of women with families. I just didn't feel that that was very inclusive. (No.07)

However, several of the women entrepreneurs commented that, because of the present underrepresentation of women entrepreneurs in the technology sector, this actually serves as an opportunity to create a sense of urgency for the government to improve gender equality in the sector. They further noted that there is a lot of networking support that includes both local business networks (eg, Women in Technology WA) and online/offline social net works (eg, Women in Social Enterprise [WISE]). The support programmes provided by local organisations (eg, Roya Automobile Club [RAC] start-up accelerator programmes) and the state government (eg, Women in Science, Technol ogy, Engineering and Mathematics [STEM] and Entrepreneurship grants) were also mentioned by several respon dents as being instrumental to their technology start-ups.

## 4.2 | Technology and women entrepreneurs' DSI

All the women entrepreneurs in our sample used digital technologies in their entrepreneurial ventures; however, there are differences in the way they engage in business and social innovation. We categorised two different ways digital technology is used in women entrepreneurs' DSI. First, social innovation is facilitated through ensuring that it is embodied in technology. The DSI products/services for this can be in the forms of digital artefacts, platforms or infrastructures (Nambisan, 2017), such as VR and Financial Technology (FinTech). Second, our findings indicate that social innovation can also be enabled by technology in that the company's existing products/services are substantially enhanced by the addition of digital technology (Lyytinen, Yoo, & Boland Jr, 2016), including social media digitalisation, and third-party digital platforms

Ten of our interviewees' ventures are in the first category of social innovation, being embodied in technology where they provided new technology products/services to address social issues. These can then be categorised into three different broad categories: (1) new products/services, (2) online marketplace and (3) mobile applications (mobile apps). For the product category, one respondent (No.01), for example, was developing and providing an all-in-one Learning Management System (LMS) for schools to help them manage the increasingly challenging learning environment that all teachers, students and parents are grappling with. This is a social innovation to hel school teachers alleviate the burden of an administrative workload, which “was crowding out teaching… so many teachers were suffering mental health issues, a lot of stress, a lot of teachers leaving or becoming really sick … [it was] absolutely diabolical for teachers” (No. 01). A distinctive feature of this LMS is its teacher-centric design to help teachers in their daily work and improve their overall wellbeing to enable them to have sufficient time to focus on teaching with much less unnecessary workload and stress. The other five women entrepreneurs pioneered technology products in digital health (No.11), cybersecurity (No.03), VR technology (No.02), FinTech (No.13) and digital automation (No.10).

For the online marketplace category, one respondent (No.15), for instance, co-founded an online marketplace an independent, non-profit social enterprise, to meet the needs of the disability community by connecting peopl with disabilities to local disability supports and services. Her DSI provides services particularly for stakeholders who are affected by the new National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS), including not only people with disabilities, their families, and disability services providers, but also government agencies involved in the NDIS.

For the mobile applications category, three DSI start-ups provided new apps to meet social needs, includin offering free meals to homeless people (No.04), reducing carbon emissions (No.09) and improving food waste man agement (No.16). Their DSI initiatives provide innovative technology applications not only for profit-seeking, but also to address social issues. The quote below illustrates an example of women entrepreneurs' social innovation that is embodied in technology:

Our business [online food delivery] is still in social entrepreneurship … We started an App initially. Every time you use our app to book restaurants, we donate meal to someone in need. So every time you eat you help someone in need to eat as well … We started it two years ago. As start-ups, you need to find product-market fit. Basically you need to come up with products or services that people will pay for, [so you can] make money out of it. (No.04)

Seven of our interviewees' ventures are in the second category of social innovation, being enabled by technology These are then categorised into three different broad categories: (1) additional digitalised products/services to accompany offline offerings, (2) social media and (3) third-party platforms. Although the market offering of thes ventures were not new digital goods, all of the seven founders ingeniously utilised various digital technologies to achieve their business and social goals. Without technology, these women entrepreneurs noted that their socia innovation initiatives would not have occurred or their progress would have been limited. For the additional dig italised products/services category, two respondents noted their offerings of digital training and consulting prod ucts/services in addition to their “offline” training and consulting (No.05; No.07), as illustrated in the quotes below:

There is an app solution and there is an online solution. it's cloud-hosted so you can play it across multiple devices, computer, tablet, phone, you can deliver it by the classroom, of course, if you wish. (No.05)

I manage my entire business now online. My first business, the one with the online program for schools, I can take 100,000 members, just ready to go. And there're no extra running costs …We've got online courses. It's all online. I deliver the content. The teachers don't have to do any more work because the content is all made. They get a lesson plan and they just have to follow it, and part of it is watching the video. They don't have to know the content which means they don't have to spend extra time. (No.07)

For the social media category, three start-ups relied on social media (eg, online networking platforms and blogs) and other digital media (eg, websites and email newsletters) for their social innovation and entrepreneurial undertaking (No.06; No.08; No.17). For example, respondent No.17 used technology to organise and run online workshops to equip women with financial literacy and skills:

I run webinars. In my last webinar, I had people from like Adelaide and Melbourne and even someone in South Africa … I do have a website. I have a Facebook page, and I also have a Facebook group. And I have newsletters and blogs. I've just got some funding to put some resources on my website. (No.17)

Finally, for the third-party platforms category, two women entrepreneurs (No.12; No.14) noted the use of cloud based software and platforms and enterprise intranets to carry out their business and DSI. Technology is used to enhance the operations of the business and increase productivity. With several of these women entrepreneurs employing females, they noted the importance of technology in enabling women to work from home and that the use of virtual platforms for remote working offers an opportunity to overcome traditional office hours. In fact, on commented that her business was a leader in the field, not only in terms of providing an opportunity for women professionals to work remotely and ensuring agility and flexibility in the operations of the business, but also about breaking down the stereotypes and perceptions:

I run a remote team. I have all the girls working at home. Through our Portal you can stay connected all the time … In terms of general business, you can get ahead of the game by embracing technology.

This is where I think some of the older men in the industry that are in businesses like mine, they wil slowly be moved out of the industry if they don't get on board with technology and automation, they'll lose out and it'll be too late by then. (No.12)

## 4.3 | Social impact of women entrepreneurs' DSI ventures

Our findings have indicated how technology is used by women entrepreneurs in their DSI, as elaborated earlier. Ou data further shed light on how technology affects the social impact of women entrepreneurs' DSI, and we found five different themes of social impact: (1) education, (2) employment, (3) environment and climate, (4) community devel opment and progress, and (5) healthcare. We summarised our findings in Table B1 outlining the social impact of women entrepreneurs' DSI, how technology is used to facilitate social innovation and social impact, and the exam ples of our respondents. Table B1 also highlights how such social impact addresses the factors inhibiting wome entrepreneurs' progress as identified in the IDTGIT.

The first impact is related to education, and this can help to address individual identity factors (enabling individ uals to build their human capital and personalising education that is tailored to their needs), social influences (widen ing access to education) and structural influences (providing support for teachers and parents in the learning process). For example, one respondent (No.01) initiated a digital platform and worked with schools to address issues of technology adoption in education, which is an essential part of national/global educational technology infrastruc ture. Another technology start-up was committed to financial education for schoolchildren and parents (No.07) to ensure the widening of access to financial education and the provision of personalised educational tools for learners.

The second theme of social impact is related to employment, and this addresses both the individual influ ences (providing mentoring and peer support) and structural influences (finding and creating employment equal ity and access). For instance, two respondents noted that their business provided employment for women professionals, particularly those with young children (No.12, No.14). They wanted to create an inclusive business practise that celebrates not only the skills and talent of women, but also addresses the inequality and work-lif balance issues faced by women with families (No.02, No.06, No.08, No.17). Our findings highlight that women entrepreneurs are increasingly incorporating digital technology and cloud capabilities in their businesses to ensure not only flexibility in work options, but also enabling their employees, including female employees employment equality and access to work outside the traditional office environment to not only address individ ual influences but also the structural challenges.

The structure of my company is only employing females. So, I set all my girls up to work from home. They all have home offices so they can always balance family and career … When women take time out to have kids, the game changes on them and then they're 10 years behind men in their career progression … I don't think you should be penalised for wanting to be a mother. (No.12)

The third type of social impact involves tackling environment and climate issues. Three women entrepreneurs ven tured their DSI in this area, to provide clean energy, reduce carbon footprint and improve waste management. This helps break down the social stereotyping and structural influences—first, women entrepreneurs can create a business that is not a hobby business, which can be scalable and can create meaningful societal impact, and second, there may be increased representation of women entrepreneurs around environment and climate issues in the near future. Fo example, one respondent (No.09) founded her technology venture to introduce renewable energy programmes for low carbon schools and communities. Another start-up (No.10) created innovative building materials and construc tion technology to build clean-energy houses. Another respondent (No.16) introduced a new approach to wast management by developing a digital app for arranging the composting of organic waste in a controlled fashion, separating the organic matter from landfill to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. This technology start-up pursues a DSI initiative, as illustrated in the below quote:

The reason we start with food waste is because of the climate change issue and food waste has to be picked up fast … We have this source separation and collection systems and apps, and we supply everything … We want to develop a process that would do 10 to 20 tons a week for a suburb. (No.16)

The fourth theme of social impact involves community development and progress. This addresses the social influences (ensuring social inclusion and equality) and structural influences (fostering public-private partnerships). Fo example, the not-for-profit organisation founded by one respondent (No.06) was a leading social enterprise in promoting diversity and inclusion in the technology sector (addressing social influences). Its DSI also included developin awareness of online and offline networks to influence women's participation and status, and developing partnership with both private and government organisations to provide support to women-led technology start-ups, including acceleration and incubation programmes for socially innovative start-ups that use novel technologies (addressin structural influences).

In the social inclusion and equality domain, several of the women entrepreneurs were inspired to give voice to other women and marginalised groups to eradicate issues of social exclusion, inequality and marginalisation. One respondent (No.13), for example, founded a FinTech venture with the social mission to protect payments to trades people, sub-contractors and small business enterprises, who are marginalised groups in the construction industry in comparison to large building companies, financial institutions and insurance companies. Her DSI incorporates tech nology to address the long-standing “broken” payment process whereby subcontractors are unfairly burdened by delayed or non-payment. Two other women entrepreneurs founded their technology start-ups to help homeles people using mobile apps (No.04) and to advocate for people with disabilities through an independent disability marketplace platform (No.15). Another respondent (No.17) runs online and offline financial literacy workshops for migrant women who previously did not have access to such information due to language or finance considerations or merely lack of awareness. She used multilingual websites and social media for communication and marketing to migrant communities, and negotiated with local councils to pay for her services and offer them free to local migrant communities. The social impact of her DSI was more than the improvement of the financial literacy of migrant women, but also included other benefits in terms of addressing mental health, domestic violence, and employment issues. The DSI of women entrepreneurs in this category was essentially to bring innovative solutions through th use of technology in tackling the problems of social exclusion and marginalisation

Another category that emerged from the data under the theme of community development and progress is public-private partnerships. Seven women entrepreneurs played the role acting as a bridge between the public sec tor/communities and the private sector/market. They sought to fill or leverage institutional void by including sociall relevant groups in their DSI activities. For example, one entrepreneur (No. 09) became a bridge between academic research and industry by introducing new renewable energy programmes to create awareness for low carbon schools and communities. The founder partners with local councils and universities to subsidise local schools and communi ties to use the company's digital tools for carbon control and environmental education.

The fifth theme of social impact involves the area of healthcare in terms of prevention and personalised healt service. This social impact addresses the IDTGIT in relation to the individual identity factors in that technology can be used to overcome women entrepreneurs' challenges such as their personal struggles, conflicting demands, fulfill ing multiple roles and managing family responsibilities. One technology venture, for instance, was promoting digita solutions for tackling mental health and safety issues in organisations (No.05) as illustrated below:

The start-up is a business of keeping work physically and mentally safe using software and data. We have both an app solution and an online solution; it's cloud-hosted. We knew that safety training wasn't being done very well. It failed to miss the mark in doing what it should do to actually keep workers safe … We know that legislation is now supporting a holistic physical and psychological requirement for organisations to provide a safe system of work for their people, so we actually made that. (No.05)

The social impact on healthcare also addresses the IDTGIT in relation to individual influences. Several of the wome entrepreneurs had businesses where they could be role models to guide and support others in relation to managing health and well-being. One start-up (No.11) co-founded a digital health company with the goal to provide bette healthcare services and experiences to all Australian families. In this way, entrepreneurship and DSI can address indi vidual identity and influences, helping entrepreneurs to not only be aware of their vulnerability to mental health chal lenges, but also providing them with guidance and applications to mitigate challenges to their psychological well being:

This is a digital health company focusing on the provision of consumer support capabilities, linking health education and social services. It is for population health as well as to provide solutions to facilitate care collaboration. It also covers education and social as well … I would have expected the jour ney for families to improve, but what I discovered is that 25 years later, it hasn't changed very much at all. And with my co-founder X, we realised that using technology, we could at least solve some of the problems, make that journey for families a whole lot easier, [and]link them to resources relevant to their needs. (No.11)

## 5 | DISCUSSION

Given that research on DSI is relatively recent (Tracey & Stott, 2017), and that studies using a gender lens to examine the nexus between technology, entrepreneurship and social innovation is limited, this study explored the aspect of DSI from the perspectives of women entrepreneurs. While we might expect that women entrepreneurs could over come the glass ceiling constraints when they start their business, our findings indicate that women entrepreneurs, including those in technology, are still facing significant barriers in the entrepreneurial space, from persistent gender bias and gender inequality, to the lack of female role models (Marlow & McAdam, 2012). These barriers have existed for many years and have been examined in the literature (eg, Jennings & Brush, 2013; Wheadon & Duval Couetil, 2019) but empirical research examining how these challenges can be overcome is less common. We foun that the women entrepreneurs who were part of our sample were not only committed to addressing commercial social gaps in the market, but also took steps to highlight and tackle issues through their technological product/ser vice offerings or through technology implementation that creates social impact, including facilitating social inclusion, empowerment and progress (Bria et al., 2015; Trauth et al., 2018). In this way, our findings indicate that technology creates opportunities for women entrepreneurs to achieve social innovation and also enable them to overcome th challenges they still face in modern-day society due to the strong masculine connotations associated with entrepre neurship, IT and innovation (Ahl, 2006; Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018; Harvey, 2003; Trauth et al., 2008).

In relation to the individual identity and influences, while we found that our sample of women entrepreneurs are driven by their prosocial motivation to be entrepreneurs and be involved in social innovation, many admit to not hav ing the relevant technology background or expertise. There are also still perceptions, even within the close famil unit, that women need to do a larger proportion of domestic duties. Such beliefs, attitudes and unconsciou stereotyping and biases create invisible barriers for women entrepreneurs to progress further. Part of the explana tion is perhaps due to the language that men and women use (Smith, Rosenstein, Nikolov, & Chaney, 2019), with women being more likely to voice, or be spoken to about their lack of understanding in a particular area, such a technology. Indeed, research suggests that language is a cultural institution that operates at the subconscious leve and that “[it] not only represents women's cultural reality, it also generates or enacts that reality” (Drori, Manos, Santacreu-Vasut, Shenkar, & Shoham, 2018, p. 408)

Our findings also illustrate how technology addresses social and structural influences for women entrepreneurs While previous work has highlighted that women are also motivated to be entrepreneurs (Dawson & Henley, 2015) our findings suggest that gender stereotyping still persists in the external environment, such as that expressed b venture capitalists. This is consistent with existing studies such as those of Ahuja (2002) and Clayton et al. (2012) where social and structural factors pose barriers for women in the IT profession. As highlighted by Clayton et al. (2012), gender stereotypes are learned at home, in communities and through the media. It is critical for a broader set of stakeholders, networks and organisations to play a more proactive role in ensuring diversity, integra tion and gender equality in technology entrepreneurship, a field where gendered contexts overlap and barriers ar exacerbated (Wheadon & Duval-Couetil, 2019). Even in a progressive society like Australia, women still perceive that there are barriers, such as the “old boys” clubs' business culture, that still continue to perpetuate in male-dominated sectors. There is also a perception of innate discrimination, even with overarching institutions such as the banking system, where the standard is perceived to be set slightly higher for women entrepreneurs, particularly those i technology-related fields, in seeking capital. These barriers not only affect women entrepreneurs' businesses, but also their social innovation efforts.

The study offers several theoretical implications. First, it identifies a number of influences of DSI in the context of women entrepreneurs, which have not been empirically explored in previous research. When examined using the IDTGIT lens (Quesenberry & Trauth, 2012; Trauth, 2002; Trauth et al., 2004), the findings support the view of vari ous factors inhibiting women entrepreneurs from starting ventures and limiting their business growth. We found that the impact of individual identity and individual influences relate to women entrepreneurs in general, not solely to women entrepreneurs involved in DSI. Despite the progress in our society for women entrepreneurs (Global Entre preneurship Monitor, 2019) and the high human capital of our sample, which was similar to Dy et al.'s (2017) study on women digital entrepreneurs, individual identity and individual influences still prove to be challenging for man women entrepreneurs involved in social innovation.

Our findings further highlight that much work remains to be done to break down the barriers of social and struc tural influences to promote women's entrepreneurship and DSI. Government must create an integrated response in order to break down the structural siloes and barriers, and members of society must adopt a more inclusive attitude to eradicate stereotypes. Additionally, women entrepreneurs need to inspire, support and employ other women as part of their social innovation to address the individual, social and structural influences. Our interviewees indicated that women professionals often needed the support and mentoring but the lack of opportunity and sponsorship i keeping them from advancing in their careers. Women should be proactive to seek advice and mentoring from other and they also need to work together to achieve equality and progress. This is what Sheryl Sandberg, Facebook' Chief Operating Officer, convincingly argues for the importance of women to “lean in” to “sit at the table,” take risks, balance their work and life, and pursue their goals and ambition (Sandberg, 2013). The more women in the work force, particularly if they are achieving success in their professional careers and financially, the more likely they are to create a sea change. This is a state at which cumulatively within a place over time these innovations transform the ways that institutions classify people based on their identity and can lead to shifts in gender ideologies that would result in greater access to resources” (Blake & Hanson, 2005, p. 693).

Second, this research enhances our understanding of the role of technology in the context of women's entrepre neurship and their social innovation ventures. Responding to Lindberg et al.'s (2016) research for exploring digita entrepreneurship and social innovation through a gendered lens, our study contributes to the mainstream entrepreneurship and IS research literature by linking digital technologies with entrepreneurship research generally and with women's entrepreneurship specifically. Our arguments also adopt Adam et al.'s (2004) view that “gender must be taken into account if we are to achieve a fuller understanding of technologies” (p. 222). We thus expanded the IDTGIT by reframing it to consider technology and women's entrepreneurship. While existing studies utilising the IDTGIT are frequently applied in the context of women professionals' careers in the IS/IT field (Quesenberry &

Trauth, 2012), arguably, there is a wealth of opportunity where women can be more involved in transforming th technology landscape to overcome such challenges. Our findings indicate women entrepreneurs are actively using technologies to pursue social innovation (Charalabidis et al., 2014; Tracey & Stott, 2017). Indeed, women entrepre neurs perceive that technology plays a crucial role in achieving gender equality (Wajcman, 2010) and particularly in empowering women to pursue entrepreneurship (Crittenden et al., 2019).

Third, our study has wider theoretical implications by examining the various social impacts of women entrepre neurs' DSI. According to Edwards-Schachter and Wallace (2017), technological innovations with social purposes cre ate social impact, and our study highlights how technology can facilitate the different types of social impact in term of education, employment, environment and climate, community development and progress and healthcare. We found that women entrepreneurs are very driven to focus on matters dear to their hearts, rather than simply pri oritising purely on economic returns. Interestingly, many of our entrepreneurs are involved in creating impact on community development and progress. DSI indeed brings in new perspectives for women entrepreneurs to use tech nology to tackle issues in communities by actively participating in and initiating changes for community benefits and progress. Their businesses are not only creating market value, but also social value (ie, benefits that go beyond the commercial gains (Phills, Deiglmeier, & Miller, 2008)

Our findings further indicate the chain reaction of DSI and social impact—women entrepreneurs are motivated to undertake DSI to address challenges and create social impact—and social impact, in turn, helps to partially address the individual, social and structural challenges. This is an important finding in that the impact of women entrepre neurs' motivations to engage in DSI not only can develop competitive entrepreneurial advantage, but can also over come the challenges imposed by the individual and external constraints (Rindova, Barry, & Ketchen, 2009). For instance, the growth in the number of working women around the world represents an opportunity for individua entrepreneurs and companies to do even more to address social and structural constraints of gender diversity an equality. Such action and commitment can trigger the transformation of social and cultural institutions over time This chain reaction should be built and capitalised upon further—the more social impact is created and the more visi ble they are, the more empowered women entrepreneurs will be to engage in DSI to make a difference in their busi ness and society.

The findings of the study also have significant practical implications. First, our findings expand the knowledge and understanding of women entrepreneurs in the IS field. While women entrepreneurs are not as extensively represented in the broader technology sector in comparison to their male counterparts (Adam et al., 2004; Ozkazanc Pan & Clark Muntean, 2018), this trend is slowly changing. There are still impediments to women entrepreneurs such as gender discrimination and inadequate access to resources, but there are changes in the external environment. Fo example, women entrepreneurs are increasingly using crowdfunding and crowdsourcing platforms (Mollick & Robb, 2016) to break down the barriers in relation to funding challenges. Understanding and accepting such gende equality also positively affects social development outcomes, such as getting more young girls to embrace STEM studies and careers.

Second, we also consider the enabling effect of technology by women entrepreneurs (Crittenden et al., 2019 Pergelova et al., 2019). The study highlights that while biases are still ingrained within entrepreneurship in the digita world (Trauth et al., 2018), more women entrepreneurs are embracing digital technology in their businesses. The fac tors influencing women's entrepreneurship, while at times inhibiting, should not be viewed as merely a threat. There is always an opportunity that can be exploited from any of the factors. For instance, if one's individual influences are not favourable, an entrepreneur can build their network connections to capitalise on structural factors or build thei knowledge to benefit from technological advances. Our findings particularly show how technology can be used to create opportunities and address the challenges imposed by the individual, social and structural factors. This ought to serve as a signal for women entrepreneurs that even though glass ceilings have not been shattered, the gende gap in traditionally male-dominated fields can be addressed through the use of technology.

Third, the findings help to inform policymakers and digital businesses of the social impact of DSI. DSI provide valuable options such as the development of institutional environments that promote equality and empowerment or the scalability of technologies for healthcare, climate change and education. In fact, the findings indicate an opportu nity for entrepreneurs to make significant investments in using technology to creating social impact and positiv change. The findings may also inform policymakers to encourage DSI among entrepreneurs, including women entre preneurs, as DSI can help in the creation of economic and social value by firms [which] are not strictly separate o necessarily at odds with each other” (van der Have & Rubalcaba, 2016, p. 1931). While we note significant progress for women's entrepreneurship, much work is still needed at the policymaking level to back new ideas to resolve bi challenges in education, employment, environment and climate, community development and progress and healthcare.

This study, however, is not without limitations. First, the findings were based on interviews with 17 wome entrepreneurs, which could be considered a small sample size, limiting the generalisability of the findings. How ever, this is consistent with the sample sizes of other qualitative studies using semi-structured interviews in th IS field (Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018; Panteli, 2012). Second, the study is cross-sectional and does not provide evi dence of a temporal relationship between the influences of DSI and the outcomes of DSI. Future studies could incorporate a quantitative longitudinal research design to assess the effects of these influences on outcome over time.

Third, our data collection does not distinguish the different stages of entrepreneurship. For example, women entrepreneurs at an early stage of entrepreneurship may face very different challenges to those that have established businesses. Our interviews, however, were guided to ask women entrepreneurs to reflect on their cur rent and/or past businesses. Several of them are serial entrepreneurs and they highlighted the common barriers the have faced throughout their entrepreneurial journeys, regardless of the stages of entrepreneurship. It would be inter esting for future research to be more explicit in focusing on the barriers, opportunities and social impacts at differen entrepreneurial stages to compare the findings.

Finally, future research can examine women entrepreneurs' DSI in different countries' contexts to draw conclu sions on the different types of DSI that can be pursued, the general trends in terms of opportunities and challenges that women entrepreneurs face and the social impacts they create. We are hopeful that this research inspires mor studies to examine and discuss more inclusive social innovation and entrepreneurship research in the IS field for fur ther theoretical and practical contributions.
