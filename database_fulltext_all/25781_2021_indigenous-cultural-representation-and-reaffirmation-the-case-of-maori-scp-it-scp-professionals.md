## Abstract

Western worldviews dominate the information systems (IS) literature, accepted and taken for granted as the natural way of doing things. While diversity in terms of gender and ethnicity has been studied in the IS field, there is scant research on the experiences of Indigenous information technology (IT) professionals. This study uses narrative inquiry to provide temporal, contextualised accounts of IT professional experiences from the Indigenous (Maori) community in Aotearoa, New Zealand. These accounts demonstrate how participants actively draw from their culture to enact their professional activities. We identify three cultural elements that differentiate the practices of Maori IT professionals from Western approaches that dominate the IS literature: whakapapa (genealogical connections), tikanga (customary traditions) and tino rangatiratanga (collective cultural determination). Further, we theorise how Indigenous IT professionals capitalise on the re-presentational power of digital artefacts as a vehicle for cultural reaffirmation to project their Indigenous identity in contemporary society. Our findings are useful for attempts to enhance Indigenous representation in the IT workforce and for designing systems and artefacts that are relevant to and inclusive for Indigenous communities.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Indigenous Peoples are culturally distinct communities descended from those who inhabited a geographical region before people of different cultures or ethnic origins arrived (Martínez Cobos, 1987). Indigenous Peoples have often been discriminated against, marginalised and even dispossessed from their land (Dahl, 2012). Consequently, Indige nous Peoples have been increasingly engaging in collective movements towards self-determination (Dyson & Underwood, 2006; Porsanger, 2010) and cultural revitalisation (Gover, 2015).

Indigenous communities are increasingly adopting technology for their own social, economic, educational and other interests (Du et al., 2015; Dyson et al., 2007; Kennedy, 2018; Landzelius, 2006). However, digital technology has also been used as a colonisation tool in attempts to assimilate Indigenous Peoples (Brown & Nicholas, 2012; Kamira, 2003b; Roy & Raitt, 2003). Grappling with this tension, Indigenous IT professionals straddle the intersection of Western-origin technical skills and their contextual Indigenous perspectives. Using audio-visual media to convey Indigenous knowledge to wider audiences (Winschiers-Theophilus et al., 2012), IS to transfer geographi knowledge (Rundstrom, 1995) or developing interfaces to capture Indigenous cultural heritage (Leavy et al., 2007) while avoiding being encaged by Western epistemologies exemplify some of the challenges. This study examines the experiences and practices of Indigenous IT professionals to understand the unique experiences that they have had and continue having with digital technologies

This research is set in Aotearoa,<sup>1</sup> where a community of Maori IT professionals has contributed innovative artefacts that reflect the uniqueness of the Maori heritage (Armstrong, 2018). These individuals use digital artefacts to represent their culturally perceived reality on a variety of dimensions, such as the relationship between individuals and groups, the connection to the natural environment and the value of their heritage. They do so while living in country where their community had been placed in a de facto subordinated position by its British coloniser (Bishop & Glynn, 2003). This backdrop leads us to ask whether there is a relationship between the creation of these digital artefacts and attempts to redress the marginalisation of Maori in Aotearoa. We examine this topic throug the following research question:

How do Indigenous worldviews shape Maori IT professional experiences and their approach towards the development of digital artefacts?

This paper is organised as follows. The next section summarises the role of diversity and culture in research on IT professionals, while the following section examines the nexus between Indigenous Peoples and digital technology Then, our study's specific research context is presented, followed by an explanation of the methodological frame work and narrative approach in Section 4. Section 5 presents the findings, followed by a discussion of our result (Section 6), where we emphasise the cultural re-presentation and re-affirmation opportunities the digital space offer to Indigenous Peoples. Section 7 presents the conclusions of this paper.

## 2 | IT PROFESSIONALS AND CULTURE

IT professional research mainly emphasises understanding IT professionals as a group of individuals operating within an organisational context. IT professionals can be distinguished from other professionals on three aspects occupational, human and technological (Prommegger et al., 2020). Studies on the unique occupational character istics of the IT profession include research on its professional culture (Dinger et al., 2015) and IT career trajecto ries (Joseph et al., 2007; Joseph et al., 2012). Research on the human aspects of the IT profession has investigated the work activities of IT professionals (Agarwal & Prasad, 2000; Chan & Thong, 2009), the demanding nature of IT jobs (Rutner et al., 2011) and gender and minorities in the IT profession (Ahuja, 2002; Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018; Armstrong et al., 2018; Cain & Trauth, 2017; Griffiths et al., 2006; Joshi et al., 2017). Finally, studies on the technological aspects of the IT profession have focussed on the knowledge and skills of IT professionals (Keil et al., 2013).

While past research on IT professionals provides insights into their work and challenges, our understanding of the experiences of ethnic minority IT professionals is limited. This is because previous studies have taken either an IT professional identity-centric view or an ethnic identity-centric view. Studies that take an IT professional identity centric view tend to depict IT professionals as a homogenous group by highlighting the normative experiences of what it means to be IT professionals, regardless of their ethnic or cultural identity. For example, Rutner et al. (2011 suggest that IT work generally involves both technical and people-facing activities, while Dinger et al. (2015) explain that dimensions of professionalism involving self-regulation, sense of calling, professional autonomy and identifica tion with professional organisations impact satisfaction, commitment and performance

On the other hand, the stream of research that takes an ethnic identity-centric view emphasises the influence of gender and race/ethnicity on IT professional experiences to reveal challenges and barriers that underrepresented groups face. For example, both women and minorities can experience role ambiguity because they are expected to fill both work and non-work roles that conflict with one another (Tapia & Kvasny, 2004). Research also shows that women and minorities can be marginalised in performance appraisals, promotion and salary (Igbaria & Chidambaram, 1997; Igbaria & Wormley, 1992); these groups struggle to find mentors or role models in their IT careers (Windeler & Riemenschneider, 2016). Despite the insights on career barriers of women and minorities that this stream of research offers, not much is known about the experiences of IT professionals from Indigenous backgrounds.

Our study draws on streams of occupational and professional research in management and sociology, whic argue that social identities (e.g. gender, race and ethnicity) are interdependent with the nature of work (Ashcraft, 2013). In particular, social identities and the nature of work are mutually influential (Tomaskovic Devey, 1993) and identity categories like race and gender shape the content, value, practice and administration of work (Ashcraft, 2013, p. 11) and vice versa. Our approach is in line with cross-disciplinary literature that has studied the professional identity of nurses (Price et al., 2013), teachers (Ruohotie-Lyhty, 2013) and accountants (Ahrens & Chapman, 2000), among others. This approach draws upon narrative identity (Connelly & Clandinin, 1999 Polkinghorne, 1996) to reveal how individuals make sense of themselves by interpreting their experiences in relation to contexts and other people. In this sense, professional identity is presented as an account of a sociocultural proces with an integration of both personal and professional sides of becoming and being an Indigenous IT professional which fits with a view of theory as narrative (DiMaggio, 1995; Sarker et al., 2018).

Regarding culture, analysis in the IT professional literature conceives culture as values focussing on national cul tural differences (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006); Hofstede's (2001) framework is commonly applied in these studies Research has shown, for example, how differing levels of individualism/collectivism and masculinity/femininity can influence working practices (Kankanhalli et al., 2004). Culture also influences the development of digital artefacts (Hidding, 1998; Leidner & Kayworth, 2006; Shore & Venkatachalam, 1995). Digital artefacts (e.g. software, games, blogs, videos) are observable phenomena that embody a group's values and tacit assumptions (Schein, 2010). In lin with this conceptualisation, there has been some exploration into how system designers' values affect design choices (Friedman et al., 2008; Kumar & Bjørn-Andersen, 1990). For example, IS designers in Canada were found to stress technical, economic and socio-political values differently than those in Denmark (Kumar & Bjørn-Andersen, 1990) However, some scholars question the conceptualisation of culture as a set of uniform values and its adequacy as a predictor of behaviour. For example, people may share similar values while remaining different in the way their cul ture influences their overall pattern of behaviour (Swidler, 1986).

Alternatively, culture has been theorised as toolkits (Swidler, 1986) or resources that include stories, rituals and worldviews that individuals use to construct strategies of action. Swidler (1986) does not use the term strategy to describe a plan consciously devised to attain a goal (p. 277); instead, strategies of action refer to a general way of organising actions and integrating them into larger assemblages. This view emphasises flexibility and the agentic use of culture. In particular, actors can construct different strategies of action by selecting certain cultural elements and investing them with meanings in their life circumstances. To understand the meanings of particular cultural elements we need to understand the strategy of action in which they are embedded. In this way,

[t]he metaphor of the toolkit shifts attention away from values as the driving force of behavior to bits and pieces of culture that can be differently assembled, opening up the possibility of a variety of outcomes, even given the same values (Giorgi et al., 2015, p. 13).

Individual agency is central in this conceptualisation of culture (Weber & Dacin, 2011). Individuals act as cultura bricoleurs who combine diverse cultural materials for their own pragmatic ends. These individuals require appropri ate skills, habits and styles to assemble bits of culture into action pragmatically. Also, structure, context, life experi ences and circumstances influence how individuals use their toolkits (Swidler, 1986).

## 3 | INDIGENOUS PEOPLES AND DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY

Indigenous Peoples have had unique and somewhat conflicting experiences with digital technologies. Indigenous Peoples, similar to other groups, use the internet and social media for personal and community purposes. It is inter esting to observe that, unlike non-Indigenous youth, Indigenous youth are interested in using social media to main tain intergenerational connections with elders (Carlson, 2017). Yet, Indigenous Peoples also have disproportionatel negative experiences on the internet and social media in the form of racist media representations, stereotyping and cyberbullying (Hunter et al., 2007).

As a consequence of the post-colonial context in which Indigenous Peoples operate, their digital technolog experiences follow three – not mutually exclusive – logics: cultural expropriation, cultural preservation and cultura reclamation. Cultural expropriation and cultural preservation have dominated the literature, while cultural reclama tion has emerged more recently, offering a promising way for Indigenous Peoples to engage with digital technology

Cultural expropriation describes persistent and ongoing attempts to apply colonial policies and practices to erad icate Indigenous cultural knowledge, traditions and identity (Janke & Iacovino, 2012). Typically, the cultural expropri ation logic often asserts positive outcomes for Indigenous Peoples such as the promotion of digital technology as a remedy to uplift Indigenous Peoples from their social and economic situation (Lin et al., 2015) and the archival of Indigenous cultural knowledge in digital artefacts as cultural heritage (Christen, 2005). In reality, the cultural expro priation logic is endowed with the hegemonic discourse that places superiority to Western knowledge that reinforces the historical conditions of domination-subjugation in which Indigenous Peoples are seen as inferior and devoiced and systematically objectified entity (Spivak, 1988, p. 5). This alleged superiority of Western knowledg denotes the power asymmetry that characterises post-colonial contexts; dominant economic, social and politica structures often place Indigenous culture in a subordinated position (Ravishankar et al., 2013). It is common in postcolonial contexts to minimise – or even ignore – the cultural and spiritual significance of Indigenous artefacts and corresponding knowledge as evidenced in museum displays (Brown & Nicholas, 2012; Whaanga et al., 2015). Also, digital technology can be utilised, in post-colonial contexts, as an oppressive tool in attempts to assimilate and con trol Indigenous Peoples (Brown & Nicholas, 2012; Kamira, 2003b; Roy & Raitt, 2003). In this study, neglecting th post-colonial context may result in an incomplete picture of how Maori IT professionals reflect their worldviews in developing digital artefacts.

There is a growing interest among Indigenous Peoples worldwide to use digital technology to serve Indigenous goals and interests, including preserving their culture for future generations or promoting their culture and heritage beyond their communities. Initiatives that embrace the cultural preservation logic are often led by Indigenous Peopl themselves or involve Indigenous Peoples in a meaningful way to produce digital artefacts that are culturally appro priate (Dyson et al., 2007). Ultimately, the authenticity of digital artefacts is given by the extent to which they reflec underlying values and are dependent on the acceptance of the communities that they intend to represent (Landzelius, 2006; Winschiers-Theophilus et al., 2012). For example, Hughes and Dallwitz (2007) suggest that the development of a digital archive database system for Anangu (Pitjantjatjara and Yankunytjatjara peoples of Centra Australia) needs an appropriate user interface that ‘can't look like something Microsoft would make’ (p. 150) Instead, the user interface has to be designed for and by Anangu with some Pitjantjatjara language

Lastly, the cultural reclamation logic goes beyond cultural preservation and embraces the sovereignty of Indige nous Peoples. Alfred and Corntassel (2005) forcefully argue for indigenousness to be ‘reconstructed, reshaped and actively lived as resurgence against the dispossessing and demeaning processes of annihilation that are inherent to colonialism (p. 612). Expressions such as cultural identity restoration (Young, 2018) and cultural revitalisation (Whaanga & Wehi, 2015) have been linked with this logic. They indicate the interest among Indigenous Peoples to reclaim thousands of years of history and culture (Hendry, 2005, p. 202) and regain control of their identity. Fo Indigenous Peoples, cultural identity restoration can be seen as ‘emancipatory experience involving connection wit community history, collective memory, and ancestors’ (Young, 2018, p. 345) to overcome oppression and restore their dignity and place in society. While digital technology can support the sharing of Indigenous information, Indige nous control over the presentation of Indigenous culture in digital spaces is paramount (Kamira, 2003b, 2007). In Aotearoa, there is a strong emphasis on the reclamation and revitalisation of Maori language and culture and in all owing Maori communities and Maori worldviews and principles to guide that process (Kamira, 2007; Stevenson & Callaghan, 2008).

Given the paucity of research on IT professionals' experiences from ethnic minorities and the promising role of digital technology for these groups, this study attempts to shed light on how the Indigenous worldviews of Maori IT professionals shape their professional experiences and approach towards the development of digital artefacts

## 4 | RESEARCH DESIGN

In this research, we aim to explain the experiences of Maori IT professionals to illuminate how they combine various cultural materials in the process of becoming and being Indigenous IT professionals. Consequently, the research con text is significant and requires an appropriate explanation. For Indigenous Peoples, research involves theorising new meanings and knowledge in ways that have legitimised colonial practices (Smith, 2012). Therefore, beyond an expli cation of context to overcome the pitfalls of many IS studies that falsely imply universalism (Davison & Martinsons, 2016), we also acknowledge our responsibility to ensure an appropriate process in research conducted with Maori individuals (Smith, 2012). This section outlines the specific historical and cultural context in which thi research takes place and explains our research approach.

## 4.1 | Research context

In Aotearoa, Maori people represent 16.5% of the 4.7 million population (Stats, 2018). The term Maori, which in th native language denotes common or ordinary , was used originally to differentiate between the native people of Aotearoa from those who were ‘different’, namely, the British settlers (Taonui, 2011). The kin-based society of the Maori before British arrival meant that Maori would identify more specifically with their whanau (family), hapu (subtribe) or iwi (tribe) (Durie, 1998; Mead, 2016). An alternative term used to describe Maori is Tangata Whenua, meaning ‘people of the land’. This name reflects a Maori worldview, which is firmly rooted in the spiritual realm and ethical rules (Henry & Pene, 2001), that emphasises the sacredness and vitality of all things including the environ ment, relationships between individuals and the significance of reciprocity in human relations.

Three Maori values would prove crucial in shaping our participants' professional trajectories: whakapapa, tikang and tino rangatiratanga. Whakapapa, literally meaning ‘to layer’, traces Maori ancestry back through the land to the atua (gods) and forward to capture future generations yet to be born (Henare et al., 2014). This whakapap connection evokes an obligation to serve as kaitiaki (guardians, caretakers) of the land and environment to ensure its survival for future generations. These practices are governed by tikanga, which refers to rules or customs considered ethically or morally correct within particular contexts (Mead, 2016). The significant aspects of human relations for Maori are reflected in whanau, whakapapa and whanaungatanga. Whanau is considered the foundation of Maor society that reflects the extended and intergenerational family (Spiller & Stockdale, 2013), while whanaungatanga acknowledges the importance of relationships between Maori people and the wider society (Mrabure et al., 2018)

Integral to understanding settler-Indigenous relations in Aotearoa is Te Tiriti o Waitangi/The Treaty of Waitang (Terruhn, 2019), signed in 1840 to protect the mutual interests of Maori as Indigenous Peoples and the British set tlers. Following the signing of Te Tiriti, colonial governance was established, and many British settlers moved to Aotearoa (Bishop, 2003). Colonial structures eroded Maori institutions and assimilated Maori into Western society (Henry & Pene, 2001). State and missionary schools diminished Maori culture, and the Maori language was forbidde (Bishop & Glynn, 2003). Traditional and cultural practices were prohibited by law, and Maori land was invaded and confiscated.

Despite this history of cultural deprivation, Maori have been engaged in cultural and political movements sinc the 1970s (Bishop & Glynn, 2003). For Maori, these movements have come as a result of continuous struggle and exemplify the notion of tino rangatiratanga through defining and establishing institutions that allow Maori to secure their own destiny (Smith & Reid, 2000). Tino rangatiratanga refers to the right to self-determination for Maori (Jackson, 2015), including political autonomy (Kamira, 2000). As an expression of tino rangatiratanga, Te Reo Maor was made an official language of Aotearoa in 1987, based on the recognition that the language was a taonga (trea sured possession) protected in Te Tiriti. Also, Indigenous education institutions were established as well as Maori-owned radio stations and a public Maori television channel (est. 2004) have been used to revitalise and promote Maori language and culture. In the digital arena, Maori scholars have pointed to the importance of self determination and control over Māori data and cultural representation (Kamira. 2000: Whaanga et al., 2015). How ever, despite these advances, social inequities in employment, income, health and education persist, reflecting th multi-generational impacts of colonialism in Aotearoa (Borell et al., 2018).

## 4.2 | Maori-centred research

Our research team of four comprises three from diverse, non-Maori backgrounds and one Maori researcher. Thus, we were conscious of the possibility of being perceived as outsiders operating from cultural perspectives that do ‘not accurately reflect the views or reality of the researched’ (Walker et al., 2006, p. 335). We refrained from using methodologies that have been specifically reserved for use by Maori. Kaupapa Maori methodologies, described as research for, by and with Maori (Smith, 2012), have been distinguished as the culturally appropriate form of Maor research characterised by full control by Maori researchers and institutions and the exclusive use of Maori language, customs and theory (Cunningham, 2000; Hudson et al., 2010). Consequently, we selected Durie's (1996) Maori-centred research framework to guide our work in generating the research aims and intentions, in engaging with participants and disseminating the findings. This framework comprises three principles, whakapiki tangata whakatuia and mana Maori.

Whakapiki tangata relates to enablement, enhancement and empowerment. At a collective level, this principl recognises the obligation to produce research that is of some benefit to Maori communities and the accountabilit of researchers to those communities. In preparation for this research, we consulted with Maori working in and experienced in IT to seek their input in framing our research aims that are relevant and beneficial to Maori Whakatuia reflects the Maori worldview described above by recognising complex connections across different dimensions, those between past and present, and body, mind and soul (Forster, 2003). This principle further rec ognises the interconnectedness of environmental. social, political and economic spheres. Therefore, we acknowl edge how Maori IT professional experiences need to be considered within the wider socio-economic and historica context, including taking for granted the legitimacy of Maori worldviews and recognising the complexity of Maori-Pakeha relations in historical and contemporary Aotearoa. Mana Maori refers to maintaining control and ownership over th research process. Engaging in consultation and ongoing dialogue with our participants about the findings helps safeguard our participants' standing and stories. To ensure authenticity in our narratives, we have collaboratively constructed find ings with our participants (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000; Ollerenshaw & Creswell, 2002).

## 4.3 | Narrative method

The narrative approach is considered respectful and congruent with the rich oral histories and cultural practices of Indigenous communities (Drawson et al., 2017; Willox et al., 2012). Commensurate with the interpretative tradition narrative research advocates pluralism, relativism and subjectivity and is often used as a means of understanding human experience (Connelly & Clandinin, 1990). In the IS literature, Hedman et al. (2019) support the use of a narra tive form as ‘an attempt to contextualise and order the multi-layered reality of people's experiences’ (p. 5). In this study, a narrative refers to a person's life history or a story about a significant aspect of their life that shapes their IT professional experiences (Myers, 2010; Riessman, 2008). A plot is the narrative structure that reflects how individua events are linked together and can be understood from the perspective of their contribution and influence on a spec ified outcome (Polkinghorne, 1995).

The narratives and plots presented in Section 5 emerge from the iterative process of data collection, dat analysis and the writing of the narratives within our participants' Maori cultural knowledge structure. This practice is in line with an Indigenous view of knowledge as situated on cultural and historical specificity (Henry & Pene, 2001). We carefully followed the methodological considerations proposed by Connelly and Clandinin (1990 and Polkinghorne (1995) that emphasise a collaborative relationship between researchers and participants (see Appendix B). The process of creating Maori IT professional narratives and plots involve four main activities: col lecting stories, analysing stories, composing narratives and emplotting strategies of action.

## 4.3.1 | Collecting Maori IT professionals' stories

In contrast to quantitative research that relies on a logical universe and statistical significance to construct a sample that is representative of the population and obtain findings that can be generalisable to the population,<sup>2</sup> qualitative research relies on a social universe and the social significance of a study to the population (Gobo, 2004). Therefore, qualitative studies mostly pursue information-rich cases and follow purposeful sampling using different strategies depending on research aims to gain an in-depth understanding of a phenomenon (Patton, 1990). In this study, we aim to develop narrative meaning by configuring actions, life events and outcomes into a meaningful, coherent whole (Polkinghorne, 1995; Riessman, 2008). We use an intensive sampling strategy to identify rich examples of the phe nomenon of interest, but not unusual cases (Patton, 1990, p. 171). Our sampling unit is an Indigenous IT profes sional's experience, defined as the sequences of life events and activities that shape professional practices and approaches to digital artefact development. The observational unit is an individual. Participants were selected because ‘they can provide substantial contributions to filling out the structure and character of the experience under investigation’ (Polkinghorne, 2005, p. 139). To obtain representativeness of concepts, we followed Gobo's (2004) guidelines, built on the principle that: representative samples are not predicted in advance but found, constructed and discovered gradually in the field (p. 418). First, we constructed provisional sampling in line with our research aim while paying attention to the sampling unit variance (i.e. Indigenous IT professional experience) on characteristics such as gender, types of digital artefact and professional autonomy. We sought Maori IT professionals who had more than five years of work experience in the local IT industry, have publicly contributed to events and conversations related to Maori engagement with IT and have worked on diverse digital artefacts.

We identified and approached four Maori IT professionals; however, one was not available for follow-up conversations Therefore, we interviewed three Maori IT professionals; two males and one female. Two of them have less professiona autonomy than the other because they work in the mainstream IT industry and thus need to find a balance between making a profit and producing meaningful artefacts in congruence with their culture. All three participants agreed to be identified Hori Te Ariki Mataki (a digital media designer), Maru Nihoniho (a game developer) and Potaua Biasiny-Tule (a digital entrepreneur and activist). All four authors listened to the interviews and independently produced accounts of how Maori culture shaped the interviewees' professional practices and approaches to digital artefact development. During our discussion, we observed that all three participants had been heavily influenced by three Maori cultural principles, namely whakapapa (gene alogy), tikanga (code of conduct) and tino rangatiratanga (self-determination). The literature confirms that tikanga, strong con nectedness reflecting the depth of relationships with current, future and past generations, and collective autonomy reflecting a pattern of volition and choice are some of the core values for Maori professionals (Haar et al., 2019; Houkamau & Sibley, 2010; Kuntz et al., 2014; Roche et al., 2018; Spiller et al., 2011). Based on the process involving the interplay between sampling and evaluating the variance of a phenomenon from the interviewed sample and literature, we conclude that w have a representative sample.

## 4.3.2 | Analysing Maori IT professionals' stories

We followed a holistic-content narrative analytical approach in which an individual's life story is taken as a whole and analysed texts are interpreted in the context of other texts in the story to find meaning convey in those stories This approach is appropriate because our study aims to develop an understanding of the meaning of Maori IT professionals' experiences from their Indigenous worldviews (Lieblich et al., 1998). We used a three-dimensional space approach (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000; Ollerenshaw & Creswell, 2002) to analyse and organise key elements in the stories. The three dimensions are: interaction, involving looking inward to internal conditions feelings, hopes, mora dispositions and looking outward to existential conditions with other people such as their intentions, purposes and points of view; temporality, involving past and present experiences or those possible in the future and situation involving context, places or the sequence of a person's places. We started the analysis by reading and re-reading the interview transcripts. Then, we constructed the three-dimensional space narrative structure for each participant (Tables C1, C2 and C3 in Appendix C present illustrative excerpts of the three-dimensional space narrative structure for each participant). These narrative structures serve as interim texts or texts situated in the spaces between fiel texts and the final narratives (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000).

## 4.3.3 | Composing Maori IT professional narratives

The next step was to compose a narrative for each participant. Lieblich et al. (1998) principles of a holistic-content narrative analytical approach suggest researchers read the material several times until a pattern emerges in the form of themes of the entire story. We looked for narrative threads and patterns within and across participants' experi ences. The composed narratives focus on the particulars of individual actions while maintaining the overall meanin of the experience (Polkinghorne, 1995)

In the field texts, participants both explicitly and implicitly referenced a Maori worldview and cultural resources while discussing their life histories and journeys to become IT professionals. The three authors who produced th narratives independently identified three Maori values (i.e. whakapapa, tikanga and tino rangatiratanga) that are prominent in all three participants' experiences. In alignment with our research principle of mana Maori, each narra tive was sent to participants through email for them to check and provide feedback. This procedure also contributed to validate the narratives of the participants' experiences (Polkinghorne, 2007). All participants agreed with their nar ratives and the chosen actions and events featured in them – and offered some minor clarifications.

## 4.3.4 | Emplotting Maori IT professionals' construction of strategies of action

The final step in the analysis was to apply Polkinghorne's (1995) narrative emplotment. The emplotment analytica process involves revisiting the stories and field texts to ensure that the emerging plots serve to impose coherence on participant's life events as well as retain the integrity of the wholeness of the participants' stories. Building on th idea of culture as toolkits (Swidler, 1986), the first plotline, re-presentation, explains how the participants variously assemble the three Maori values to construct strategies of action and re-present these values in digital artefacts Drawing on the concept of cultural reclamation, the second plotline, re-affirmation, explains how the participant manage to reclaim their culture and restore Maori identity through their IT professional experiences. These two plot and their subplots are presented in the next section after the presentation of their narratives

## 5 | FINDINGS

The following narratives of Hori, Maru and Potaua begin with their personal life stories. We used the tables in Appendix C to craft these narratives. The narratives then describe how their interpretations of Maori values shaped them as the became IT professionals and how these interpretations are embedded in the digital artefacts they produce.

## 5.1 | Hori Te Ariki Mataki

Growing up in Christchurch, Hori knew little about his Maori heritage and could not speak Te Reo Maori. After inter nalising what it means to be Māori and becoming fluent in the language during high school vears. Hori now makes sure that his Maori heritage influences everything he does as a digital designer.

## 5.1.1 | Whakapapa

Hori exudes a deep connection to his roots: This is where I am from, this is my land, this is my mountain . At 22, Hor started his digital design company Ariki Creative, straight out of the design school. Hori explains how whakapapa is integrated into Ariki Creative's works: ‘We learn about different tribal designs… tribal stories and integrate [them to] those that [they] should be connecting with… Once [the stories] are connected, then it carries that legacy’ Whakapapa also guided his recent work for the Tower Junction, a shopping mall in Christchurch:

[It is] a building that has got zero to do with culture… but it is owned by [the tribe Ngai Tahu]. They [asked] us to give a different way of thinking… that no one else could really do. We have done these sort of traditional things and modernised them. So we are still keeping a tether to the old traditions and giving a modern twist to things.

Hori emphasises, ‘I like telling, learning your stories, I like things to be connected’. Hori immersed into Maori culture at a young age, ‘starting [from] high school’. This cultural immersion continued after high school: ‘I did a diploma in Maori studies. ## 5.3 | Potaua Biasiny-Tule

Based in Rotorua, a town with a strong Maori presence, Potaua has been involved in Maori rights activism since his youth. His whanau has had an association with Rotorua since his ancestors arrived 800 years ago. He discovered digital technology's potential while living in South Korea and introduced new tools into his activism, such as setting up www.tangatawhenua.com, a Maori news website, in the mid-2000s. He has continued developing Maori digital content, including translating Google's search interface into Maori and is cur rently focussed on increasing Maori involvement in the IT industry, both as individuals and corporate bodies In 2016, he co-founded the Digital Native Academy to train local youths in digital skills and Basecamp, a shared working space for entrepreneurs.

## 5.3.1 | Whakapapa

Potaua laments that there was no mention of local tupuna (ancestors) in the curriculum during his school days, and he knew more about Western history than his own whakapapa. Potaua wants future generations to know about their ancestors and their achievements and disseminate Maori stories widely: ‘If we do not tell our stories, someone else will poorly tell it for us’. For example, he was disappointed that the Fenton Agreement, which was the British colonial government's agreement with the iwi (tribe) from Rotorua, has been forgotten The lack of awareness of Maori history has led to an intention to ‘develop a game around our atua [gods]… and tupuna [ancestors] . He wants to represent Maori familial connections more effectively and sees potentia in using blockchain technologies to resolve this issue by building a ‘whakapapa software’. In Potaua's words ‘European ancestry, it is like a family tree… Ours is more like a forest and so how do you identify that one lit tle branch in the whole forest?

He is involved in the development of Fierce Girls, a web animation series about young Indigenous female characters, providing him with another avenue to remind youth about their links with their forebears:

We need to get our own superheroes. Superheroes get superpowers by finding a ring, getting bitten by a spider, so it is like a science fiction transformation rather than a spiritual transformation. Where do you get your superpowers from? From your ancestors. I think that is still coming through.

He also wants to enhance the Arataki Cultural Trails (https://poutama.co.nz/arataki-cultural-trails/) walking guid app using augmented reality and virtual reality (VR) technology. Another example is using VR to help Maori carver think they were carving on totara, a less prevalent native timber, when they were actually practising on abundant pine.

## 5.3.2 | Tikanga

Potaua views himself as ‘a cultural guardian’ who is aware of his community responsibilities:

When we turn up to our ancestors after we pass, and they ask us the big questions: ‘What did you do? Who did you affect? Whose lives did you impact?’ We could stand there with all honesty and say, ‘We tried our best; you know, we did this and this and this and this’. ‘Come on in!

For Potaua, the juxtaposition of digital technology and ancient customs is not an odd pairing. He laments that many Maori IT professionals working in corporations are told to ‘put their culture in their back pocket’, acknowledg ing that ‘if you do that long enough you forget who you are’

Potaua is frustrated by the continuing lack of engagement with Maori by government agencies to ensure cultur ally appropriate processes and outcomes. The recent development of the Te Aka Mauri Health Hub in Rotorua di not incorporate Maori ceremonies and even used symbols that were chosen simply because they ‘look Maori’. Fur thermore, he laments:

There is a whole suite of apps that are not useable for our community. People get funded to create things that no one uses. You need to get the community before you get the technology, and the community will shape the technology.

Potaua maintains customary practices such as the hongi (to press noses in greeting) and saying a karakia befor meetings. He is firm on the central role of women in his work, which for him is historically consistent with their rol in Maori society. Besides observing tikanga in his daily activities, Potaua is passionate about embedding tikanga in digital artefacts. One example is artificial intelligence (AI):

When you listen to the world talk about AI is going to take over, but our problem is because the people creating the AI do not have good tikanga. The technology does not have tikanga. How do we have the conversation so that AI has some sense of values?

He is also keen on developing artefacts that help Maori fulfil their customary obligations, such as donating money to support the communities. Despite his passion for technology, Potaua believes that community life activi ties, such as funerals, should not be digitised, interpreted as culturally inappropriate.

## 5.3.3 | Tino rangatiratanga

Potaua is intent on using the digital space so that his community can regain its self-sufficiency. The government's shift to a strong free-market approach in the 1980s led to severe dislocation in Maori communities across the coun try, meaning that Potaua grew up in an environment of high ‘unemployment, hopelessness and gangs’. To him, thi was ‘an ongoing cycle, a remnant of colonisation’. He sees technology as providing Maori youth with options that his generation and the ones before him did not have:

Technology is in our DNA. It has always been there from our tupuna. We read the stars. We have our karakia and our stories. I feel like in the last 100 years we have not been allowed to dream. They are taking our best, slotting them into a desk to slave for 20, 30 years and when they are done, ‘Kick them out’. That is what happened to my dad, my grandfather and my great-grandfather. We have to team up to make sure it does not happen again, so our kids can walk into more options. If we don't take these opportunities, Maori will once again be invisible.

His goal is to build up locally-rooted global citizens:

When they finally leave Rotorua, and they go to Auckland or go to Australia, or go to America, their feet are on the ground, but their heads are up here… Keep passionately our traditions, our culture, our language, our heritage.

Achieving this requires a support network, or else ‘if [the children] do not have the support and scaffolding, the are going to fail as we did’. Potaua disagrees with many iwis' dominant investment in primary industries, such as for estry, which have high death tolls and leave iwi as ‘price-takers’ in global export markets. To Potaua, these are ‘sun set industries ; he believes iwi investment in the tech sector will transform their communities:

In five years, I hope to see a DNA [Digital Native Academy] in every town. There is plenty of office spaces or shops that are run-down, so a lick of paint, broadband and… But we do not have any R&D money invested into Maori communities. So how do we make this transition, instead of asking the government? When will our tribe invest in the future, in animation and technology? It is still not there yet

Potaua also views digital skills as useful for creating digital artefacts to remedy past wrongs and resist continued colonisation of Maori in the digital realm. For example, Potaua's work involves creating artefacts to capture, express and share forgotten historical knowledge. An example of this is a 65-inch touchscreen that depicts the Fenton Agreement (Figure 4), a document central to the history of Rotorua and the dispossession of the local Maori from their land. For Potaua, this artefact was a way to ‘start the conversation… Now kaumatua [respected elders] can go with their mokopuna [descendants] and start learning about the Fenton’. It was not only to teach the local childre about their history but also to question the government's actions, many of which had had negative effects on Maori For example, the Tohunga Suppression Act in 1907 forced tohunga (traditional Maori healers) to hide and removed the community from its source of healing.

Potaua wants to stop ‘digital colonisation’, which occurs when Maori information is given away to the govern ment for managing Maori affairs – for example, Maori land records, which are especially valuable as Maori ar increasingly living away from their ancestral homelands. This situation has prompted him to begin working on devel oping new software to manage these data.

## 5.4 | Archetypal plots

The narratives above detail the life experiences of the three Maori IT professionals who participated in our study These narratives highlight the importance our participants grant to their Maori cultural identity. To answer ou research question, we identified common plots that pertain to the two aspects of our research question: participants approaches towards the development of digital artefacts and their experiences as IT professionals. Two intertwine plots emerge from this analysis: (1) the re-presentation of Indigenous culture in digital artefacts and (2) the re affirmation of their Indigenous identity through digital artefacts. We present these insights as archetypal plots below along with their supporting subplots.

## 5.4.1 | Re-presentation

We build on the concept of cultural reclamation (Hendry, 2005) by showing how our participants capitalise on th digital medium to create a platform of cultural re-presentation. This digitally mediated cultural re-presentation, whil naturally emerging from their strong attachment to their Maori culture, does not occur spontaneously; it is inten tional. The narratives reveal that our participants value the expression of culture in the digital realm. This is consis tent with the emphasis in the literature identifying Indigenous-led efforts to re-present Indigenous culture in digita forms (Csoba DeHass & Taitt, 2018; Wachowich, 2018). In different ways, each of our participants seeks to assembl cultural resources and re-present them as a unique and valuable component of Aotearoa in a digital realm, as explained below.

## Recognising Maori underpinnings

Although our participants vary in their comfort and confidence with Te Ao Maori and Te Reo Maori, they all strongly identify as Maori individuals. Hori was educated at a young age in his culture and language. Coupled with earl access to technology and role models, he can confidently draw on cultural perspectives in his approaches to work experimenting with Maori language, storytelling and design. Hori constantly considers the history and genealogy of places in his design. He explicitly wonders how his ancestors created knowledge while attempting to do the same in the digital medium. Maru is less confident with the language and with Maori customs. Her mother's generation was taught that they did not need to teach their children the language. So, she has always looked to her mother for guid ance in understanding Te Reo Maori and tikanga. Consequently, she is more concerned with appropriately conveying Maori culture and customs to both local and international audiences through her game development. Potaua's perspectives have been shaped by his early experiences and his family's background as tohungas; his grandfather was minister of Ringatu, a M aori Christian denomination. He regrets that Maori traditions were largely ignored in favour of Western history in his schooldays and is committed to using digital technology to revert what he perceives as a process of cultural dispossession

## Legitimising the Maori perspective in the IT profession

Our participants express their pledge to bring their cultural identity to the fore in their professional activities Although they are now IT professionals, none of them had formal training in the IS field. More importantly, far from trying to blend in a typically Western-dominated profession, they make their Maori perspective noticeable. Hori's design business explicitly incorporates elements of whakapapa. Every commissioned work reflects the history of th land and the people associated; his digital designs keep a tether to the old traditions . Moreover, he cultivates Maor traditions among both Maori and non-Maori staff members in his firm. Maru, a self-taught entrepreneur with sub stantial business experience, but no technical experience at all, infuses the ‘Maori's point of view’ into her develop ments, many of which go beyond Aotearoa. Her company has gained international recognition in the highl competitive world of online games. Potaua's overseas experience exposed him to digital technology for the first time He sees the potential of digital technology for Maori advancement. He was a pioneer in using digital technology for political activism (using emails and mobile phones to mobilise the masses, and setting up the first Maori website) Potaua's establishment of the Digital Native Academy and Basecamp were preceded by his engagement by Google to help set up a Maori version of the Google home page.

## Re-embodying Maori culture

Our participants purposefully develop digital artefacts to circulate re-presentations of Maori culture in the form of ethnically inspired designs (as Hori does), online games (as Maru does) or training programmes on coding for children and digitising historical events (as Potaua does). They are continuously remixing the traditional with the new, rendering innovative cultural re-presentations and dynamic expressions of culture in the digital realm. Firstly, the digita artefacts mentioned in this study embody the notion of whakapapa; they tell stories of the people and the land that link the past with the present and the future. Secondly, our participants observe tikanga in their artefact design. Thirdly, when our participants engage in the development of digital artefacts, they do so to champion Maori culture in a self-determined way (i.e. tino rangatiratanga).

In summary, the conception of the digital artefacts our participants develop can be traced back to their deep connection to Maori culture and their willingness to convey this cultural identity to the IT profession. Therefore these digital artefacts are not culturally empty objects; instead, they are devices that reflect a living culture (Janke & Iacovino, 2012).

## 5.4.2 | Re-affirmation

Historically, Indigenous Peoples have had their identities and culture subject to ridicule, oppression and marginalisation (Smith, 2012). In the previous section, we explained how our participants demonstrated agency in developing digital artefacts to reflect their living culture in meaningful ways (Landzelius, 2006). However, we observe that their digital artefact development actions go beyond re-presenting Maori culture in the digital realm; they actively re-affirm their Maori identity. This re-affirmation exercise is part of a process of reclaiming their identity in a world that had tried to extinguish them’ (Hendry, 2005, p. 195). We examine how whakapapa, tino rangatiratanga and tikanga guided our participants in developing digital artefacts to re-affirm their Maori identity in response to th subduing of their communities.

## Prioritising Maori cultural contents in digital artefacts

Our participants conspicuously display Indigenous cultural elements in the artefacts they develop. They do not just sprinkle Maori items to embellish their artefacts or make them exotically attractive. Maori content is at the centre of what they present; it is their motif – for example, language, designs and narratives. Our participants unashamedl declare that Maoridom is essential to their digital creation and use digital artefacts as a platform to celebrate thei Indigenous identity. As Hori says, Maori stuff is our foundation . In the case of Maru, through her games, she prioritises the message about living in harmony with the environment, which is fundamental for Maori. Similarly, Potaua fervently states that his motivation for training young people in digital skills with an emphasis on Maori culture is to offer them better opportunities in life while maintaining ‘our traditions, our culture, our language, our heritage’.

## Promoting Maori perspectives through digital artefacts

Besides prioritising Indigenous content, our participants promote Indigenous perspectives through the artefacts they develop. Their artefacts are not inanimate things filled with Maori content; they are living creations with cultural con notations. Hori unambiguously states that his videos convey the ‘Maori perspective’ with the intention to dissemi nate Indigenous worldviews. Similarly, the presence of Maori cultural elements in Maru's Guardian game is, as sh unequivocally explains, to portray the ‘Maori point of view’. That is why she pays special attention to genuineness in her development endeavours. Potaua makes his devotion to digital activism palpable with his initiative of developing software to manage Maori land records as an effort to stop digital colonisation . Fundamentally, our participants use artefacts to legitimise and position Maori perspectives on equal terms with the predominantly Western worldview in the digital space.

## Restoring Maori dignity via digital artefacts

The prioritisation of Indigenous content and the promotion of Indigenous worldviews are deliberate steps our partic ipants take. Their ultimate goal is to recover the sense of pride in their cultural roots and reverse the notion of inferi ority that might have been inculcated due to colonisation. For instance, Hori's concerns about the negativ stereotyping of Maori people do not stop with a lament; he actively uses digital technology to counteract this preju dice. Furthermore, he intentionally uses his company as a platform for advancing Maori culture. For Maru, the dis covery of how prevalent mental health issues were among Maori youth proved to be an epiphany that signalled a new direction on her game development. Since then, she prioritised developing online games that were meaningfu and inspiring for Maori children and teenagers. In the case of Potaua, his perspectives have been shaped by hi understanding of the colonial and more recently neoliberal impacts on his whanau and Maori more broadly. He is adamant about looking to the future and ensuring Maori control over the expression of Maori culture in the digita realm. He does not want the next generation of Maori ‘be invisible [again]’.

We observe that while our participants could easily operate in a Western environment, they refuse to be invisible and actively bring their cultural background to the forefront. In developing digital artefacts, they re-affirm thei Maori identity and exercise the ‘defense of the local as prerequisite to engaging with the global (Escobar, 2012, p. 212).

## 6 | DISCUSSION

This research sought to understand how the worldviews of Maori IT professionals shape their work experiences and their approaches towards the development of digital artefacts. In doing so, this research serves to augment the dearth of literature that considers the experiences of IT professionals from ethnic minorities or non-Western populations (Cain & Trauth, 2017; Joshi et al., 2017). The three narratives presented in the previou section showed that each of our participants had very different trajectories: Hori has steadily pursued his path of being a digital design expert with Maori roots; Maru made a transition from being a designer of culturally agnosti games to a Maori-centred game developer and Potaua has consistently sought different avenues for his social activ ism, cumulating in his current community ICT-based entrepreneurship and training enterprise. However, despit these differences, their experiences were aligned with their Indigenous values, morals and traditions (Craven et al., 2016).

Our findings confirm how culture, through the assembling of cultural resources (Swidler, 1986), can impact arte fact design (Hidding, 1998; Leidner & Kayworth, 2006; Shore & Venkatachalam, 1995). Each of our participants highlighted the Maori worldview by articulating various Maori values that shape their perspectives and artefact design. While the participants identified some limitations of the structures and rules that govern the production of digital artefacts, they do not let these restrictions limit the possibilities of Indigenous representation. Instead, because Maori culture is dynamic and has changed and adapted as necessary to the colonial context of Aotearoa (Mead, 2016), they view Indigenous culture as being active and performative. It derives, produces and renews it meaning through its practice in situ (Nakata et al., 2014).

Instead of being confined to a portentous colonial past, a situation which still affects many Indigenous groups, our participants enact the affordances of digital technology to create a space for cultural re-presentation. Our partic ipants seek to legitimate their Maori culture through digital artefacts and tell the ‘narratives that [they] want to experience and tell, about themselves and about others (Pentland, 1999, p. 8). In demonstrating the re-presentationa acts by our participants, we also shift from identifying how culture influences IT, to how IT and culture are inter twined. Winschiers-Theophilus et al. (2012) reflect: ‘As [Indigenous] knowledge and rituals, themselves are continu ously adapted to new circumstances, technology is yet just another medium contributing to the transformation of representation’ (p. 459). Whereas the creation of digitised artefacts itself constitutes a new construction, rather than merely a cultural manifestation translated into a new digital locale, these artefacts contribute to maintaining Indige nous culture alive in digital form (Csoba DeHass & Taitt, 2018; Wachowich, 2018).

## 6.1 | The intertwining of Indigenous re-presentation and re-affirmation

By having a presence in a digital environment, Indigenous Peoples contribute to modifying it to express their cultura identity (Landzelius, 2006). Moreover, in the contemporary context of high mobility and globalisation, digita technology can support cultural re-affirmation by ‘bringing Indigenous communities together with each other and with the wider world’ (Gish Hill, 2018, p. 68). Each act of digital re-presentation serves to create space for broader participation and cultural re-affirmation. As Gish Hill and Csoba DeHass (2018) forcefully assert, ‘the right of self representation is an expression of sovereignty’ (p. 44)

Instead of contributing to the development of digital artefacts that reinforce the dominance of ‘mainstream worldviews (or merely being passive recipients of alien content), we observe Maori IT professionals vigorously engaged in taking control of their own narratives in the digital realm. Our participants strive to take ownership of dig ital objects (Whaanga et al., 2015) and define their own rendition in the digital arena (Kamira, 2000). Thus, the devel opment of digital artefacts with Maori worldviews is for re-presenting cultural elements and re-affirming thei identity in the digital space. Figure 5 depicts the three Maori values – whakapapa, tikanga and tino rangatiratanga – that our participants, as cultural bricoleurs, combine in their IT professional practice to re-present their culture and re-affirm their Maori identity.

The relationship between re-presentation and re-affirmation constitutes an ongoing cycle where both plots complement and reinforce each other. Both plots are similar in that they are underpinned by an awareness of Maor culture but are different in that they operate at different levels of analysis: re-presentation at the artefact level and re-affirmation at the community level. Re-presentation entails rectifying the cultural imbalance in the digital medium which is largely saturated by Western content. Constructing new, culturally informed digital creations (re-presenta tion) requires confidence to foist such new creations onto the mainstream. This belief in oneself and the desire to legitimise one's presence as an Indigenous professional in mainstream society (re-affirmation) is manifested in encouraging the promotion of Maori worldviews in activities where technology may or may not be centrally embedded, such as branding campaigns, community health centres or video games. The more often such worldviews are seen, the more accepted they will be, encouraging further attempts among the Indigenous community to move beyond preservation and claim sovereignty of Indigenous Peoples. As re-presentation is pursued, the perception of Indigenous cultures as being dormant and passive is replaced by an understanding of its dynamics, which supports attempts to restore the dignity of Indigenous Peoples in mainstream society

## 6.2 | Theoretical contribution

This research contributes to knowledge in three ways. Firstly, this work contributes empirically to the literature on culture and IT professionals. It goes beyond an IT professional identity-centric or an ethnic identity-centric view espoused in previous studies to reveal how Indigenous IT professionals actively draw from their culture as resources (Swidler, 1986) to enact their professional activities, particularly artefact development. In this way, this work counterbalances the overwhelming dominance of the US-centric perspective that has cast the non-Western or Indigenou to the margins (Jack et al., 2013; Ruwhiu & Elkin, 2016), while highlighting the uniqueness of a cultural group (i.e. Maori) and avoiding the trap of conflating experiences from multiple minority groups in a ‘one-size-fits-all’ type of study (Tapia & Kvasny, 2004). Our study also diverges from the majority of existing studies that focus on the view of culture as values by examining the influence of national culture (Choi et al., 2005; Clemmensen, 2012; Couger, 1986; Hidding, 1998; Kankanhalli et al., 2004; Kumar & Bjørn-Andersen, 1990; Reinecke & Bernstein, 2013; Shore & Venkatachalam, 1995; Slaughter & Ang, 1995) to examine the influence of Indigenous culture within a settler-colonial context. We agree that to avoid the ‘institutionalisation’ of practices that ignore non-Western constructs and populations, more diversity is required in both IS research populations and theory (Davison & Martinsons, 2016). Consequently, we assert that any rigorous investigation of groups that sit outside the typica Western populations such as that provided here will make valid contributions to the IS literature.

Secondly, the narratives we present here provide meaningful accounts of Maori IT professional experiences that are unique and not fully replicable (Bruner, 1986). Their stories represent knowledge that explains events and actions that contribute to their progression as IT professionals and their approach to digital artefact development. Thes stories provide important explanations for us to understand better why Maori IT professionals take actions the wa they do. We elucidate the essence of being an Indigenous IT professional involved in developing digital artefacts to re-present and re-affirm their culture in the digital realm. It is important to recognise that the way this elucidation has been produced is in line with Indigenous cultures' knowledge system that explains the social world couched within a particular cultural narrative (Purcell, 1998)

Thirdly, our research corroborates that the post-colonial context cannot be ignored in research that involves societies that have been colonised, especially those with Indigenous Peoples (Lin et al., 2015; Ravishankar et al., 2013; Young, 2018). Not paying attention to a country's colonial experiences is likely to put researchers at risk of missing insights into the phenomena being studied. In contrast to previous studies that focus on how digital tech nology has been used to increase the dominance of Western values (Alcaraz et al., 2012), our study demonstrate how Indigenous IT professionals develop digital artefacts for Indigenous People with Indigenous knowledge – that is, re-presentation – to rectify power imbalances – that is, re-affirmation. These changes have enabled subordinated, colonised groups to circumvent the rules of the game in the IT industry, which is dominated by corporations from colonising countries, such as the USA and UK

## 6.3 | Practical implications

Our study's findings bring to the fore the typically unnoticed activities – at least for the mainstream IT field – per formed by Indigenous IT professionals. Far from taking the role of ‘passive users’ of digital technology developed in a non-Indigenous context, our participants intentionally engage in the development of digital artefacts in a way that is aligned with their culture. Their actions constitute proof that it is possible to maintain and promote cultura authenticity in IT development projects. Our participants' stories offer three specific practical lessons. Firstly, they involve other members of the Maori community in their projects in the form of internships or IT training. Othe Indigenous IT professionals may consider taking a similar nurturing path to promote their culture to reinforce thei cultural ties in the digital realm. Secondly, our participants deliberately take an inquisitive approach to understan the Indigenous traditions and the history of Indigenous places and people, which are subsequently incorporated into their artefacts. Indigenous IT professionals or ‘mainstream’ IT firms working with Indigenous communities can adop a similar approach to develop digital artefacts that genuinely characterise Indigenous culture.

Thirdly, and as a consequence of the previous points, locally specific Indigenous customs and processes should inform the operational aspects of such initiatives. For example, when working with Maori artefacts (digital or physical) Maori concepts and principles should always be discussed. Also, some processes are expected when working in collabo ration with Indigenous groups to determine the project's plans and objectives and acknowledge the responsibility to present content authentically. While the outcomes of the project might not be specific to an Indigenous community, they should still be aligned with principles and values central to the Indigenous community. In the context of Aotearoa one example would be incorporating kaitiakitanga when considering outcomes (Kamira, 2003a). Traditionally, IT projects emphasise minimising the risk and maximising the return to the specific organisation funding the project. This short term and narrowly focussed objective contrasts with kaitiakitanga, where the goal is to protect and grow resources fo future generations. As IT is used more extensively in society and has impacts beyond the immediate developer and user adopting a kaitiakitanga perspective when considering project outcomes may raise important questions such as: From whose perspective are returns and risks being examined? Over what timeline are these outcomes being considered? What criteria are being used to judge value and risk? Asking such questions will encourage project funders to reflect on the potential contributions of the projects to Indigenous communities in the present and the future

## 6.4 | Future research avenues

We suggest some possibilities for future research. Firstly, our participants were Maori IT professionals who chose to become entrepreneurs. However, we posit that this paper's findings are also relevant to Indigenous IT professional who are not self-employed. There are two aspects to this argument. The first is that IT careers are the prototypica model for boundaryless careers (Arthur & Rousseau, 2001); being self-employed is a common aspect of many IT profes sionals' careers, especially with the growth of platforms such as Github. As Indigenous IT professionals traverse between being self-employed and being organisationally employed, it is possible that the impact of their worldview and principles on their approaches to developing artefacts and the type of artefacts they choose to be involved in varie in its extent across their careers. We suggest future studies extend to Indigenous IT professionals who are part of non-Indigenous organisation to understand how that context may play a role in their experiences and the digital arte facts that they develop or co-develop. The second aspect of this argument is that while such individuals usually work on projects assigned by their employers and will thus be less likely to engage in re-presenting Indigenous culture digitally, we expect that their worldviews and principles will influence how they do their work and which elements they emphasise when they carry out their work (Staniland et al., 2019, 2020), thus re-affirming the identity of their mar ginalised Indigenous community. For example, they could abide by customary Indigenous practices when meeting others, put forward Indigenous perspectives when designing artefacts, advocate for including Indigenous cultural ele ments in designs and encourage the hiring of Indigenous colleagues and vendors. Thus, the applicability of the paper' findings to Indigenous IT professionals who are not self-employed is not a boundary condition but an empirical issue.

Secondly, future research could assess IT projects that are driven and involve collaboration between Indigenous and non-Indigenous groups as well as projects in which multiple Indigenous groups participate. It is important to emphasise that research with Indigenous groups should make careful choices of theories and methodologies that are commensurate with Indigenous contexts. We suggest that further research could use narrative inquiry to produc research texts using different narrative forms such as metaphors and visual narratives as well as producing narrative in Indigenous languages to provide other explanatory mechanisms to deepen our understanding of Indigenous IT professional experiences. For example, some Maori researchers acknowledge that the translation of Maori value into Western concepts is akin to defining the undefinable (Haar et al., 2019; Spiller et al., 2011). In other words, sev eral Maori values such as tino rangatiratanga as expressed in Te Reo have a much deeper and multi-faceted meaning than an English translation of collective cultural determination and self-determination. Therefore, if one can represent IT professional narratives in Indigenous languages such as Te Reo, one can preserve these values' richness and multi-facetedness to advance knowledge from an Indigenous perspective.

## 7 | CONCLUSIONS

While this paper theorises how cultural values influence the approaches and experiences of Maori IT professionals we would like to place greater emphasis on the relevance of these findings, as we believe this is where there is potential for real change in the IT profession. The findings of this study have direct relevance for attempts to enhance Indigenous representation in the IT workforce, as well as for designing digital artefacts that are relevant and inclusive for Indigenous communities. Without the unique experiences, perspectives and values of Indigenous Peo ples, the IT profession risks excluding Indigenous communities from digital spaces and the opportunities digita mediums provide. Ultimately, by demonstrating how cultural values influence participants' approaches to work an design, we seek to encourage better engagement with Indigenous communities and users

## APPENDIX B.: METHODOLOGICAL CONSIDERATIONS IN NARRATIVE STUDIES

<table><tr><td>Methodological considerations</td><td>Our study</td></tr><tr><td>Narrative inquiry occurs within the relationship between researchers and participants best described as a caring community (Clandinin &amp; Connelly, 2000)</td><td>We made sure that participants feel a sense of equality by using greetings in Māori ('kia ora') and encouraging them to speak openly about their experiences. For the second interview, three of the authors spent a few days with Potaua. He invited us to visit places of cultural significance to learn more about Māori history in Rotorua. We also participated in Māori tikanga, such as prayers before meetings and hongi greetings.</td></tr><tr><td>All participants have a voice within the relationship (Clandinin &amp; Connelly, 2000)</td><td>We listened to the participants' stories without interrupting them. Some of our interviews took a whole day, a few hours in the morning, followed by more conversations in the afternoon. Participants showed us their workplaces and introduced us warmly to their colleagues and, in one case, to his family members.</td></tr><tr><td>Different data collection methods are possible as the researcher and participants work together in acollaborative relationship (Clandinin &amp; Connelly, 2000)</td><td>Besides the two rounds of interviews with each of our participants, we got insights into their digital artefacts. Hori shared his digital designs with us; Maru shared the pre-launch and later on the launch version of her interactive fiction adventure game, Guardian and Potauashowed us his touchscreen work in the Rotorua public library. We primarily used textual data from the interviews with the support from visual data from participants' work in our analysis.</td></tr><tr><td>Building on the assumption that human actions are unique (Bruner, 1986), narrative analysis uses narrative reasoning to produce knowledge that focusses on the particular characteristics of each action while maintaining the meaning of the story (Polkinghorne, 1995)</td><td>Although we identified the same Māori cultural values that all three participants embrace in their IT professional being and doing, we presented narratives with their unique life events, actions and individual interpretations of these same Māori values.</td></tr><tr><td>Although there are no universally agreed criteria to evaluate narrative inquiry, plausibility and understandability can be used as criteria to judge the adequacy of the analysis (Polkinghorne, 1995, 2007)</td><td>Our narrative writing and emplotment process began with the outcome or who these IT professionals have become. Maru is now a game developer who prefers to develop games embedded with purpose and meaning. Potaua is a digital entrepreneur and activist who takes pride in his Māori heritage and looks for projects that benefit younger generations of Māori. Hori is a digital media designer who traverses comfortably in a mainstream commercial practice while embracing his Māori heritage in his design. The three narratives that we developed are a series of events and actions that contribute to characterising these individuals' professional trajectories. Similarly, the re-presentation and re-affirmation plots and their subplots explain how the participants variously assemble the three Māori values to construct strategies of action and re-present these values in digital artefact to reclaim their culture and restore Māori identity.</td></tr></table>
