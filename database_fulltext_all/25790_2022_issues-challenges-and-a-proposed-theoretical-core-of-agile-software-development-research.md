## Abstract

Over the last two decades, agile software development (ASD) has garnered much attention in both research and practice. Several ASD methods and techniques have been developed and studied. In particular, researchers have provided several theoretical perspectives on ASD and contributed rich insights to the ASD practice. Still, despite calls for a more unified theoretical understanding of ASD, a theoretical core of ASD has not been identified. This paper offers a theoretical core of ASD research, clarifying what is essential and what is less essential for IS agility, hoping to spark a scholarly discussion, and provides implications of such a core for understanding method tailoring.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Since the advent of the Manifesto for Agile Software Development (Manifesto) in 2001 (Beck et al., 2001), agile soft ware development (ASD) has gained widespread use in the software development industry (Bustard, 2012; Surendra & Nazir, 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2020; West et al., 2010). Although the Manifesto espouses values and prin ciples of ASD, different perspectives persist concerning what it means to be “agile”, leading to conceptual ambiguity which poses challenges for conducting and reviewing ASD research. One particular challenge for ASD researchers in Information Systems (IS) has been satisfying the field's thirst for theory (Burton-Jones et al., 2015; Gregor, 2006; Markus & Saunders, 2007; Weber, 2012) while seeking to understand a phenomenon that was born from and con tinues to be nurtured in practice. This issue particularly salient in the premier IS journals, which place heavy emphasis on theoretical contribution. While early ASD research struggled to keep up with practice and was criticised for its lack of theoretical underpinnings and empirical support (Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2008), over the last decade, numerous empirical studies have been conducted that apply theoretical lenses (e.g., control theory, complex adaptive systems) to examine various aspects of ASD (Harris et al., 2009; Strode et al., 2012; Vidgen & Wang, 2009). In previou attempts to provide greater theoretical support, scholars' varied interests in ASD research have contributed to diverse views of the phenomenon (Conboy & Duarte, 2010; Ghobadi & Mathiassen, 2017; Wang et al., 2012) Despite the many contributions of past studies, studies vary widely in how they define, measure and report ASD use, which poses significant difficulty for future ASD research within the IS field. Thus, ASD research is still said to lack a theoretical core (Diegmann et al., 2018; Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2008; Yu & Petter, 2014), or a unified way of understanding ASD across various methods. Overall, past studies employ a miscellany of existing theories that tap into dif ferent aspects of ASD, but none inherently capture the unique characteristics of ASD by being derived from agil principles and practices themselves. Such a theoretical core has not been proposed explicitly, but is needed to cap ture the central aspects of ASD in a coherent manner.

Although recent studies provide recommendations (such as describing and measuring team and environmenta characteristics) for increasing the rigour of ASD research in the absence of a theoretical core, in this study, we pro pose such a core to clarify what it means to be “agile” and help researchers to theorise more centrally to ASD. Thi paper aims to highlight major issues facing ASD research in IS, discuss common approaches of increasing rigour in I research, and propose a theoretical core that can be debated, refined and used in future research.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 provides a brief overview of ASD research in IS. Section 3 highlights the major issues facing ASD researchers. Section 4 discusses approaches for improving ASD research using traditional theoretization. Section 5 proposes a theoretical core. Section 6 concludes the paper by dis cussing some theoretical and practical implications.

## 2 | ASD RESEARCH IN IS

ASD originates from practice where a group of software developers addressed the changing needs of the software development practice with the publication of the Manifesto for Agile Software Development (Beck et al., 2001). Since the advent of the Manifesto in 2001, ASD has gained widespread use in the software development industry and garnered significant attention in both research and practice (Surendra & Nazir, 2019; West et al., 2010). The Manifesto proposes a better way of developing software in four foundation statements that place a higher value on individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration and responding to change than aspects of waterfal methods (Beck et al., 2001). ASD methods (e.g., Scrum, eXtreme programming [XP]) refer to approaches to systems development that share the basic tenets of the Manifesto, and ASD techniques (e.g., paired programming and sprint planning) are the sub-practices of ASD methods (Abrahamsson et al., 2003; Cohen et al., 2004; Iivari & Iivari, 2011) The growth of ASD has led to several methods and frameworks, extensions (e.g., SaFE), professional organisation (e.g., Agile Alliance, Scrum Alliance), conferences (e.g., Agile conference), and various research efforts. The sustained interest in this topic is reflected in practice along with IS most notably in conference proceedings (i.e., tracks at AMCIS, ICIS and HICSS) and software engineering (SE) publication outlets. Recent studies highlight the shift in practitioner's interest from employing to sustaining ASD use (Barroca et al., 2018; Gregory et al., 2016; Senapathi & Drury-Grogan, 2020), organisation-wide transformations (Conboy & Carroll, 2019; Paasivaara et al., 2018), and over coming issues in globally distributed environments (Calefato et al., 2019; Shrivastava & Rathod, 2017). Additionally recent surveys indicate that ASD adoption rates continue to increase as organisations employ ASD methods to a higher percentage of their overall software projects (Version One, 2018, 2019)

The rapid growth of the ASD practice has challenged the IS research community to keep pace. Initial ASD research provided overviews of ASD methods that describe what ASD is and how it should be applied (Beck, 1999 Highsmith, 2002; Schwaber, 2004) including some empirical results from practice (Merisalo-Rantanen et al., 2005; Sharp & Robinson, 2004). However, many early studies were criticised for lacking theoretical underpinnings an empirical support (Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2008). After calls for more rigorous academic research (Abrahamsson et al., 2009; Dingsøyr et al., 2012), numerous empirical studies have been conducted (i.e., mostly case study and fiel survey methods) (Huber et al., 2020; Kude et al., 2019; Maruping & Matook, 2020). These studies, some of whic applied theoretical lenses to examine ASD, have helped understand how practitioners perceive, deploy and deal with challenges concerning ASD methods. Yet, if one looks at the premier IS journals from 2010 to 2020, ASD studie make up less than 1% of the total annual publications in the majority of the Senior Scholars' Basket of 8 journal (MISQ 0.56%, ISR 0.45%, JMIS 0.58%, JAIS 0.69%, EJIS 1.02%, ISJ 2.94%, JSIS 0.54% and JIT 0.30%). Given the IS field's foundation of applied computer science (CS) in organisations and ASD's combination of social and technica software development techniques, it is rather surprising that the IS community has published far less work on ASD than software engineering and computer science journals (Diegmann et al., 2018; Hummel, 2014). The number of recent ASD studies in IS journals is particularly low in the top tier journals where theoretical contribution is emphasised (Diegmann et al., 2018; Hummel, 2014). For example, between 2017 and 2020 11 ASD studies wer published in the Senior Scholars' Basket of 8 versus 291 ASD studies published in two SE Journals, JS&S and IS&T, as shown in Figure 1.

Given the sustained interest in ASD research and practice, it begs the question, why have so few ASD studies been published in premier IS journals?

## 3 | THE ISSUE OF A MISSING THEORETICAL CORE

Although there could be several reasons for such low numbers of ASD papers in premier IS journals such as th field's interest in a plethora of other IS topics, limited journal space and lengthy journal review cycles, we believe that the premier journals' emphasis on theoretical contribution (Hirschheim, 2019) along with the absence of an ASD theoretical core seems to be of particular importance. Consequently, current agile research faces several conceptua challenges, which we review in this section.

## 3.1 | Challenges of identity

There is little doubt that prior research has contributed helpful insights into understanding the ASD phenomenon suc as highlighting motivations for ASD adoption in organisations (Cram & Newell, 2016; Vijayasarathy & Turk, 2008) and types of adaptations needed during ASD implementations (Cao et al., 2009). Still, after nearly two decades of research and practice, what constitutes “agile” theoretically is said to be unclear (Rathor et al., 2016; Vial & Rivard, 2015) as it has been conceptualised many different ways (Abrahamsson et al., 2009; Highsmith, 2002; Lee & Xia, 2010; Lyytinen & Rose, 2006; Sarker & Sarker, 2009). Definitions and conceptualizations of “agile” (e.g., “ASD”, “IS development agility” and related terms) range from broad notions such as a software development team's ability to respond to and incorporate changes (Highsmith, 2002; Lee & Xia, 2010), to adherence to the values of the Mani festo and (the whole or a set of) its principles (Agile Alliance, 2020; Beck et al., 2001), to the more specific adoptio of one or more agile methods or techniques (Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2010; Wang et al., 2012). Despite prior attempt to better define agility in software development (Abrahamsson et al., 2002; Conboy, 2009; Hummel, 2014) and expand theoretical insights, questions of what is considered agile and why it is considered agile, have not been suf ficiently answered (Rathor et al., 2016; Vial & Rivard, 2015). Although scholars agree that the Manifesto and its 12 principles have been instrumental in shaping how ASD is thought about and practiced, a key limitation of the Manifesto and its 12 Principles is that they are not formal definitions of IS development agility, but rather value statements and guidelines for ASD delivery, respectively (Dingsøyr et al., 2012; Strode, 2005). The Manifesto and its 12 principles are guidelines that allow for adherents to develop ways of developing software from a set of common values (Agile Alliance, 2020). Scholars argue that the Manifesto has not been sufficient in functioning as a theoretical core as th 12 principles do not apply to all methods, and the current ASD practice has extended these principles in several way (e.g., globally distributed versus face-to-face development environments). ## 3.2 | A unified understanding

A theoretical core should constitute what it means to be “agile", articulating a clear understanding of the centra aspects of ASD that can be found across all of the commonly acknowledged ASD methods (Strode, 2005). Such theoretical core would provide a more unified way of understanding ASD. Conversely, the lack of unified theoretica understanding of ASD has resulted in different views of the phenomenon with some researchers confidentl asserting their own understanding of what “agile” means, which often differs from other conceptualizations of ASD (Wang et al., 2012). In practice, we can observe many methods and techniques that are classified under the umbrella of agile , even though these varied approaches differ in significant ways (see section 5.3). With so many different views of agile , a theoretical core would help bring a more coherent and unified understanding of what agile is

## 3.3 | Challenges in evaluating knowledge claims

The lack of a common understanding of ASD leads to further challenges in conducting research and evaluating knowledge claims. Researchers are challenged with attempting to convince readers that the phenomenon that the are studying is really 00agile or constantly providing evidence of its differences from waterfall methods without a uni versally accepted way to report and evaluate such claims. Since what constitutes an “agile” method is not clear (i.e., has not been established theoretically), would-be researchers are subject to questions such as how do we know you are studying ASD and not another method, even when studies report the whole or partial use of well-recognised

ASD frameworks and methods like Scrum and XP. Thus, ASD researchers find themselves in a quandary of examining a practice-based phenomenon, yet having to demonstrate its theoretical value without a core theory or a unified understanding of the phenomenon. In the next sections, we describe common approaches to improving the theoreti cal contribution in agile research within IS and discuss how the identification of a theoretical core that is informed b practice can be helpful for ASD research.

## 4 | THE COMMON CONCEPTUAL APPARATUS FOR THEORISING ASD

Previous research calls for the adoption of rigorous theoretical ASD research. Efforts to improve rigour in IS research often employ the common conceptual apparatus for theoritization found in positivist research (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Gregor, 2006), which prioritises definitions, categorizations and models of explanation and prediction, and aims to define the boundary conditions of the theory. Under the status quo, to achieve a more mature state of theory, ASD studies must provide focused measures and new, or specific, theoretical contributions (Edmondson & McManus, 2007). Recent recommendations echo this sentiment (Tripp et al., 2018). Thus, we treat these recommen dations as those provided in the spirit of improving the quality of ASD research and those that have been and ma currently be used to evaluate ASD research submitted to premier IS journals (Liu & Myers, 2011), which tend to pri oritise rigorous theoretical research using the common conceptual apparatus. Our analysis of these recommenda tions for improving ASD research finds both promise and potential unintended consequences that could limit th publishability of some ASD research, particularly that which captures ASD in a real-life context. As such, although we acknowledge the conceptual challenges that have resulted from a lack of theoretical core in ASD research, we feel it necessary to comment on current recommendations that line up with the common conceptual apparatus in the attempt to broaden the discussion concerning how practice-based phenomenon such as ASD can be con ceptualised and theorised in IS research

## 4.1 | Measuring ASD techniques and their project context

Recent studies argue that ASD is a multidimension rather than monolithic construct with researchers describin dimensions of agility (Batra et al., 2016; Lee & Xia, 2010; Rathor et al., 2016) and the extensiveness of ASD use (Senapathi & Drury-Grogan, 2017; Senapathi & Srinivasan, 2014). Therefore, some scholars suggest that ASD researchers improve the scientific rigour of their work by specifying what is meant by ASD use rather than simpl stating that they studied “agile” methods, and providing empirical support for such ASD use (Maruping et al., 2009; Recker et al., 2017; Tripp et al., 2016). For instance, some studies measure the use of specific ASD techniques during software development projects and the factors that control the influence of such techniques (Balijepally et al., 2009; Maruping et al., 2009; Venkatesh et al., 2020). Scholars also stress the importance of understanding ASD's project context and rigorously examining if, how, why and when ASD impacts outcomes in order to strengthen our theoreti cal understanding of ASD (Fruhling & De Vreede 2006; Lee & Xia, 2010; Moe et al., 2008). While we agree that mea suring the specific level of ASD use and its project context is warranted, some studies advocate for th measurement of techniques over methods due to their claim that few teams adopt ASD methods as a whole (Gren & Lenberg, 2020).

Indeed, one might argue to those that advocate for measuring the use of an ASD method that a given ASD method can be measured as the sum of its individual techniques. However, while describing and measuring ASD use, its impact on and how it is impacted by specific organisational characteristics is important for improving rigour, on must be careful to avoid the pitfalls of reductionism for several reasons (Sarker & Valacich, 2010). First, decomposing ASD methods such as XP into a list of technigues misses that the technigues are said to be a “system of practices (Highsmith, 2010, p. 86), reinforcing and tightly coupled (Auer & Miller, 2001; Beck, 1999; Martin, 2002; Stephens &

Rosenberg, 2003). Because the most widely used ASD techniques (Version One, 2019) are those introduced as sub components of a specific method (e.g., unit testing from XP), an understanding of how different ASD techniques relate with one another is needed, especially concerning the method from which they were introduced. The interdependence of ASD techniques does not rule out method tailoring, but it seems to point to an underlying core that is necessary for IS development agility. This may also explain some practitioners' reticence to remove techniques from ASD methods due to the perceived embedded nature of these techniques and the uncertainty about the effect of removing some of them (Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2007). Further study is needed to articulate the underlying theoret ical core of ASD methods and understand the effects of removing specific techniques. We address these issues in Section 4.

Second, since the extant literature and practice show the presence of both wholesale and partial implementations of ASD, we do not adopt the view that agile should be studied as a list of individual techniques only (i.e., implemented a la carte), but rather as a way of organising and helping teams to anticipate, respond to, cre ate, and learn from user requirements changes, which undoubtedly utilises a set of ASD techniques. We define wholesale as the implementation of all the techniques of a given method in an “off the shelf” manner. Prior studies cogently point out that many organisations tailor ASD methods by selecting specific ASD techniques or combining methods (Cram, 2019; Fitzgerald et al., 2006; Ramasubbu et al., 2015) rather than employing an ASD method wholesale, yet other studies (Overhage & Schlauderer, 2012) and anecdotal evidence from practice (Case Studies, 2017; Scrum Case Studies, 2017) also point out that many organisations have implemented ASD methods wholesale. Give this evidence, it is questionable to claim that wholesale implementations are few<sup>1</sup> and that they should be excluded from the study criteria. Perhaps, the more concerning issue is not whether wholesale implementations are few, but the fact that they are being ignored by academic research. We argue that not only should wholesale implementations be accounted for, but that the inability to account for wholesale ASD implementations leads to a narrow context of study results and is not truly reflective of practice. Moreover, wholesale implementations rather than partial ones yield the greater potential impact of the method on the organisational constituents since it involves the least degre of method tailoring, inducing a greater degree of organisational change. Without accounting for wholesal implementations, ASD research will be approached, investigated, and reported on in a one-sided fashion. Moreover we contend that the burden should rest with the research community to understand and provide holistic insight about the phenomenon (as it occurs) not for the phenomenon to be shoehorned into a theoretical box

Overall, reductionist strategies may yield efficiencies that serve macro-level research such as surveying a large sample of respondents and regressing the use of ASD techniques onto theoretical outcomes, but these strategie often miss more context-rich research such as understanding issues surrounding wholesale ASD implementations in an organisational context. In reference to the agile phenomenon, which is a highly diverse, adaptable, contextual, sit uated practice, we encourage the IS field to consider recommendations for studying ASD that are not simply mallea ble to traditional theorization in academic research but how such approaches would benefit and reflect th phenomenon (actual practice).

## 4.2 | Theorising exclusively to ASD

Scholars advocate for a stronger connection between ASD and its theoretical base (Dingsøyr et al., 2012; Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2008; Highsmith, 2010). To strengthen our understanding of ASD concepts and their theoretical underpinnings, prior studies argue that researchers should seek to understand the conditions under which ASD is effective and the factors that can control its influence on outcomes through empirical examination (Lee & Xia, 2010). Such insights may represent part of the expected theoretical contribution coveted by premier IS journals. Yet, scholars dif fer in their understanding of what these relationships tell us about the core of ASD and what a theoretical contribu tion might entail.

While many identify the core of ASD as centred around agility (Conboy, 2009; Gren & Lenberg, 2020; Lee & Xia, 2010), or the ability to respond to change, and theorise based on this core, others suggest that an ASD core ha yet to be discovered and that traditional theorization will help researchers identify it. We find promise in the former approach as it leverages the strengths of the measurement recommendations previously discussed (i.e., measurin agility) by highlighting which aspects of ASD may impact outcomes in a given context. Concerning the latter view, Tripp et al. (2018, p. 5467) argue that there is an absence of a theoretical core in ASD research and as such researchers need to show why agile is different from other contexts [methods] and identify the boundary condition that make agile different . The authors believe that the theoretical outcomes that come as a result of illustrating the oretically how the unique nature of ASD (i.e., ASD techniques) influences outcomes in a specific context will help to identify the core of ASD. While we agree that identifying the boundaries of the applicability of a theory can be usefu to the validation of said theory, we hesitate to apply these arguments as standards for conducting and evaluating ASD research too strictly for several reasons:

First, although we will later argue for a theoretical core of ASD, as a growing and evolving practice, the bound aries for applying ASD have shown to be somewhat “fuzzy”. The notion of boundary conditions is rooted in realist ontological and positivist epistemological perspectives representing the extent to which the theory is generalizabl or holds across contexts (Whetten, 1989). Because theories in the social sciences are not applicable irrespective of context (Busse et al., 2017), we need to understand the perceived boundary conditions of the ASD practice throug several contextual factors. While some early applications of ASD were said to fit boundary conditions such as smal team sizes and highly dynamic environments (Boehm & Turner, 2003), recent ASD practice continues to push previ ously set boundaries so that their limits are fuzzy, or uncertain. In response to the needs of practice, ASD method have been extended beyond the scope of the 12 principles, including scaling ASD to fit large projects and moving beyond face-to-face communication to fit globally distributed teams. We echo Busse et al.'s (2017) sentiment for th need to consider boundary conditions along the salient dimensions while understanding that there will generally be at least some contextual conditions that have not yet been investigated and are difficult to anticipate. Buss et al. (2017, p. 582) explain,

…organizational reality is vibrant and dynamic, meaning that temporal contexts also change more or less continuously. In consequence, we must assume that no boundary conditions exist that are or can be fully known (Ketokivi & Jokinen, 2006).

Aiming for complete exploration of any theory's boundary conditions represents a life-long research program rather than the achievable content of a single manuscript.

We advocate for a broader use of theory to include a wider spectrum of theory development stages (e.g., nascent, immediate and mature, Edmondson & McManus, 2007). This broader notion of theory is important for providing new insights into a phenomenon like ASD that is widely used, yet still being newly adopted, adapted and extended as it continues to evolve as a practice. Our theoretization of ASD should accommodate both matur aspects of the practice that may be understood with boundary conditions that are perceived to hold across multipl contexts, and emergent or less understood aspects that may challenge existing boundaries. In doing so, theoretica contributions become less rigid and narrowly defined and focus on offering new perspectives or ways of understand ing a phenomenon that might include developing new theory or applying existing theory to understand aspects of ASD use (Leidner, 2020).

In our view, applying existing theory is valid if the theory is tailored to the ASD context and produces novel the oretical insights. These insights do not need to include comparisons to other methods (see next section), but should point out what parts of the existing theory hold in the ASD context and why. Researchers that apply existing theory would do well to consider the conditions that favour and disfavour a theory before applying it to ASD (Gray & Cooper, 2010).

Second, the differences between ASD and traditional methodologies have been thoroughly documented in the early ASD literature (Baskerville et al., 2003; Ceschi et al., 2005; Nerur & Balijepally, 2007). The values and principles articulated in the Manifesto, over 20 years of practice and thousands of research studies, bear out these differences Given the known differences between ASD and traditional methodologies, we believe this core has been largely uncovered, but needs to be formally articulated, adopted and perhaps refined for future academic research. As evi dence, even in the absence of a widely recognised and established theoretical core, the collective contributions of past research point to the link between theory and its underpinning to ASD. One common focus among these stud ies is responding to changing user requirements (Gren & Lenberg, 2020). Recent studies have moved beyond com paring ASD and traditional methods to understanding the complexities of a now mature ASD practice, which i reflected in multiple calls for papers (Abrahamsson et al., 2009; Dingsøyr et al., 2012; Dingsøyr & Lassenius, 2016). For instance, Drury-Grogan et al. (2017) examine decision characteristics and challenges for ASD. The exploratory case study involves interviews with ASD practitioners concerning particular decision characteristics across the fou values highlighted in the Manifesto. Even though the study is steeped in the ASD context (e.g., participants, literature), some could require that the authors argue for why ASD is different from traditional methods, which takes th focus away from pertinent ASD-related issues to continue to draw its distinction from traditional methodologies. In our view, applying this criterion too strictly is like “reinventing the wheel”, taking up valuable pages in our articles where the foundations of ASD are already well-documented

Third, Tripp et al. (2018) assert that until the theoretical core is identified, it will be difficult to apply what has been learned about ASD clearly to other areas. However, this has already been done in both research and practice (Baham et al., 2017; Pries-Heje et al., 2018; Sutherland & Altman, 2010). Thus, instead of arguing when, how, and why ASD methods and techniques will result in different theoretical outcomes vis a vis other methodologies to hel identify a theoretical core only, we suggest the opposite; drawing upon the Manifesto, 20 years of practice, and extensive research efforts to solidify a core in order to help explain when, how and why ASD methods and tech niques impact these theoretical outcomes.

Overall, we find that employing traditional theoretization in ASD research may produce some helpful insights, but, if applied too rigidly or as a means of discovering the core of ASD, could also lead to unintended consequences such as a perpetual search for “theory” (i.e., applying the common conceptual apparatus to discover theory) that is largely disconnected from practice. In our view, such an application is too narrow (i.e., reducing techniques down to lists of variables to predict outcomes), and misses the nuance of how organisations actually use ASD in systems development. We believe that the IS field needs to resolve the tension between theoretical contribution and practi cal relevance for understanding IS phenomenon like ASD that originate from and are driven by practice, and are stil evolving. Otherwise, in the case of ASD, the field will lack insights concerning the principal approach for system development, ironically in which case, systems development is foundational to the IS discipline. In the next section we propose a core for ASD research that is theoretically sound and practically relevant.

## 5 | PROPOSED SOLUTION: FORMULATING A THEORETICAL CORE

We contend that the most expedient way to overcome challenges in theorising ASD is first to develop a theoretica core that is informed by practice. Such a core is useful for both research and practice because it can help us bette define what agile is (or means), measure it, and evaluate related knowledge claims. Moreover, a theoretical core that is derived from the agile principles and practices themselves can help researchers to theorise more centrally to ASD. Indeed, how ASD is defined affects how it is understood, studied, practiced, and differentiated from related concepts. By establishing a common theoretical understanding of ASD, we can reduce fragmentation in the way ASD is conceptualised and ground future research. We believe that the identification of a theoretical core can play a critica role in addressing the challenges listed in Section 3 and in turn, address the relatively low number of ASD studies in the premier IS journals that may be due in part to concerns about a lack of rigour and theoretical differentiation and contribution. We advocate for theoretization with a greater focus on practice rather than the common conceptua apparatus. Therefore, to develop a theoretical core, we draw upon existing knowledge of ASD (i.e., the values an principles found in the Manifesto, ASD methods, and the extant literature) to identify some core theoretical concepts that have collectively helped to differentiate ASD from other IS development methods

## 5.1 | ASD foundations

## 5.1.1 | ASD methods: Operationalization of ASD values and principles

Unlike traditional project management approaches with more rigid and prescriptive rules, the evolved diversity of ASD methods, techniques, and frameworks share underlying principles (codified in the Manifesto) which help to cou ple the variety. However, as previously mentioned, the 12 principles do not apply to all methods, and the curren ASD practice has extended these principles in several ways. ASD methods are said to share the values espoused in the Manifesto (Agile Alliance, 2020), and the majority of ASD techniques are derived from ASD methods. Thus, we observe that ASD methods and their accompanying techniques effectively demonstrate how ASD has been operationalised in practice. Because a theoretical core should cut across virtually all ASD methods (Strode, 2005), widel recognised ASD methods can play a key role in identifying core concepts of ASD in a coherent manner. In addition the extant literature offers several definitions for ASD and related concepts. We review some of these definition for core concepts that we can then compare to ASD methods

Conboy (2009) extends this notion of agility by highlighting “continual readiness” as an aspect of agility, which relates to sensing (Li et al., 2010) and anticipating that further distinguishes it from related concepts of leanness and flexibility. Conboy (2009) and others also emphasise “learning from change” as a key aspect of IS development agility (Lyytinen & Rose, 2006; Nerur & Balijepally, 2007; Rathor et al., 2016). In the software development context, ther can be various changes with which teams may have to deal (e.g., human resources, technical resources) (Ratho et al., 2016), but the most fundamental change that differentiates ASD from traditional software development methods is that of changing user or customer requirements. Taken together, we can refer to ASD – in the context of complex organisations where the goal is to deliver working software to a customer – as a software development team's ability to anticipate, create, learn from and respond to changes in user requirements through a process of continua readiness.

## 5.2 | Core theoretical concepts

Historically, ASD emerged as a response to the document-driven and heavily regimented procedures of traditiona approaches, which were seen as inadequate for meeting the needs of Internet-speed development. Challenges to the software development practice such as software team environmental uncertainty, changing user requirements, and the subjectivity in interpreting user requirements increased the complexity of the software building process and spawned the need for IS development agility. Compared to traditional software development methods, ASD defini tions (see Table 1) show that ASD uniquely prioritises anticipating, creating, learning from and responding to changes in user requirements. We examined reviews of ASD methods (e.g., Abrahamsson et al., 2002; Cohen et al., 2004) and methodology guides (e.g., Beck, 1999; Sutherland & Schwaber, 2016) to identify the most basic, parsimonious, and a common set of concepts found across virtually all ASD methods that enable agility (i.e., anticipate, create, learn from and respond to change) in the software development context. Appendix A details how we arrived at the final set of concepts. Similar to the Crystal ASD method (Cockburn, 2004), we examined ASD practice and attempted to distin guish between mandatory and non-mandatory concepts. Mandatory concepts are viewed as ASD enablers that were critical to achieving agility and, if removed, would hinder any team's ability to anticipate, create, learn from, and respond to change. Non-mandatory concepts are viewed as ASD enablers that, while helpful if removed, do not immediately prevent a team's ability to anticipate, create, learn from, and respond to change. Upon examination, w can observe specific concepts that relate to all ASD approaches

We find that the hallmark of ASD methods – the ability to anticipate, create, learn from and respond to user requirements changes – can be (and has been) accomplished in several ways, but most commonly through the fou core concepts<sup>2</sup> of: (1) incremental design and iterative development, (2) inspect and adapt cycles, (3) continuous customer involvement and (4) working cooperatively/collaboratively/in close communication. Our proposed theo retical core is meant to bring clarity not serve a dogma. We recognise the command and control mechanisms that the agile signatories were attempting to escape. At the same, the evolving ASD practice should be able to specif what “agile” means, how it can be achieved, and demonstrate ASD's business value (e.g., favourable impact on pro ject outcomes) and comparative advantages over alternative systems development methods. Thus, what we pro pose is not a traditional “theory”, but an attempt to capture the central concepts of ASD in a coherent manner These concepts do not take away from the natural outcome of a flexible, adaptable and human-oriented practice Instead, they ground IS agility, so that we can better understand it as it evolves. We discuss each of these core concepts and explain why these four were identified among other notable ASD concepts. We first discuss concept 1 and 2 together since they are inextricably linked to one another. We next discuss concepts 3 and 4 together sinc they too are highly linked.

## 5.2.1 | Incremental design and iterative development (ID2) and inspect and adapt cycles (IA)

The most fundamental and universal approach to achieving agility was to breakdown down the development process and code delivery into small increments. In doing so, ASD methods challenge the assumption that software require ments can be fully defined upfront using a traditional, sequential approach. By taking an incremental approach, ASD teams relax the assumptions of traditional forms of design that rely on upfront, blueprint style planning (Faludi, 1973), and instead, focus on understanding requirements as the software is being built to deal with change Through iterative development, ASD teams build and deliver software in small increments, allowing the team to solicit feedback and course-correct as they gain new knowledge. The feedback opportunities afforded through itera tive development assist software teams in understanding past issues, sensing current issues and responding to future issues (Rathor et al., 2016). Additionally, short iterations lessen feedback loops, reduce rework and enable software teams to keep pace with the rate of change (Vidgen & Wang, 2009). As a result, software teams can spend less tim trying to interpret user requirements (i.e., scope) and spend more time producing working software, thereby reducing time to market and overall cost. Both incremental design and iterative development were found in virtually ever ASD method that we examined, are in line with the values of the Manifesto, and are referenced explicitly in the 12 principles.

The iterations described above are one of several examples of the inspect and adapt cycles (IA) used across ASD methods. These cycles accommodate change by allowing teams to examine and reflect on the work that has been done and the ability to course-correct as needed. In conjunction with ID2, these cycles relate to both technical and business domains. Whereas the latter optimises the code to allow for continuous improvements, the former allow reprioritizing requirements to allow software teams the ability to work constantly on the items most pertinent to the user.

ASD teams use inspect-and-adapt cycles to ensure the fulfilment of the correct (i.e., up-to-date) user requirements. These cycles allow teams the ability to detect and correct issues early when they are less costly to fix. How ever, they differ in both frequency and level of organisational engagement. Concerning the former, Sutherland (2015), Scrum co-creator, notes,

Agile methods rely on frequent inspect-and-adapt cycles. These cycles can range from every few minutes with pair programming, to every few hours with continuous integration, to every day with a daily standup meeting, to every iteration with a review and retrospective.

## 5.2.2 | Working cooperatively/collaboratively/in close communication (WC) and continuous customer involvement (CCI)

Working as a team captures the Manifesto's value of placing “individuals and interactions over processes and tools”. ASD methods place a strong emphasis on maintaining open communication channels and close communications between team members, including customers. ASD teams, which are ideally self-directed and autonomous, employ techniques like XP's paired programming to promote mutual participation in working toward a common goal. Fur thermore, ASD techniques like Scrum's sprint planning and scrum meeting promote teamwork during each iteratio of the project. Traditional methods focus on upfront planning to thoroughly define a fixed set of requirements fo the team to follow with little to no changes. Scope/requirements changes are discouraged to prevent delays in the delivery of the project. Although limiting customer interaction once the requirements have been determined is sup posed to increase predictability, requirements changes have been shown to occur with regularity in many, if not most, software projects. In order to maximise a team's ability to respond to change, teams need to be empowered to make decisions. Team autonomy enables teams to leverage and create change by building software in service to customers who drive the team's priorities and overall design. The ASD community places emphasis on selforganisation to ensure that teams have the autonomy to make decisions while working in close collaboration with customers. In order to reduce the lengthy team-customer feedback loop and solicit timely feedback, many ASD methods make the customer (or customer representative) a member of the team, such as XP's onsite customer and Scrum's “product owner” role. The degree of customer involvement (i.e., continuous in ASD) marks another major difference between traditional and ASD methods.

Through continuous customer involvement, ASD teams can focus on customer relations and build a share understanding of user requirements. Working in close collaboration with customers is said to increase the predict ability of the resulting software and its acceptance by the customer (Cohn, 2009). Thus, ASD's constant focus on customer needs should enhance a software team's ability to deliver business value and, in turn, customer satisfac tion. In fact, Wood et al. (2013) suggest that ASD team success in delivering high-quality software solutions is due to higher client and team foci from using specific ASD techniques (e.g., ASD customer planning and team code manage ment) and not merely an enhanced use of teamwork.

In summary, ASD enables software team agility by executing tight, work collaborations between team members and customers to deliver small increments of working software in regular, short intervals. This process affords team the ability to inspect their work and adapt to changes in user requirements and broadens the team's interactions wit stakeholders beyond the IT group. Although the explicit focus on software development agility distinguishes ASD from traditional IS development methods, ASD represents a combination of traditional and contemporary ideas tha have evolved (Agile Alliance, 2020). Thus, ASD methods incorporate elements of prototyping and iterative develop ment found in other post-waterfall methods (e.g., Spiral, RAD) and add a greater emphasis on customer involvement and collaborative work practices. The resulting ASD methods differ significantly from traditional methods tha restrict requirements changes and the degree of customer collaboration during software projects

In studying how IS development agility has been achieved, we observed four core concepts that were found across virtually all ASD methods. The intersection of ASD methods/techniques with the core concepts (1) highlights area where specific aspects of IS development agility can be enabled within a given method, and (2) helps explain the con sequences of method tailoring. We subdivide these techniques into two categories: project management (PM) and software engineering (SE) (Tripp et al., 2016). PM techniques describ tasks that are more people-centred. Whereas, SE techniques describe the more computer-centred tasks. We find that both PM and SE techniques support ASD's primary goal of anticipating, creating, learning from and responding to changes in user requirements, but in different ways. Overall, SE techniques tend to impact the IT group level as they are engaged by developers and have limited interaction with the business. These techniques focus on coding software quickly and delivering it frequently in lean, small increments. In conjunction with incremental design an iterative development, SE techniques use inspect-and-adapt cycles to ensure software quality before delivery. In contrast, PM techniques are typically found at the centre of cross-functional team interactions, which may involv stakeholders from across multiple functional units and organisational levels. PM techniques focus on the gatherin of relevant stakeholders to share pertinent information related to the project. PM techniques also use inspect-and adapt cycles to renew the collective focus, work priority and direction of the project.

At the method level, both XP and Scrum contain enough techniques to cover each core concept.

## 6 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Prior IS research argues for a greater emphasis on theoretical contribution in ASD research (Abrahamsson et al., 2009; Dingsøyr et al., 2012) and recommends ways of increasing the rigour therein, in the absence of a theo retical core. This research proposes such a theoretical core, which can be further refined and built upon in futur research. In doing so, this research provides both a foundation for understanding and a platform for growth and evo lution concerning the core of ASD. There are several theoretical and practice contributions.

First, identifying a theoretical core directly addresses what constitutes “agile” in the context of IS development This research contributes to ASD theory by proposing a previously elusive theoretical core of four concepts base on research and practice. Whereas most prior attempts to theorise ASD use existing theory to tap into different elements of ASD, our theoretical core captures the unique characteristics of ASD and helps researchers to theoris according to the ASD principles and practices themselves, which will help to advance ASD research, both theoreti cally and practically. The theoretical core presented in this study merges practice with research. Instead of having two different groups – academics and practitioners, both engaged in their interpretations of ASD, we offer a unifie view of ASD. We draw upon extant theory, guiding principles (Manifesto and its 12 principles), and ASD methods which demonstrate how the guiding principles have been operationalised using a set of techniques, and articulate a clear understanding of the central aspects of ASD. The resulting theoretical core – consisting of four core concepts – provides a unified theoretical understanding of ASD that can be used to ground future ASD research. As previously highlighted, the extant literature points to several facets of IS agility; however, some of these facets were developed from industries outside of software development (Conboy, 2009). We believe these four core concepts reflect a basic, parsimonious, and common set of concepts found across virtually all ASD methods that enable IS development agility (i.e., anticipate, create, learn from and respond to change) in the software development context. As such, they can help researchers balance the pursuit of theoretica understanding with applying the level of nuance needed (e.g., studying wholesale and partial ASD approaches) to make an impact in both research and practice

Figure 2 presents a framework for conceptualising IS agility in practice, synthesising what has been learned and illustrated in this study for future research and practice. As previously noted, the core concepts found in this paper were drawn from the values and principles articulated in the Manifesto, the ASD methods found in practice, and aca demic research. The framework illustrates how elements of IS agility (e.g., contextual factors, core concepts, etc.) can be integrated to understand situated ASD implementations and their impact on outcomes. For instance, each ASD implementation utilises a method or set of techniques (the what, or means of implementation) in a specific context (the environment in which an implementation occurs). With each implementation, the core concepts help us to understand how methodological choices might enable IS agility and why (the how and why [of agility], or explanation of how agility is realised). These choices should carefully consider ASD principles that guide how the techniques are executed to achieve targeted outcomes (the how [of implementation], or manner of implementation). Future research can draw upon our framework to explain when, how, and why ASD methods and techniques impact theoretical outcomes or determine the extent to which IS agility is being realised. Concerning the former, the framework can be used to answer questions such as which ASD techniques could be adopted to enable IS agility in a given context? Which cor concepts might yield the greatest impacts on desired outcomes such as time to market and software quality and why? Why different sets of ASD techniques might yield different outcomes? Concerning the latter, consider a new company that is exploring a lean startup approach for IS development using build-measure-learn (guiding) principles (Ries, 2011). Although these principles are in line with the Manifesto's, they do not inform the company about how to achieve IS agility on their own. To achieve a better change response, the company might consider several ASD tech niques (or an ASD method) that will operationalise IS agility in accordance with these principles in a tangible way Additionally, the core concepts can inform the company concerning the aspects of IS agility that each technique might realise and whether they have covered the domain of IS agility (e.g., needing to add more techniques) Although the interaction of the core concepts, methods and techniques and guiding principles considered in thi paper are specific to ASD, future research could emplore the use of the general elements of this framework for understanding other practice-oriented phenomenon in IS

Second, this research offers the ability to better evaluate claims made by researchers who study ASD, which i often difficult to ascertain in an individual paper. Had the organisations studied really adopted ASD or just some variant or some specific techniques? What does it mean to use ASD theoretically? To wit, the theoretical core presented in this study provides a vehicle through which to think about what is and what it is not fully “agile” as it relates to theory and practice. This research provides an understanding of how a particular implementation, wholesale or par tial, constitutes IS development agility. The theoretical core is also useful for explaining how and why the technique of existing and future “agile” methods cover the domain.

The theoretical core is particularly helpful for evaluating method tailoring approaches, as we illustrated in Tables 3 and 4. The theoretical core allows more informed tailoring and a better understanding of the role of each technique a it relates to a software team's ability to be agile, or anticipate, create, learn from and respond to changing user requirements. We implore managers to use our framework to foster a deeper understanding of IS agility within ASD teams rather than simply adopting an uninformed set of techniques. This can help organisations better understand the costs and benefits of tailoring decisions. Additionally, a set of techniques can be compared to the core concepts to identify gaps in IS development agility and find ways to fill and fortify them. As we have noted, a theoretical core can be used to understand knock-off effects , or the effects of removing specific techniques during method tailoring (Conboy & Fitzgerald, 2007). With the term “agile” being applied to a wide range of software development approaches, the issue of what is and is not agile has been a thorn in the side of the ASD literature in that it is difficult to understand what a researcher means when they say they studied “agile”. The theoretical core helps to distinguish between “fully agile”, partially agile and non-agile software development implementations. The distinctions between these categories has managerial implications for organisations seeking lean ASD implementations that do not compromise software team agility. Past studies identify complementariness and redundancy among ASD techniques across two or more methods (e.g., Scrum and XP) (Fitzgerald et al., 2006). For instance, while it is reasonable that one technique (e.g., Sprint Plan ning) may be chosen over another (e.g., Planning Game) for project planning to prevent redundancies, removing a specific technique (e.g., Planning Game from XP) may be detrimental to IS development agility if its value is not accounted for by other techniques. Establishing a theoretical core may help draw the distinction between strategic method tailor ing, which adapts the method to the organisational context and is shown to assist the team in anticipating and responding to changing user requirements, and placing an “agile” label on a way of building software that remains inflexible and unresponsive to changes in user requirements. The theoretical core can help organisations better under stand the consequences (e.g., knock-off effects) of their method tailoring choices.

Third, the theoretical core provides the building blocks for teaching, professionalisation, and a better under standing of how ASD can be applied to other areas. Although IS textbooks contain limited information on ASD (May et al., 2019), researchers have conducted several studies that explore ways of teaching ASD in a university settin (Devedzic, 2010; Kamat & Sardessai, 2012). Many of these studies describe adaptations of ASD methods and tech niques in the classroom (Baham, 2019; Masood et al., 2018). Our research provides a theoretical basis for ASD which may lead to a more informed ASD method tailoring in both academic and professional settings. This is benefi cial for certifying organisations that provide education for practitioners concerning how to achieve IS development agility in organisations. The four core concepts can be compared to other forms of agility, such as firm agility and IS recovery agility to highlight the unique context of software development.

Like all studies, our has limitations. The four core concepts proposed here are not immutable, but open to debate Future research can critique the core concepts presented here and contextualise related non-core concepts. In searching for concepts that were found across all ASD methods, we noted several concepts such as self-organisation that were mentioned across multiple ASD methods. Elaboration on the role of widely adopted, non-core concepts can both solidify the core and clarify the role of non-core concepts. In pursuit of parsimony, there is limited discussion of add-on effects , or adding specific techniques during method tailoring. Future research could elaborate on the bene fits, costs and complexities of specific overlapping techniques. Given that ASD techniques are said to be tightl coupled, there may be conditions in which similar ASD techniques provide particular benefits to an ASD team. Despite the study limitations, we believe this research proposes a useful theoretical core that is advantageous for both research and practice. Hopefully, the theoretical core presented here will lead to a debate about what the core concepts of ASD are, and allow others to add to the debate. In other words, we are not offering a concrete theory that is set in stone but is a proposal which the community can use as the basis of an intelligent discussion about ASD theory.
