## Abstract

Losing the ability to communicate inhibits social contact, creates feelings of frustration and isolation and complicates personal comfort and medical care. Progressive diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and multiple sclerosis (MS) can cause severe motor disabilities that make communication through traditional means difficult, slow, and exhausting, even with the support of augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) systems. Using a design science research approach, we seek to improve the communication process for individuals with severe motor disabilities. We develop a series of design requirements to inform the creation and evaluation of an artefact, an AAC system that incorporates context-aware user profiles to improve the communication process for individuals with severe motor disabilities. We derive prescriptive knowledge through the creation of design principles based on our findings and justify these design principles using the lens of media synchronicity theory (MST). This research identifies opportunities for further research related to MST and provides insights to inform those designing communication systems for individuals that rely on AAC systems.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Todd<sup>1</sup> was a 40-year old male who became paralysed because of genetic mitochondrial myopathy, a progressive muscle-wasting disease. Todd was cognitively intact and maintained his sensory functions, meaning he kept his sens of humour, eyesight, hearing, sense of smell, and desire to watch basketball on television. As his disease progressed Todd lost muscle function until he had little to no control of his body. He could move involuntarily to breathe, laugh or cough; however, Todd could not move his hand to scratch an itch, shift his body to see the television or visitors nor communicate with friends, family, or caregivers using speech or typing. Todd had some control over his ey movement, which allowed him to answer “yes” or “no” to questions asked by those who could understand his responses; yet, Todd's control over his eye movements was insufficient to use an eye-tracking system to communicate.

The inability to communicate with others is one of the most challenging experiences that human beings may endure (Brady et al., 2016). Communication is a process of exchanging information with others to reach a share understanding (Rogers, 1986). Communication processes promote shared understanding as individuals express thei needs, desires, and information to others as they seek to accomplish work-related tasks (Dennis, Fuller, & Valacich, 2008) or better their quality of life (Grether, 2017). Often, individuals who cannot communicate to express their needs or engage in social relationships feel isolated and encounter challenges in maintaining personal comfort and adequate levels of medical care (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, 2019). Over 4 million peopl in the United States have limited communication ability due to sensory or motor disabilities (American Speech-Lan guage-Hearing Association, 2019).

A disability is assessed by the degree an individual can function and interact with their environment (Brady et al., 2016). Todd's inability to communicate was due to a severe motor disability known as locked-in syndrome, a rare neurological disorder (National Organization for Rare Disorders, 2018). Individuals with locked-in syndrom experience little to no control of voluntary muscular function, have normal cognitive function, and may have sensor disabilities. A person may experience locked-in syndrome due to stroke, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or othe non-specific muscle-wasting disease, cerebral palsy, Parkinson's disease, or a traumatic head injury

Over 1 billion people have some form of disability worldwide (World Health Organization, 2018), and a subset of individuals experience severe disabilities that require “extensive ongoing support in more than one major lif activity<sup>2</sup> in order to participate in integrated community settings and to enjoy a quality of life that is available to citizens with fewer or no disabilities (TASH, 1991, p. 19). Individuals with disabilities often use assistive technologie to support their ability to engage in various life activities. Research on assistive technologies often seeks to design new or improved systems to positively impact an individual's quality of life.

Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) systems are a form of assistive technologies that support the communication process to enable individuals with communication disabilities to express themselves in a manner similar to standard speech (McNaughton & Light, 2014). However, a person's motor skills can limit the effectiveness of an AAC system. Individuals with severe motor disabilities, such as Todd, cannot express their thoughts throug typing, pointing at words, or using other traditional motor-based inputs. Those with severe motor disabilities must rely on non-muscular channels to serve as an input to an AAC system, such as minute changes in brain signals, bloo oxygenation, or skin conductance (Chaudhary, Xia, Silvoni, Cohen, & Birbaumer, 2017; Randolph & Moore Jackson, 2010).

Although AAC systems allow people who have limited motor abilities to engage in some communication processes, many AAC system users struggle to express their desires and maintain relationships with others. Crafting message using an AAC system to express a need or answer a question (beyond a simple yes/no response) can take several minutes to create and share with others. This extremely slow transmission rate of information from th sender to the receiver degrades the process of communication and the ability to reach a shared understanding between the AAC system user and their communication partner.

Therefore, our research study seeks to answer the following question: How should we design AAC systems to improve the communication process for individuals with severe motor disabilities? Our research process begins by identi fying the challenges that Todd and others with similar disabilities encounter when communicating with others. W use the design science research (DSR) methodology developed by Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, and Chatter jee (2007) to understand the problem, identify design requirements, develop an artefact, or solution, to the problem and evaluate our artefact.

Our design science approach revealed the opportunity to improve the communication process by re-thinking how AAC system users select letters, words or phrases as they communicate with others. Therefore, one contribu tion of this research is the creation of context-aware user profiles as a new element in the design of AAC systems Incorporating context-aware user profiles within an AAC system enables the system to predict words, phrases an conversation topics specific to the time, location and communication partner. Our second contribution is docu menting prescriptive knowledge, or design principles, for designing AAC systems for individuals with severe moto disabilities. These design principles are justified using the lens of media synchronicity theory (MST) (Dennis et al., 2008). As a theory of communication performance, MST explains how communication media capabilities affect media synchronicity, which is a pattern of coordinated behaviour as communication partners work together at th same time with a common focus (Dennis et al., 2008). We identify theoretical implications by identifying boundary conditions and new research opportunities related to MST and offer implications for practice

This paper proceeds as follows. The next section provides background on AAC systems and MST. Then, w describe our DSR approach for creating and evaluating an artefact to address our research question. We describe our artefact as we explain the problem, identify design requirements and develop a solution, which is context-awar user profiles. Next, we explain how we evaluated the use of context-aware user profiles in the context of communi cating medical and comfort needs. Then, we discuss the prescriptive knowledge gained through the DSR process and apply the theoretical lens of MST to justify our design principles. Finally, we identify implications for theory and prac tice and opportunities for future research

## 2 | BACKGROUND

This section explains the range of AAC systems available for individuals with severe motor disabilities and opportuni ties to improve the design of AAC systems. We also provide an overview of MST and its applicability to AAC systems.

## 2.1 | Augmentative and alternative communication

Individuals with severe motor disabilities may have limitations that prevent their ability to speak and express them selves through typical means for conversation (Keates, Langdon, Clarkson, & Robinson, 2002). An AAC system sup ports the communication process by enabling individuals to choose among combinations of symbols (eg, letters words, phrases, pictures) using one or more selection techniques (eg, pointing, eye gaze, keyboard, mouse) (America Speech-Language-Hearing Association, 2018). Different types of AAC systems exist to support individuals who hav variations in the severity and nature of their motor disabilities.

One dichotomy for AAC systems is unaided communication systems versus aided communication systems Unaided AAC systems rely on gestures, eye gazes, sign language or other cues from bodily movement to select among symbols. Aided AAC systems employ assistive devices, which may include digital technology (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, 2018; National Academies of Sciences, 2017). When Todd communicated with visitors by shifting his eyes to signal “yes” or “no” to a question, this is an example of an unaided AAC system However, if Todd communicated by pointing to a symbol or word on a board, typing his thoughts, or using a nonmuscular form of input (such as a neural input), then Todd would be using an aided AAC system. Individuals may use a single AAC system or multiple AAC systems to communicate with others (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, 2018) based on the communication need, context and disability

When an individual relies on an aided AAC system that uses technology, there are many required components, such as the hardware used to select inputs and display outputs and software to represent language through alpha bets, symbols, or words (National Academies of Sciences, 2017). Individuals with severe motor disabilities who hav limited ability to speak or use gestures for communication are typically more reliant on technology-enabled AAC sys tems. For those with severe motor disabilities who cannot gesture or type words reliably, an aided AAC system must have an input device appropriate for the individual, given their disability, to select symbols, letters, words, or phrases Then, the AAC system displays the user's selections on a screen or synthesises the selections as speech; this allows other conversational participants to read or hear the selections made by the AAC system user (National Academie of Sciences, 2017). This research study focuses on aided, technology-enabled AAC systems for individuals with severe motor disabilities who have limited ability to transmit messages.<sup>3</sup>

In many AAC systems, the system displays individual letters to allow the AAC system user to spell any word the wish to express. However, spelling each word for conversation is quite slow. Some AAC systems allow individuals to select common phrases to increase the rate of transmission of messages within a conversation (McNaughton & Light, 2013). Another approach to increase the rate of conversation is the use of word prediction systems. As an AAC system user begins to spell a word, word prediction systems identify which words the user is most likely trying to generate based on the initial letters selected. Word prediction systems can increase the transmission rate of mes sages in a conversation, but correcting word prediction errors can be tedious and slow in an AAC system context (Trnka, McCaw, Yarrington, McCoy, & Pennington, 2009). Providing a set of common words or using word prediction systems increases the transmission rate for messages within a conversation. However, the AAC system user has less flexibility in communicating their needs, desires or information since these approaches only tend to identify or pre dict commonly used words.

As a result, for individuals with more severe motor disabilities, communication is often restricted to answering questions with a “yes” or “no” response. The limited choices for responses increase the transmission rate for com munication; however, the individual can only respond to questions asked and cannot initiate conversation. Initiating conversation enables more autonomy and quality of life for individuals with severe motor disabilities. Many agencie and groups consider the ability of disabled people to communicate as a fundamental right (Brady et al., 2016)

In contrast, clear speech occurs at 100 words per minute (wpm), and typical speech rates for adult conversation average 270 wpm (Calvert & Silverman, 1983; Koch & Janse, 2016). For those with little to no motor ability, neurally controlled technology, such as brain–computer interfaces (BCIs), can serve as a non-muscular channel to select among symbols within AAC systems (Combaz et al., 2013; Lesenfants et al., 2014; McFarland & Wolpaw, 2011; Wol paw et al., 2018). There are several means of input for BCIs, each with varying levels of performance and different means to communicate. One of the fastest BCIs reported to-date enabled participants to transmit information quickly, but the interface only allowed individuals to select among six pre-selected phrase or options (Dimitriadis & Marimpis, 2018). These faster BCI transmission rates dramatically limit the AAC system user's selection options, preventing the individual from having higher levels of autonomy when engaging in communication with others.

## 2.1.1 | User profiles

Visitors frequently feel uncomfortable communicating with individuals with severe motor disabilities due to the lim ited feedback and awkward lulls that occur when a conversational participant is using an AAC system. Furthermore conversational participants tend to feel awkward listening to communication at rates slower than typical speec (Goodenough-Trepagnier, Galdieri, Rosen, & Baker, 1984; Street & Brady, 1982). One approach to improving the communication process for and with AAC system users is to incorporate user profiles to filter information, much in the same way that information-filtering “recommender” systems incorporate user profiles for increased personaliza tion of product search results (Bobadilla, Ortega, Hernando, & Gutiérrez, 2013; Middleton, Shadbolt, & Roure, 2004)

Many recommender systems use algorithms to predict a user's preferences based on information and rating provided by the user or provide predictions based on information and ratings of others who engage in patterns or behaviours similar to the user. User profiles support information filtering based on a user's personal characteristics (Hanani, Shapira, & Shoval, 2001), interests or preferences (Kassab & Lamirel, 2006; Khemiri & Bentayeb, 2012 Kim & Chan, 2007; Singh & Mehrotra, 2016), and behaviours (Schiaffino & Amandi, 2009). Filtering information against a user profile improves the relevance of information that is presented to the user (Vertommen, Janssens Moor, & Duflou, 2008; Wu & Chen, 2000).

Knowledge about users and their preferences must be acquired via explicit or implicit means to create user profiles (Fischer, 2001). When user profile knowledge is acquired explicitly by asking users to provide information, this is considered a knowledge-based approach; when user profile knowledge is implicitly inferred based on a user's patterns or actions, this is known as a behaviour-based approach (Middleton et al., 2004). A knowledge-based approach requires active user involvement by answering questions, which is resource-intensive (Spoerri, 2007). One example of an explicit approach to create a user profile occurs when a user completes a questionnaire or selects from a predefined set of profiles (Hanani et al., 2001). In contrast, a behaviour-based approach requires no active user involvement. A behaviour-based system records the user's behaviour and makes inferences about the relevancy of information based upon the user's actions within the system (Hanani et al., 2001). Many consumer websites, such as Amazon.com and social media platforms, customise and personalise products and service recommendations by employing behaviour-based user profiles as they infer their customers' needs based on the users' interactions with the website (Ellison, 2007; Manber, Patel, & Robison, 2000; Weigend, 2003).

## 2.1.2 | Opportunities in AAC system design

AAC system developers and users seek to achieve faster transmission rates without compromising the accuracy of messages. However, improving the speed of AAC systems that rely on physical inputs is limited due to human physiology constraints (Riedl & Léger, 2016). In a literature review of language used in AAC systems, Speier, Arnold, an Pouratian (2016) encourage AAC system designers to use conversational (as opposed to written text) language whe creating letter and word prediction algorithms. They further encourage the development of AAC systems that adapt to the user's preferences and context. In addition to these suggestions, the consideration of media capabilities ma provide additional ideas on how to improve AAC system design to improve the communication process for individ uals with severe motor disabilities.

## 2.2 | Media synchronicity theory

## 2.2.1 | Theory overview

Information system research has long considered technology's role as a medium in communication processes (Mason, 1978). MST posits that “communication performance comes from the matching of media capabilities to th communication processes required to accomplish a task” (Dennis et al., 2008, p. 579, emphasis in original). Further, MST defines communication performance by the shared understanding reached through the act of communication (Dennis et al., 2008).

Synchronicity is the “shared pattern of coordinated behaviour among individuals as they work together” (Denni et al., 2008, p. 575). MST states that the level of synchronicity required for successful communication varies based on the nature of the task, which affects: (a) the type of communication processes used; (b) the capabilities of the media used for communication; and (c) the familiarity among the parties with one another, the task and the media Both information transmission and information processing lead to shared understanding among the conversationa participants. Information transmission includes preparing information, sharing information through a medium, and receiving information; information processing is applying the information received (Dennis et al., 2008).

Media synchronicity is affected by the capabilities of the medium (or media) used for communication. Communi cation media can range from face-to-face communication, email, voicemail, postal mail, videoconferencing or any other mediums that allow individuals to communicate with one another. Media capabilities increase or decrease the ability of media to support coordinated behaviours. The five media capabilities identified by Dennis et al. (2008) include transmission velocity, parallelism, symbol sets, rehearsability and reprocessability. Past research examining MST explains how the fit of media capabilities and the purpose of communication can influence the level of media synchronicity. For example, a study of global software development teams found that team members experienc frustration with communication when a poor fit exists between the media capabilities and the communication pro cess (Niinimäki, Piri, Lassenius, & Paasivaara, 2012). In a study of media synchronicity during a humanitarian crisis parallelism emerged as a critical media capability, and high media synchronicity was critical for conveyance and con vergence communication processes (Muhren, Van Den Eede, & Van de Walle, 2009).

MST asserts that all tasks require a certain level of conveyance processes and convergent processes, but some tasks will rely more on one process than the other. Tasks relying more heavily on convergence processes are interac tions which are related to an “individual's interpretation of a situation, not the raw information itself” (Denni et al., 2008, p. 580, emphasis in original). These tasks require less information processing and seek to find agreement among the participants in the communications process. Convergence processes benefit from higher levels of syn chronicity in communication media because the messages shared within this communication process require less information processing (Dennis & Valacich, 1999). Higher levels of synchronicity allow sensemaking among the com munication process participants to occur more quickly. Tasks that primarily require a conveyance process need higher levels of cognitive processing as the sender and receiver share new information. The cognitive load for con veyance processes is higher as individuals exchange and process new, diverse information “to enable the receiver to create and revise a mental model of the situation” (Dennis et al., 2008, p. 580, emphasis in original). Conveyance processes are less reliant on synchronicity, given the higher levels of cognitive processing involved in communicating and receiving information (Dennis & Valacich, 1999).

MST states that the fit of media synchronicity with the communication processes influences communication performance (ie, the development of shared understanding among participants), which influences task performance (Dennis et al., 2008). Tasks are defined in MST as the underlying conveyance and convergence processes (Dennis et al., 2008, p. 581). Because tasks are viewed in terms of communication processes, this enables MST to be applie to both organisational contexts (DeLuca & Valacich, 2006; Niinimäki et al., 2012) and non-work settings, such as education (Muhren et al., 2009; Sun & Wang, 2017). However, the study of MST traditionally assumes that commu nication participants are non-disabled individuals. It is unknown how MST applies in the context of individuals with severe motor disabilities using AAC systems. Hence, we use MST as a lens to examine how to improve the communi cation process for individuals with severe motor disabilities

## 2.2.2 | Application of MST to AAC systems

For those with severe motor disabilities who must use AAC systems to communicate, reaching shared understandin has challenges because communication is mediated through the AAC system. According to MST, media capabilities “impact how individuals can transmit and process a message” (Dennis et al., 2008, p. 576, emphasis in original). An AAC system user is highly dependent on the system's media capabilities, which affects various aspects of the com munication process and communication performance, measured by the amount of shared understanding obtaine among individuals.

For those engaging in communication with individuals with severe motor disabilities, the interaction tends to be a one-way dialogue with the visitor doing most of the talking (Carroll, Schlag, Kirikci, & Moore, 2002) and bearin the burden to repair communication breakdowns (Midtlin, Næss, Taxt, & Karlsen, 2015). MST recognises that medi capabilities impact the ability to create synchronicity among the communication participants. The level of synchro nicity among participants ultimately affects communication performance. When an individual communicates wit the assistance of an AAC system, the media capabilities of the AAC system will impact the communication process between the AAC system user and the communication partner. ## 3 | METHOD

To improve the design of AAC systems to support communication processes for individuals with severe motor dis abilities, we use a DSR approach (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004). DSR seeks to develop an artefact to address need or improve the current state-of-the-art by applying existing knowledge (Vaishnavi & Petter, 2019). Researchers may create one or more different types of artefacts, such as a construct, method, process, instantiation, theory or descriptive knowledge, to address the research problem (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Gregor & Jones, 2007; March &

Smith, 1995). After creating an artefact, the researchers evaluate the artefact for its benefits and efficacy (Hevner et al., 2004; Venable, Pries-Heje, & Baskerville, 2016).

When using a DSR approach, different DSR strategies may be used depending on the nature of the researc question. As Iivari (2015) explains, Strategy 1 develops an artefact to solve a class of problems that are often inspired by a problem occurring in practice; Strategy 2 develops an artefact to solve a specific problem within a context and seek to glean knowledge from the development of the artefact. This research study follows a Strategy 2 approach in that we identified a problem, developed a solution, and then sought to create new knowledge through the creation of design principles informed by theory. Strategy 2 is less exemplified within the IS literature (Iivari, 2015), but it can provide useful research contributions.

Because we seek to solve a specific research problem, we adopt an idiographic design genre (Baskerville, Kaul, & Storey, 2015) and follow the design science research methodology (DSRM) as described by Peffers et al. (2007). A DSRM approach seeks to develop a useful artefact, which may or may not be designed based on formal theory, and evaluates the created artefact based on whether or not the artefact works across contexts (Peffers, Tuunanen, & Niehaves, 2018). The DSRM prescribed by Peffers et al. (2007) separates DSR into two processes: artefact creation and artefact evaluation. Artefact creation includes activities to (a) identify a problem that needs to be addressed, (b) define the objectives, or requirements, for the solution, and (c) design and develop an artefact to address th problem based on the specified objectives. Artefact evaluation involves (d) demonstrating how the artefact addresse the solution objectives, (e) evaluating the artefact for its usefulness or value, and (f) communicating the findings to others (Peffers et al., 2007). Although the activities within the DSRM are identified as discrete activities, often researchers must iterate between and within various activities in the research process (Vaishnavi & Petter, 2019).

In this research study, we identified a problem based on interactions with individuals with severe motor disabil ities who relied on AAC systems for communication with caregivers, friends and family. We considered the limita tions of the existing AAC systems and postulated how we could modify existing ideas and/or create new solution to address the problem. Through continued experience interacting with individuals with severe motor disabilitie using AAC systems to communicate, we continued to contemplate how we might refine the design of AAC system to improve the communication process for AAC system users. This process yielded the idea that context-aware user profiles could offer an alternative design for AAC systems to improve the communications process by providing an appropriate set of words or phrases determined by the environment, time of day, and conversational partner

We evaluated context-aware user profiles using an artificial evaluation approach and a more naturalistic evalua tion approach to establish artefact validity. These approaches were used to assess whether the artefact achieves th intended goals (Gregor & Hevner, 2013). Through the process of artefact creation and evaluation, we identified prescriptive knowledge (Chandra et al., 2015), informed by MST, to develop design principles for AAC systems that can improve the communication processes for AAC system users.

## 4 | ARTEFACT DESCRIPTION

This section provides information about the research context of individuals with severe motor disabilities and thei communication processes. We describe a series of design requirements that we identified for a potential solution Then, we explain our process for designing, developing, and demonstrating how an AAC system can be designed to improve the communication process by incorporating context-aware user profiles.

## 4.1 | Context and problem

Although some individuals with severe motor disabilities live at home, many live in long-term care medical facilitie in which they can receive medical and hygiene care from orderlies, nurses and doctors. These individuals spend most of their life alone in their room with only a television for stimulation until a caregiver or loved one stops by for rounds or a visit. None of the individuals with severe motor disabilities we interacted with for this research had access to an AAC system unless researchers were present

Two of the authors have extensive experience in communicating with individuals with severe motor disabilities. When visiting an individual and initiating a conversation, we assess the person's comfort or medical needs using a protocol where we ask the individual a series of yes/no questions. If the person is able, this activity is performed without the assistance of an AAC. The individual moves their eyes, twitches a muscle or uses another voluntary movement to answer the series of questions. Making these small, voluntary movements can be quite tiring, and physical fatigue can limit one's ability to respond. In some cases, the individual with a severe motor disability can manipulate and use an AAC system that incorpo rates an iterative speller. An iterative speller enables the user to select each character to create words and/or sentence using a scanning interface. In these cases, the individual using the AAC system can ask questions, express concerns, or initiate conversation topics. While the speller provides ultimate flexibility for the AAC system user to engage in conver sation, the trade-off for this flexibility is the time required to spell out each word. When an AAC system user is trying to communicate with a visitor in real time using an iterative speller, the visitor must wait for several minutes to understand the AAC system user's message. Selection errors for letters or words further slow the rate of communication. Thes delays in the communication create frustration for both the sender and receiver of the message.

If Todd used an AAC system with BCI input and an iterative speller, he could spell out words that would allow him to transmit his needs without being asked a specific question. It would likely take several minutes for Todd to spell out something simple, such as “L ELBW ITCH” (for “left elbow itch”) with a traditional AAC system with a BC input. For Todd to express this with a visitor in the room, the visitor would need to wait several minutes to receive and respond to Todd's message. If Todd had access to the AAC system without visitors present, he could develop this message and display it to a visitor upon their arrival.

Unfortunately, Todd only had access to an AAC system with a BCI when researchers came to visit. At the beginning of one visit with Todd at a long-term care facility, we noticed that he was in a state of discomfort. He seemed to be agi tated, which he displayed through his involuntary movements and reactions. Before setting up the AAC system, the team sought to learn what was wrong using a series of yes/no questions and by examining Todd's body and medical equipment We realised that Todd's gastric feeding tube had become dislodged, and he was lying in a pool of liquid food. If Todd had continuous access to an AAC system with a BCI, it would have taken several minutes for Todd to communicate an appropriate message using an iterative speller. Nevertheless, Todd's effort to communicate a message could have alerted care givers earlier to his condition or, at the very least, would have allowed Todd to feel less helpless in this situation.

Although the individuals with severe motor disabilities with whom we interacted for this research had access to AAC systems when researchers were present, communication was still challenging. The inability for them to express their medical or comfort needs was one of our primary concerns. Furthermore, because the interactions tend to be question-driven by the caregiver or visitor, the individual with a severe motor disability has little opportunity to initi ate meaningful conversation (Carroll et al., 2002) or express a specific medical or comfort need. Despite their severe motor disabilities, we know these individuals have rich lives, varied interests and stories to share. These memories and ideas often remained locked inside the individuals' minds because their bodies and available technology coul not allow them to communicate their thoughts with others

We learned that caregivers and loved ones encounter challenging interactions due to the mostly one-sided nature of communication with the individual with a severe motor disability. These loved ones want to engage in meaningful conversations. However, they are unable to engage in deeper conversations due to the limited responses that can be initiated by the individual with a disability. Many loved ones and caregivers expressed feelings of help lessness as they knew that their friend, family member or patient had needs, desires, and ideas that could not b communicated due to the AAC system's constraints and the limitations of the individual's disabilities. These concerns further prompted the need to identify an alternative design for AAC systems to improve the communication process particularly for the population of AAC system users with severe motor disabilities.

## 4.2 | Requirements for a solution

We identified several objectives, or design requirements, as we considered our understanding of the problem. In thi activity, the goal is to identify the goals for a solution (or artefact) by considering “What would a better artifact accomplish? (Peffers et al., 2007, p. 54). We inferred our design requirements based on interactions with individual with severe motor disabilities and their healthcare providers, friends, and family members. We also considered our prior knowledge and research related to AAC systems as we developed the design requirements. Our initial focu was identifying requirements for AAC system users with the most severe motor disabilities (ie, individuals wit locked-in syndrome) who often have no other options than a BCI for input for an AAC system. However, thes design requirements are also applicable to any individual with severe motor disabilities who relies on AAC system for communication.

First, there is a need to improve the rate of transmission for AAC system users with severe motor disabilities who have limited input options. From our interactions with individuals with severe motor disabilities, we are familia with the challenges that arise in the communication process due to the slow transmission rates experienced by AAC system users. The ability to advance technology capabilities to improve transmission rates within AAC systems can be limited for systems that translate a physiological response (eg, eye movement, muscle tensing, brain signa changes) into a unit of communication (eg, a letter, word, or phrase) because of inter- and intra-individual difference of humans (Riedl & Léger, 2016). Therefore, we needed to consider other approaches to improve the communication process for AAC system users. One option is to customise the language within the AAC system for the context and preferences of the user (Speier et al., 2016). These considerations led to the first design requirement.

Design Requirement 1. Improve the transmission rate for AAC system users by addressing deficiencies in the communi cation process instead of seeking computational advances

Based on our interactions with AAC system users, we identified two primary reasons for communication. First, a caregiver often seeks to identify the individual's medical or comfort needs. For those familiar with the person and the task of assessing medical and comfort needs, the yes/no protocol in which a person asks the individual with a severe motor disability about their medical or comfort needs takes several minutes. For visitors less familiar with (or unknown to) the individual, the process of assessing medical or comfort needs requires even more time and effort for both parties. Patients with effective systems for communicating their medical needs have a greater sense of quality in the medical care received and a better relationship with their healthcare provider (Chumbler et al., 2007; Schein, Schmeler, Saptono, & Brienza, 2010). We developed the following design requirement based on our concer for ensuring the AAC system user could express their medical or comfort needs to visitors.

Design Requirement 2. Improve the AAC system user's ability to communicate their medical and comfort needs to others.

The second reason for communication among AAC system users is to engage in meaningful conversations with caregivers or loved ones. As noted earlier, most conversations between individuals with severe motor disabilities and visitors are related to the individual's medical care (Adams et al., 2003). Enabling an individual to engage in more extensive communication topics could enrich interactions among the individual, caregivers and loved ones. Engaging in meaningful conversation is difficult to achieve because the non-disabled person must typically ask yes/no questions to generate a response from the individual with a disability. The AAC system user often must respond to ques tions with binary or simple responses. Only through spelling, which is slow and tedious, can the AAC system use fully communicate their thoughts. Therefore, we identified the following design requirement:

Design Requirement 3. Improve the ability for AAC system users to engage in meaningful conversations with others

For many AAC system users with severe motor disabilities, the conversation participant who has a higher trans mission rate for communication drives the conversation. The user can typically only respond to questions asked of them. As shown in the transcript in Table 3, one person initiates conversation, and the other person responds with a yes/no answer. Improved AAC systems would give more autonomy and voice to the individual with a severe motor disability and enable caregivers to attend to medical and comfort needs more quickly. For an AAC system user who wants to engage in non-medical topics of conversation, the individual could initiate a conversation about last night's basketball game, ask questions about a family member or share a favourite joke with others.

Design Requirement 4. Enable an AAC system user to initiate communication with others

Based on our interactions with AAC system users, caregivers, and loved ones and through an analysis of transcripts of interactions with AAC system users, we also recognised the variation in conversations with AAC system users. Some individuals were familiar with the person with a severe motor disability. In conducting the yes/no protocol for medical needs and comfort, a familiar caregiver could identify the responses of the person with a disabilit more quickly and with fewer errors than unfamiliar caregivers. Familiar visitors also knew the AAC system user' preferences, habits or interests. Our transcripts revealed that family members or caregivers familiar to the individua would often discuss topics with the individual with a disability that may range from medical care, updates about fam ily members, shared memories and similar interests

When an unfamiliar caregiver conducts the yes/no protocol, they may struggle with interpreting the responses provided by the individual with a disability. New caregivers need to ask more questions because the individual's pref erences are unknown to an unfamiliar visitor. The high turnover rate of personnel at long-term care facilities mean that individuals with a disability need to communicate with someone new. Our interactions with AAC system user also reveal opportunities to engage in meaningful conversations with unfamiliar visitors. For example, a family mem ber's new spouse may want to engage in conversation, but they may not know how to engage in the communication process with an AAC system user. This diversity of conversational partners led to a fifth design requirement

Design Requirement 5. Support communication across a range of conversational participants, both familiar and unfamiliar.

Next, we sought to identify how we might address these design requirements to improve communications pro cesses for AAC system users.

## 4.3 | Artefact design, development, and demonstration

We considered a wide range of ideas that could be adapted or extended to address our research problem and fulfi the design requirements. We iterated between the activities of “Design and Development” and “Demonstration” of the DSRM by Peffers et al. (2007) to refine the artefact. In developing a solution, we considered advancements within the context of BCI, AAC systems and assistive technology. We relied on advances in these domains because of our prior research with individuals with locked-in syndrome or other severe motor disabilities that required BC input for AAC systems. Our knowledge regarding AAC systems, particularly those using BCI input, led us to conside whether we could improve transmission speed by considering alternative symbols or language within AAC systems (consistent with Speier et al., 2016). Since BCI input usually only allows for a few selections per minute, we realise that moving beyond spelling words to selecting words and phrases could improve the transmission rate within th communication process. Consistent with Design Requirement 1, we sought to develop an AAC system design to improve the communication process by selecting words, phrases or concepts.

One source of inspiration was flexible dialogue systems. These communication systems predict words based on context cues within a conversation to generate and send messages more quickly among the conversing parties, with out sacrificing intended conversational topics (Lemon & Gruenstein, 2004). We often use flexible dialogue system when a system generates word suggestions when typing a text or when a system offers sample phrases whe replying to emails. Adding a layer of user profiling further filters information based on a user's personal characteristics (Hanani et al., 2001). For example, if an individual loves basketball, but has no interest in football, the system would be more likely to predict words or phrases related to basketball rather than football

We also discovered we could improve upon prediction technologies and user profiling by including the element of context-awareness after considering one author's work to integrate context-awareness with BCI control of a wheelchair (Reference Anonymized). With a context-aware user profile, the AAC system would be aware of the envi ronment (eg, time, location and people) due to its embeddedness within a larger system for communication and envi ronmental control. A context-aware user profile provides options for phrases specific to the time, location, and people involved. By filtering a large corpus of information based on the user's preferences and environment, context-aware user profile reduces the selection space to allow the user to express themselves more quickly.

By including context-aware user profiling within an AAC system, the AAC system user could continue to spel out words and phrases using a traditional speller as is common with general AAC systems. However, the AAC system user would also have alternative symbol sets with topics, words, and phrases related to their preferences and contex tual situation. Using a behaviour-based approach to develop a user profile, if the AAC system learned that Todd pre ferred to face the window in the morning and the TV in the afternoon, the system would provide words and phrases to allow Todd to easily specify these requests. The AAC system with context-aware user profiles would also allow Todd to change his preferences and would learn from his preferences over time.

To further identify if context-aware user profiles could fulfil the design requirements, we considered how we might create context-aware user profiles for AAC system users and how the AAC system may incorporate user pro files to enable the AAC system user to engage in conversations with others. Therefore, we observed and transcribed interactions between Todd and his visitors and confirmed that communication between Todd and his visitors wa mostly one-way communication. We created a medical and comfort ontology and an AAC system prototype for the evaluation that allows users to express medical and comfort needs with and without context-aware user profiles (see Appendix A).

Ten non-disabled university students (five male and five female) participated in the evaluation. Our small sample size and use of non-disabled subjects are typical when evaluating the proof-of-concept of an AAC system before testing with a more sensitive user group (eg, Yu et al., 2014; Zander & Jatzev, 2012). Each participant completed a practice communication task with the AAC system without context-aware user profiles to familiarise themselves with navigating through the interface and making selections. Then, each participant completed three communication tasks. The tasks varied in difficulty and were representative of conversations between visitors and individuals with severe motor disabilities based on an analysis of transcripts between visitors and AAC system users. The participants conveyed the following utterances using the AAC system with and without context-aware user profiles:

Task A. “I'm tired”

Task B. “Adjust head to the middle”

Task C. “Ears sore”

We varied the order of systems used to complete each task for each participant to reduce the threat of learning effects. Each participant completed all three tasks with and without context-aware user profiles (ie, six tasks total: three tasks without context-aware user profiles and three tasks with context-aware user profiles).

We calculated the time each user needed to complete each communication task with and without the context-aware system. As shown in Table 7, the average completion time for each task improved between nearly 20% and 60% wit the incorporation of context-aware user profiles. After completing each communication task, the participant provided their perception of the speed of selection on a 9-point Likert scale (with nine being the fastest). The participants consid ered the perceived speed of completion for communication tasks with a context-aware user profile faster than complet ing communication tasks without a context-aware user profile. Lastly, we identified the error rate for selections. Users could make two types of errors: (a) selection errors in which the participant made an incorrect selection and (b) oversca errors in which the participant missed selecting the appropriate response as the options “scrolled by”, which required th participant to wait for the next iteration. No participants made selection errors in any of the tasks. Two participants mad selection errors for one task for the context-aware user profile. This selection was easy to miss since the context-aware user profile had “head” as the first item and the participants were less familiar with the system.

We used this evaluation to consider how to improve the design of AAC systems. The context-aware user profile improved the transmission rate of a need (ie, I'm tired ) with no difference in the accuracy of the message. Our novel AAC system design, in which individuals express needs using symbols and phrases, emphasises the value of developing AAC systems to initiate communication of specific needs (eg, medical and comfort needs) that use alter native symbols and selections to improve communication. Considering the insights gained throughout the research process, we leverage our new knowledge to create design principles that capture prescriptive knowledge (Gregor Kruse, & Seidel, 2020).

## 6 | DISCUSSION

In this section, we derive a series of design principles, explain how our findings inform existing MST research and practice and discuss limitations and opportunities for future research.

## 6.1 | Design principles

These prescriptive design principles explain the knowledge developed through this research effort that can be used to improve the design of AAC systems for individuals with severe motor disabilities.

## 6.1.1 | Improve transmission velocity

The first design requirement states the need to improve the transmission rate by AAC systems. The evaluation identifies that an AAC system design with context-aware user profiles improves the perceived and actual transmission rate for com municating medical and comfort needs

MST recognises the importance of transmission velocity as a media capability that affects the communication processes. Media with high levels of transmission velocity enable the sender and receiver to send and respond quickly, which improves coordination and focus (Dennis et al., 2008). As observed, face-to-face interaction with an AAC system and a non-disabled person consists of asymmetric transmission velocity. One party has low transmissio velocity because of the mediating role of the AAC system, and the other party has high transmission velocity as a person without the need for an AAC system for communication. Consistent with MST and our evaluation of context-aware user profiles, improving the transmission velocity for the AAC system user can support more coordi nation and shared understanding among the communication participants.

However, in this application of MST, there is an additional consideration. Proposition 1 of MST identifies that higher synchronicity media will improve communication performance for convergence communication pro cesses; lower synchronicity media will improve communication performance for conveyance communication processes. For many AAC system users, media synchronicity is extremely low primarily due to the AAC system' low levels of transmission velocity. Media synchronicity is “the extent to which the capabilities of a communication medium enable individuals to achieve synchronicity , and synchronicity is a state in which individuals are working together at the same time with a common focus” (Dennis et al., 2008, p. 581). Proposition 1 of MST likely assumes individuals are using communication media that do not have the same limitations as AAC systems for transmitting messages. Dennis et al. (2008) compare various types of communication media, such as face-to-face communication, email, instant messaging, fax and documents. While some AAC system user can receive and process information at the same rates of these communication media, other AAC system user with severe motor disabilities may experience transmission velocity more comparable to telegraph or sema phore. As a result, AAC system designers should improve transmission velocity in whatever means are possibl without concern for identifying if the communication task is comprised of mostly convergence or conveyance processes.

Design Principle 1. AAC system designers should improve transmission velocity for both conveyance and convergenc communication processes for AAC system users because of the low transmission rates in AAC systems for user with severe motor disabilities.

## 6.1.2 | Support alternative symbol sets

Context-aware user profiles provide a means to restrict the range of selection options available to an AAC system user. This filtering of options lessens the AAC system user's selection space, which provides a way to increase transmission velocity. Consistent with our observations of how context-aware user profiles could be used for meaningfu conversation, we suggest additional alternatives to improve symbol sets in AAC systems. For example, once th user's profile is established, the word basketball or a graphic of a basketball or favourite team logo could be th symbol used within the AAC system. This enables the user to select a single value as opposed to spelling out letter by letter the word, “basketball”, as a topic for conversation. Because the user profile is also context-aware, once “basketball” is selected, other choices could be presented based on user preferences, the time of year, team schedules and team performance. As another example of how we can vary or filter options for communication is the AAC system prototype developed specifically to enable individuals to communicate their medical and comfort needs more intuitively. The prototype (see Appendix A) enables AAC system users to select an area of the body, which narrow selection options to identify the area of the body that needs attention and the type of attention required

According to MST, another media capability that affects media synchronicity is the choice of symbol sets used for communication, including physical gestures, spoken word, written text, emojis and pictures. Proposition 4a and 4b state that more “natural symbol sets” and “a symbol set better suited to the content of the message” will support media synchronicity (Dennis et al., 2008, p. 586). With AAC systems, the symbols used, whether they are letters words, phrases, icons or other symbols that represent the intended message, affect the level of media synchronicit supported by the system.

In this research, we identified the critical role of symbol sets as a media capability that can improve the communication process for both medical and comfort needs (Design Requirement 2) and meaningful conversation (Design Requirement 3). With a context-aware user profile, the user can select among conversation topics based on the shared preferences and past interactions and the visitor. Some AAC systems already enable individuals to hav reduced symbol sets by enabling the user to choose among specific phrases for communication (McNaughton & Light, 2014). If these phrases are chosen based on generic or common phrases or words, then it may be difficult fo an AAC system user to share that they want to talk about last night's Toronto Raptors basketball game or that the want to face the window. Our AAC system prototype used body-focused symbols to enable AAC system users to easily select and communicate needs, such as being tired or wanting to face a different direction. The evaluation pro vides insights that alternative symbol sets can be used in conjunction with context-aware user profiles to furthe improve the communication process. Our research demonstrates that when the range of the communication topic is narrow (such as medical and comfort needs), symbol sets applicable to the intended message offers a means for AAC system users to express their needs (consistent with Proposition 4b in MST (Dennis et al., 2008)). However, additional creativity may be needed to extend similar creative symbol sets to improve media synchronicity when the con versation topic is wide-ranging or highly specific to the user (eg, meaningful conversation about hobbies, interests o family members).

Design Principle 2. AAC system designers should choose intuitive, specific, and narrow symbol sets for the AAC system user (as opposed to generic or non-specific symbol sets) to improve media synchronicity because of the low trans mission rates in AAC systems for users with severe motor disabilities

## 6.1.3 | Enable rehearsability

The fourth design requirement states that an AAC system user should be able to initiate communication with others Traditionally, many AAC system users can only respond to questions asked by the other parties in the conversation Our artificial evaluation (Section 4.3) and natural evaluation (Section 5) demonstrates the value of context-awar user profiles to enable an AAC system user to specify the topic of interest or identify the medical or comfort need without having to answer a series of questions by others. In addition to initiating topics of conversation or specifying a medical or comfort need without being asked, another opportunity to improve AAC system design is to enable users to queue messages for caregivers or other visitors during plentiful idle time. As explained in our discussion of the context of the problem, many individuals with severe motor disabilities spend large amounts of time alone. Fur thermore, many of these individuals we interacted with did not have access to an AAC system unless a researcher was present. Having an AAC system to develop messages for visitors before they arrive can facilitate the communi cation process and enable the AAC system user to initiate the communication process with a caregiver or loved one

According to MST, rehearsability reduces media synchronicity because it lowers the shared focus of the communica tion participants since the medium allows individuals to take more time in crafting messages (Dennis et al., 2008). Th delay in crafting and sending a message lowers the shared focus among the communication parties, meaning media syn chronicity is lower when the media capability of rehearsability is present. In this context, we propose that media synchro nicity can sometimes be improved or reduced by incorporating rehearsability, which is inconsistent with MST.

As context-aware user profiles and alternative symbol sets improve transmission velocity for AAC system users, a disparity between the transmission velocity for AAC system users and non-disabled users will still exist. The AAC system user must encode and transmit communication messages through an AAC system. Rehearsability that allows an AAC sys tem user to queue messages to expected visitors could improve media synchronicity to allow a conversation to occur as a visitor enters the room. The AAC system user would have the time to select and craft messages without the pressur of others watching and would have time to make corrections before the visitor's arrival. This would improve the commu nication process for the AAC user to enable them to initiate topics of conversations with visitors

Furthermore, the AAC system could support rehearsability by enabling the AAC system user to confirm that th selection was correct before displaying the result to the other conversational participant. If the user meant to select “Ears sore”, but accidentally selected “Head sore”, the caregiver would begin an unnecessary intervention that ma result in additional pain. Enabling the AAC user to confirm the message before sharing it with others would limit th number of errors and provide further improvement to the communication process.

However, rehearsability that allows the AAC system user to confirm a message before the other party receive the message would slow down transmission velocity, consistent with Proposition 5 of MST. If a message is not queued, the non-AAC system user must wait for the AAC system user to not only encode the message in the system through selection, but also confirm or correct the message. While rehearsability reduces transmission velocity rehearsability could improve shared understanding among conversational participants. Since the communication pro cess seeks to arrive at shared understanding (Dennis et al., 2008), rehearsability can still improve the communication process even if transmission velocity is slower with this design feature

Design Principle 3. AAC system designers should improve rehearsability for AAC system users to improve communicatio processes because rehearsability empowers the AAC system user to initiate communication with others.

## 6.1.4 | Encourage adaptability

Design requirement 5 states the need for a solution to allow an AAC system user to engage in communication across a range of conversational participants, both familiar and unfamiliar. Through our observations of individuals wit severe motor disabilities, many lived at a facility and some received care at home. Most of the time, these individual were around familiar people, such as loved ones or caregivers who regularly attended to their physical, medical, and comfort needs. However, sometimes new visitors, such as a new caregiver or a new friend or family member, woul want to engage in conversation with the individual. When an unfamiliar person engages in conversations, there can be challenges in reading communication cues with or without an AAC system. Our context-aware user profile design addresses this issue by creating a user profile for the AAC system user and familiar visitors. Known visitors woul have an established profile, and the system could adapt based on the past interactions between the user and visitor Unknown visitors would not have an established profile, and the AAC system could offer different choices to th AAC system user to adapt to this circumstance

MST acknowledges differences in communication processes as contexts and parties range from familiar to nove (Dennis et al., 2008). Proposition 7 of MST states that when tasks, media, and communication participants are more familiar, there is less need for media with high levels of media synchronicity; as tasks, media, and communication participants become less familiar, having media with higher levels of media synchronicity becomes more important. In this case, we are not advocating for decreasing the media's ability to support media synchronicity. However, we acknowledge that media synchronicity will likely be lower when elements of the context are unfamiliar. There is potential to improve the communication process by adapting AAC system elements, such as adjusting the symbo sets available for selection when an AAC system users engage with an unfamiliar visitor or topic. Additionally, the context-aware user profile could learn and adapt as topics and visitors become more familiar to the AAC sys tem user.

Design Principle 4. The AAC system should adapt to AAC system user needs by adapting elements (such as the context aware user profiles and symbol sets) to adjust for the familiarity of the conversational participants, tasks an media.

## 6.2 | Implications for theory and practice

We gained new insights related to MST through our DSR approach to improve communication processes for AAC users with severe motor disabilities. First, there can be a high degree of disparity in the transmission velocity among communication participants due to differences in the inputs used for the communication media, such as when a non disabled person and an AAC system user are engaging in a face-to-face conversation. In this scenario, the communi cation participants are face-to-face. One can communicate via speech, which ranges from 100 to 270 words pe minute (wpm); the other participant uses an AAC system with only 13 selections per minute. These individuals have different input rates for their communication media, which results in an unequal level transmission velocity among the conversation participants. This disparity in the rate of transmission velocity can occur in non-AAC system con texts as well. For example, assume two individuals are communicating via chat message. One communication partici pant uses a computer with keyboard as the input device and types at a speed of 80 wpm; the other participant use a virtual keyboard on a smartphone as an input device and can only type at a speed of 25 wpm. Although the com munication media is the same (ie, chat), the transmission velocity can vary among the individuals in part due to differences in the input devices used.

Little research has specifically examined transmission velocity in the context of MST (eg, Wang, Pauleen, & Zhang, 2016). Future research could more specifically examine the role of transmission velocity and its impact in th communication process, particularly if there are differences in the rate of input among individuals using a communi cation media. MST, as developed and described by Dennis et al. (2008), does not consider variations in transmission velocity among communication participants. Therefore, future research could examine whether transmission velocity disparities among communication participants is a potential boundary condition that affects the propositions identi fied within MST

Second, although MST defines tasks broadly, the communication media typically considered part of this theory has been more consistent with a workplace (eg, face-to-face, telephone, email, fax, documents, among others) and activities that occur in a work environment. However, the theory is broad enough to enable applications of MST to a range of contexts, such as education (Sun & Wang, 2017) or humanitarian efforts (Muhren et al., 2009). While ther are some stated boundary conditions of MST, such as the expectation that individuals are seeking to collaborat (as opposed to deceive) during communication, there may be other boundary conditions of this theory to explore, particularly as it relates to identifying the minimum levels of media capabilities required for synchronicity. If medi synchronicity is low due to low levels of specific media capabilities (eg, transmission velocity of the AAC system) then certain propositions regarding the need for lower levels of media synchronicity for certain types of communication processes may become irrelevant. Media capabilities can degrade in certain contexts, such as natural or manmade disasters, when communication media (eg, phone and internet service) are unavailable or exhibit latency at a time when communication is necessary. Our context reveals the need to examine the minimum threshold for media synchronicity before considering whether media synchronicity needs to be adjusted higher or lower to support con veyance or convergence processes. This provides an interesting opportunity for additional research to examine the minimal levels of media synchronicity needed to support various propositions within MST.

Also, several elements of MST extended quite well to our research context and offered additional insight on communication processes. Considering how various communication tasks with a person with severe motor disabil ities can have differing degrees of conveyance and convergence communication processes offers insights on how AAC systems need to adapt and shift to changing environments. One implication for both research and practice is for AAC system designers to consider the need for flexibility in AAC systems when creating these systems.

As a contribution to practice, we develop and explain how context-aware user profiles offer one potential solu tion to address the design requirements and design principles. Context-aware user profiles offer an opportunity to improve multiple media capabilities simultaneously, which increases media synchronicity. In addition, context-awar user profiles help humanise individuals with severe motor disabilities by highlighting their shared interests wit others.

We also identify a series of design principles, informed by the theoretical lens of MST, to identify how to improve media synchronicity for AAC system users. We specifically created these design principles to be indepen dent of the use of context-aware user profiles to achieve these goals. This approach yields flexibility for others that seek to find alternative options (as opposed to context-aware user profiles) to address the problem identified in thi research.

Developing AAC systems with increased media synchronicity provides an opportunity to increase the quality of life for an individual with severe motor disabilities. AAC systems using context-aware user profiles and embracing the design principles could enable the individual with a severe motor disability to better express their needs to care givers and maintain and establish social relationships with loved ones. Caregivers, such as doctors and nurses, could provide a better standard of care by knowing more about the needs and preferences of a patient who has little abilit to communicate with others, leading to a better patient–caregiver relationship. Friends and family could have mor engaging conversations and better understand the hopes, desires, and interests with an individual who must commu nicate using an AAC system.

This research can also stimulate new ideas on how to further improve AAC systems' design beyond the communication process itself. While our research intent and research approach focused on how to improve the communication process, the benefits of context-aware user profiles could provide additional benefits. For example, integrating an individual's electronic medical record with the context-aware user profile could improve one's medical care. An individual expressing specific combinations of medical needs or a frequent request for the medical or comfort need may indicate a problem that needs to be addressed (eg, warning sign for potential illness, adjustment needed to bed ding). The AAC system could also be a source of information, particularly to new caregivers or visitors about the individuals' preferences, current mood, and preferred conversation topics, which can generate rapport and improve the quality of interactions for both the individual with the severe motor disability and the visitor.<sup>4</sup>

## 6.3 | Limitations and future research

Our Strategy 2 approach (Iivari, 2015) and outcome-focused DSR methodology (Peffers et al., 2018) provided both opportunities and challenges. We began this research effort with a specific context in mind (ie, individuals with severe motor disabilities using AAC systems). We were able to identify a solution to address a specific need by creat ing a new design of AAC systems, one with context-aware user profiles. However, this approach to identifying research question and solution can present challenges in identifying opportunities to provide insights and generalisa tions beyond the initial context (Iivari, 2015). To achieve this goal, we provide a set of design principles related to AAC system design based on our research findings and the theoretical lens of MST.

Additional limitations of our research are related to our approach to evaluating our artefact. Implementing and embedding context-aware user profiles within an AAC system is quite complicated, particularly when working wit individuals with severe motor disabilities. For example, we could not incorporate a context-aware user profile to examine if this intervention would allow Todd to engage in meaningful conversations with others because Todd pas sed away during this research study. Hence, the evaluation remains at the conceptual level. Furthermore, there ar logistical challenges to identifying participants, obtaining consent among participants, and controlling for disabilit differences if we were to include the intended population of AAC system users within the study. Our protocol is consistent with other studies that evaluate assistive technologies on non-disabled participants first. Future researc should further develop implementations of context-aware user profiles and the design principles within AAC system and evaluate these systems among AAC system users with motor disabilities

Our artefact design and design principles found support to develop AAC systems with improvements in thre media capabilities identified in MST: transmission velocity, symbol sets, and rehearsability. We did not consider how parallelism or reprocessibility could be included or excluded to affect media synchronicity among AAC system users Future research should consider additional media capabilities that can further improve media synchronicity for AAC system users with severe motor disabilities.

Finally, the scope of our research has focused on individuals with severe motor disabilities that impact com munication. Our research considered individuals with full cognitive and sensory abilities (eg, sight and hearing) We did not consider the design of AAC systems for those with cognitive or other sensory disabilities. Further more, many individuals within this population with severe motor disabilities may have conditions that worsen over time. Individuals with diseases or disorders that cause progressively worse symptoms may experience a decline in their cognitive and sensory abilities. As a result, the individual, unfortunately, may no longer be able to use the original AAC system or the system would need to adapt to the individual's abilities. Future research ma consider developing systems or design principles to support individuals who have disabilities that become pro gressively worse over time.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

This research seeks to identify how to improve the design of AAC systems to improve communication processes for individuals with severe motor disabilities. To achieve this goal, we used a DSR process to identify a possible solution: context-aware user profiles incorporated in the design of AAC systems. We evaluated how context-aware user pro files could support AAC system users with severe motor disabilities to engage in conversations about medical and comfort needs and more meaningful conversations with visitors. We examined our findings using the theoretical lens of MST to identify design principles that can be used by AAC system designers to improve communication processes for AAC system users. While our design principles are consistent with the incorporation of context-aware user profiles within an AAC system, this prescriptive knowledge is abstract enough to encourage others to consider addi tional solutions to further improve the design of AAC systems. We also identified implications for theory an practice based on our findings. Our results are intended to improve communication processes that would enabl individuals with severe motor disabilities to experience significant enhancements in their quality of life, thereb enabling them to participate in society more fully.

## A. APPENDIX: DEVELOPMENT OF A MEDICAL COMFORT ONTOLOGY

Before we could create a context-aware AAC system for medical and comfort needs, we needed an ontology for thi domain. To create a medical comfort ontology, we extracted information regarding the comfort and care of peopl with severe motor disabilities, terms and phrases from various sources, including related literature and patient observation.
