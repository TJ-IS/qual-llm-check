## Abstract

Regulations, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), establish standards to protect patients' medical records from security breaches. Insiders' prosocial misbehaviour within healthcare organisations can cause significant damage to relevant stakeholders. Such behaviour without malicious intention needs to be better understood and carefully managed from the perspective of prosocial behaviour. For this study, a research model was developed that includes the factors influencing student nurses' intention to disclose patient health information. The model was empirically tested with nursing students in South Korea with a scenario-based experiment. We find that both altruistic (impact on others) and egoistic (impact on the self) motivations are significantly important in raising situational empathy. On the other hand, an egoistic motivation (impact on the self) significantly affects people's perception of their responsibility, which mediates the relationship between situational empathy and prosocial intention to disclose. The implications of our findings are discussed.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Protecting patient health information is one of the key security issues for stakeholders, such as patients and hospi tals, in the healthcare industry. Security breaches in the healthcare industry have significantly increased in recent years (Abouelmehdi et al., 2018). In 2016, an average of one healthcare breach per day was reported in the Unite States; over the course of that year, over 27 million patient records in 47 states were affected (Dietsche, 2017) Forty-three percent of the breaches were caused by insiders, over 80% of whom were healthcare provider (Dietsche, 2017). Costs associated with data breaches in the healthcare industry are as high as \$5.6 billion annuall (Munro, 2015).

Many countries have laws and regulations in place to protect patient health information. For example, in the United States, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) established standards for healthcar organisations and stakeholders to comply with for the protection of individuals' medical records and health informa tion. Similarly, South Korea enacted the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) in 2011 (Greenleaf & Park, 2012); however, prior studies noted that regulations alone cannot safeguard patients' medical information (Parks et al., 2017). Despite regulations, insider security breaches take place at many health organisations (Blanke & McGrady, 2016).

Managing security breaches by insiders is crucial to ensuring information security. Insider abuse, includin breaches of privacy, is reported to be a prevalent cause of data leaks and accounts for more than 40% of reporte incidents (Peters, 2009). Insider breaches, which are more prevalent than breaches caused by outside hackers, can be classified into two categories: malicious abuse and non-malicious misbehaviour. Non-malicious misbehaviour is more common than malicious abuse but can still cause serious damage to organisations. Crossler et al. (2013) note that research on non-malicious misbehaviour is needed to expand the understanding of insider abuse and to improve the protection of information systems (IS) from insider abuse. Prior studies on security management consider this insiders' misbehaviour a deviant and non-compliant behaviour that can take place when committing the behaviour is in their interest and controls are insufficient. From this point of view, managing self-interests and increasing securit controls can reduce deviant behaviours by insiders; however, this view may limit the understanding of non-maliciou misbehaviours.

A non-malicious misbehaviour by insiders is often pro-social, involving complex assessments of situational, moti vational and personal factors. This behaviour is neither a simple act of pure selfishness nor an act of altruism. Prior research suggests that a non-malicious misbehaviour by insiders has a prosocial nature influenced by both altruistic and egoistic motivations (Dozier & Miceli, 1985). This non-malicious misbehaviour is called a prosocial rule-breaking (PSRB) behaviour that occurs at organisations when employees intentionally violate policies/regulations to promote the benefits of organisations and stakeholders (Morrison, 2006). Insiders consider many factors as they attempt to satisfy stakeholders' needs while perceiving themselves trapped by rigid rules. Prior prosocial behaviour studies (Eisenberg & Spinrad, 2014; Penner et al., 2005) emphasise a need for studying the combination of various factors associated with prosocial behaviours. Investigating these factors and their relationships would be useful to improv the understanding of insiders' PSRB and to help healthcare organisations improve their security management.

Motivated by this research gap and need, we investigate factors related to PSRB in the healthcare context i which committing PSRB could reduce perceived impacts on others and the self. In doing so, we purposefully examine a situational factor at two different levels (e.g. HIV and mild cold). Drawing on Dozier and Miceli's (1985) PSRB model in the context of whistle blowing, we find that people assess a given situation in terms of impacts to other and themselves before committing PSRB behaviours. While personal characteristics have been discussed in IS security studies, they have received little attention in insiders' PSRB in the healthcare security context. In this research we tested the relationships between the two situationally induced personal characteristics (empathy and responsibil ity) and the elements of the PSRB model adapted from Dozier and Miceli (1985).

One of the most vulnerable insider groups within healthcare organisations is the group of student nurses who work at hospitals as part of their training (Cannon & Caldwell, 2016; Skiba, 2011; Thompson & Bell, 2007

Wimberley et al., 2005). Student nurses are still in the process of developing their internal value systems and may not fully understand the importance of patient privacy and the impact of privacy violations. Nursing schools educate students on the significance of patient information security by including various security awareness programmes on health information security regulations and the severity of punishment for breaches in the curricula (E. H. Par et al., 2017). Thus, student nurses are taught that patient information should be protected regardless of the severit of the health issues involved. They are also taught that hospitals deploy security technologies to detect any security breaches (E. H. Park et al., 2017); however, the way other factors, such as situational factors (disease severity), influ ence a nurse's intention to disclose patient health information is not known.

In this paper, we investigate the factors influencing insiders' intention to disclose patient health information and their relationships. An in-depth review of prior literature revealed three types of factors affecting PSRB: (i) situatio (i.e. disease severity), (ii) motivational assessment (i.e. perceived impact on others and the self) and (iii) situationall induced personal characteristics (i.e. situational empathy and responsibility). These factors can help understand stu dent nurses' PSRB behaviours, such as disclosing patient health information. Based on the literature review, w attempt to address the following research questions:

1. What are the roles of motivational assessment factors (i.e. perceived impact on others [patient and family] and the self) in the context of a patient's disease severity?

2. What are the roles of the two personal characteristic factors, namely situational empathy and responsibility, in disclosure?

To investigate the research questions, we developed an integrated model that incorporates the factors men tioned. Our study has several contributions to extant research on information security by filling the research gap in understanding the roles of motivational assessment factors and situationally induced personal characteristic factors in PSRB intention. First, we provide a novel view of PSRB and expand the understanding of health information security violations and PSRB intentions in the healthcare context. Second, our study identifies motivational assessment and situationally induced personal characteristics that influence the prosocial intention to disclose patient healt information. Third, we identify how motivational assessment factors work differently and how situationally induced personal characteristics, such as responsibility, play mediation roles in PSRB intention. We empirically examined the model by collecting and analysing data from student nurses in South Korea. In the following section, we review th related literature and present the theoretical model. We then report our research methodology and the empirica tests of our model. Finally, we discuss our findings and present our conclusions.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

In this section, we first briefly discuss the relevant literature on PSRB and health information security violation that informed the development of our research model. Next, we explicate the development of the hypotheses in th research model in detail.

## 2.1 | Prosocial rule breaking

PSRB occurs when an individual intentionally violates a regulation, policy, or ban to facilitate the well-being of stakeholders (Morrison, 2006). Prosocial behaviours refer to the actions that are performed with intentions to facilitat the well-being of other people or a group (De Groot & Steg, 2009). Here, rule refers to an explicitly established pol icy, prohibition or regulation in formal organisations or government bodies that prescribes members' responsibilitie and requirements regarding how to execute their work (Morrison, 2006).

Prior prosocial behaviour studies (Eisenberg & Spinrad, 2014; Penner et al., 2005) emphasise a need for studying the combination of various factors associated with prosocial behaviours and for developing a broad and integrativ understanding. Prior studies (shown Table 1) have identified factors associated with situations, motivation, personali ties and propensities in PSRB behaviours. Although prior studies show that these factors influence prosocial behav iours, their examination is not sophisticated enough to explain the complicated prosocial rule-breaking behaviours of student nurses, for which dynamics of various factors are involved.

Our aim was to fill the research gap mentioned by developing and empirically testing a research model that is based on decision-making models proposed by prior studies (Dozier & Miceli, 1985; Gundlach et al., 2003; Latané & Darley, 1970). These models explain that individuals assess a certain situation and induce their responsibility for a prosocial behaviour to address the situation (Dozier & Miceli, 1985; Latané & Darley, 1970). Individuals also analyse a particular situation, which triggers their emotions, ultimately leading to a prosocial behaviour (Gundlac et al., 2003). Our research model includes situation factors, motivational assessments and situationally induced indi vidual characteristics (see Figure 1). Our basic model explains that given a certain situation, subjects are motivated to assess the situational impact on others and the self. This assessment would induce an individual's situational propen sities, which in turn influence the subject's PSRB. Situational factors indicate external circumstances or environment that are likely to affect a prosocial behaviour (Penner et al., 2005). They affect an individual's perception of externa environments (Johnston et al., 2016). Factors related to motivational assessment explain how individuals are motivated to adopt a prosocial behaviour (Batson et al., 2008). Individuals' motivational assessments can involve cognitiv processing, such as awareness of others and the self and perspective taking of others (Segal, 2011). Situational factors can influence individuals' assessment of altruistic and egoistic motivations (Malti & Dys, 2018). In particular when faced with a certain situation, individuals assess the impact on others (altruistic motivation) and the impact on the self (egoistic motivation). This assessment may trigger situationally induced personal characteristics, which are strong predictors of prosocial behaviours (Penner et al., 2005; Segal, 2011). ## 2.2 | Health information security violation as a PSRB behaviour

Information security violation in the healthcare context is a socially complex behaviour that requires a nove approach to investigate. In prior IS security behaviour studies, the violation of information security was investigated from various perspectives: computer abuse by unauthorised, deliberated misuse of IS assets (Lowry et al., 2015; Willison, Lowry, & Paternoster, 2018; Willison, Warkentin, & Johnston, 2018), unethical, illegal use of IT (Chatterjee et al., 2015), violation of policy by negligence (Siponen & Vance, 2010), security violation and non-compliance with out malicious intentions (Guo et al., 2011; Willison & Warkentin, 2013), and IS security policy abuse by unauthorised access to or selling confidential data (Hu et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2019). Unlike these prior IS security studies, our study views disclosing patient health information as a prosocially motivated rule-breaking behaviour, which is a viola tion of laws and regulations, such as HIPAA (Hader & Brown, 2010) and PIPA (Greenleaf & Park, 2012). Hader and Brown (2010) reported the following PSRB case that resulted in a violation of law: with good motive, a nurse at a physician's hospital disclosed a patient's pregnancy and request for advice about an abortion to the patient's mothe over the phone. The patient's mother was upset by the news. The patient claimed that she suffered reputational an emotional harm as well as financial losses and damages. The court accepted the claim and ruled that the physician was liable for this case (Hader & Brown, 2010; Hobbs v. Lopez, 2002).

PSRB has also been observed in student nurses' disclosing behaviours, which is our interest, Student nurses who have prosocial motives (e.g. promoting good relationships with family to improve patient care) may intentionally dis close patients' health information, resulting in the violation of laws and regulations pertaining to health informatio protection, such as HIPAA (Wimberley et al., 2005) and PIPA (Greenleaf & Park, 2012). By committing such deviant behaviours, student nurses, as a group of healthcare providers, can inflict financial loses, reputational damage and psychological distress on patients, their families and even the student nurses themselves (Hader & Brown, 2010)

Despite the significant findings of PSRB in organisations, there are several research gaps. First, considering its potential significance and complexity, PSRB in the context of healthcare information security must be better understood. Second, although prior PSRB studies (Dahling et al., 2012; Morrison, 2006) recognise the well-being of others and of the self as fundamental motivations for PSRB, the motivational mechanism leading to disclosing patient health information must be investigated. Drawing on the PSRB literature, we aim to achieve an integrative understanding of PSRB intention. Figure 1 shows Dozier and Miceli's (1985) PSRB decision-making model developed from whistle-blowing literature. Explaining how assessment and personal responsibility affect PSRB, this model provides the basic theoretical lens for our study, which extends the model in the context of healthcare information security. According to Dozier and Miceli (1985), when an individual becomes aware of a situation, s/he assesses whether PSRB (in their case, internal whistle blow ing) ought to be committed. Then, s/he considers whether s/he has the responsibility to commit PSRB, which in tur affects his/her willingness to commit PSRB. This basic PSRB model with three elements is based on Latane and Darley's bystander intervention model in an emergency context (Latane & Darley, 1968). In an emergency, individ uals commit PSRB behaviour for common benefits without considering wrongdoings. Therefore, this basic PSRB model is applicable to internal non-malicious misbehaviour. Extending the basic PSRB model, our research model is comprised of the following paths: (i) a situational factor (disease severity) to factors related to motivational assess ment (perceived impact on others and the self), (ii) factors related to motivational assessment to situationally induced personal characteristics (situational empathy and responsibility), (iii) situational empathy to responsibility and (iv) situationally induced personal characteristics related to PSRB intention (Figure 2).

## 2.2.1 | Situational factor

Prior studies suggest that situational factors directly and indirectly influence prosocial behaviours. For instance, th perception of rewards equity, a situational factor, is found to directly influence prosocial organisational behaviour (McNeely & Meglino, 1994). In a PSRB study, it was found that situational factors, such as autonomy (Morrison, 2006; Youli et al., 2014) and ethical climate (Vardaman et al., 2014), may affect rule-breaking behaviours. In contrast, situational factors may indirectly affect prosocial behaviours by influencing an individual's emotions (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986), perception (Archer et al., 1981) and motivation (Jex et al., 2003; Sherrod & Downs, 1974). For example, organisational climate and stressors influence feelings of dis/satisfaction of employees which in turn likely influence prosocial behaviours (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986). Similarly, a situational factor may influence people's altruistic/egoistic motivation in the context of PSRB (Jex et al., 2003; Sherrod & Downs, 1974).

This study identifies disease severity as a situational factor. Prior studies note disease severity as an essentia construct influencing healthcare professionals' assessments of various factors and their behaviours in the context of medical care (Menon et al., 2012; York & MacAlister, 2015). Disease severity refers to how serious a patient's diseas is (Goldbeck & Melches, 2006). For example, a minor flu is a low-level disease, while HIV is a highly severe on because it involves a major reduction in estimated life expectancy and a high level of residual risk without proper medical treatments (Goldbeck & Melches, 2006). Patient's disease severity is likely to increase tension levels and security concerns among various stakeholders, such as healthcare professionals, family members, domestic violenc victims and gang members (York & MacAlister, 2015). When patients are severely ill, emergency department healthcare professionals are more likely to use personal health records, which allow them to practice speedy medica care (Menon et al., 2012). Disease severity also negatively influences the quality of life of patients and caregiver (Goldbeck & Melches, 2006).

## 2.2.2 | Motivational assessment

Processing a given context, individuals are aware of the distinction between the self and others. They can take the perspective of the other and assess that the impacts to the self and others may be different (Segal, 2011). Regardless of the noble appearance of prosocial behaviours, the motivational assessment for PSRB can be either altruistic or egoistic. With altruistic motivation, benefiting others is the ultimate goal. With egoistic motivation, benefiting other is a means to benefit the self and thus the ultimate goal is to benefit the self (Batson, 1987; Batson et al., 2008). Th reason for altruistic motivation can be one's desire for ‘another person to be happy and free of distress, not because it is upsetting to them if he or she is not…, but because they care about the other's welfare as an end in itself (Batson et al., 2008, p. 140). Thus, it is essential for persons to consider their own actions' ‘impact on others’ and the relevant risk. It is worth noting that there is a different school of thought suggesting that for altruistic motivation although persons are motivated to promote the other's welfare, they would attain personal pleasure by achievin prosocial goals (Batson et al., 2008). Thus, it is argued that appearance is like altruism, but indeed, it is an outcome of egoism.

A reason for egoistic motivation is that persons can reduce their empathetic arousal and avoid possible socia and self-punishment for failing to help (Batson et al., 2008). Another reason is the motivation to minimise costs whil maximising rewards (Penner et al., 2005). Thus, persons with egoistic motivation are relatively rational. ‘Impact on the self’ from their own actions and the associated risk is important as it is directly related to self-interest. The third reason is the desire to show self-efficacy by helping others (Penner et al1995)

Similarly, the motivations for PSRB behaviours are to promote the well-being of others (such as helping a co worker or subordinate in need and satisfying a customer by offering good services) and ultimately to facilitate th well-being of self (such as their own job execution in a responsive and responsible manner) (Morrison, 2006). ## 2.2.3 | Situationally induced personal characteristics

Personal characteristics develop over time and exhibit different levels per each individual (McElroy et al., 2007). As shown in Table 1, prior studies on PSRB investigated the roles of personal characteristics, such as empathy, risk taking propensity (Morrison, 2006), big five personalities (Curtis, 2010), conscientiousness (Dahling et al., 2012) and neuroticism (Vardaman et al., 2014). Prior studies have shown that personal characteristics are stable and do not change easily (Johnston et al., 2016; McElroy et al., 2007); however, other studies have shown that a particular situation may affect individuals' characteristics (Butrus & Witenberg, 2013; Eisenberg et al., 1994; Fultz et al., 1986) Based on these findings, Preckel et al. (2018) noted that situationally induced personal characteristics need to be captured and treated differently from dispositional characteristics. Situationally induced personal characteristics such as social emotions (e.g. empathy), can predict prosocial behaviours (Preckel et al., 2018).

In this study, as personal characteristics, empathy and responsibility are a particular focus, and their roles are investigated for two reasons. First, empathy is one of the most important individual characteristics because it can facilitate communications with patients, their families and healthcare professionals, thereby improving patient car (Williams & Stickley, 2010). Second, responsibility is another essential characteristic prescribed in the code of ethic for nurses, which emphasises responsibility in promoting patients' health, safety and rights (Lachman et al., 2015 Winland-Brown et al., 2015).

Prior studies (Eisenberg et al., 1994; Fultz et al., 1986) have identified two types of empathy: dispositional empathy and situational empathy. Dispositional empathy refers to individuals' general sensitivity to the emotional experi ences of others and ability to take the perspective of others’ (Morrison, 2006, p. 16). Here, prior studies (Davis, 1983b; Fultz et al., 1986; Mehrabian & Epstein, 1972) capture dispositional empathy as trans-situational dis positional differences. The link between dispositional empathy and prosocial behaviours has been well-established For instance, persons with a high level of empathy tend to recognise others in need and tend to have an altruistic motivation to increase others' well-being (Batson & Moran, 1999). Dispositional empathy has a positive relationship with empathic emotion and personal distress beyond the impact of situational factors and also positively affect helping behaviours (Davis. 1983a)

In contrast, situational empathy differs from dispositional empathy, in that individuals experience empathy in various ways based on particular situations others are in (Fultz et al., 1986; Gurthrie et al., 1997). These difference may result from several factors (Fultz et al., 1986): previous experiences, perceptions associated with the situation and relationships with the persons in the situation. In empathy-inducing contexts, when engaging in a motivationa assessment of the others and the self, each individual tends to cognitively assess the situation in certain way (Segal, 2011) and hence to experience empathy differently (Holmgren et al., 1998). Prior studies show an important role of situational empathy in prosocial behaviours. For example, in a situation devoid of the potential for a negative social evaluation for not helping others, self-reported empathic emotion has a positive relationship with helping behaviours (Fultz et al., 1986). An empathy-related response to a particular event/situation is a significant predictor of prosocial behaviours (Eisenberg et al., 2010; Holmgren et al., 1998). Our study particularly focuses on situationa empathy rather than dispositional empathy because the aim was to investigate the role of motivation assessment activated in a particular medical situation

Responsibility is identified as a significant antecedent to predicting prosocial behaviours in prior studies (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986; Penner et al., 1995). Prior studies (C. Park et al., 2009; Segal, 2011) suggest that a situation assess ment leads to a strong feeling of responsibility to take an action. For instance, in a whistle-blowing study of an IT project, when assessing a troubled project, individuals tend to experience a significant responsibility to report bad news (C. Park et al., 2009). Penner et al. (1995) view the ascription of responsibility (here, referring to the ‘tendency to accept responsibility for the consequence of one's actions’) as a prosocial personality battery (Penne et al., 1995, p. 149).

## 3 | HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

## 3.1 | Situational factor (disease severity) to motivational assessment (impact on others and the self)

Prior studies have identified the influences of disease severity on patients and their families. Severely ill means that patients are critically compromised from a physiological perspective and are closer to death (McMahon Jr & Newbold, 1986). For their families, as the severity of a disease increases, the societal and financial burdens of the disease (e.g. nursing care, physician care and costs of other medical services) increase (Hux et al., 1998). In addition non-financial burdens (e.g. psychological distress experienced by family members) increase (Goldbeck & Melches, 2006).

Thus, we argue that as the severity of a patient's disease increases, student nurses are prosocially motivated to assess the given situation (specifically, altruistic and egoistic motivational assessments) and are more likely to per ceive the serious negative impact on others (i.e., family and patient) if they disclose the patients' health information In a similar situation, student nurses may also assess the well-being of the self (egoistic motivation) and perceive th negative impact on the self by disclosing a patient's health information.

The perceived impact refers to the perceived magnitude of the negative consequences of an impact (C. Park et al., 2009). Perceived impact on others refers to the degree of impact on stakeholders from disclosing health infor mation as perceived by a student nurse. Here, stakeholders, except the self, are the patient and family who ar directly influenced by student nurses' deviant behaviours (Hader & Brown, 2010). From an altruistic motivationa assessment point of view, these two perceived impacts are conceptually grouped together as an impact on others to test the relationships with other constructs. Similarly, perceived impact on the self indicates the degree of impact from disclosing health information that the student nurse would perceive as affecting himself or herself. As patients suffe from serious diseases, it is more likely that student nurses are prosocially motivated to assess the situation and mor easily perceive the significance of the negative impacts of disclosing patient health information (such as financial, reputational repercussions) on patients, their families and themselves. We aim to test how a situational factor, dis ease severity, affects three different stakeholders (family, patient and self) differently from nursing students' point of view. We therefore hypothesise:

H1. Disease severity is positively associated with the perceived impact on (a) the family, (b) the patient and (c) the student nurse (the self).

## 3.2 | Motivational assessment to situational empathy

The assessment involves taking others' perspectives and being aware of distinctively the self and others (Segal, 2011). Such motivational assessment triggers situation-specific empathy that varies based on individuals' dif ferent perceptions and prior experiences associated with the particular situation (Fultz et al., 1986). Individuals expe rience empathy as they consider the others in need, assess impact on others in a situation and value the well-being of the persons (Batson et al., 2008). Here, altruistic desire to reduce another's distress increases empathy. In a simila vein, we posit that student nurses engage in motivational assessment when they face patients and their families in need. Student nurses prosocially motivated to help them tend to feel empathy. With altruistic motivation, the stu dent nurses who assess high negative impacts on others (patient and family) by disclosing patient health information (e.g. a status of HIV screening and treatment) are more likely to feel empathy. Thus, we hypothesise the following:

H2. Perceived impact on others is positively associated with situational empathy.

Regardless of the salient appearance of prosocial behaviour to help patients and their families, student nurses may have egoistic motivation. Motivational assessment with egoistic motivation (ultimately benefiting self) is also likely to induce situation-specific empathy for three reasons (Batson et al., 2008). First, the feeling of empathy for the persons suf fering is distressing, and helping others is a way of reducing such an empathic feeling. Second, individuals learn their obli gation to help others via socialisation. When individuals feel empathy for those in need, they tend to face self or socia censure if they do not help them. They want to avoid consequential punishments. Third, when persons feel empathy, the are likely to consider receiving rewards, such as praise, honour or pride, by helping those in need.

Similarly, student nurses assess negative impacts on themselves from disclosing patient health information. Stu dent nurses who are aware of the social aspect of their behaviour tend to feel empathy when they consider self an social punishments due to their failure to help others (patient and family) (Batson et al., 2008; Penner et al., 2005) ‘What will others think [or what will I think of myself] if I don't help when I feel like this?’ (Batson et al., 2008, p. 141). Through socialisation at schools and hospitals, student nurses learn the obligations of helping patients and their families according to the code of ethics for nurses that prescribes the mandatory ethical duties and responsibili ties of nurses (Winland-Brown et al., 2015). Such socialisation educates student nurses on the importance of having empathy for the suffering patients and their families and helps them internalise empathy (Batson et al., 2008 Hoffman, 2008). Assessing the impact on the self with egoistic motivation, student nurses with socialisation education are prosocially motivated and are likely to continue empathising with others in need. Note that egoistic motiva tion is one of the bases of prosocial motivation to promote prosocial behaviours (Batson et al., 2008; Penne et al., 1995). Hence, we posit that student nurses who perceive high impacts on the self by disclosing patient information tend to feel empathy towards others in need. We hypothesise the following:

H3. Perceived impact on the self is positively associated with situational empathy.

## 3.3 | Motivational assessment to responsibility to disclose

Prior prosocial behaviour studies show that individuals' assessment of a particular situation may lead to their respon sibility to take an action (Dozier & Miceli, 1985; C. Park et al., 2009). For instance, a study of bystanders' helping behaviours (Dozier & Miceli, 1985; Latane & Darley, 1968) shows that bystanders' responsibility to take an actio involves several steps. First, the bystander is aware of the situation. Second, s/he recognises the situation as an emergency, and third, s/he perceives a responsibility to help others in need. Prior studies suggest that responsibilit tends to be associated with altruistic motivation (De Groot & Steg, 2009; Schwartz, 1968; Steg & De Groot, 2010).

With altruistic motivation, individuals perceiving a high negative impact on others if they fail to help are likely to experience responsibility (De Groot & Steg, 2009; Schwartz, 1968; Steg & De Groot, 2010). In our study, responsibil ity to disclose refers to individuals' perception of being accountable for informing the patient's family of the patient' health information to promote others' well-being or the self's job execution in a responsible and responsive manner. This concept is different from the concept of responsibility to report, which prior studies have investigated in the context of whistle-blowing situations. Prior studies show that individuals who perceive the serious impacts of IT project risk (Smith et al., 2001) and failure (C. Park et al., 2009) are more likely to assess their status as needing to be reported. Our study is different from these studies because we investigate the roles of student nurses' prosocia motivational assessment and the responsibility to disclose patients' health information in the context of healthcare Specifically, student nurses assessing a high impact to patients and their families by disclosing patients' health infor mation are less likely to feel the responsibility to disclose. Thus, we hypothesise the following:

H4. Perceived impact on others is negatively associated with responsibility to disclose.

With egoistic motivation, individuals are fundamentally concerned with self-interest and attempt to minimis costs to themselves and to avoid self and social punishment (Batson, 1987; Batson et al., 2008). When the perceived negative impact on student nurses themselves is high, they are more likely to consider the costs involved and the potential consequences (e.g. punishments) of disclosing health information as severe and are thus less likely to fee the responsibility to disclose. We therefore hypothesise the following:

H5. Perceived impact on the self is negatively associated with responsibility to disclose.

## 3.4 | Situational empathy to responsibility to disclose

Chapman et al. (1987) argues that although empathy is considered a key factor for motivating prosocial behaviours, it may not be sufficient. Empathy and a sense of responsibility for others in need are correlated, suggesting that both empathy and responsibility need to be considered when studying prosocial behaviours (Chapman et al., 1987). Similarly Segal (2011) suggests that as individuals feel empathy towards others in need, they are more likely to perceive responsi bility to help them. Here, empathy indicates situation-specific empathy induced by cognitive activities, such as self/other awareness and considering others' perspectives in a given situation. Thus, such empathy promotes individuals' sense of responsibility in terms of prosocial behaviours. In the context of health information security, when student nurses feel situational empathy towards patients suffering from serious disease(s) and their families who are concerned about patient care, they are more likely to feel responsibility to help them. Here, we posit that student nurses with high empath towards patients and families in need would be more likely to feel the responsibility to disclose patient health information to promote communication and to improve patient care. We therefore hypothesise the following:

H6. Situational empathy is positively associated with responsibility to disclose.

## 3.5 | Situational empathy and responsibility to PSRB intention

Prior studies suggest that empathy is a strong predictor of prosocial behaviours (e.g., Bagozzi & Moore, 1994; Batson et al., 2008; Roberts & Strayer, 1996). For instance, Roberts and Strayer (1996) found that empathy is an important contributor of prosocial behaviours, such as helping, sharing and cooperation. Cialdini et al. (1987) report that empa thy for a victim positively influences helping the victim. Batson and Moran (1999) suggest that empathy increase participants' cooperation to give greeting cards to a suffering person to cheer her/him up. Individuals can experience strong empathy when they are aware of others who suffer due to unfortunate situations and take their perspective in the particular situation (Hoffman, 2008). Such empathising with others' emotional states and situations can lea them to feel distressed. Both the concern for others in need and the desire to reduce the feelings of distress can pro mote individuals' prosocial behaviours (Batson et al., 2008; Morrison, 2006). In the context of health information security, as student nurses are more likely to empathise with a patient's suffering and pain from a disease and the family's concern regarding patient care, they are more likely to engage in prosocial behaviours to promote communi cation and relationships with family for improving patient care. Hence, they are prone to have an intention to dis close patient health information, which is a type of PSRB, to promote patient care. We thus hypothesise the following:

## H7. Situational empathy is positively associated with PSRB intention.

Prior studies also show that responsibility is an essential predictor of prosocial behaviours (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986; De Groot & Steg, 2009). The persons with a strong sense of responsibility tend to hold consistent beliefs regarding what they should do (Harrington, 1996). Individuals with high responsibility for others in need are more likely to donate to help others (Willis & Goethals, 1973). A study on whistle-blowing behaviour shows a posi tive relationship between individuals' responsibility to report and their willingness to report bad news for common benefits (C. Park et al., 2009). Individuals who deny their responsibility towards others can develop antisocial behaviours (e.g. computer abuse) (Siponen & Vance, 2010). In the context of health information security, student nurses who feel responsible to disclose patient information would have strong intentions to do so to help patients and thei families, thereby promoting active communications to improve patient care. We posit that student nurses with a strong sense of responsibility to disclose would be more likely to have intentions to disclose information. We therefore hypothesise the following:

H8. Responsibility to disclose is positively associated with PSRB intention.

## 4 | RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

We conducted a laboratory experiment by measuring dependent variables while manipulating independent variables in a controlled environment (Benbasat et al., 1987; Boudreau et al., 2001). The experiment was based on hypotheti cal scenarios to test the proposed research model. We controlled for disease severity. Two scenarios with variou treatment and non-treatment conditions were randomly assigned to participants

The hypothetical scenario approach is an appropriate methodological option because one of the objectives of this study was to evaluate an individual nursing student's intention to disclose patient health information or not (Straub & Karahanna, 1998). Our experiment included a one-factor, two-cell design with one exogenous variable (dis ease severity), which was manipulated at two levels (high/low). We developed two treatment scenarios with two levels of disease severity, as shown in Appendix A. minor flu and HIV infection were used to manipulate disease severity. Both diseases were well-known to subjects, who could easily recognise the level of disease severity. In addition, the scenarios told subjects that while minor flu has little impact on a patient, HIV is lethal without proper treatment. We conducted one pilot test to refine the treatment scenarios and to validate the measures

The participants in our laboratory experiment included 153 Korean student nurses as subjects. Our objective was to develop and test our research model and to determine how the perceived impact on stakeholders (patien: and family) and the self in healthcare organisations would affect student nurses' intention to disclose patient healt information. Prior studies on information security and control commonly used student participants (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Shropshire et al., 2015; Wright & Marett, 2010; H. Xu et al., 2011). Furthermore, many student nurses in our study have working experience as interns at hospitals and intend to become professional nurses a hospitals.

A paper survey was distributed to students during class. Thirty subjects were excluded due to a manipulation check (20) and their incomplete/inconsistent data (10) input. The data from the remaining 123 subjects (19 male and 104 females) with an average age of 21.1 years were used for the analysis. To ensure anonymous data collection and therefore to reduce common method bias, no identification information was requested. Anonymous data collec tion decreases the tendency of subjects to provide answers that are socially desirable or that best meet th researcher's expectations.

All measurement items except for three perceived impacts were drawn from prior research and adapted to ou study context. Particularly, we adapted measures of situational empathy from reviewing both literature on dispositional empathy and situational empathy. Studies with dispositional empathy (e.g., Morrison, 2006) informed us of what to measure, and studies with situational empathy (Fultz et al., 1986; Holmgren et al., 1998) informed us of how to frame the questionnaire to capture empathy experience as a result of reading an assigned scenario. We used a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Appendix B shows the constructs and measures used in the study.

We developed measures of perceived impact on patient, family and the self based on relevant literature (Batson et al., 2008; C. Park et al., 2009; Penner et al., 1995) and followed the four steps suggested by Hinkin (1995) MacKenzie et al. (2011) and Chu and Chau (2014). In the first step, we specified the domain by defining the perceived impact on patient, family and the self by drawing on perceived impact literature (Batson et al., 2008; C. Park et al., 2009; Penner et al., 1995). In the second step, we used a deductive item generation method (Hinkin, 1995) to develop three items per perceived impact and modified the wording of each perceived impact according to the target subjects of impact. We also assessed the content validity of the items using a pilot test with 20 student nurses. Al items demonstrated strong content validity. In the third step, we conducted instrument purification. Following Chu and Chau's (2014) guidelines, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis. All items per perceived impact excee the threshold (0.40) suggested by Hinkin (1995). In the fourth step, the perceived impacts of related constructs, such as empathy and responsibility, were assessed via a confirmatory factor analysis. The results showed that three per ceived impacts exhibited decent fit and were distinct from other constructs.

Table 3 provides the summary statistics for the PSRB intention (PI) by each treatment condition manipulated with disease severity. A preliminary analysis was performed to determine the effects of disease severity on prosocia intention to disclose. The analysis revealed a significant effect associated with disease severity (p < 0.05). This means that subjects are more engaged in PSRB intention when disease severity is low (e.g. very minor flu).

We first compared the means and standard errors of the measurement items. We then used a partial least squares (PLS) analysis of the structural model using SmartPLS 3.2. PLS was considered appropriate for this study because it has minimal demands in terms of measurement scales and distributional assumptions (Ringle et al., 2012). Compared with other similar approaches, such as covariance-based SEM and MANOVA, PLS can capture statistically significant relationships as it exhibits high statistical power even with a small sample size (Hair et al., 2017). In addition, PL allows researchers to examine structural paths among outcome variables and to reduce the effects of omitted vari able bias, thereby leading to more accurate and insightful findings of the phenomenon of study (Streuken et al., 2010). PLS is suitable for predicting theoretical models in the early stages (Lowry & Gaskin, 2014). Our study i explorative and predictive in nature as it focuses on theory development rather than theory confirmation. Compared with regression, PLS is better suited to evaluate a model with multidimensional second-order constructs, such a impact on others (Kim et al., 2014). Following standard procedures, we first assessed the measurement model and then the structural relationships.

## 5.2 | Common method variance

We attempted to avoid CMV by ensuring respondent anonymity. Furthermore, we conducted a CMV analysis using a latent variable test (Schwarz et al., 2017). A latent variable test can detect CMV stemming from baseline, scale format, item priming and embeddedness; however, this test cannot estimate CMV from ambiguous questions and negative words. Our questionnaire's (including scenarios) Flesch–Kincaid reading level is 8.9 and does not contain man negative words, which might have a significant impact on the measurement model. Therefore, we chose a latent vari able test to check CMV. A common factor was added to our model that reflectively comprised all principal construct indicators. Then, we calculated each indicator's substantively explained variance using both the principal construct and the method (Liang et al., 2007). The average substantively explained variance was 0.919, whereas the average method variance was 0.004. The ratio of substantive variance to method variance is about 259.1:1. The small magni tude of the method variance indicated that CMV is not a concern in our study.

## 6 | DISCUSSION

For this study, we developed an integrated model that incorporates a situational factor (disease severity), motiva tional assessment (impact on others and the self ) and situationally induced personal characteristic factors (situationa empathy and responsibility to disclose) to explain the PSRB intention to disclose patient health information. Without a motivational assessment and situationally induced personal characteristic factors, disease severity negativel affects PSRB intention. Our study further investigates how these factors affect the relationship between diseas severity and PSRB intention. Next, we discuss the implications for research and practice in detail

## 6.1 | Implications for research

First, this study contributes to the information security research by providing a novel view of PSRB and expandin our understanding of health information security violations. Prior studies have identified deviant security behaviours from various perspectives: the deterrence theory (e.g., Hensel & Kacprzak, 2020; Kuo et al., 2020; Vance et al., 2020; Willison, Lowry, & Paternoster, 2018), neutralisation (e.g., Gwebu et al., 2020; Siponen & Vance, 2010 Vance et al., 2020; Willison, Warkentin, & Johnston, 2018), rational choice and beliefs (Bulgurcu et al., 2010), emo tions, such as fear, anger and shame (Boss et al., 2015; Vance et al., 2020; F. Xu et al., 2020) and reactance and fairness theories (Lowry et al., 2015). Our study identifies motivational assessment factors and situationally induced personal characteristics that affect PSRB in the healthcare context. In response to the call for research to develop a more integrative and broader understanding of prosocial behaviours (Padilla-Walker & Carlo, 2014; Penne et al., 2005), we developed and empirically tested an integrated PSRB intention model that combines a situationa factor, motivational assessment and situationally induced personal characteristic factors.

Second, Crossler et al. (2013) urged future researchers to separate insider misbehaviours from deviant securit behaviours and to explore the motivations and psychological influencing factors accordingly. Our study answers this call by investigating the motivations and psychological processes when student nurses consider whether to disclos patient health information. Student nurses' disclosing behaviours can be a type of insider misbehaviour rather than a deviant security behaviour because they are acting based on prosocial motivation. They intentionally break rules due to prosocial motives, although the consequences of these behaviours can cause equal damage as that of devian behaviours due to malicious motives.

Prior studies have focused on and explored various phenomena on the violation of information security: unauthorised misuse behaviour on IS assets (Willison, Lowry, & Paternoster, 2018; Willison, Warkentin, & Johnston, 2018), unethical, illegal IT use behaviour (Chatterjee et al., 2015), negligence and ignorance behaviour in policy violation (Siponen & Vance, 2010), non-malicious behaviour in security policy violation (Willison & Warkentin, 2013) and unauthorised access to IS (Wang et al., 2019). To our knowledge, this study is the first to explore the mechanism behind an insider's healthcare security breach from the prosocial behaviour perspective

Third, our study shows an interesting contrast in the relationships between the motivational assessment (impact on others vs. the self) and the situationally induced personal characteristics (situational empathy and responsibility to disclose). When student nurses with altruistic (and maybe some egoistic) motivation perceive the negative impact of a disease on others (patient and family), they are more likely to feel empathy; however, a negative relationship between impact on others and responsibility to disclose is not supported. On the contrary, student nurses with ego istic motivation are more likely to feel empathy while less likely to perceive their responsibility to disclose. We also found a significant positive relationship between situational empathy and responsibility to disclose. These result may offer a plausible explanation for why the negative relationship between impact on others and responsibility to disclose is not supported.

Fourth, our study contributes to the IS research by identifying a situational factor (i.e. disease severity) in the context of health information security. Prior studies (Flores & Ekstedt, 2016; Johnston et al., 2016) urge an explo ration of situational factors as predictors of security policy (non)compliance behaviours because understanding their roles helps develop controls for security violation. Our study identifies disease severity as a critical situa tional factor that can lower patients' quality of life (Goldbeck & Melches, 2006) and place significant financial bur dens on family, friends and even society (Goldbeck & Melches, 2006; Hux et al., 1998). Our study, to our knowledge, is the first to examine how disease severity influences individuals' motivational assessments fo prosocial behaviours in the context of health IS. Our findings suggest that student nurses consider the significant impact on others (patient and family) with an altruistic motive and themselves with an egoistic motive according to the severity of a disease.

Fifth, our study explains the interesting mechanism in which situationally induced personal characteristics (situa tional empathy and responsibility to disclose) play a crucial role in PSRB behaviour in the context of healthcare. A prior study (Johnston et al., 2016) suggests that dispositional personal characteristics are stable over time and can influence security policy violation behaviours and urges further investigation of the roles of personal characteristics Our research answers this research call and contributes to the security compliance research by suggesting that situa tionally induced personal characteristics also influence security policy violation.

We found several notable mediation effects from three variables: responsibility to disclose, situational empathy and impact on the self. In particular, responsibility to disclose fully mediates the relationship between situationa empathy and prosocial intention to disclose. Morrison (2006) noted a non-significant relationship between disposi tional empathy and PSRB but speculated that empathy may have some interaction with situational variables. Callin for a study to assess the relationship between empathy and responsibility towards others in relation to suffering Chapman et al. (1987) suggested that a positive relationship between empathy and helping prosocial behaviours ma exist. Our study adds insights to the role of empathy in a PSRB study. We found that situationally induced empath indirectly influences prosocial intention to disclose via responsibility to disclose. Our study implicates that healthcar organisations need to carefully examine the mediation effects of responsibility, empathy and impact on the self to improve their IS.

Finally, our findings are consistent with those of prior prosocial behaviour studies, suggesting that responsibility (Brief & Motowidlo, 1986; De Groot & Steg, 2009; C. Park et al., 2009) is a significant predictor of prosocial behav iours. In the context of health IS violation, we found that the responsibility to disclose positively affects PSRB inten tion. Harrington (1996) reports a moderating role of responsibility in the relationship between IS codes of ethics an computer abuse intention. Ifinedo (2014) found a positive relationship between responsibility and security compli ance intention. Our study adds insights to these studies by finding a strong positive relationship between the respon sibility to disclose and PSRB intention.

## 6.2 | Implications for practice

The results of this study highlight the importance of education on health security regulations and policies as well as security training in nursing school curricula and healthcare organisations. Student nurses may commit PSRB due to inexperience or lack of knowledge of regulations. With enhanced social responsibility through education, student nurses may develop proper prosocial responsibility that supports the overall well-being of a society without being overly influenced by empathy (Hoffman, 2008). The findings of our study provide important practical implications fo both educators in nursing schools and managers at hospitals. Student nurses are more willing to disclose patient health information when they feel that they have a responsibility to disclose information to promote patient care, resulting in a potential violation of regulations and policies. Situationally induced empathy can trigger the responsibil ity to disclose and therefore positively affect the PSRB behaviour of disclosing information. Educators in nursing schools should make significant efforts to develop school curricula that can improve students' awareness of th security regulations and facilitate their understanding of legal and ethical behaviours. Hospital managers should implement proper security management practices to positively influence nurses' responsibility to follow the rules while reducing their responsibility to disclose

Our findings offer important implications to practitioners in the healthcare industry by suggesting the factors related to prosocial, motivational assessments and the roles of situationally induced personal characteristics in PSRB A prior study (Morrison, 2006) suggests that employees, including hospital managers, with prosocial motives ma break a rule for various reasons: enhancing job efficiency, helping a subordinate and a colleague and improving ser vices for customers. Prosocial factors may influence healthcare workers to break rules (Hyde et al., 2013). Our find ings in the context of PSRB suggest factors this study identifies may be applicable not only to student nurses bu also to other healthcare professionals.

## 7 | LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

Laboratory experimentation imposes certain methodological limitations, although it does provide a highly con trolled environment for hypothesis testing. First, our results are based on the subjects' intention to disclose patient healthcare information rather than their actual behaviour. Prior studies noted that behavioural intention can be a reliable indicator of actual behaviour (Venkatesh et al., 2003); however, capturing and analysing actua behaviour instead of behavioural intention is recommended because behavioural intention may not always lead to actual behaviour in the context of information security (Crossler et al., 2013). Second, in this experiment, the sub jects' decision choices are necessarily simplified options. The situations were framed such that the subjects had two options: to disclose or not to disclose a patient's health information to his or her spouse. In a field setting, a student nurse could have other choices in such a situation. A student nurse could report the situation to senior nurses or doctors who are responsible for the patient. Third, our data collection is limited to student nurses in South Korea. Therefore, our findings may not be generalisable; however, our intention was not to generalise our findings to a particular population but rather to theorise by testing our research model. Fourth, student nurses in South Korea may have their own specific characteristics (e.g. cultural traits) that may affect PSRB. Investigating the unique characteristics was beyond our research scope and can be investigated by future research. Fifth, a concept of others in PSRB can be extended to include organisations. Prior PSRB studies typically include customers as others to consider. Our study included patients and their families as others to consider. Future PSRB studies in the healthcare context can include hospitals as others. Finally, our experiment is based on role-playing scenarios that represent a context of healthcare information security. While practicing nurses checked whether the scenarios were realistic, we sought to control unnecessary variance and provided only the essential information for decision-making in the given scenarios. Finally, because our research particularly focuses on investigating the role of a situational factor triggering student nurses' prosocial motives and their motivational assessments in a healthcare context, disease severity is identified as an important situation factor. Future research should examine other potential situational factors influencing PSRB behaviours in various organisational contexts: rewards (McNeely & Meglino, 1994), autonomy (Morrison, 2006; Youli et al., 2014), ethical climate in an organisation (Vardaman et al., 2014), information sensitivity (Yang & Wang, 2009) and intended purpose of information (Anderson & Agarwal, 2011).

## 8 | CONCLUSION

Insiders' prosocial misbehaviour may cause significant damage to stakeholders, including the insiders themselves, in healthcare organisations. Our study examined internal data breaches from the PSRB perspective. We developed a research model that incorporates a situational factor (disease severity), prosocial motivational assessment (impact on others and the self) and situationally induced personal characteristics (situational empathy and responsibility to dis close) and investigated how these factors affect PSRB intention in the context of healthcare. The results suggest that understanding the relationship among these factors would help better manage insiders' misbehaviours that are prosocially motivated. Our study indicates that prosocially motivated security breaches at healthcare organisations require careful consideration.
