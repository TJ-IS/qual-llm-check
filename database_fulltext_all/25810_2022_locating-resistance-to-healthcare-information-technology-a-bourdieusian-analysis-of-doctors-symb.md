# Locating resistance to healthcare information technology: A Bourdieusian analysis of doctors' symbolic capital conservation

## Abstract

This research examines the socially significant issue of doctors' resistance to healthcare information technology (HIT) from the radical power perspective. It adopts Bourdieu's social practice theory to examine the interaction of HIT with the reproduction of doctors' historically rooted social standing through the doctor-patient-interaction (D-P-I) practice. Findings from our ethnographic enquiry at a large corporate healthcare organisation in India link doctors' historically rooted social standing to the symbolic recognition of their embodied emotional capital existing in tandem with their habitus. The symbolic recognition of emotional capital provided a better valorisation of clinical capital and allowed the accumulation of other forms of capital—institutionalised capital, social capital and economic capital—that formed doctors' capital structure and contributed to their social status. Doctors produced emotional capital by putting their habitus into practice and, in the process, reproduced its symbolic status and their social status linked to it. HIT challenged doctors to put their habitus into practice, thereby creating a perception of threat to emotional capital. Doctors' HIT resistance was a conservation strategy to reproduce their historically rooted higher social status. Findings from this study contribute to the literature on Power and IT resistance.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Resistance to information technology (IT) is an outcome of perceived threats (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) to work routines (Laumer, Maier, Eckhardt, & Weitzel, 2016), power (Markus, 1983) or changes in equity (Joshi, 1991). In th context of healthcare, doctors' resistance—emanating from perceived challenges to their powerful position arising from the clinical expertise —is one of the primary reasons why benefits of healthcare information technologies (HIT in the form of improved, cost-effective and smart healthcare systems (Agarwal, Gao, DesRoches, & Jha, 2010; Kohli & Tan, 2016) remain largely unrealised (Doolin, 2004; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Samhan & Joshi, 2015). While literature emphasises the role of power in IT and HIT resistance, it has largely focused on understanding power from an organisational perspective. For instance, in the healthcare context, studies have examined how resistance results from doctors' perceived threat to dominant standing vis-à-vis the administrators or the nursing staff in a healthcare organisation (Doolin, 2004; Rivard, Lapointe, & Kappos, 2011). However, research also argues that doctors' powe originates from their socially and historically rooted “professional” status— “occupations with special power and prestige… [based on] special competence in esoteric bodies of knowledge linked to central needs and values of the socia system” (Sarfatti Larson, 1977, as cited in Walter & Lopez, 2008, p. 207). This historically rooted social standing grants them the freedom and autonomy to practice their knowledge in the best interest of society (R. L. Cruess & Cruess, 2008; S. R. Cruess, Johnston, & Cruess, 2002; Kane & Labianca, 2011), and, also reject any form of contro by administrators in a healthcare organisation (Kohli & Kettinger, 2004). Thus, existing literature, with its focus on power relations within an organisational boundary, does not fully capture the interaction between HIT and doctors socially rooted power. Capturing this interaction requires adopting radical perspective of power (Jasperson et al., 2002) as a lens to focus on the foundations of this deeply rooted social standing that exist in doctors' dail interactions with patients (Nimmon & Stenfors-Hayes, 2016).

The practice-based perspective foregrounds the centrality of power reproduction in the actors' daily activities (Marabelli & Galliers, 2017; Nicolini, 2012; Peppard, Galliers, & Thorogood, 2014). In particular, Bourdieu's practice theory emphasises the actors' daily practices through which historically and socially rooted social order (with it inequality and domination) is reproduced (Ellway & Walsham, 2015). With its interrelated conceptual triads—field, habitus and capital-—and introduction of capital in various forms (eg, economic capital, cultural capital and social cap ital), Bourdieu's theory enables examination of historically rooted social inequalities that are not necessarily eco nomic (Bourdieu, 1984). It provides the necessary apparatus to locate the interaction of IT with the power (re) production taking place through the accumulation of capital structure in the daily performance of micro-level human practices (Levina & Arriaga, 2014). In this article, we adopt Bourdieu's theory to study the doctor-patient-interaction (D-P-I) practice for answering: how HIT interacts with the reproduction of doctors' historically rooted social standing?

To answer this question, we conducted a two-year-long ethnographic study at a large corporate hospital in India—the Indian healthcare organisation (IHP)—that was facing doctors' resistance to using electronic medica records (EMR) in D-P-I practices. We found that doctors' social standing was linked to the symbolic status of their embodied emotional capital that they recurrently (re)produced through D-P-I practice. The symbolic status of emotional capital contributed to the (re)production of doctors' social standing by allowing accumulation of various othe forms of capital to take place through the practice– providing higher valorisation of intertwined clinical capital, enriching institutionalised capital (eg, legitimacy of clinical degrees), building social capital (eg, long term doctorpatient-relationships) and increasing economic capital. However, as emotional capital was embodied capital, it existed in tandem with doctors' habitus—durable bodily and mental dispositions. Thus, doctors' habitus was crucia for the production of emotional capital, and for the reproduction of its symbolic status in the practice. The introduc tion of EMR interacted with the habitus, and doctors perceived it as a challenge in the reproduction of the symbolic capital (embodied emotional capital). Resistance to EMR was thus a conservation strategy employed by doctors to protect the symbolic value of emotional capital, which was instrumental in continuance of their historically rooted social standing.

These findings make significant theoretical contributions. While doctors' status has traditionally been linked to clinical expertise, our radical perspective on power, and practice-based examination reveals that their social status is deeply interwoven with their embodied emotional capital. Resistance is a result of a perceived threat to the regular reproduction of emotional capital. This finding, on the one hand, extends our understanding of doctors' power and resistance by locating a new source of threat. On the other hand, it informs the larger IT/HIT resistance and the Information Systems (IS) and power literature to pay diligent attention to the power relations that may be roote in the larger social system. The extant IS and Power literature has primarily focused on power relations within an organisation. Our study shows how the interaction of IT with the micro-level human activities holds significance for the reproduction or change of historically rooted societal power structures. We reveal emotion as a source of the social and historical power structure that manifests through actors' durable dispositions in their practices. An IT implementation interacts with the practices and it may be perceived as a threat to this socially rooted power structure. Our findings on the role of the emotional component of practice on the use of an IT application also aligns wel with the calls in IS research to account for the emotion and the “felt quality” of socio-material practices (Stein, Newell, Wagner, & Galliers, 2014). Our contextual findings also extend Bourdieu's theory and its application to th IS literature. Bourdieu has primarily focused on actors' capital “accumulation”. Our findings add to Bourdieu b showing that capital de-accumulation can also happen as a result of the interaction between IT and the daily prac tices of an individual.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section 2, we review various perspectives of power in the larger IS lit erature and (Healthcare) IT resistance literature. We discuss why a radical perspective of power is appropriate for this study. In Section 3, we discuss the key concepts of Bourdieu's practice theory and its applications in the IS literature. In Section 4, we detail our research methodology, and in Section 5, we present our empirical construction of the field. Section 6 presents our research findings, and Section 7 discusses those findings. In Section 8, we discuss the limitations of the study and possible future research directions. We conclude our paper in Section 9.

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 | Power perspectives in IS literature

Power is a multifaceted concept that has been theorised and studied in multiple forms in IS literature. Among the three dominant paradigms of power (Bradshaw-Camball & Murray, 1991), the functionalist perspective assumes power as an objective reality defined in terms of the key organisational position or access to key organisationa resources. The interpretive perspective defines power as the capability to control meaning about th organisational events, and therefore, focuses on the “perceptions and process that shape them” (Jasperso et al., 2002, p. 412). Radical perspective defines power as a historically created reality that is the outgrowth of socia structures such as class, gender or institutional structures, and it is deeply rooted as long-standing assumptions i the larger social context (Jasperson et al., 2002).

The existing “IS and power” literature has traditionally been dominated by the functionalist perspectives of power (Jasperson et al., 2002; Simeonova, Karanasios, Galliers, Kelly, & Mishra, 2018). Recently, we have seen a growing interest in the interpretive approach, focusing on meaning-making, through which power is created and exerted in the organisation (eg, Azad & Farai. 2011: Doolin. 2004: Markus, 1983). The study by Markus (1983 showed how the change in access to information led to an interpretation of redistribution of power structure during the implementation of a financial information system. Azad and Faraj (2011) examined the exercise of meaning power through the process of contentious framing around “certain aspects of IT design and development” (p. 55) Hekkala and Urquhart (2013) adopted an interpretive approach to examine the “hard to observe” power issues being played out in day-to-day activities in an inter-organisational information system (IOIS) project. Silva and Backhouse (2003) and Smith, Winchester, Bunker and Jamieson (2010) adopted the circuit of power perspective to examine the formation of symbolic power relations around rules and meanings in the form of a social integra tion circuit”. Howcroft and Light (2006) showed the exercise of symbolic power by IT consultants throug operational languages and organisational myths in selecting and adopting a software package

We found few studies from the radical power perspective (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Müller, Mathiassen, Saunders, & Kræmmergaard, 2017). These studies have explained how an IT innovation project is influenced by the broader social and political structure (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007). Such an approach is significant for our study to attend to doctors' historically rooted social standing having source in the D-P-I practice (Nimmon & Stenfors-Hayes, 2016). A radical perspective would enable us to focus on “domination, asymmetries of power, and conflict, while examining activities in terms of whether they contribute to the overthrow or maintenance of the larger societa power structures” (Jasperson et al., 2002, p. 413).

## 2.2 | Power and (healthcare) IT resistance

Resistance is an oppositional behaviour that results from a perception of “threat” based on the actors' evaluation of the inevitable interaction between IT and the broader social and political structure (Meissonier & Houzé, 2010 Samhan, 2018; van Offenbeek, Boonstra, & Seo, 2013). The perceived threat may lead actors to engage into vari ous forms of resistance on the continuum of severity: from passive resistance, to active resistance, to aggressiv resistance (Coetsee, 1999; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Murungi, Wiener, & Marabelli, 2019).

IT resistance studies conducted across various contexts (see Ali, Zhou, Miller, & Ieromonachou, 2016; Laumer & Eckhardt, 2012) have revealed various “threats”. It includes threats related to a change in work routine (Laumer et al., 2016), a change in power structure (Markus, 1983), a change in the equity structure (Joshi, 1991), a switching cost involved (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009) or the control style used by the controller (Murungi et al., 2019). In the healthcare context, particularly doctors' resistance has been linked to their powerful status and the threat to their status caused by the HIT. Doctors enjoy a socially rooted professional autonomy ; a special social prestige based on their clinical expertise (S. R. Cruess et al., 2002; Sarfatti Larson, 1977) that also make them occupy a higher status in the power relations within a healthcare organisation (Kane & Labianca, 2011). Literature has reported that due to a perceived threat to clinical expertise (Walter & Lopez, 2008), doctors perceive a threat of losing their higher status in the organisation vis-à-vis other actors, and engage into resisting HIT (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007; Doolin, 2004; Kane & Labianca, 2011; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Rivard et al., 2011). Although these studies establish that doctors resistance is a result of a perceived threat to their status vis-à-vis other actors within an organisation, as doctors' status is rooted historically in the wider society, the existing studies, due to their focus on power relations within an organisational boundary (see Appendix A.1), inform little on interaction of HIT with the larger power structure, and how this interaction may lead to threat to the doctors' social standing and could also cause resistance. Unless this theoretical knowledge on the interaction of HIT with doctors' historically rooted social standing is developed, the phenomenon of doctors' resistance would remain only partially understood. This would require adopting a radica power perspective to examine the interaction of HIT with the D-P-I practice through which doctors' historically rooted social standing is recurrently produced. This would, in turn, require adopting a practice-based approach, particularly Bourdieu's theory, to zoom-in on the practice and look at how power is exhibited in the daily lives of the actors (Nicolini, 2012).

## 3 | BOURDIEU'S SOCIAL PRACTICE THEORY IN IS STUDIES

## 3.1 | Bourdieu' social practice theory

Bourdieu proposed a conceptual trilogy (field, capital and habitus) to study how the social world is routinely mad and re-made through micro-level human practices [Equation (1) below, Bourdieu, 1984]

$$
[ (\text { habitus }) (\text { capital }) ] + \text { field } = \text { practice }......\tag{1}
$$

According to Bourdieu, the world is made of several relatively autonomous fields . Each field is regulated by field-specific logic, which defines the rules and stakes of the field and its power relations. The relative positions of actors within a field are linked to their capital structure. For Bourdieu, capital is a accumulated labour that may exist i three forms—economic capital (eg, wealth), social capital (eg, connections) and cultural capital (eg, inherited resources) (Bourdieu, 1986). The cultural capital is of three types—embodied capital (durable bodily and mental dispositions), objec tified capital (eg, cultural goods—instruments) and institutionalised capital (objectification of cultural competence—eg, educational degree). Working in relation with the field logic, one species of the capital receives the recognition of “sym bolic capital”—the worthiest form of capital. Symbolic capital awards position recognition—it instils trust, goodwill and faith in its possessor, enables rich conversion to the other forms of capital, which, in turn, results in special authority for regulating the day-to-day practices in their favour (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992). The power relation in a field is defined mainly in the (unequal) distribution of symbolic capital—an actor with a richer possession of symbolic capital occupies a relatively powerful position. Thus, in Bourdieu's theory, power is viewed in terms of relative positions actors occupy in a particular field, based upon their capital structure and authority to regulate practices.

The power relation defined around symbolic capital is temporary as symbolic capital is a temporary status of a capital awarded based on at the moment collective commitment (Calhoun, 2003). It can perpetuate only as long as the monopoly of the symbolic capital continues. The holders of “current” symbolic capital continuously engage in conservation strategy to conserve the symbolic capital, and oppose any action from the other actors found challeng ing the symbolic status of current symbolic capital, to reproduce their higher status (Emirbayer & Johnson, 2008) When the symbolic capital is embodied capital, there is a higher significance of actors' habitus.

Habitus refers to the internalised social order of the field (including its power relations) in actors' durable bodil and mental dispositions, reproducing itself in and through human practices. Habitus is the result of a mundane pro cess of inculcation of the social order as actors engage with it through their daily activities. As durable bodily an mental dispositions, habitus works as the second nature of the actors— history turned into nature (Bourdieu, 1977 p. 78)—and works as the “principles of the generation and structuring of practices and representations which can be objectively regulated and regular” (p. 72) by providing them with a “practical sense” to carry out their day-to-da activities at the subconscious level, reflecting the broader social order. It disposes actors to carry out their practices in the ways that unavoidably reflect their relative positions in the field such that, through their practices, actors reproduce that social order (Emirbayer & Johnson, 2008).

The embodied capital exists in tandem with habitus in the form of durable dispositions and enters into being only through the production of habitus in the practice (Bourdieu, 1984). Therefore, when the symbolic capital i embodied, for the social order to reproduce, the actors with the rich embodiment of “current” symbolic capital hav the interest to recurrently reproduce habitus.

The conceptual triad (habitus, field, capital) of Bourdieu's theory and its introduction of capital in various forms (eg, economic capital, cultural capital and social capital) provide a rich theoretical framework to study how historicall rooted social order and power relations are generated through micro-level human practices (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992). In the context of our study, this framework provides us the required theoretical “apparatus” (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011) to locate the source of doctors' historically rooted social standing by examining how doctors accumulate their capital structure through daily D-P-I practices.

## 3.2 | Bourdieu in IS literature

A number of studies have leveraged Bourdieu's theoretical concepts to study the interaction of IT applications wit various social orders such as the digital divide (Kvasny & Keil, 2006), digital inclusion (Newman, Browne-Yung Raghavendra, Wood, & Grace, 2017), online status (Levina & Arriaga, 2014) and historically rooted power relation within an organisation (Richardson & Howcroft, 2006). We identified two broader categories within these few stud ies. The first category of articles examine how IT implementation efforts, intended toward restructuring the deepl rooted social order, often reproduce it. Richardson and Howcroft (2006) illustrated how implementing a Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system, intended to empower call centre workers, interacted with the old-style cal centre management practice and reproduced the existing inequality in favour of supervisors. Newman et al. (2017 leveraged the concept of various forms of capital to study the inequality that exists in digital inclusion among young people with disabilities. They examined how the uneven distribution of economic, social and cultural capital in th offline world may lead to unequal digital inclusion among these people. Kvasny and Keil (2006) examined two digita divide initiatives. They showed the interaction of digital initiatives with the actors' habitus and capital structure that led to the reinforcement of the status quo.

Another set of studies examined how IT contributes to capital accumulation and enhancing social status.<sup>1</sup> Levin and Arriaga (2014) showed how actors used platform features to bring their offline capital in online fields an engaged in various capital accumulation activities online to enhance their status, which further contributed to thei status offline. Zifla and Wattal (2019) further examined the role of IT in improving social status and increasing sale on online platforms.

The above studies signify the analytical power of Bourdieu's theory to capture the interaction between IT and social order (including power relations) by allowing the examination of (a) how IT contributes to enhancing social status by enabling capital accumulation and (b) how IT interacts with the deeply rooted social order to reproduce the order. However, the current application of Bourdieu provides limited insights on how an IT implementation on it interaction with micro-level human practices may potentially alter the social and historically rooted order such as doctors' socially embedded status. We build upon the proven analytical power of Bourdieu's theory to capture th interaction of IT with deeply rooted social order. We draw upon Bourdieu's conceptual trilogy (field, habitus and cap ital) to examine how the EMR implementation interacted with doctors' capital accumulation efforts through D-Ppractice, having significance for their historically rooted social standing.

## 4 | RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## 4.1 | Context of the study

With roots of Indian Medicine in Vedic Culture and Ayurveda, doctors have traditionally been seen as “Healers”<sup>2,3</sup>; having direct connection with God, and possessing deeper social, cultural and personal understanding about the patients, and bringing this knowledge to provide care in a family-kind of relationship (Bode & Hariramamurthi, 2014; Saini, 2016). This has also historically made them enjoy high social prestige and God-like status in the larger society.

The shortage of “Doctors”,<sup>4</sup> reflected in the below par doctor-patient ratio<sup>5</sup> and long waiting line have made public hospitals an unattractive option for the patients (Bajpai, 2014; Sengupta & Nundy, 2005). As a result, patients have historically turned to parallel doctor-run small clinics. These clinics are owned and run by the doctors independently. Patients come to these clinics based on the doctor's name, and over a period of time a long-term familial-typ relationships develop between the doctor and the patient (and family members). As an alternative to both public hos pitals and doctors' clinics, corporate hospitals have evolved recently to overcome the difficulties faced in the publi hospitals, and at the same time, provide care under a larger hospital setup and hospital brand

Our study was carried out in one such corporate hospital (hereafter referred to as “IHP”) that began operations in 2014. The hospital offered out-patient care, in-patient care, critical care and laboratory services. It was a 500 bed ded<sup>6</sup> multi-specialty hospital based in Ahmedabad, India. It had more than 100 doctors spread across various clinica specialties, nursing staff, administrators and other administrative staff, all working collaboratively to deliver patient care (Figure 1). There were two broader categories of administrators—general administrators and medical administra tors. The general administrators included the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) and various functional heads such as Chief Operations Officer (COO), Chief Information Officer (CIO) and the Head of International Relations. The medi cal administrators included the medical officer (looking after the coordination of doctors), the nursing head (coordi nating the nursing activities) and the heads of various clinical units (eg, the Head of Critical Care and Head of Laboratory Services).

When the IHP was set up, administrators decided to implement an EMR<sup>7</sup> in the Out-Patient Department (OPD). Patient care in OPD was delivered in one-to-one interactions between doctors and patients. EMR provided the functionality to store and retrieve medical records. It also provided an option to write electronic prescriptions while inter acting with patients.

## 4.2 | Research method and data collection

We adopted an interpretive approach to carry out a two-year-long ethnographic study at IHP. The interpretive epis temology assumes that reality is “socially constructed”. It enables researchers to present “interpretations of other people's interpretations” (Walsham, 1995, p. 78) as they examine a phenomenon embedded in a complex web of social relations and that involves subjective meaning-making from the actors (Klein & Myers, 1999). The interpretiv position was essential for us to interpret how doctors' assigned meaning to the interaction of EMR in their D-Ppractice.

This particular study is part of a larger research project concerning EMR implementation at IHP. We started this project at the beginning of the EMR implementation and carried out data collection in three phases. Each phase adopted different data collection modes serving different purposes (Table 1). In the initial two phases we primarily focused on interviewing participants and observing the practices at multiple sites in the hospital We also explored the features of EMR and collected multiple photographs and other relevant documents (eg, consultation sheets, medicine forms and registration forms). Based on the participants' consent, interview were tape-recorded and later transcribed. The data collected from these two rounds helped us understand the hospital's various practices, and the actors involved with them (Figure 1). At the end of two rounds, we were able to identify that a critical challenge the administrators faced in implementing EMR was to making doctors use it in clinical practices. However, we were still unable to fully explore the nature of this challenge and more importantly, why this challenge was emerging. It required us to enter the field—a critical moment in an ethno graphic enquiry “when a researcher's status changes from that of an outsider to being a member (albeit onl temporarily) of the practice world (Chughtai & Myers, 2017, p. 798); to be accepted and be there along wit the participants, living with them in their everyday situations (Kunda, 2013) . (p. 797, emphasis in original) Entering was crucial for this study to uncover how EMR interacted with the doctors' everyday lives and to b able to interpret the meanings they ascribed to it (see Appendix B.2).

Therefore, in the third round, we decided to be an “insider”, that is moving from “getting in” (physica access, being felt like an outsider) to “getting on” (access to participants' world, being felt like an insider) (Cunliffe & Alcadipani, 2016). To get on , we adopted a novel data collection mode of interpretiv shadowing” (see Appendix B.3, Table B3). Shadowing means “closely following a member of an organization over an extended period of time” (McDonald, 2005, p. 456), producing “running commentary” while observ ing and interacting with the participant to get a “multi-dimensional picture of the role, approach, philosophy and task of the person being shadowed (McDonald, 2005, p. 457). Unlike interviews and observation shadowing allows the unlimited access to understanding what participants say (interview) or what is observed (observation), and also to uncover the intentionality behind the actions produced by the actors (McDonald, 2005). Understanding the intentionality is critical to interpretive study, and particularly to our study, as it believes that the world is made of “Intentions” (Alvesson & Sköldberg, 2013); “essentially person exists only in the performance of intentional acts” (Heidegger, 1976, p. 73, as cited in Butler, 1998, p. 289) One researcher shadowed five doctors from different specialties for 2–5 days (Appendix B.4 for their profile). Shadowing produced 38:47 hours of recording, 376 pages of transcriptions and 352 pages of ‘running commentary.

We followed Walsham (2006) throughout our research enquiry. In line with Walsham's (2006) observation that a theoretical framework may be adopted at different stages of the research, the theory came much later in our study. As this particular study was part of a larger research project, we approached data collection without any prior theoretical framework. At the end of every day's field study, the first author used to write field impressions and discuss with the other three co-authors in the initial two rounds. Toward the end of two rounds, after a series of discussions on field impressions and notes, we were convinced that a significant challenge to EMR implementation was in mak ing doctors use it. Subsequently, we adopted interpretive shadowing in the third round. Along with writing everyday field impressions, we also started open coding (Saldaña, 2021) and creating the data-theory link (Walsham, 2006 p. 325). We organised all the data in MAXQDA and followed an iterative process of coding to develop three levels of code (codes, categories and themes- Appendix B.5). Each author coded the data individually first and then shared the same. This iterative process was done until all the researchers reached a consensual agreement with the codes generated. In this entire coding process, whenever a particular theoretical framework or any theoretical concept appeared appropriate, they were recorded in the form of “memos”. During the whole process of coding, we also developed several data-theory links, and discussed with each other (see Appendix B.6 for one representative link). In this process, we understood Bourdieu's concepts as the ripe theoretical concepts for this study. Thus, the selection of Bourdieu's theory for this study was because it “spoke” (Walsham, 2006, p. 325) as we simultaneously carried out data collection and analysis.

## 5 | FIELD: DOCTORS' ACCEPTED DOMINATING STATUS

We started our analysis with the construction of the field—the empirical space within which the phenomenon mani fests itself through the micro-level human activities. The field was constructed around the D-P-I practice. We first considered IHP itself as a field, and followed an iterative process to reach its final construction. While D-P-I was carried out within IHP's physical boundaries, the actors involved and their relative positions reflected their position in the larger society and were accepted as a normal order. It appeared natural to all the actors that IHP, as a hospital stands for delivering patient care (field logic). Notwithstanding the differences in their relative positions, they unani mously agreed that doctors would occupy the higher status. All the actors showed commitment— albeit only temporarily—to deliver better patient care within this social order. For instance, the administrators perceived doctors higher status as natural and legitimate because they believed that doctors, who are regarded highly in the broader society, would obviously be the most important actors at IHP.

“Because, see, hospital industry, let me tell you very honestly and very upfront, hospital industry is driven by doctors. Okay. They are THE verge and they have to be taken care (of)”. (CIO)

This acceptance essentially reflected the acceptance of larger social order— order of things [with its relation of dominations] as “natural, self-evident, and legitimate” by the actors involved (Emirbayer & Johnson, 2008, p. 31). Thus, while we initially defined the physical boundary of IHP as the field, we gradually discovered that the IHP itself was embedded in the larger social system. Patient care was the operating logic of a hospital in this larger system, which also perpetuated to work as the operating logic of IHP and defined the resulting power relations withi it. Thus, the D-P-I practice at IHP was being carried out within this larger social system, and through its daily produc tion, the wider field structure was reproduced. The socially rooted order in the favour of doctors was accepted a the natural state of order within the logic of patient care.

While doctors currently occupied a higher position, it could perpetuate only as long as doctors reproduced th symbolic capital attached to this power relation (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992) through their daily D-P-I practice (Nimmon & Stenfors-Hayes, 2016). Therefore, we performed a granular examination of D-P-I practice and studied how EMR interacted with the reproduction of symbolic capital and doctors' overall capital structure linked to their social standing.

## 6 | FINDINGS

## 6.1 | Doctor-patient-interaction: A routinised reproduction of doctors' intertwined clinical and emotional dispositions

D-P-I comprised activities such as taking a patient's clinical history, writing prescriptions and communicating clinica diagnosis and medicine plans; all being delivered using routine bodily and facial movements at the subconsciou level. For instance, talking to the patients and writing prescriptions on paper at the same time happened naturall without requiring a conscious effort.

“And like I am talking to you and I am writing. I am talking to you and I am writing something. It is in my practice itself” (Doctor 1)

We found that clinical work was only one aspect of D-P-I. Along with the clinical aspects, the practice also had an interwoven emotional aspect. Doctors appeared to be doing both in an interdependent or inseparable manner to the extent that one made the other possible. For instance, doctors made eye contact with patients while listening to clinical complaints; thus, building an emotional connection at the same time. This, in turn, was crucial for reaching a clinical diagnosis. While writing a prescription, the doctor listened to the patient and responded by nodding hi head and acknowledging in a calming voice (eg, “hmm” “hmm”) to ensure the emotional connection alongside per forming the clinical activities. Communicating clinical findings was not just revealing the diagnosis; it involved bodil and facial expressions such as voice modulation, facial expression, etc., in order to show their concern for the patient while revealing the diagnosis. While revealing bad news, doctor used words like I am treating you, you should b fine along with voice modulation and a concerned facial expression. The following quotes explain how doctor brought emotional and clinical aspects together in the practice.

“Sometimes, it so happens that you talk more socially and personally, rather than medical. Medica things come within 2 to 5 minutes, things are over and you come to know what the diagnosis is….. tell them in a very subtle way, very subtle, you know mixing the words with sugar and honey. But I communicate, and at the end of communication, I see that I have convinced [them] about what prob lem they have, and what I am going to do” (Doctor 2)

Thus, our research revealed two crucial things about the practice. First, the routinised and subconscious deliveryindicated the ordered structuring of the practice through doctors' habitus (Bourdieu, 1977). Second, habitus had two strongly interwoven components —clinical and emotional. However, they were so closely interwoven in th practice that on the surface they appeared to be “one”. In the next subsection, we build on it to flesh out how doc tors' capital structure was shaped through their practice. The detail on shaping of capital structure enabled us to locate the source of doctors' social standing in the practice (Bourdieu, 1984).

## 6.2 | Emotional capital—Symbolic capital of the field

Emotional dispositions not only enabled the performance of the emotional aspects of the practice but also enabled doctors getting critical information (eg, smoking habits) that helped them reach the clinical diagnosis. By using emotional dispositions, (for instance, voice modulation and facial expression), doctors were able to better communicat adverse clinical findings. Indeed, the doctors in our study believed that while clinical is one skill, emotional skill is essential for the effective delivery of practice

“This is the inherent capacity of a doctor or you have to imbibe somebody; this is the best way of communicating with patients. That skill I have imbibed from one of my seniors. I had one who taught me what to see in the patient clinically and another boss who taught me how you communicate with your patients” (Doctor 2)

“The patients and relatives have emotional attachment with me and I think we must devote most of our time talking to them and maintaining eye contact” (Doctor 3)

However, while both skills were manifested in an inseparable manner, doctors seemed to believe that their emotiona disposition was the more important skill necessary to elicit a patient's positive acceptance of their clinical expertise.

“So, this is the way the practice is done anywhere. Personal touch or patient should be familiar with the psyche of the doctor and vice versa. Many times, patients have problems, where there is no solution. We know and patients also know. But when they come here, they feel satisfied that ok whatever length of the period we have to live, we are in safe hands, and we are taking these medicines and we will be better” (Doctor 2)

Patients also recognised doctors' emotional skills—that is, their ability to build emotional connections throug their ways of talking, showing concern and communicating clinical findings—to be crucial. Excerpt from one D-P-I Practice:

## 6.3 | EMR and D-P-I interaction: Perception of threat to social standing

Continuous interactions with the doctors and our extended observations while shadowing indicated that EMR interacted with the routine delivery of practice. In a traditional practice, the moment patients entered the room, the doc tor looked at them, greeted them, made them sit closer and initiated personal and social talk by alternately looking into their eyes and at the medical records. All these activities resulted in an emotional connection between the doc tor and the patient. However, with the introduction of EMR, the moment a patient entered, the doctor would have to be looking at the computer system to read the patient's record. In this process, doctors could miss critica moments of building emotional connections. Further, while traditionally doctors talked to the patient and wrote th prescription simultaneously, without necessarily dividing their attention as both were “one” practice, with the intro duction of EMR, writing on the system appeared to doctors as an additional activity in the practice. Doctors realise the need to make a conscious effort to type into the EMR system, and in the process a resulting split of attention More attention on typing and less on talking to the patient appeared to result in a failure to build an emotional con nection (Figure 3).

“Because here we are attending the patient, we are directly with human touch, we are not computer.

There is always a difference between human touch and computer. And if I am doing computer work

more, then I am losing emotional connect with patients there” (Doctor 1)

“A doctor can't just type on the computer and simultaneously develop patient rapport or patient encounter” (Doctor 7)

The apparent challenge on the subconscious delivery of the D-P-I practice leading to a split of attention indi cated a challenge to maintaining doctors' habitus that they had regularly put into practice. However, the habitus wa the means for the production of emotional capital and for the reproduction of capital structure linked to it. A chal lenge on habitus, thus, created a threat to doctors of losing their historically rooted status being reproduced throug it. For instance, a doctor indicated a perceived threat to his status as an emotionally competent person — a credence that influenced patients when awarding the status of the most competent person—due to the introduction of EMR in the practice.

“It may not be possible, and you get a little disassociated with the patient. Your patient has a feeling that okay your doctor is now doing the clerical work. So that makes the difference” (Doctor 2)

## 6.4 | Manifestation of resistance

At IHP, doctors' resistance to EMR varied on the continuum of severity level. For instance, one doctor (Doctor # 1) developed a negative attitude and removed the EMR system from his consultation table. Another doctor (Doctor # 3) went a step further and threw (to indicate the intensity in removal and expression used in his response) the system from the consultation room, and engaged in negative conversation about the EMR to discourage others from using it. Doctor # 4 and Doctor # 7, meanwhile, had the EMR system installed on the consultation table, but they were not using it during the D-P-I practice. Doctor # 7 revealed that while he had initially tried using EMR, he realised a challenge to rapport building with patients and discontinued using it and also talked negatively about it to the others Doctor # 4 used EMR only for his research purposes and not in his D-P-I practice. While Doctor # 4 talked negativel about the existing EMR, he took an interest in suggesting improvement. Doctor # 6 did not use EMR, but similar to Doctor # 4, he took keen interest in making suggestions. Doctor # 5 also had a system installed on the consultation table but did not use it in his practice; however, unlike Doctor # 4 or Doctor # 6, he did not engage in any talk about

EMR (he held a neutral view). The reaction from Doctor # 2 was totally different. While he refrained from using EMR in D-P-I practice, he used it to see number of patients waiting for consultation.

All the above responses reflected the various forms of resistance in terms of the level of severity (Table 3) (Coetsee, 1999; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). For instance, while the reaction from Doctor # 3 appeared to be the highest from the aspect of severity (Aggressive Resistance), the reaction from Doctor # 4, Doctor #6 and Doctor # 7 appeared more active (Active Resistance), whereas the reactions from Doctor # 1 showed lack of interest (Apathy) and Doctor # 2 and Doctor # 5 seemed to show a passive resistance.

As the resistance was coming from the doctors who currently occupied the higher status, we interpreted doc tors' resistance as conservation strategy, to conserve the symbolic capital and reproduce the power relations in their favour (Emirbayer & Johnson, 2008). Doctors resisted EMR to conserve the symbolic value of their emotional capita and to reproduce their historically rooted social standing linked to it.

## 7 | DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

We adopted a radical perspective of power and drew upon Bourdieu's practice theory to study the D-P-I practice for answering how HIT interacts with the reproduction of doctors' historically rooted social standing. We found that doctors' social standing was linked to symbolic recognition of their embodied emotional capital. Patients' positive recognition to embodied emotional capital allowed doctors to accumulate various other forms of capital— (a) providing higher valorisation of clinical capital, (b) legitimacy to the institutionalised capital (clinical degree), (c) the social capital in the form of long-term doctor-patient relationship, and (d) the economic capital. Together, emotiona capital and its conversion to other forms of capital formed doctors' capital structure and contributed to doctors social standing in two ways— their relative position within the hospital and social standing in patients' eyes. Thus, doctors' embodied emotional capital was working as a symbolic capital, which, in its positive recognition and the con version to the other forms of capital, contributed to the reproduction of doctors' socially rooted position. As an embodied capital, emotional capital, however, existed as part of doctors' habitus. The interaction of EMR with doc tors' habitus in D-P-I practice led to a perceived challenge to reproduction of the symbolic value of emotional capita and hence their social standing linked to it. Doctors' resistance to EMR was a conservation strategy in response to the perceived threat to (re)production of embodied emotional capital's symbolic value. Thus, using Bourdieu's theory allowed us to reveal doctors' capital structure embedded in the practice, and locate the micro-foundation(s) of doctors' resistance to EMR in the perceived challenge to accumulating capital structure

The findings extend our understanding of doctors' power and contribute to the literature on doctors' power and HIT resistance. Traditionally, doctors' power has been associated with clinical expertise, and the literature has reported that resistance is a result of the perceived threat to clinical expertise (Doolin, 2004; Walter & Lopez, 2008). Adopting radical power perspective to perform a practice-based examination of doctors' historically rooted socia standing embedded in D-P-I revealed that emotional expertise provides higher valorisation of clinical expertise. The routinised reproduction of emotional capital in D-P-I is the source of doctors' historically rooted social standing. Doc tors' resistance to HIT results from the perceived threat to social standing due to a perceived challenge of reproducing emotional capital. In doing so, we point to a new source of doctors' power beyond their clinical expertise. Also, perceived threats are not necessarily linked to doctors' clinical expertise but also to the reproduction of their emotional capital. This perceived threat to (re)production of emotional capital is why doctors resist HIT. The finding also makes a significant addition to the broader IT resistance and IS and Power literature. The extant literature is dominated by the functionalist and interpretive perspectives that study power relations within an organisationa boundary. However, this makes a significant omission to exploring the power relations rooted in broader social systems and having implications for IT implementation. We fill this gap by adopting a radical power perspective. Drawing upon Bourdieu's practice theory concepts, we examined how EMR implementation interacted with doctors social standing and its reproduction through the D-P-I practice. Adopting a radical perspective allowed us to revea emotion as a source of socially rooted power and its interaction with IT implementation within an organisation. Wit the help of these findings, we show how the interaction of IT applications with the daily activities is consequentia for “overthrow or maintenance of the larger societal power structures” (Jasperson et al., 2002, p. 413) and how these power structures may hold utility for IT implementations within an organisation. In particular, we revealed th analytical power of Bourdieu's practice theory to capture this interaction. To that extent, our study makes anothe addition to IS and Power literature by showing the utility of practice theory to advance the scholarship on radica power perspective, which remains less studied because “researchers are sometimes so immersed in the underlyin power phenomenon they may be totally unaware of larger power structure (Jasperson et al., 2002, p. 413). With it centrality of power reproduction in the actors' daily activities (Marabelli & Galliers, 2017; Nicolini, 2012), practic theory would enable researchers to attend to the larger power structure while still focusing on activities within an organisation. Our findings on the interaction of HIT with the emotional aspect of clinical practice and its significanc on HIT use also aligns well with the calls made to account for the “felt quality” of socio-material practices in studying IT implementation projects (Stein et al., 2014). We revealed that the interaction of IT with the routinised practice might challenge the actors in expressing their embodied emotional skills, which might further influence their adop tion and use of IT.

The extant literature employing Bourdieu has shown its appropriateness to study the “accumulation” of capita structure enabled by IT applications (eg, Levina & Arriaga, 2014), and the reproduction of deeply rooted social order (eg, Kvasny & Keil, 2006). However, we found that an EMR implementation, on its interaction with doctors' habitus, created a challenge to capital accumulation. This is a critical addition to existing knowledge—the IT implementation may also lead to the de-accumulation of capital structure, further restructuring the current social order. This contex tual revelation adds to Bourdieu. Bourdieu brought to the fore actors' capital “accumulation” taking place through everyday activities wherein they continuously strive to enhance the value of their capital and enrich the composition of their capital structure (Bourdieu, 1984). We extend Bourdieu's analytical power to also examine capital deaccumulation efforts through micro-level human practices. This might be significant in studying human practices in today's age of online fields (Zifla & Wattal, 2019), wherein actors engage with various IT applications in their activities. Further, Bourdieu noted the role of symbolic capital in capital accumulation. Bourdieu proposed that th holders of the “current” symbolic capital hold advantage to get a richer conversion of symbolic capital to accumulate other forms of capital. Therefore, they engage in conservation strategies and oppose any activity from the other actors perceived to be legitimising other forms of capital as symbolic and overthrowing the current one (Emirbayer & Johnson, 2008). We add that when the current symbolic capital is embodied, a challenge to its status may come even from the interaction of IT applications with actors' habitus (without necessarily a direct attempt from other actors to overthrow it). Thus, we extend Bourdieu's theory by showing that an additional source of a threat to symbolic capita could be an interaction of IT application with habitus.

In contrast with the studies which report that inculcation of clinical expertise is preferred over emotional exper tise (while learning) in the United States (Hojat et al., 2004; Silverman, 2012), our study shows that, in India, doctor inculcate emotional expertise as an embodied resource. Doctors sub-consciously bring this expertise while delivering care through D-P-I. The importance of embodied emotional capital resonates with the historical healing nature of clinical practice in India, where doctors bring in-depth social, cultural and personal understanding of the patient and deliver care within a social and personal relationship (Bode & Hariramamurthi, 2014; Shankar, Lavekar, Deb, & Sharma, 2012). Implementation and adoption of HIT without giving due emphasis on emotion may decrease this his torical significance of emotion in clinical practice.

Our findings echo the literature that argues for the value of emotion and empathy in clinical practice (Jeffrey, 2016). As a result, our findings have implications for the design and development of HIT specificall and IT more generally. Our findings recommend designers to refrain from designing an HIT that substantiall alters the clinical practice to the extent of jeopardising the role of emotion(s). The study also alerts the IT designers toward a value-sensitive approach to designing. Designers should be sensitive toward the value of direct and indirect stakeholders in their specific socio-cultural settings of IT use, and adopt an iterative process of conceptualising, validating and designing an IT solution by making a suitable value trade-off(s) (Friedman Kahn, & Borning, 2008). Hospital administrators would benefit from alternative modes of digitising healthcar practices instead of forcing doctors to use HIT while interacting with patients. Doctors' training in India is not technology-focused. Further, there are long queues of patients waiting to meet the doctor. Therefore, in th short run, we recommend hiring dedicated transcriptionists to digitise patients' records in the EMR. Having an extra person appears feasible in the Indian context due to the relatively cheaper and easily available manpower However, this may not hold true in the contexts where manpower cost is higher. Therefore, in the long run, w recommend the need for integrating EMR with AI-based transcription solutions that can create digital reposito ries using speech recognition during D-P-I.<sup>9</sup>

## 8 | LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

We have explicated the interaction of EMR with the emotional capital and power dynamics in an OPD practice setup. An OPD practice consists mostly of one-to-one interaction between a doctor and a patient (and family mem bers). Whereas practices in other settings, such as the in-patient department or the emergency ward, may be more collaborative and they may include the nursing staff, and other clinical and administrative staff. Further, in OPD there is limited use of technology or complex biomedical instruments in the practice. However, the practices in othe settings may also involve a greater extent of biomedical instruments. Therefore, the extent of emotional capital, it manifestation and the power dynamics in these settings may differ from the OPD practice. It would be, therefore interesting to extend our understanding of the OPD practice to these distinct settings as well (Kumar, Singh, Chan dwani, & Gupta, 2020).

The second limitation pointing to future research relates to the varied expression of resistance by the doctors Our findings highlighted various forms in which doctors' resistance to EMR became manifest. However, to maintain the focus of current study, we did not attempt to explain why doctors exhibited different forms of resistance behaviour. Our initial impressions from the field hint that it may have link to the doctors' clinical specialty, and varied sig nificance of emotional aspect and different levels of technology use in each specialty (The practice of a specialist such as a Cardiologist' may have relatively higher emotional aspects because of the nature of this specialty that deals with more critical illnesses, which also require extended follow-up visits and long-term doctor-patient relationships It may make a cardiologist resist HIT perhaps more severely as compared to a Gastroenterologist who mostly treat acute illness and the number of follow-ups and the related long-term relationships and the emotional component may be relatively lesser in the practice). Future research may usefully conduct a detailed examination of whether an how doctors' clinical specialty matter in their resistance.

Further, to maintain focus of this study, we could not examine the potential role of differences in various special ists' spatial arrangements of practice locations. Depending on the specialty, each doctor may practice at multiple loca tions (eg, OPD, Cath Lab, Critical Care, Operation Theatre, etc.). Since prior literature has acknowledged th significance of spatial arrangement on the conduct of practice (Reckwitz, 2012), we believe it would be interesting to examine whether the spatial arrangement of practice location affects doctors' HIT resistance

While our findings inform the significance of emotional capital in a corporate hospital, it would be intriguing to examine whether and how emotional aspect manifests in a public hospital. As the patients' queue and workload ar huge, it would be interesting to know how doctors bring their embodied emotional capital and accumulate their capi tal structure in a public hospital. A future comparison study examining the interaction of HIT with the delivery of D P-I in public hospital may provide this crucial insight.

## 9 | CONCLUSION

We carried out an ethnographic study to explain the interaction between EMR and doctors' historically rooted socia standing. Findings from our research contribute to the literature on doctors' HIT resistance, IS and power literatur and add to the analytical power of Bourdieu's practice theory. Doctors' power has traditionally been associated wit their clinical expertise, and prior literature has explained that doctors' HIT resistance results from their perceived threat to clinical expertise. Our study found that the source of doctors' socially and historically rooted power is i their embodied emotional capital (in addition to their clinical expertise). Resistance to HIT may also result from a perceived threat to the reproduction of their embodied emotional capital in practice. Our finding adds to the IS and power literature by revealing emotion as a source of socially and historically rooted power and its interaction with the IT implementation efforts. Our contextual finding also extends the analytical scope of Bourdieu's practice theory. While the extant literature has employed Bourdieu to primarily study capital “accumulation” efforts in everyday human activities, our study revealed its analytical power also to capture capital “de-accumulation” that may occur when an IT application interacts with the micro-level human practices. This is a critical addition to Bourdieu's theor as the world is moving toward more digital and various online fields are likely to emerge, creating newer interac tion between IT and human practices.

## Entering the field

Entering the field in an ethnographic inquiry is a critical moment “when a researcher's status changes from that of an outsider to being a member (albeit only temporarily) of the practice world” (Chughtai & Myers, 2017, p. 798); to “be accepted and be there along with the participants, living with them in their every day situations (Kunda, 2013) . (p. 797, emphasis in original). Thus, entrance into the participants' world is the first encounter of this complex world of phenomenon. The researcher, embedded in her own social world of meaning-making (world of literature), requires to venture out from own world to another's (participant's world to become part of the larger whole to seek meaning (Alvesson & Sköldberg, 2013). This requires the extraction of knowledge from the principle of the hermeneutic cycle that suggests that human understanding is achieved by the iteration between the interdependent parts (part of the researcher's world and part of the participant's world) and the whole (Alvesson & Sköldberg, 2013). These principles of “hermeneutic cycle”, “dialogical reasoning” and “social construction of knowledge” form the core principles of interpretive study (Klein & Myers, 1999).

## Interpretive shadowing

Shadowing a target means a researcher closely following a member of an organization over an extended period of time” (McDonald, 2005, p. 456). The researcher follows wherever the targets go from the time they begin the work day until they leave the workplace. Throughout the shadowing, in addition to producing several observation notes, the researcher asks questions about the actions being performed and produces the running commentary. At the end, the researcher gets a first-hand detailed “multi-dimensional picture of the role, approach, philosophy and task of the person being shadowed (McDonald, 2005, p. 457). This information gives access to those detail that may seem trivial but are actually important as well as information that is difficult to articulate. Thus shadowing not only provides the totality of actions being performed by the target but also the perspectives and the purposes that shape those actions. By following the target, shadowing also provides the opportunity to access those sites of the organisation where the researcher would otherwise be unable to go. Thus, shadowing allow researcher an opportunity to fully enter the actors' world. Shadowing would also allow the researcher unlimited access to understanding what participants say or what is observed, but also to uncover the intentionality behind the actions produced by the actors by providing live commentary (McDonald, 2005). Understanding the intention ality is critical to interpretive study as it believes that the world is made of “Intentions” (Alvesson & Sköldberg, 2013); “essentially person exists only in the performance of intentional acts” (Heidegger, 1976, p. 73 quoted in Butler, 1998, p. 289).
