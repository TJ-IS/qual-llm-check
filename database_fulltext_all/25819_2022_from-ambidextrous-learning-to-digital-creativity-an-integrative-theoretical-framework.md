# From ambidextrous learning to digital creativity: An integrative theoretical framework

Zhen Shao<sup>1</sup> | Xixi Li<sup>2</sup> | Qian Wang<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Economics and Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China <sup>2</sup>School of Economics and Management, University of Science and Technology Beijing, Beijing, China

Correspondence Xixi Li, School of Economics and Management, University of Science and Technology Beijing, Beijing, China. Email: cici.li.hongkong@gmail.com

Funding information National Natural Science Foundation of China, Grant/Award Numbers: 71771064, 71490721, 71701110

## Abstract

Digital creativity (DC) stands for employee's generation of useful and fresh ideas through the use of digital technologies, which is one of the prominent consequences of effective digital technology use. Drawing insights from the tripartite view of technology use (ie, technology, individual and task elements) and the social role lens, our study proposes and tests an integrative theoretical framework to understand how female and male employees progress from ambidextrous learning in digital technology use to DC. We first interviewed five frontline employees and then surveyed 221 employees that were different from the interview sample from eight organisations. All participants utilised a similar version of internet-of-things (IoT) in their daily work. We find that (a) exploitative use has a stronger influence on DC for women than for men, while explorative use displays a higher impact on DC for men than for women, and (b) technology digital affordance (TDA), digital knowledge (DK), and task variety (TV) exhibit significant influences on both exploitative and explorative uses to varying extents. The post-hoc analysis reveals that exploitative use mediates the influences of TDA and DK on DC only for women; explorative use mediates the impacts of TDA and TV on DC only for men. Our study advances the understandings of the downstream impact of technology use in the digital context.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

A new generation of digital technologies has emerged and evolved in business applications over the past decade, such as social media, internet-of-things (IoT) and artificial intelligence. Digital technologies differ from the early generation of technologies, such as the internet and email, in their editability, flexibility, and transfigurability (Kallinikos, Aaltonen, & Marton, 2013; Schmitz, Teng, & Webb, 2016). These technologies further differ from traditional enterprise information systems in their ease of use, ubiquity and empowerment (K. C. Lee, 2013) Thus, organisations from all industries, ranging from Sephora, Target and Cadillac to the New York Times and the Weather Company, are keen to embrace digital transformation in order to gain a competitive advantage and achieve success in the global market (Gupta, 2018; Nambisan, Lyytinen, Majchrzak, & Song, 2017; Oldham & Da Silva, 2015). According to International Data Corporation (IDC, 2019), digital transformation spending on a global scale will reach USD \$7.4 trillion over the period of 2020 to 2023, with a compound annual growth rate of 17.5%. Although organisations have accelerated their investment in digital technologies, they often encounter difficulties in utilising digital technologies to drive revenue growth and improve customer experiences (Albanese & Manning, 2016; Nambisan et al., 2017; Oldham & Da Silva, 2015). For example, more than half of executives reported that their organisation does not have a clear strategy in developing the needed skills for th digital workforce (Accenture, 2015).

Indeed, one major issue with the so-called “digital impasse” is that frontline employees often lack digital creativ ity (DC) (Saldanha, 2019) and fail to utilise digital technologies to achieve expected job performance (IDC, 2017; Kiron, Kane, Palmer, Phillips, & Buckley, 2016; Nambisan et al., 2017). Although scholars have long discussed how technologies foster creativity in general across individual, team and organisational levels (Chatterjee, Moody, Lowry Chakraborty, & Hardin, 2021; K. C. Lee, 2013; M. R. Lee & Chen, 2015; Zagalo & Branco, 2015), there is limited understanding available regarding how to motivate frontline employees' DC—the generation of novel and usefu ideas and solutions through using emerging digital technologies. Given that digital technologies are characterised with agility and innovation in peripheral users (Accenture, 2015), frontline employees serve as the critical agents to facilitate the assimilation of the technologies into business processes and creatively utilise them to improve business offerings (Kiron et al., 2016; Nambisan et al., 2017). In other words, frontline employees transform the effective use of digital technologies into DC, thereby unleashing the value potential of digital innovation. The downstream impact of technology use on employee performance has received considerable research attention across various enterpris system contexts, such as knowledge management systems (X. J. Zhang, 2017) and customer relationship management systems (R. R. Chen, Ou, Wang, Peng, & Davison, 2020). Nevertheless, understandings of the relationship between technology use and performance in the digital technology context remain limited. Given that learning is an imperative part of effective technology use (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013), our study develops an integrative theo retical framework to understand the path from ambidextrous learning in digital technology use—exploitative use and explorative use—to DC

Our study has two particular research objectives. First, we draw on the tripartite view to identify the ke technology, individual, and task factors that influence frontline employees' digital technology usage behaviours. While scholars have long emphasised the significance of the tripartite factors of technology, individual and task in user adaptation and innovation behaviours (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013; Burton-Jones & Straub Jr., 2006; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994), the uniqueness of the digital technology use context warrants additional research efforts Second, we incorporate the lens of social role to understand gender differences in the path from ambidextrous learning to DC. Although empirical evidence indicates little inequality in either technology use (Gebhardt, Thomson, Ainley, & Hillman, 2019; Tao, Scott, & McCarthy, 2020) or creativity (Amabile, 1996; Baer, 1998) in biological sex, gender stereotypes persist in today's organisations (Luksyte, Unsworth, & Avery, 2018; Wang, Chiang, Tsai, Lin, & Cheng, 2013). Therefore, we examine how such gender-based social roles (Eagly, 1987; Eagly & Wood, 1991 construct different patterns among women and men in their journeys from ambidextrous learning to creativity in using digital technologies.

Overall, the integrative theoretical framework on how women and men progress from ambidextrous learning to creativity in the digital context has four major contributions. First, our study offers a contextualised understanding of the downstream impact of technology use on performance in the digital technology use context. Specifically, we investigate the influences of exploitative and explorative use on DC through the integration of two theoretica lenses—the tripartite view on technology use and the social role perspective. Second, we decompose the anteced ents of ambidextrous technology usage behaviours in terms of technology, individual, and task factors, and highligh the importance of technology digital affordance (TDA), digital knowledge and task variety, respectively. Third, we examine gender differences in the relationship between ambidextrous use and creativity in the digital technolog context. We first differentiate between biological sex and gender-based social roles, and then portray how gender-based social norms and expectations pave different paths for men and women as they move from learning to creativity when using digital technologies at work. Fourth, our findings, derived from both informal interviews and field survey data, provide practical guidance regarding the motivation and management of frontline employees effective use of digital technologies.

We organise the remainder of this paper as follows. We lay out the theoretical foundations in Section 2, an develop the theoretical model and hypotheses in Section 3. We describe the procedures of informal interviews survey instrument design, and data collection in Section 4, and present the statistical analysis results in Section 5 Section 6 discusses the theoretical and practical implications of our findings, and Section 7 draws the conclusion.

## 2 | THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS

We first conceptualise DC together with the ambidextrous exploitative and explorative use of digital technologies We then articulate a tripartite perspective of technology use. which informs our selection of the key predictive fac tors in the digital context. Finally, we review the extant literature on creativity and technology use that pertain to biological sex and gender stereotypes, and explain why we use the gender lens to enrich our theoretical framework.

## 2.1 | Digital creativity and ambidextrous learning in technology use

We define DC as employees' generation of fresh and useful ideas or plans to achieve better job performance through the use of new-generation digital technologies, for example, mobile applications, cloud computing, and IoT. The extant literature on DC mainly discusses how technologies foster creativity in general across individual, team, and organisational levels (M. H. Chen, 2006; K. C. Lee, 2013; Thatcher & Brown, 2010; Zagalo & Branco, 2015). While these discussions inform our conceptualization of DC, nevertheless, they demonstrate a limited focus on (a) the new generation of technologies that have evolved in the Fourth Industrial Revolution over the last decade, and (b) technology use as an integral part of creativity. We consider DC to be a contextualised form of creativity—the novel and valuable ideas raised by employees that usually involve changes in products, services, and processes in organisational settings (Amabile, 1988; Amabile, 1996). For example, when employees have instant connections with their colleagues through social media, they can initiate an open call for the crowdsourcing for ideas and solutions whenever needed (Blohm, Leimeister, & Krcmar, 2013). With the help of cloud computing, employees have synchronised access to work-related documents and can thus engage in real-time learning and improvisation (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010). The IoT provides an important source of big data and enables discovery of internal and external customers' needs (Côrte-Real, Ruivo, & Oliveira, 2019; Lehrer, Wieneke, Vom Brocke, Jung, & Seidel, 2018).

DC extends the emerging notion of digital innovation from the organisational level to the individual level. Crea tivity differs from innovation in two ways. First, creativity occurs at the stage of novel idea generation, while innova tion belongs to the next stage of idea implementation (Anderson, Potonik, & Zhou, 2014). Second, creativity signifie the idea generation behaviours of “an individual or a small group of individuals working together”, wherea innovation entails the idea implementation practice within an organization (Amabile, 1988, p. 126; Oldham & Cummings, 1996). Specifically, digital innovation at the organisational level involves actual changes in products, ser vices, processes, and business models that are enabled by the organisation-wide adoption and use of digital technol ogies (Barrett, Davidson, Prabhu, & Vargo, 2015; Fichman, Dos Santos, & Zheng, 2014; Fichman, Nambisan, & Halpern, 2014). Digital innovation involves digital technologies as an integral part, manifesting in innovation out comes through digital technology use, digital infrastructures that facilitate digital technology use, and digital context that diffuse digital technology use (Nambisan et al., 2017). At the individual level, employees serve as important agents of innovation within organisations who closely interact with, make sense of, and utilise digital technologies to seek new ideas and solutions to support job performance (Nambisan et al., 2017; Ogbanufe & Gerhart, 2020). As such, employees' DC pertains to one of the outcomes of the effective use of digital technologies—to use digital tech nologies for the generation of original ideas and solutions in order to support “the goals for using the system” (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013, p. 633).

Just as digital innovation is closely associated with organisational learning (Barrett et al., 2015; Kohli & Melville, 2018), DC is integral to individual learning when using digital technologies. IS scholars have discussed two forms of technology use that entail ambidextrous forms of individual learning, namely exploitative use and explorative use (Li, Hsieh, & Rai, 2013; Luo, Zhang, Xu, & Ling, 2015; Shao, Feng, & Hu, 2017). The ambidextrous exploita tion and exploration concepts firstly emerge from organisation learning theory: exploitation refers to the extension and improvement through the utilisation of existing resources; exploration involves experimentation and innovation with the aim of discovering new opportunities (Guisado-Gonzalez, Gonzalez-Blanco, & Coca-Pérez, 2017; Jansen, Vera, & Crossan, 2009; March, 1991). In the context of digital technology use, exploitative use stands for employees use of digital technologies in ways that involve repetition, refinement, and extension; explorative use entail employees' use of digital technologies with experimentation and innovation. In short, DC emphasises employees attainment of novel ideas to support job performance through digital technology use, while ambidextrous usage behaviours focus on rich learning experiences embedded in digital technology use. Therefore, the relationship between ambidextrous learning behaviours and DC essentially contextualises the general path from technology use to job performance (eg, R. R. Chen et al., 2020; Sykes & Venkatesh, 2017; X. J. Zhang, 2017) into the digital technol ogy use context.

## 2.2 | A tripartite view on technology use in the digital context

Since understanding socio-cognitive frames of digital innovation constitute a significant part of digital innovation management theory (Nambisan et al., 2017), we apply and contextualise the classic tripartite framework to identify the salient technology, individual, and task factors, and examine how they influence employees' learning and creativ ity when using digital technologies. The tripartite view highlights the importance of technology, individual and tas factors in the technology use context and has been widely applied to understand user learning and creativity, such as user adaptation behaviours (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Schmitz et al., 2016) and effective technology use (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013; Burton-Jones & Straub Jr., 2006). Therefore, the tripartite view on technology use suits our research purpose of understanding employees' digital technology usage behaviours

First, TDA is a suitable technology factor that potentially influences user learning and creativity in the context of digital technology use. Specifically, TDA refers to the extent to which users perceive a particular technology to enable digitization in their work process and content. The affordance perspective differs from most previous studie that examine the deterministic impacts of generic technology features on technology use, such as system capability (Hsieh, Rai, & Keil, 2011), system complexity (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997; Kang & Santhanam, 2004; Liang, Peng, Xue, Guo, & Wang, 2015), and system type (Bagayogo, Lapointe, & Bassellier, 2014), and thus, is especially suitable for understanding the usage behaviours of the new technology generation (Leonardi, 2011). Instead, the affordance perspective adopts a relational view, depicts the interactive tension between users and technology, and highlights “the possibilities for goal-oriented action afforded to specified user groups by technical objects” (Dremel et al., 2018; Markus & Silver, 2008, p. 622). Thus, when employees perceive the digitization functionality of a technolog to support business needs and work performance, they are likely to feel motivated to take action and realise th goals of learning and creativity in using digital technologies

Second, digital knowledge captures the essential individual factor in user learning and creativity in the digital technol ogy use context. Digital knowledge stands for users' experiences, ideas, and cognitive schemas regarding know-how, know-what and know-why in using digital technologies. There is convergence in both the creativity and technology use literature regarding the importance of user knowledge. On the one hand, employees' knowledge and skills have been long recognised as the prominent components of creativity (Amabile, 1997; Anderson et al., 2014). On the other hand, users knowledge is the most salient source that supports effective technology use (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013; Marcolin Compeau, Munro, & Huff, 2000) and facilitates diffusion of the new generation of technologies (Calantone & Rubera, 2011; Ifinedo, 2011; S. M. Lee, Kim, Choi, & Lee, 2009). When employees possess digital knowledge as the fun damental cognitive resource that enables learning and effective technology use, they are likely to exhibit creativity and innovation in using digital technologies (S. M. Lee et al., 2009; Seo, Chae, & Lee, 2015).

Third, task variety is an especially important task factor in the context of digital technology use. According to job characteristic theory (Hackman & Oldham, 1975), task variety means the extent to which employees need to com plete a wide range of tasks, which possibly involve the frequent occurrence of unexpected and novel events. Previ ous literature has underlined the significance of task autonomy in the traditional enterprise system context, that is, the extent to which employees have discretion and independence in scheduling and determining (Ahuja, Chudoba, Kacmar, Mcknight, & George, 2007; Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Sharma & Yetton, 2003), but have paid limited attention to task variety. Notably, digital transformation implies business process automation and empowerment for both employees and consumers, which guarantees task autonomy but expands task variety (Fitzgerald, Kruschwitz Bonnet, & Welch, 2014; Nambisan et al., 2017; Vial, 2019). Since task variety provides exceptional, non-routine scenarios that give rise to technology-related learning and creativity (Jasperson, Carter, & Zmud, 2005; Sun, 2012), we consider it to be an important driver of user learning and creativity in the digital technology use context.

## 2.3 | Gender differences in creativity and technology use

In addition to the tripartite view, we attempt to understand gender differences in learning and creativity i employees' digital technology usage behaviours. While sex stands for biological features, gender is constructed and learned through socialisation processes. Social role theory is one of the most popular theories that explains th qualities or behavioral tendencies believed to be desirable for each sex , that is, gender-based social role (Eagly, 1987, p. 13; Eagly, Wood, & Diekman, 2000). In particular, social role theory denotes the majority of norma tive beliefs and expectations about genders: women are communal, expressive and relationship-oriented, while men are agentic, instrumental and task oriented. Next, we synthesise both the creativity and technology use literature relating to biological sex and gender stereotypes.

## 2.3.1 | Creativity literature

On the one hand, biological sex does not seem to make a difference in terms of the creativity exhibited by women and men. Early studies on creativity used only female samples (eg, Amabile, 1983) and later (eg, Amabile, 1996) chan ged such a practice because gender [sex] differences in creative performance have tended … to be rare . (Baer, 1998, p. 21). Other creativity scholars have concurred that, although there could be considerable evidence of differences in patterns and areas of strengths” between women and men, “relative equality” in creativity across sex still exists (Baer & Kaufman, 2008, pp. 1–2; Kogan, 1974)

On the other hand, social role theory explains the gender stereotypes relating to job preferences (Konrad, Corrigall, Lieb, & Ritchie Jr, 2000), role congruity and prejudice in general work behaviours (Eagly & Karau, 2002) and the discretionary evaluation of innovative work behaviours (Luksyte et al., 2018). Specifically, according to thi theory, women are interdependent and cooperative, maintain good relationships with coworkers, and process information in detail; men are independent and risk-taking, desire leadership and power, and rely on schema-based processing rules at work (Baumeister & Sommer, 1997; Gilligan, 1986; Konrad et al., 2000). Gender stereotypes that have been socially constructed pose significant influences on employees' perceptions and interpretations of genera work behaviours (Wang et al., 2013). Likewise, innovative work behaviours usually involve risk-taking and masculin activities and thus tend to be ascribed more to men than to women (Luksyte et al., 2018)

## 2.3.2 | Technology use literature

Biological sex appears to be an important digital inequality factor in the past but recent evidence indicates a reverse trend. For example, compared to men, women display lower levels of computer access, computer usage and computer self-efficacy (Felter, 1985; Hunt & Bohlin, 1993; Kafai, Fishman, Bruckman, & Rockham, 2002; Wei, Teo Chan, & Tan, 2011). Scholars find similar patterns in the contexts of e-government portal (Venkatesh, Sykes, & Venkatraman, 2014), collaboration technology (Maruping & Magni, 2012), instant messaging (Shen, Lee, Cheung, & Chen, 2010), and web design of e-commerce (Cyr, 2014). Meanwhile, women exhibit a higher level of computer anxi ety than men (Igbaria & Chakrabarti, 1990; Morrow, Preix, & Mcelroy, 1986; Rosen & Maguire, 1990), acquire usag skills more slowly (Gefen & Straub, 1997), and display a lower tendency in technology exploration (Maruping & Magni, 2012). Nevertheless, recent studies observe conflicting evidences. Specifically, although African American parents encourage technology use more for sons than daughters, the female adolescents show a higher level of con fidence in technology use than their male counterparts (Tao et al., 2020). Worldwide statistical reports on compute and information literacy (CIL) indicate that female students display better capabilities in both general and specialised CIL skills than male students, but their self-reported confidence is similar to their male counterparts (Gebhardt et al., 2019).

In addition, because women and men are, respectively, communal and agentic, researchers consistently find gender-based social role patterns across different technology use contexts. For example, women are likely to adopt collaboration technology when they perceive that their surrounding peers are also using the technology; men would be motivated to use the technology when they perceive it as instrumental for goal achievement or task improvement (Brown, Dennis, & Venkatesh, 2010; Niehaves & Plattfaut, 2014). Male employees engage in exploration of collaboration technologies in an autonomous task environment (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005 Maruping & Magni, 2012), while the exploration behaviours of female employees are driven by social activities, such as information exchange and knowledge sharing among peers (Maruping & Magni, 2012). Women's usage behaviours of mobile internet are more influenced by non-instrumental factors such as effort expectancy, and men's usage behaviours are utilitarian-oriented and schema-based, and are thus subject to the influences of habit (Venkatesh, Thong, & Xu, 2012). Whereas men tend to be instrumental in gaining prestige in social hierar chies, women consistently prefer to use social network sites (R. Chen & Sharma, 2015; Krasnova, Veltri, Eling, & Buxmann, 2017), virtual communities (Hampton, Goulet, Marlow, & Rainie, 2012; Ma, Khansa, Deng, & Kim, 2014), and blogs (Chai, Das, & Rao, 2011; Wattal, Racherla, & Mandviwalla, 2010) to connect with other and enhance intimacy in personal relationships. Still, some scholars conceive technology to be an instrumenta tool to empower women (Oreglia & Srinivasan, 2016; Pozzebon, Mackrell, & Nielsen, 2014; Suseno & Abbott, 2021) or to even help balance gender representation in male-dominated industries (Johansson, Morell, & Lindell, 2020). While substantial evidence shows gender differences in terms of how various factors drive tech nology use behaviours, there is limited understanding of how gender differences manifest in the downstream impact of technology use, especially in the digital context

In light of the reviewed literature on both sex and gender, we do not expect too much inequality in creativit and technology use between women and men today. However, since technology use (Venkatesh et al., 2012 Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003) and creativity (Amabile, 1996; Amabile & Conti, 1999; Shalley, Gilson, & Blum, 2000) are largely subject to the social and normative influences in organisations, we assume that gender-based social roles account for the different patterns between women and men in the path from ambidextrous learn ing to DC.

## 3 | RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESES

Integrating the social role lens together with the tripartite view of technology use, our study develops an integrativ theoretical framework to understand the influences of ambidextrous learning on creativity in the digital technology use context (Figure 1).

## 3.1 | From ambidextrous learning to digital creativity

As defined, exploitative use refers to users' continuous extension and refinement in using digital technologies, and explorative use represents users' experimentation with and discovery of new functionalities when using digital tech nologies (Li et al., 2013; Schmitz et al., 2016; Shao et al., 2017). Both exploitative and explorative use signify user learning that is specific to digital technology use, though in different forms. Exploitative use symbolises users' in depth appreciation of the existing functionalities of digital technologies; explorative use represents users' under standing of the value potential embedded in the functions and features that were unknown before.

Organisational research has long discussed the importance of learning, in both forms of exploitation and explora tion, for advancing innovation. For example, “a firm's ‘learning’ or ‘absorptive capacity’” plays a significant rol across various management contexts, such as knowledge management and research and development (R&D (Cohen & Levinthal, 1989, p. 569; Lane, Koka, & Pathak, 2006; Zahra & George, 2002). Similar insights are found in the marketing domain, where both exploitative and explorative market learning help accumulate heterogeneou forms of capital and resources, and facilitate new product development (Kim & Atuahene-Gima, 2010). IS scholar also indicate that learning is a necessary factor for digital innovation at the organisational level because learning enables organisations to identify and seize the opportunities for digital innovation (Barrett et al., 2015; Kohli & Melville, 2018).

Therefore, we expect both exploitative and explorative use to contribute to DC, that is, employees' generation of useful and fresh ideas through digital technology use, at the individual level. Specifically, repetition, refinement and extension in digital technology use enable employees to understand the nuances of existing functions and fea tures of technology. Employees thus tend to develop a thorough understanding of the characteristics of and compat ibility among technology functions, thereby generating novel ideas or solutions through a combination or substitution of existing functions and ultimately achieving DC at work. In addition, the experimentation with and exploration of digital technology features allow employees to uncover new features and functions, which wer unknown before. Employees who are aware of various technology features and the unexpected ways of using them tend to have more opportunities to identify new problems or resolve problems in novel ways, thereby achieving DC at work. Hence, we propose that:

H1a. Exploitative use has a positive influence on employees' digital creativity.

H1b. Explorative use has a positive influence on employees' digital creativity

## 3.2 | A tripartite view decomposing the ambidextrous usage behaviours

We then draw on the tripartite view to understand how TDA, digital knowledge and task variety affect the ambidextrous usage behaviours of exploitation and exploration. First, TDA refers to the extent to which users perceive a certain technology to be capable of realising digitization at work. Affordance perceptions serve as a prominent signa and motivator for users to engage in goal-oriented actions (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013; Volkoff & Strong, 2013) When users perceive a technology to be capable of actualizing some business functions, for example, digitalization they are motivated to use the technology in a way that aligns with the goal of the particular business functions (Chatterjee, Moody, Lowry, Chakraborty, & Hardin, 2015; Leonardi, 2011). IS researchers have identified different key technology affordances tailored to various technology contexts, including information technology application (Chatterjee et al., 2015), social media (Karahanna, Xu, Xu, & Zhang, 2018; Leidner, Gonzalez, & Koch, 2018), and elec tronic health record (Strong et al., 2014), and have validated their importance in facilitating organisational and individual performance.

In the digital technology use context, employees, to a varying extent, recognise that digital technologies can enable digitization, which in turn facilitates work processes and resolves business needs. As such, employees digital affordance perceptions are likely to inspire them to engage in learning activities and to use digital tech nologies effectively. Specifically, employees would be willing to use digital technologies in a repetitive and rou tine manner so that they can save their energy and efforts for business operations (Davison & Ou, 2016) Moreover, employees would also be intrigued by the flexibility of the digital affordance and would explore the new features of the technology to support performance (Schmitz et al., 2016; Yoo, 2010). Hence, we pro pose that:

H2. Technology digital affordance has a positive influence on employees' (a) exploitative use and (b) explorative use.

Second, digital knowledge represents employees' know-how, know-what and know-why with regards to dig ital technology use. Accumulated knowledge usually allows organisations to develop rules and enables individ uals to formulate schemas, which facilitate and optimise resource integration and utilisation (Duncan, 1995 Jasperson et al., 2005). In the technology use context, a high level of system knowledge usually signifie employees' competence in the effective use of technologies to support job-related goals (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013; Marcolin et al., 2000).

In the digital technology use context, digital knowledge is the most important form of cognitive capital that enables employees to use digital technologies with sophistication and flexibility at work (S. M. Lee et al., 2009; Seo et al., 2015). On one hand, employees' refinement and extension of digital technology use largely depends on thei knowledge structure (Schmitz et al., 2016). In other words, when employees have rich experiences using digital tech nologies, they are likely to understand existing features and functionalities in more depth and will be capable of com bining or refining the functions to support business activities. On the other hand, knowledge in general enhances employees' creativity and endows them with intellectual resources to perform innovative activities through technol ogy use (Che, Wu, Wang, & Yang, 2019; Leonard & Sensiper, 1998). As such, employees who are experts in usin digital technologies tend to be capable of experimenting and exploring new features and functions throughout the usage processes. Therefore, we propose that:

H3. Digital knowledge has a positive influence on employees' (a) exploitative use and (b) explorative use.

Third, task variety signifies the extent to which employees perform a wide range of tasks at work (Hackman & Oldham, 1975). A high level of task variety is usually associated with employees' frequent confrontations with nonroutine events, which, consequently, create rich learning opportunities in technology use (Jasperson et al., 2005; Sun, 2012). For one, different assignments offer employees valuable opportunities to practice and master different tasks. As such, they are likely to develop a deep understanding of task processes and structures. For another, nove assignments for existing tasks may trigger employees' innate desire for challenges (Peng & Guo, 2019), which in tur drive their learning motivation and intention

In the digital technology use context, the large-scale automation of business processes makes the factor of task variety critical for employees' learning activities in using digital technologies. For instance, when faced with various task assignments, employees would be willing to develop routines when using certain functionali ties in order to improve efficiency and enhance their multi-tasking skills. In addition, a broader spectrum of tas variety also provides opportunities for employees to think beyond the existing routine use of digital technolo gies and to try out new features to support unexpected incidents at work (Peng & Guo, 2019). Hence, we propose that:

H4. Task variety has a positive influence on employees' (a) exploitative use and (b) explorative use

## 3.3 | Gender differences in the path from ambidextrous learning to digital creativity

We further depict how gender-based social roles (Eagly, 1987; Eagly & Wood, 1991) construct different paths whe female and male employees progress from learning to creativity in using digital technologies. Evolved from typica social norms and expectations on the biological sexes, women are relatively interdependent, cooperative, and pay attention to details, while men are independent, risk-taking, and use schema-based information processing rules at work (Darley & Smith, 1995; Konrad et al., 2000). Such social roles are also observable in technology use behaviours Specifically, women are likely to avoid risks in using technologies and they attain effective technology use behaviours mainly through careful processing of detailed information (Brown et al., 2010; Chen & Sharma, 2015; Krasnova et al., 2017; Venkatesh et al., 2012). In contrast, men, who are oriented towards performance outcomes and achieve ments, tend to have strong intentions to explore and innovate with technologies (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Mar uping & Magni, 2012; Niehaves & Plattfaut, 2014).

In the digital technology use context, the social role differences are visible in the different learning paths through which female and male employees achieve DC. Compared with male employees, female employees tend to be mor conservative and capable of assimilating and processing surrounding information in detail when using digital technol ogies. As such, female employees pursue the learning path that involves less risks but requires more attention to seemingly fragment information, that is, to rely on the combination, refinement, or incremental extension of existin technology functionalities to realise DC. In other words, female employees are more likely to generate new idea and solutions at work through exploitative use of digital technologies than their male counterparts. On the contrary, male employees would be more motivated to demonstrate competitiveness and innovativeness than femal employees when using digital technologies. Therefore, compared with female employees, male employees are more inclined to engage in DC through radical forms of innovative use, such as exploration and experimentation, which i risky and challenging. The above analysis leads to the following hypotheses:

H5a. The influence of exploitative use on digital creativity is stronger for female employees than for male employees.

H5b. The influence of explorative use on digital creativity is stronger for male employees than for female employees.

## 4 | RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## 4.1 | Instrument design

We referred to the extant literature to develop the survey instrument. Items of DC were adapted from the measure of creativity in the organisation context (Zhou & George, 2001). Items for exploitative use and explorative use wer adapted from Shao et al. (2017) and Schmitz et al. (2016). We consider self-reported measures as appropriate for th assessment of DC and the ambidextrous forms of use. DC involves ideas generated at the initial stage that are not yet implemented or even shared with others, and are thus difficult to observe by a third-party. Likewise, exploitative use and explorative use are not necessarily visible to either peer colleagues or supervisors. A similar self-assessment approach is common for organisational behaviours in general (eg, knowledge sharing, Reinholt, Pedersen, & Foss, 2011) and technology use in particular (eg, innovative use, Li et al., 2013)

We followed Chatterjee et al. (2015)'s study to measure employees' perceptions of technology affordances to enable digitization. Items of digital knowledge were adapted from Seo et al. (2015), and those of task variety wer based on Morgeson and Humphrey (2006) and Liang et al. (2015). Moreover, we controlled for digital self-efficac (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Tierney & Farmer, 2002) and task autonomy (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Morris &

Venkatesh, 2010), as well as for user demographic information, including gender, age, education and work experience. All constructs were measured with a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree (1)”, “neutral (3)” to “strongly agree (5)” (see Table A1 and Appendix A).

The instrument was firstly translated from English into Chinese by a senior IS professor, and then from Chinese back into English by another senior IS professor. We compared the two English versions of the instrument and fur ther refined the Chinese version. As such, we ensured the quality of the Chinese-version instrument (Brislin, 1970). We then invited two IS doctoral students and three EMBA students to proofread the Chinese-version instrument to check if there were still ambiguities or inaccuracies. With content validity ensured, we conducted a pilot test to examine the construct validity of the instrument. We invited a class of 60 college students in an IS introductory course to participate in our pilot test, 43 of which provided valid responses. We obtained satisfactory psychometri properties with all constructs in our research model with the inputs from the 43 participants. We slightly polished the wording of the Chinese-version instrument based on the feedback from the pilot sample

## 4.2 | Data collection

Our target technology is IoT since it has been recognised as a representative digital technology that effectively enables automatic information identification and exchange (Fitzgerald et al., 2014) and has gained vast popularit over the past decade. We contacted a third-party provider of IoT in southeast China and selected eight out of it 30 clients for survey administration. The eight client organisations had a similar number of employees and revenue volumes and were spread across various industries, ranging from manufacturing, finance, and logistics, to real estate retailing and IT service. All eight organisations had used a similar version of IoT provided by the third-party provider for more than 1 year, and had progressed into a mature stage of IoT usage. Frontline employees usually installed a mobile version of the IoT on their personal smart phones; they could also use on-site tablets with the mobile application in their offices, warehouses or stores. The target IoT is a mobile computing system that contains a location mod ule, a smart office module, and an enterprise social media module. The location module allows frontline employees to have instant access to their own and their colleagues' location and routing information during worktime. Th smart office module supports automated or remote control of energy consumption such as lighting, heating and cooling in offices, warehouses or stores. The enterprise social media model facilitates the instant communication among employees and enables them to schedule meetings, list to-dos, as well as download and upload documents from clouds.

We conducted informal interviews with five frontline employees from three of the eight organisations, including two frontline workers in a manufacturing company, two carriers in a logistics company, and one salesperson from a retailing store. The five interviewees, though from different industries, showed convergence regarding their exploit ative and explorative use of the IoT application. Specifically, the interviewees confirmed that they often combined various functions across different modules to resolve a specific business problem, and explored and experimented with the smart functions in the location or the smart office module to support their task performance. The inter viewees also provided concrete examples of DC. The frontline workers shared that they developed new approache for energy management across different warehouses with the big data obtained from the smart office module. The carriers indicated that they formulated flexible scheduling rules and agile delivery methods with the location and rou ting data of the frontline colleagues. The salesperson shared that he/she generated new ideas for store layout an dynamic online–offline promotion methods based on the location data available in the IoT application. Overall, th IoT application provided frontline employees with new and useful ideas to support their task performance.

We then collected data through an online survey from September to November, 2018. Our target sample was frontline employees who used the IoT on a daily basis to support their job performance. We randomly selected 350 frontline employees from different functional departments in the eight organisations, so as to ensure a diversi fied background in our sample (Liang et al., 2015; Peng, Sun, & Guo, 2018). The survey instrument was comprised of three sections. The first section explained our research motivation and informed the employees that their participa tion was voluntary, and that their personal information would be kept confidential. The second section enclosed self-reported measurements for the eight constructs in our research model (see Table 1). The third section asked questions about demographic information, including gender, age, education, and work experience. We offered each participant a reward of RMB-5 upon completion of the questionnaire. We administered the questionnaire to th 350 selected employees through distribution of a QR code linked with the online survey and obtained 285 responses We retained 221 valid responses after cleaning the missing and dirty data. Our sample size of 221 can satisfy th sample requirement of CB-SEM—10 times the number of items in the construct with the most measures (ie, thre items for each construct), or 10 times the total number of predictors for the same dependent variable (ie, eight pre dictors including six independent variables and two control variables) (Bentler & Chou, 1987). ## 5.1 | The measurement model analysis

We first assessed the psychometric properties of the eight latent constructs in terms of construct reliability and con vergent validity. As illustrated in Table 3, the composite reliability and Cronbach's alpha of each construct ha exceeded the reference value of 0.70 (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Straub, 1989). Additionally, the average variance extracted (AVEs) of each construct was high above the threshold value of 0.50 (Chin, Marcolin, & Newsted, 2003) Overall, the analysis results indicate good reliability and convergent validity of the measurement model.

We then assessed the discriminant validity among the eight constructs based on the following three criteria. First, the square root of AVEs of each construct was larger than its correlations with other constructs (Chin et al., 2003) (see Table 3). Second, the Heterotrait–Monotrait (HTMT) values were far below the reference of 0.85 (see Table 4) (Henseler, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2015; Voorhees, Michael, Roger, & Edward, 2016). Third, we performed the confirmatory factor analysis using AMOS 23.0. Discriminant validity is supported when the original measurement model displays a significantly better model fit than any other possible model where the covariance between any pair of constructs is constrained to one (Gefen, Karahanna, Straub, & W., 2003; Segars, 1997). We find that the measure ment model of the eight constructs displayed the best model fit $( \chi ^ { 2 } ~ = 3 8 4 . 1 9 9 , ~ d f ~ = ~ 2 2 4 , ~ { \sf C F I } ~ = ~ 0 . 9 5 3 ,$ ${ \tt R M S E A } = 0 . 0 5 7 , { \tt S R M R } = 0 . 0 3 7 )$ (Bentler & Bonett, 1980; Hair, Black, Babin, & Anderson, 2009), and the model fit deteriorated when the covariance between any two constructs were set to one. In addition, we find that all variance inflation factors (VIFs) were lowe than the conservative threshold of 3.3, indicating that multicollinearity was not a serious issue in the data analysi (Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009).

Considering that our data were collected using a survey method, the analysis results may be subject to the influ ence of common method variance. As such, we adopted three ways to evaluate potential common method bia resulted from the single-wave self-reported survey instrument, including Harman's single-factor test (Podsakof et al., 1986), the common-method-variance factor test (Podsakoff, Mackenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003), and the marker variable test (Lindell & Whitney, 2001) (see Appendix B). ## 6 | DISCUSSIONS

Our study proposes and tests a comprehensive research model regarding the link between frontline employees ambidextrous learning in digital technology use and DC, and obtains the following key findings. First, frontlin employees leverage the ambidextrous learning paths to realise DC, and the influences of exploitative and explor ative use on DC vary across the two gender groups. Specifically, exploitative use exhibits a stronger influenc on DC for female employees, while explorative use plays a more salient role in promoting DC for male employees. Second, we identify TDA, digital knowledge, and task variety as the important tripartite factors of technology, individual, and task, respectively, and find that each factor displays a significant influence on th ambidextrous usage behaviours. Third, the post-hoc analysis results suggest that exploitative use significantl mediates the effects of TDA and digital knowledge on DC for female employees, whereas explorative us significantly mediates the influences of TDA and task variety on DC for male employees. Next, we discuss th theoretical and practical implications of the findings.

## 6.1 | Theoretical implications

This study makes three major theoretical contributions to the IS literature. First, we are among the first studies to concep tualise and operationalise the concept of DC at the individual level. We define and investigate DC as emplovees' genera tion of novel and valuable ideas or solutions through using digital technologies at work. DC differs from the emerging notion of digital innovation (Fichman, Dos Santos, et al., 2014; Kohli & Melville, 2018; Nambisan et al., 2017) but extend organisation digital innovation to the individual level. Our conceptualization and investigation of DC also diverges from recent studies that examine the role of digital technologies in stimulating general creativity among employees (K. C Lee, 2013; Oldham & Da Silva, 2015; Zagalo & Branco, 2015). The construct of DC entails two key elements: (a) novelt and value that collectively constitute creativity in general, and (b) individual creativity that is enabled through digital tech nology use. Since, in the digital age, digital technologies permeate organisational management and customer services, DC treats digital technology use as an integral part of employees' creative job performance rather than an independent set of traditional technology tools that are available for employees to complete their jobs

Second, we develop an overarching framework to understand the ambidextrous learning paths that lead to employee DC. The IS literature has established the path from technology use to job performance in a number of technology contexts, for example, traditional enterprise systems (Sykes & Venkatesh, 2017), knowledge management systems (X. J. Zhang, 2017) and customer relationship management systems (R. R. Chen et al., 2020) Still, there have been limited research efforts to understand the impacts of technology use behaviours that entai different forms of learning, namely exploitation and exploration (Li et al., 2013; Schmitz et al., 2016), on creativ job performance through technology use. In addition, IS scholars usually approach effective technology us behaviours from one or two aspects of the tripartite framework, which includes technology, individual and task factors (eg, Li et al., 2013; Maruping & Magni, 2014; Schmitz et al., 2016; Sykes & Venkatesh, 2017), and there is a lack of empirical validation with a holistic view (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013). Our study not only identifies the salient factors that potentially influence employee learning and creativity in the digital technology use context, but also examines their nuanced influences on DC through the ambidextrous usage behaviours that ar contingent upon gender. As such, our study responds to the call for developing context-specific theories in emerging new research contexts (Davison & Martinsons, 2016; Johns, 2006, 2017).

Third, our study revisits social role theory in the context of digital technology use and explicates the boundary condition of gender for the ambidextrous learning behaviours of exploitation and exploration at the individual level Specifically, we attempt to contextualise social role theory from two aspects. On the one hand, we extend the appli cation of social role theory to the context of digital technologies, which differ from either early generation technolo gies (eg, Banerjee, Kang, Bagchi-Sen, & Rao, 2005; Gefen & Straub, 1997) or traditional enterprise systems (eg, Maruping & Magni, 2012; Venkatesh et al., 2014) but offer much potential for flexibility, empowerment and innova tion. On the other hand, we appropriate social role theory to explain employee creativity that is particularly enable through technology use, instead of technology use in general, which has been well investigated in the prior literature (eg, Brown et al., 2010; Venkatesh et al., 2012). In addition, while gender-based social roles have been widely applied to understand the contingency among various drivers of technology use behaviours (Brown et al., 2010; R. Chen & Sharma, 2015; Krasnova et al., 2017; Maruping & Magni, 2012; Venkatesh et al., 2012), our study is among the first to examine the downstream impact of technology use through the social role-lens. Finally, our study provides evi dence of the equality between female and male employees to achieve DC, albeit through different learning paths thereby responding to the call for theory building research on gender equality by management scholars (Joshi, Neely, Emrich, Griffiths, & George, 2015).

## 6.2 | Limitations and future research directions

Our study has several limitations that offer the opportunity for future research. First, we use self-reported, cross sectional data for hypotheses tests. Although the results of the three statistical methods suggest that our data di not suffer from serious common method bias, future studies can replicate our hypotheses tests by using multiple wave and multiple-sourced data. For example, future studies can employ longitudinal survey designs or fiel experiments to further examine the causal relationship hypothesized in our study. While we have explained tha self-reported measures might be suitable for the assessment of initial ideas (ie, DC) and concrete usage behaviour (ie, exploitative and explorative use), future studies may consider objective construct measures, such as the number of generated new ideas and the launch of new business processes, within a certain period of time. Furthermore middle managers or peer colleagues may serve as good sources to evaluate employee DC

Second, we conceptualise and operationalise DC at the individual level. In light of Amabile's (1988) definition of creativity, there exists three levels of creativity, namely individual, team and organisational levels. Given that our conceptualization of DC extends the notion of digital innovation from the organisational level to the individual level, future studies may conceptualise and investigate DC at the team level or may adopt a multilevel perspective to understand DC (M. Zhang & Gable, 2017), thereby extending the extant literature on creativity to the virtual team context (Chamakiotis, Dekoninck, & Panteli, 2013; Nemiro, 2002; Ocker, 2005). Scholars can also advance the understanding of DC through the gender diversity lens at the team level (Joshi et al., 2015), instead of the gender-base stereotypes at the individual level, as explored in our study.

Third, we identify TDA, digital knowledge, and task variety as the salient technology, individual, and task factors in driving digital technology use and creativity, with digital self-efficacy and task autonomy considered as the control variables. For one, our theoretical framework is yet to incorporate real structuration—the interactions among th technology, individual, and task factors. For another, broader contextual factors, such as industry type or organisation environment, can be taken into consideration in the future. Researchers can also enrich the understanding of employee DC and explore other antecedents from alternative theoretical perspectives, such as motivation theor (Deci & Ryan, 2002) and social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1997).

Fourth, although we tested our research model with survey data from the users of the same IoT across different organisations and industries, the empirical evidence is still only specific to China. Therefore, future studies can repli cate our study across different countries and regions to see if culture plays a boundary condition for the proposed theoretical framework. In particular, since gender-based social roles are often subject to cultural influence (Bussey & Bandura, 1999; Eagly et al., 2000), researchers may pay attention to the nuanced interplay between gen der and culture in the digital technology context.

## 6.3 | Practical implications

Our findings also offer managerial insights to practitioners. First, managers are advised to adopt a holistic view that incorporates the factors of technology, individual, and task, so as to nurture a learning culture among employees Specifically, managers can provide effective support resources, for example, training workshops and help centers to facilitate employees' understanding and appreciation of the digitization functionalities of the implemented tech nologies. Managers can adopt practices that facilitate knowledge exchange and sharing, for example, both onlin and offline team building and informal social activities. Managers also need to be aware of the influences of task characteristics on employee learning in using digital technologies. Particularly, task varieties need to be guaranteed when organisations undergo restructuration in digital transformation and redesign jobs for individual employees Additionally, managers should consider gender-based social roles when executing the management practices that correspond to the two learning paths. Specifically, digital affordance is important for both women and men in digi tal technology use, but affects their DC through different learning paths—women prefer to refine and exploit digital technologies, while men are willing to explore and innovate with the technologies to achieve DC. Digita knowledge significantly affects women's DC through exploitation learning, since women need social affiliation and are altruistic and willing to help others. Task variety significantly influences men's DC through exploration learning because men emphasise career development opportunities that are oriented towards performance outcomes and achievements.

Second, managers should also recognise the equality between female and male employees in achieving DC. Women have been positioned in a relatively disadvantaged status in using technologies (eg, Maruping & Magni, 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2014) and undertaking technology-related professions (Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018 Craig, 2016). Our findings offer managers a new perspective in which women and men are equal in utilising digita technologies to attain creative job performance. As digital technology usage permeates organisational management and a new generation of digital natives enter the labor market, managers are advised to be cautious when enforcin unequal gender stereotypes in technology-related contexts and to especially avoid discouraging the creativity of women that can be potentially enabled through digital technology use. Furthermore, managers need to leverage th learning capabilities and pathways that are uniquely associated with women and men when cultivating their DC. Ou findings suggest that women and men realise DC through different learning paths that result from gender-based social roles. While women excel at utilising and refining existing resources in digital technology use, men are comparatively more risk-taking and willing to engage in experimentation and innovation in technology use. Therefore, man agers need to be equally patient when women gradually make progress in refining digital technology use and men experience trial-and-error in their experimentation with technologies. Overall, since diversity in gender is inevitable in organisational human resource management, managers need to maintain a balance in adopting the recommended practices so as to ensure that both women and men can effectively utilise digital technologies to realise innovatio and improve their job performance.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

Our study examines the downstream impact of technology use in the digital technology use context. We identif three salient factors that contribute to frontline employees' ambidextrous usage behaviours of exploitation and exploration, as well as DC. Specifically, TDA affects DC through exploitative use for women, while impacting DC through explorative use for men. Digital knowledge influences DC through exploitative use for women, whereas tas variety impacts DC through explorative use for men. Our research provides fresh insights into the effective use of digital technologies, which opens a promising avenue for future research.

We adopted the following practices to evaluate possible common method bias resulted from the single-wave single source survey instrument (Podsakoff et al., 2003). First, we employed the Harman's single-factor test to perform preliminary diagnosis on the common method bias with our data (Podsakoff et al., 1986). Second, we conducted common-method-variance-factor test in AMOS 23.0 (Podsakoff et al., 2003).
