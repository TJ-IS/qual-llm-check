## Abstract

The opinions and behaviours of others are recognised as powerful mechanisms for social influence in the digital sphere. The former, often referred to as electronic word of mouth (eWOM), is a thoroughly researched topic in the Information Systems literature. Conversely, the digital display of users' behaviours (e.g., number of past purchases) is less well understood despite the widespread adoption of this practice on digital platforms. Quantitative research has explored this interesting domain and found that observing others' behaviours entice observers to follow suit, but has left unaddressed the question of what sensemaking users derive from behavioural information. This is problematic as behavioural information is more open to interpretation compared to eWOM. In this article, we adopt the concept of electronic word of behaviour (eWOB) to denote such behavioural information. Through the lens of basic psychological needs theory and the qualitative means-end chain approach, we expose how eWOB is interpreted and used by users of a digital platform, the music service Spotify. We find that eWOB leads to satisfaction of the basic psychological needs for relatedness and competence when observing others' behaviours. We also show how exposure to one's own past behaviours can yield a positive sense of self when presented in meaningful and private manners, but that it can also negatively impact users when their needs for autonomy and competence are not heeded by the digital platform. Finally, based on our empirical findings we offer a set of design implications for how digital platforms can optimise the use of eWOB.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Further developments in communication technology will enable people to observe on request almost any desired activity at any time on computer-linked television consoles. (Bandura, 1986, p. 70)

It is truly remarkable how psychologist Albert Bandura's prediction about how behaviours would become mor readily observable foresees the opportunities of the digital landscape of the 2020s. Nowadays, users of digital ser vices and platforms are presented with (often real-time) information about other users' behaviours, like informatio about what one's friends are listening to on Spotify; how many users have purchased a specific product on Groupon; what physical activities (running, swimming, triathlon, etc.) friends are engaged in on Garmin, and how many user are looking at a particular accommodation on Airbnb. All of the above examples (i.e., music listening, product pur chases, exercise activities and accommodation scouting) represent behaviours that in Bandura's time of writing wer only observable to those in one's immediate vicinity, but which are now widely available due to digitalization wher most user actions leave digital traces of behaviours.

Such behavioural traces are recognised as important implicit inputs in recommender systems (Oard & Kim, 1998). However, in the examples of Spotify, GroupOn and Airbnb the traces of user behaviour are not only used as invisible inputs for recommendation engines but they are also actively presented to users in the user inter face, alongside electronic word of mouth (eWOM) in the shape of reviews and ratings. As such, the digital traces o user behaviour are turned into a form of observable digital communication, recently referred to as electronic word o behaviour (eWOB) by Kunst and Vatrapu (2019), and defined as published accounts of behavior, based on th unobservable digital traces of consumers' behaviors' (p. 324).

Despite a recent widespread adoption of various types of eWOB among digital platforms (Tucker & Zhang, 2011),<sup>1</sup> eWOB remains significantly underexplored compared to its conceptual sibling, eWOM. While eWOM research provides us with rich and nuanced insights into its many facets (see King et al., 2014, for a thorough review research on eWOB remains surprisingly narrow in its focus with little attention to how the individual user interpret and acts on eWOB.

At times referred to as ‘popularity information’ (Thies et al., 2016; Tucker & Zhang, 2011), ‘action-based information (Cheung et al., 2014), and observational learning information (Chen et al., 2011), researchers acros Information Systems (IS) and Marketing disciplines have hitherto mainly approached the topic of eWOB through large-scale experiments drawing on observational learning theory. Observational learning theory describes how humans learn from their observation of other peoples' behaviours (Bandura, 1986). Applied to a digital platform set ting, the observational learning perspective predicts that users often follow the behaviours of other users as it reduces the cognitive complexity of having to choose between thousands of product/content options (Cheung et al., 2014; Duan et al., 2009), and that information about other users' behaviours presumably helps users to assess product and service quality (Thies et al., 2016; Tucker & Zhang, 2011). While observational learning no doubt pro vides a helpful framework for understanding how users tend to follow the observable behaviours of others on digita platforms, our current understanding of eWOB is limited to observing behavioural reactions

How eWOB is being interpreted, and how it can potentially spur emotional and cognitive reactions beyond the adop tion of the observable behaviour, is entirely overlooked when viewed through the lens of observational learning. This gap is particularly relevant for the following two reasons: First, as alluded to by Aral and Walker (2011) and Chen et al. (2011 behavioural information is less rich compared to eWOM where, for example, sentiment is explicitly expressed in rating and/or words in the form of reviews. On the contrary, eWOB is more neutral as it merely represents published account of behaviour. This makes eWOB extremely open to, and dependent on, interpretations by users. However, to the best of our knowledge, no such empirical investigation of how users interpret eWOB exists. Second, the rich literature on the mul tifaceted sensemaking and roles of consumption, that is, consumers' product-related behaviours (e.g., Belk, 1988 Fournier, 1991; Lehdonvirta, 2009; McCracken, 1990) provides well-grounded reasons to believe that observable behav iours convey additional meanings, and afford additional uses beyond aiding user decision-making. Relatedly, the eWOM lit erature demonstrates that more subtle motivations and use cases for eWOM exist beyond guiding user decision-making (e.g., Hennig-Thurau & Walsh, 2003; King et al., 2014). Accordingly, we argue that the area of eWOB warrants further inquiry into its potential extended use cases seen from the perspective of the user.

Consequently, just like recommender systems cannot rely solely on implicit inputs but often need additional data to support the inferences that lead to useful recommendations (Koren, 2008), in this article we investigate the research ques tion of how users interpret and act upon eWOB. The need for applying a user perspective is echoed by Freelon (2014) who in a review of the interpretation of digital trace data in social computing research, called for more qualitative research into user-interpretations of others' digital traces. Similarly, Mäntymäki et al. (2020) recently warned against the mechanisti implementation of social features’ in digital services, and called for more attention to the user context and potential valu for the individual user. Finally, Kunst and Vatrapu (2019), in a review of eWOB as a concept, called for more research into potential extended uses of eWOB beyond its decision-guiding function

We situate our study in the empirical context of the popular music streaming platform Spotify. To extend th current literature and guide our efforts to understand how users interpret and appropriate eWOB, we employ in depth interviews with Spotify users and the means-end chain (MEC) method (Gutman, 1982; Walker & Olson, 1991)

This article makes the following contributions. First, it deepens the existing knowledge about eWOB through the provi sion of empirical insights into extended user interpretations and uses of eWOB. Through a qualitative approach, we empiri cally confirm what has hitherto only been assumed namely that eWOB is being interpreted by users of a digital content based platform as an indicator of popularity with an intended guiding function. However, we also demonstrate that there is more to eWOB than merely indicating popularity as it can provide users with a sense of (imagined) community, a sense of feeling special, and of social recognition, even from simply observing other (often unknown) users' behaviours. Moreover, eWOB acts as a social facilitator of interactions and provides users with a sense of social validation of their choices. Seen through the lens of the basic psychological needs (BPN) theory, we then tie these extended meanings and uses of eWOB to the positive stimulation of the basic psychological needs for competence and relatedness

Second, this article broadens the existing knowledge of eWOB by assessing types of eWOB that have hitherto been largely left unexplored. Whereas the majority of extant literature about eWOB focuses on observing the behaviours of others, we also find that being exposed to one's own behaviour, in this case, one's past music listening behaviours, carries significant meaning and opportunities for action to users related to memories and, ultimately, an enhanced sense of self. The latter is especially important given the increasingly digital lives we live, where many identity-supporting artefacts, such as music, books, and movies are consumed digitally, and thus no longer are on dis play as subtle, daily reminders of one's identity, and sense of self. Finally, we also identify several negative aspects of this particular type of eWOB, namely that it can leave users to feel exposed and confused which we tie to a negativ impact on users' basic psychological needs for autonomy and competence. Based on our findings we draw implica tions for future research as well as recommendations for how content-based digital platforms can present eWOB to meet users' individual and social needs and concerns.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows. In Section 2, we provide a brief review and critique of th literature about eWOB and introduce the theoretical lens of basic psychological needs that guides the analysis. In Section 3, we introduce the methodological approach and the dataset. In Section 4, we present the findings, whic we then discuss in Section 5 along with an outline of our contributions to research and practice. Finally, in Section 6 we elaborate on this study's limitations as well as avenues for further research.

## 2 | RELATED WORK AND THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS

## 2.1 | Electronic word of behaviour

The rise of the internet and the associated wide scope of products and services that are available for consumers to choose from have spurred researchers' and practitioners' interest in how to assist consumers in this overwhelming landscape of decision-making (Duan et al., 2009). Two common decision-guiding mechanisms are: (1) the disclosure of others' opinions (i.e., what users state they like) and (2) others' past behaviours (i.e., what choices they have made). Such information about other users' opinions and behaviours has been referred to in the IS and Marketing literature as (online) social interaction information (Godes et al., 2005; Thies et al., 2016). The opinion-based type is commonly referred to as eWOM (Cheung et al., 2014; Thies et al., 2016), whereas behaviour-based information was recently conceptualised as eWOB by Kunst and Vatrapu (2019) to better understand the phenomenon of digital platforms' use of observable behaviours in digital inter faces, and to overcome the diverse terminology in existing research on this phenomenon.

Unlike eWOM, which has long been a topic of scholarly investigation across the IS (e.g., Jiang & Guo, 2015) and Marketing literature (e.g., Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004; King et al., 2014), the use of eWOB in digital platforms is still rela tively unexplored (Cheung et al., 2014; Thies et al., 2016). The research on eWOB that does exist, has primarily taken quantitative approach, and has successfully, through large-scale experiments empirically demonstrated that users across different types of digital platforms are influenced by the display of past behaviours of other users. Specifically, eWOB seems effective in influencing decision-making in settings where users are faced with many options (Duan et al., 2009) and in situations where the quality of product/service is difficult to assess before purchase (Thies et al., 2016). This has been demonstrated across different empirical contexts, including crowdfunding (Thies et al., 2016), sales of digital cameras on Amazon (Chen et al., 2011), software downloads on CNET (Duan et al., 2009), wedding services (Tucker & Zhang, 2011), and music downloads (Salganik & Watts, 2008). Moreover, research shows that the display of past user behaviour within digital platforms can effectively nudge users to adopt desired behaviours. For example, in a large-scal experiment on the music streaming platform ‘Last.fm’, Bapna and Umyarov (2015) demonstrated that the platform could increase uptake of its paid plan by simply displaying premium users' status as paying users. Additionally, eWOB has also proven effective as an integral product design element to facilitate a viral spread of the product to new users, referred to as viral product design (Aral & Walker, 2011; Bapna & Umyarov, 2015; Dou et al., 2013). A prominent example of this is Aral and Walker's (2011) study in which they test a feature that automatically shares user actions within an app to the user's Facebook wall, and hence also to the user's Facebook friends (akin to the Nike Run Club ap that can auto-post users' running achievements to Facebook). They found a significant impact of this behaviour-based information on the adoption of the app among a user's Facebook friends; that is, users who saw friends' app activity on Facebook were more likely to also start using that app.

However, the dominant lens of observational learning still leads to only limited insights into the effects beyon the adoption of the observable behaviour. This is in stark contrast to the scholarly efforts put into understanding the many nuances of eWOM from both a sender/receiver and quantitative/qualitative perspective (see King et al., 2014 for a thorough review of the eWOM literature). A similarly deep and nuanced understanding of eWOB has yet to b developed, and it is essential if digital platforms are to effectively make use of eWOB (Cheung et al., 2014). The need for a more nuanced understanding of eWOB is further underlined as eWOB contains much fewer detail (e.g., sentiment) than eWOM (Chen et al., 2011) and therefore is more open to interpretation. In other words, users exposed to eWOB are left with the task of interpreting this potentially ambiguous information, such as when a friend has listened to a specific song on Spotify, or that 5000 people have watched a video on YouTube

Despite a recent call for more qualitative research into the user interpretation of consumers' digital trace (Freelon, 2014), only a few studies focus on the user perspective. An example of such as study is Luarn et al.'s (2015) work which investigates user-motivations to ‘check in’ on locations through location-based services, such as Facebook and FourSquare. This quantitative study found that the potential response from one's social connections is an important driver for one's own check-in behaviour. This suggests the potential for extended meanings of eWOB beyond that of signalling product popularity and quality which is the motivation for our qualitative inquiry into th user perspectives on eWOB.

## 2.2 | Basic psychological needs theory

To investigate users' thoughts and feelings as well as subsequent behaviours, when users observe eWOB, we rely on the theoretical framework of BPN (Ryan & Deci, 2017) – a sub-theory of Ryan and Deci's (2017) broader theory of self-determination. BPN theory posits that humans have fundamental psychological needs for autonomy, compe tence and relatedness. Each of these needs must be nurtured and satisfied for individuals to thrive

Autonomy refers to circumstances where one's behaviours are self-endorsed, or congruent with one's authentic interests and values (Ryan & Deci, 2017, p. 10). It has to do with freedom of decision and the personal meaningful ness of the task at hand (Sailer et al., 2017). Competence refers to a basic need for being able to master something be it sports, cooking, and math, and generally operates efficiently within important life contexts (Ryan & Deci, 2017) Finally, the need for relatedness describes human beings' need to feeling cared for and belonging to social groups. An important component of relatedness is to give and contribute to others, be it close ties or larger social group (Cunningham, 1986; Korchmaros & Kenny, 2001).

BPN theory is well-suited to complement the observational learning theory, which so far has been the dominant theoretical perspective in prior empirical investigations of eWOB. However, observational learning theory unintentionally restricts insights as it only explores observable behavioural outcomes. That is, it overlooks users' interpretations of eWOB, which might explain to what use they incorporate (or reject) eWOB. Moreover, BPN would be well-suited for topics related to digital platforms and user interfaces, as it emphasises the influence the surroundin environment has on satisfying basic psychological needs (Garn et al., 2010; Ryan & Deci, 2017; Sheldon et al., 2011) Research in the area of online games (Ryan et al., 2006), gamification design elements (Sailer et al., 2017), fitnes technology, and exercise goals (James et al., 2019), and discussion forums (Li et al., 2012) has demonstrated how the inclusion of specific design elements (such as badges, leaderboards, feedback mechanisms, etc.) in user interfaces can contribute to the satisfaction of basic psychological needs.

Additionally, the satisfaction of basic psychological needs has been shown to positively affect the user experi ence and intended ongoing use. In their study of online gaming, Ryan et al. (2006) found that the satisfaction of the need for autonomy, competence, and relatedness led to an increased game enjoyment and intention to keep playing in the future. This finding emphasises the managerial importance of understanding how digital interfaces stimulate basic psychological needs. In summary, we find that BPN theory serves as a useful lens through which we identif and analyse core constructs related to information about user behaviours.

## 3 | RESEARCH DESIGN AND METHODOLOGICAL APPROACH

In this section, we first briefly describe the empirical context of Spotify followed by an introduction to the meansend chain approach, the dataset, and the analytical process.

## 3.1 | Empirical context: Spotify

Spotify is an international music streaming platform with over 356 million users across 178 markets.<sup>2</sup> Spotify was chosen as the focal platform for our interviews because of Spotify's extensive and varied integration of behavioural information in its user interface. We find it useful to classify Spotify's various types of eWOB into four types, based on two dimensions: (1) whether the eWOB observed is based on other users' behaviours or one's own behaviours and (2) whether the behavioural information has been processed (e.g., aggregated) or it is presented in a raw format.

As can be seen from Figure 1, four overall types of eWOB are present. Starting from the right-hand side popular ity information resembles the type of eWOB mostly researched in the existing literature. Here, the behavioural infor mation is based on others' behaviours, and has been processed into some meaningful format, for example, by being aggregated across many users, across geographies, and so on. The user activity type also presents behavioural information that stems from other users, however in a rather raw format. An example is when Spotify displays what spe cific song a user's friend is listening to right now. On the left-hand side, we find eWOB that is based on the observing user's own behaviours. Here, the self-activity type appears mainly when the user goes to his/her profil page and sees an overview of his/her recent listening activity, however in a raw format. In contrast, in the last type of eWOB, that is, the self-tracking, the behavioural information has been processed in various ways, presumably to appear more meaningful to the user. An example is Spotify's annual wrap-up of individual users' listening behaviours the past year, including a top list of songs/artists for each user for that particular year. Despite displaying information about the behaviours of specific others, the music platform does not allow any user interaction on the platform (e.g., ‘liking’ a friend's specific listening activity). As such, users will have to interpret the intentions of others' behav iours without the ability to verify such information, at least not on the platform, which also makes it a relevant envi ronment to study the use of eWOB.

## 3.2 | The MEC approach

To uncover deeper-lying interpretations and possibilities for action it is pivotal to go beyond the surface sense-making that users reveal which often only represents more rational and conscious reasoning. The MEC approach involves two parts; an exploration of expressions that uncover deeper meaning constructs, and a laddering process that links such constructs to higher (or lower) rungs on the conceptual ladder (Jung & Kang, 2010; Reynolds & Gutman, 1988). The exploration pro cess provides deep insights into informants' interpretation (sensemaking) of a given topic (Ringberg & Gupta, 2003). In ou article, the focus is on the users' interpretations and actual use of eWOB in the Spotify interface. The MEC proces involves asking informants about their interpretations of situations across time and circumstances, and as such provides, at least implicitly, a longitudinal account. In this way, the MEC approach surpasses more traditional, observation-based quali tative approaches such as participant observation and ethnography as it explores users' sensemaking processes across numerous events rather than their actual behaviour when online. Moreover, as users' sensemaking of others' (and own behaviours on Spotify happens during all hours of the day and night as well as across many usage situations (e.g., work and home), it would make it challenging to capture these moments in real-time as they occur. The MEC approach can account for these moments by stimulating informants' memories and associated sensemaking related to activities across time. More specifically, the MEC approach applies open-ended (unbiasing) questions to explore deeper layers of infor mants' experiences, such as ‘what do you mean by … [topic just stated by informant]?’ and ‘tell me about a specific epi sode where this instance took place .

The second part of the MEC involves pushing informants' responses either up the ladder by asking whether a core concept (which they have stated) is important, and if so, then why? (Jung & Kang, 2010; Kjaergaard & Jensen, 2014) o down the ladder by asking about ‘what circumstances led to [a topic mentioned by informant, already]?’ to uncover the antecedents of constructs mentioned by informants. Finally, if negative issues are introduced, informants are probed with questions, such as ‘why is that something you want to avoid?’ The process of ‘pushing’ up or down the means-end lad der helps making evident how products/services attributes connect with psychosocial consequences and end-values in their lives (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988; Walker & Olson, 1991). The ‘means’ are the products/services attributes (e.g., the bubbling sensation of a Coke, the welcome greeting of a service employee) that lead to the ‘valued states of being (Gutman, 1982, p. 60), that is, the meaning and values these attributes bring to informants' lives (e.g., social belonging happiness, and love). From the most concrete to the most abstract level, a ladder or chain consists of four main elements: attribute, functional consequence, psychosocial consequence and end-value (Reynolds & Whitlark, 1995; Walker & Olson, 1991) as illustrated in Figure 2. The laddering approach of MEC has been used in the IS field to shed light o topics such as adoption of knowledge management systems (Braganza et al., 2009), (repeat) purchase intentions in e-commerce (Chiu et al., 2014), and users' hierarchical value structures associated with smartphone usage (Jung, 2014).

An initial, very brief interview guide (with open-ended questions) was used to guide the unbiased questioning and explore the different perspectives of eWOB information applied by Spotify users. This was tested in two pilot interviews which led to a few adjustments in the final interview guide.

## 3.3 | Informants

We settled on a combination of convenience and purposive sampling approaches (Ritchie et al., 2003). Mor generally, in the field of business and management, both convenience and purposive samplings are very common methods (Bryman, 1989; Ferber, 1977). Purposeful sampling enables researchers to identify and explore con structs that are relevant to the studied topic. It is important that the sample size is adequate for analytical purposes and representative of the characteristic of the population studied. The sample size in in-depth interview typically consists of between 10 and 15 informants extracted from the core user group, and validation studies in qualitative research found that as little as 10–12 in depth-interviews can provide 90% of the key constructs and links between these compared with much larger sets of interviews (Zaltman & Coulter, 1995; Zeitham et al., 2020). In our case, we included 21 informants (see overview in Table 1). Their age distribution largely matches that of Spotify's (and competitors' such as Apple Music and Pandora<sup>3</sup>) dominant age segment of (25– 34 years) with 15 out of 21 informants being in this range. Moreover, we aimed at obtaining a gender distribu tion (52% male/48% female) that reflected Spotify's subscriber base (i.e., 56% male and 44% female<sup>4</sup>) and which also is relatively close to a main competing service (i.e., Apple Music with 44% male/56% female). Finally, the informants were all active users of the paid version of Spotify with extensive experience with its services (average 5 years of use).

Moreover, the identification of informants was done such that only those informants participated wit whom the interviewer was not acquainted. The shared workplace affiliation was not a concern for informants. It enabled that rapport (i.e., trust and openness) was easily established. Trust and openness are especially important factors when conducting in-depth interviews (Qu & Dumay, 2011). In addition, the interviewer explicitly made it clear, at the beginning of each interview, that we were solely interested in the informant's use of Spotify as a pri vate person and not as an employee of the telecom company, and that all information was confidential. The recruitment of informants was done by publishing a post on the company's intranet site, which sought subject ‘for interviews about music streaming’. Informants were rewarded with vouchers for a movie streaming service and debriefed after the study.

All interviews were conducted at the workplace of the informant in a meeting room. Reflective notes were taken following each interview and the interviews were also audio-recorded and later transcribed ver batim for analysis. The informants were instructed to bring along their device(s) used for Spotify, and the point of departure was the informants' personal Spotify interface and their records of actual listening history on the Spotify platform. This approach was chosen to elicit concrete narratives about the actual use of eWOB, and to discuss specific elements of interest. In line with Reynolds and Gutman's (1988) methodologi cal advice, informants were told by the interviewer, at the start of each interview, that some clarifying questions might seem obvious and repetitive but that this was to ensure a full understanding of the informants sensemaking.

## 3.4 | Data analysis

The first step of the analysis was to read through the post-interview memos as well as all the interviews to gain a richer understanding of the contents of the data. In the next step, the data was imported into the thematic analysis program MAXQDA. The interpretive analysis followed a sequential process in which each unique meaning construct within each interview was labelled with a code. Codes that resemble each other semantically were then merged into constructs (i.e., meta-codes). We then linked these constructs following each narrative. During the analysis, w tacked back and forth between identified conceptual strings and their contextual meaning (also see Thompson, 1997). This ensures that layers of meaning constructs were made apparent. The identified themes were modified as the analysis progressed and as new constructs emerged. Eventually, shared means-end chains were iden tified and illustrated visually (see Figure 3). Finally, the themes were interpreted in the light of the three basic psy chological needs as outlined in Ryan and Deci's (2017) basic psychological needs theory.

## 4 | FINDINGS

We now present our findings from the MEC analysis. In our means-end chain, the attributes represent the different types of eWOB present in the Spotify interface. The functional consequences are the immediate, sometimes tangibl outcomes of particular attributes. Psychosocial consequences reveal more about abstract meanings, experiences, an emotions that informants connect to specific eWOB attributes. Finally, end values rest on the fulfilment of deeper psychosocial needs and desired end-states that can be said to be universally attractive to humans. We generate thi last level by interpreting informants' statements through the lens of BPN theory. As such, in our framework, the end values represent the satisfaction (marked with ‘+’) or deprivation (marked with ‘ ’) of basic psychological needs tha derive from the users' exposure to eWOB in Spotify.

As can be seen from Figure 3, our analysis results in 13 chains (of varying length) across the four types of eWOB outlined in Section 3; (1) Popularity information (based on others' behaviours presented in a processed/aggregated format); (2) User activity (based on others' behaviours, presented in a raw format); (3) Self-tracking (based on own behaviours, presented in a processed/aggregated format) and (4) Self-activity (based on own behaviours, presented in raw format). The four eWOB types constitute the attributes in the MEC approach. For each attribute, we observe a number of chains, which are anchored around how users interpret and act upon (behaviourally as well as emotion ally) the observation of eWOB. We will now unfold these findings.

## 4.1 | eWOB type: ‘Popularity information

The type of eWOB that best resembles ‘popularity information’ (e.g., Thies et al., 2016; Tucker & Zhang, 2011 Zhang et al., 2015) is present in Spotify when behavioural listening information is aggregated across many users Examples of this type in Spotify are the total number of listens to a song or the number of monthly listeners of a spe cific artist, sometimes broken down by city or country. At the functional level, this type of eWOB is unanimously interpreted and used by Spotify users as a tool for guiding their choice of music. This is in line with the extant litera ture, and the dominant theoretical concept of observational learning, where information about other users' behav iours apparently guide the observing party through an ongoing stream of consumer choices (Chen et al., 2011 Cheung et al., 2014; Duan et al., 2009). As informant KJ explains:

You can see, among other things, what's popular with other people right now. So you could say there's a pretty good chance of finding some good songs. (KJ, m, 29)

A similar view is expressed by informant RJ, who in line with Thies et al. (2016) views a high number of listen for a particular track as ‘a quality indicator’:

I don't have some need to have the music for myself. (…) To me, it [the number of listens for a song] is an indicator of quality. (RJ, m, 29)

Similarly, a female user tells how she uses the number of listens to steer her away from niche music. In the follow ing excerpt, she is looking at a song that has been played 10 000 times, which she regards as a low number of listens

I think that it must be something pretty niche (…) I don't really analyze it too much because it's so special for me. (IR, f, 27)

As such, it is clear that users tend to use the eWOB type ‘popularity information’ to guide them towards alread popular songs, as also demonstrated in a similar empirical context by Salganik and Watts (2008). However, this herding-type of behaviour is not found across all informants. On the contrary, we find that some users use this type of eWOB to guide them away from popular artists/songs:

I don't know why, but I have a tendency to be like ‘well it is not so cool if everyone else thinks it is’. (MJ, m, 27)

Similarly, another user, AKP (f, 56), tells how she takes pride in finding songs with few listen, and plays them to her friends. As such, popularity information also seems to also have an inverted effect.

Moving to the psychosocial level in Figure 3, we start to see uses and consequences of the popularit information-type of eWOB, which go beyond just guiding users to either popular or niche content. Specifically, we see how users, (1) gain a sense of community leading to the end value of relatedness, (2) gain social recognition leading to the end value state of competence and finally (3) gain a sense of feeling special leading to the end value of competence.

We see that gaining a sense of community can happen merely from observing the popularity information-typ of eWOB without any interaction with other users. This is illustrated by an informant who talks about the number of monthly listens of one of her old-time favourite bands:

It tells me that there is a good size fan base that also listens to the music, just like me. I mean, I think that they're some extremely nice people that like the same music as me. I think it just gives me a bit of peace of mind. Because I'm a huge fan of [name of band], and I think it's really nice to know that other people also really like to hear them. (LTF, f, 24)

The informant's description of the band's listeners (to her unknown) as ‘extremely nice people’ suggests that the eWOB here creates a sense of community in the broader sense of an imagined community (Anderson, 2006), and in a narrower sense a community of interest (Fischer, 2001). Seen through the lens of BPN, this can be argued to sup port the user's basic need for relatedness.

Similarly, another informant who looks at the number of listeners of her favourite teenage band reflects

That's good. Then I'm not the only one at 28 years who still thinks it's cool. (AA, f, 28)

Next, we find that the described inverted effect of popularity information-type of eWOB can lead to felt socia recognition, which can lead to the end value of positive stimulation of the basic need for competence. As one informant tells about her experiences of finding and sharing with friends music with a low number of listens:

It's a little bit like getting a pat on the back when they [her friends] say that what you've found is pretty good. (AKP, f, 56)

Finally, the inverted use of popularity information-type of eWOB can lead to a feeling of being special and music competent, which can be positively tied to the basic need for competence. In the following, a user enjoys how th popularity information-type of eWOB enables him to benchmark himself against and steer away from the main stream music taste:

I think it's pretty fun like that, if there's something that's really popular in Denmark for example and you're maybe, what should I say, a bit more out of the mainstream. (MJ, m, 27)

Interestingly, none of the informants expressed distrust in the aggregated number of listens related to an artist or song. This is noteworthy given the unfortunate tendency of ‘social media fraud’ (Paquet-Clouston et al., 2017)

Actors that engage in social media fraud buy likes, followers, and views (the latter representing eWOB) on social media services like Instagram and YouTube but also Spotify plays can be purchased.<sup>5</sup> This activity, while being appall ing, also speaks to the importance of eWOB

Taken together, our findings regarding the type of eWOB that is most prevalent in the existing literature, that is popularity information, provide qualitative insights that confirm popularity information to be widely perceived b users as a mechanism to guide their choices. However, we demonstrate that users do not always equal high popular ity with good quality, and, as such, popularity information can be used to steer away from popular items.

## 4.2 | eWOB type: ‘User activity’

This type of eWOB resembles the type that has been investigated quantitatively among others Aral and Walker (2011 and Cheung et al. (2014). With this type of eWOB users are exposed to the behaviours of other specific users, either fri ends or simply other individual users. In the present case of Spotify, users can follow other users and/or connect thei Spotify account with Facebook and follow those Facebook friends, who also use Spotify. By doing so, users can see in real, or near real-time, what their friends are listening to right now or have recently listened to

As can be seen from Figure 3, the user-activity-type of eWOB is, at the functional level, largely interpreted as some thing meant to work as inspiration for finding new/relevant music. This is illustrated by the following two quotes:

Spotify has this social feed so you can see what your friends are listening to. Sometimes it's like ‘Ah right, that song – I haven't heard that in a super long time!’ Other times it's completely new music. (JC, m, 38)

Yeah, I can check and see what playlists he has, and even what he listened to last (…). And then I can listen to it to find out if it's something I'm also into. (MGJ, m, 26)

As Figure 3 shows the user activity-type of eWOB entails psychosocial consequences that go beyond its func tion as an inspirational tool. The most prevalent role it serves is as a social facilitator. Specifically, several users stat how observing friends' music listening activity makes it easier to keep up with friends' lives and/or start conversa tions in real life. This is illustrated in the following quote:

It could be one of my friends, who I would never imagine is into hip hop, because I consider her the ‘pop type’. And then [I see] she listens to Kanye West a lot. So maybe the next time we catch up we have something to talk about if we talk about music. And in this way you get to know your friends in a different way than if you just sit and talk together. (MGJ, m, 26)

This active engagement in conversations with other users is in line with the perspectives of BPN theory, where people are thought to be actively seeking out and cultivating relationships (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Interestingly, w also find that the user activity-type of eWOB can help maintain or expand physical relations by mere observation. On user explains, when talking about the social feed that showcases what his friends in Spotify are listening to right now:

It's mostly just for the fun of it, and to see what friends listen to. (…) well, it might just be to keep up with what they are up to and what they are listening to. (CJE, m, 26)

Here, an individual's music listening activity is used as a measure of what friends are ‘up to’ and a way to keep up with the whereabouts of peers, akin to the typical use of social media (Sheldon et al., 2011). Moreover, a user explains how he can learn new sides of his friend from merely observing his friend's music listening activity:

It's pretty fun to discover that your friend also like some of the music you're into, and to build a connection that you didn't know about. Then you somehow become a little closer to each other in that way. (MGJ, m, 26)

Several informants tell tales of social interactions that take the form of ‘friendly mocking’, and directly stem from the user activity-type of eWOB. Here, this type of eWOB again acts as a social facilitator of conversation beyond the Spotify digital platform. As one user recalls:

For example, this guy I know, Alex. I sometimes write him a message when I see he listens to something that isn't his typical style. Because then we can joke around and have some fun with that. One time he listened to a band (…), it was like this, sort of, high-pitched voice, and I couldn't understand why he listened to them. It didn't make sense. I tried to listen to it for myself, and then I wrote to ask him what the heck it was. And then, yes, I found out that he had listened to it while having dinner with his girlfriend, which meant that it was a little ‘out there’. So you can use it like that once in a while. (GS, m, 29)

Together, the psychosocial consequence of social facilitator helps users maintain and expand their social rela tions which in the end can be positively tied to the end value level and the basic need for relatedness. Secondly, we observe how the user activity-type of eWOB can provide users with a sense of social recognition. As one user explains, when talking about Spotify's ‘social feed’ displaying the current or recent listening behaviours of her Spotify connections:

I also have a good ‘warm’ feeling when I see my siblings, family, or close friends listen to music that have sent them or something we have in common (…). It's just a kind of recognition that okay, I am not completely lost when it comes to music. I like music and it looks like you also like what I listen to. So that does make me happy. (IR, f, 27)

Here, the user is receiving social recognition from friends or family, albeit indirectly. She infers, from simpl observing their listening behaviours, that she has had some sort of impact on them, in line with the need of having an impact on one's environment (White, 1959). When probed further about what she means by warm , the use seems to derive some fundamental value of felt competency from such experiences:

I do get happy (…) because it might be that I don't play an instrument, and that I don't have a singer's voice, which I really wish I had, but then it's really nice to see that some of the music I like, that a person who is kind of, like, musically gifted, actually appreciates it. (IR, f, 27)

Finally, as seen in Figure 3, the user activity-type of eWOB can lead to the psychosocial consequence of social validation of users' choices. Whereas all of the above examples operate at the content (i.e., song) or artist level, one user describes how seeing friends' activity in Spotify's ‘social feed’ validates his overall choice of Spotify as a music service:

I get a sense that there are a lot of people that use this service, you know? So it's not just me who is weird and actually paying for it cause, you know, there just isn't a great alternative which is better, or which others are using, meaning I am the only one left on Spotify. If I reflect on this a little, then I'd say it gives me a sense of not being alone on this service – that I have somebody in my network who is also using it. So, in that way my use of Spotify is being validated. (CES, m, 43)

This strive for social validation through the user activity-type of eWOB can be related to the human propensity to strive for cognitive consonance (Festinger, 1957) through seeking information that confirms one’s existing beliefs The mere presence of others makes him feel that ‘there just isn't a great alternative’ from which we can infer that this informant does not feel the need to go out and perform cumbersome research on alternative music streaming platforms. Seen from the perspective of BPN theory, we can say that the informant CES has completed the task of choosing a music streaming service in an effective manner, which satisfies his basic need for competence. Conse quently, this finding suggests that platforms can use the inclusion of the user activity-type of eWOB as a way to socially validate users' choice of platform, and thus make users keep using it.

## 4.3 | eWOB type: ‘Self-activity’

We have hitherto analysed how the individual user interprets and reacts to information about other Spotify users' behaviours within the Spotify interface. We will now elaborate on the findings related to users' experi ences of observing their own past behaviours in Spotify, starting with the type labelled self-activity . This typ manifests itself in Spotify as a user's profile page where the user's recent listening activities are listed (e.g., recently listened to artists, songs, playlists, etc.). Moreover, a user's behaviours might also be shared to the aforementioned ‘social feed’, although most of the informants had disabled this function, for reasons we elaborate on below.

At the functional level, the self-activity type of eWOB was first and foremost interpreted and used as a shortcut to their favourite music at the moment, that is, as a form of guidance, as seen from Figure 3 and illustrated in the fol lowing quotes from two female users:

It's usually the easy access point to the music I know I like. (AA, f, 28)

It made it easy to just go in here and put it on fast. (COHP, f, 27)

This functional consequence of guidance did not seem to lead to further psychosocial consequences nor to end values of BPN stimulation, though. A second functional consequence was hinted at by some users, namely that of being an image-building tool, albeit in a negative way. Here, one user tells how her ‘recently listened to’ overview, on her user page, is flooded with songs that have been played during her recent participation in a music festival; song which she did not herself put on:

This here is not representative of me. It is representative of ‘[name of informant]’s phone is placed on top of the stereo during a party at a festival’. (LTF, f, 24)

Along the same lines, an informant tells how he might modify his music listening if it were to be disclosed to a wider audience:

I would probably be a little more conscious about what I listen to if I had more followers (…) you want to show your followers that ‘the music I listen to is “the right” music to listen to’. (CM, m, 24)

For some respondents, the self-activity-type of eWOB further led to the psychosocial consequence of feelin exposed. Privacy concerns about what information is transmitted, to whom, and in which context were raised. Specif ically, some users expressed discomfort because they felt that music consumption was tightly knit with their person ality and/or mood.

Music is feelings. And then, sometimes you can listen to something on repeat because it touches you. But you would never stand there and laugh or cry on repeat in front of your entire collection of Facebook friends. (RH, f, 30)

The prospect of revealing such information to others would presumably allow observers to infer personal detail about one's personality, mood, and whereabouts. This led some informants to entirely turn off the options for behav iour disclosure (e.g., by disconnecting their Spotify and Facebook accounts or listening in ‘private mode’) despit their enjoyment of receiving eWOB about others:

I try to keep it somewhat private because music is such a big part of me that I would feel that people would come a bit too close (…). I cannot control on Spotify whether some completely random person is lurking and seeing what music I'm listening to. (LTF, f, 24)

On a related note, one informant elaborates on the feeling of talking to a big and unknown audience, if he were to make his own music consumption observable in Spotify. The consequence for this user is that he deactivated al social features in his use of Spotify:

I can't really put it into words why I would find it uncomfortable talking to this broad audience. But it is clearly this feeling that is the reason [why he has not connected his Spotify account with Facebook]. (KJ, m, 29)

Clearly, this psychosocial consequence of feeling exposed is negative. It can be negatively tied to the basic nee for autonomy, in the sense that users seem to want to control who they let into their private worlds, either to protect a self-image of having a cool music taste or not to reveal very private sides of themselves. Apparently, the setting Spotify provides (or lack thereof) are not enough for users to feel comfortably in control, so they resort to the ulti mate control to maintain a sense of autonomy, namely to turn off entirely the social sharing features. Secondly, the psychosocial consequence of confusion was expressed by some informants. In the following quote, an informant i looking at his own ‘recently played’ overview

There's no logic to this. I don't know how much you can tell about me from this alone (…) when looking at the songs, I can't even remember why I was listening to these ones. (GS, m, 29)

Likewise, in another interview the informant CEA expresses similar confusion:

It actually makes me wonder. Cause I actually don't think that this one… it's pretty weird. There again comes something where I, like, know for sure that I listened to it (…) But these, I don't know. Nor him. It might be that I have – without remembering it (…). I actually get quite confused. (CEA, f, 44)

What is especially interesting here is that informant CEA is a self-proclaimed music enthusiast with a home ful of LPs. Consequently, one would expect such an enthusiast to be capable of accounting for her recent past music lis tening behaviour. However, on the contrary, CEA seems utterly confused when faced with her ‘recently played overview in Spotify and is not able to recognise herself in her own music consumption. Although she goes on to tal about another topic, she cannot take her eyes away from the overview of her recently played songs, still trying to find meaning in it, and whether she has actually listened to all the artists displayed. Moving further upwards toward end value, this confusion can be tied to a sense of loss in competence, given the informant's strong self-perception as a music enthusiast.

## 4.4 | eWOB type: ‘Self-tracking

The final type of eWOB discussed in the interviews is the ‘self-tracking’ type. This type is somewhat similar to the popularity information type. However, whereas popularity information comes from other users, self-tracking pre sents users with their own past behaviours. In Spotify, this manifests itself mainly in the platform's annual reviews o each user's listening behaviours called ‘Spotify Wrapped’. As one user explains:

It is a fun thing to talk about and share on social media (…). That is, I received a message from Spotify about what I had listened to during the year….you have played this album with this song. Based on that Spotify makes this playlist for you. (CES, m, 43)

As such, the self-tracking-type of eWOB has the functional consequence for the user as content for sharing on social media. A related functional consequence is that of an archive-like function. Several informants shared how they had for many years used a music service called Last.fm, which has a plugin to Spotify. Through thi plugin to Spotify Last.fm generates an overview of all songs ever listened to by the user. As one informant explains:

I signed up as a user [of Last.fm] in 2004 or something like that. (…) it's really cool to have a database over all the music I have played since I was… [indicates a young version of him]. (RJ, m, 29)

And another informant further elaborates:

One can sort one's song list into top artists, top songs, and what else there might be each month, and which songs I listened to mostly last year or in the month of May. (HS, m, 26)

These two functional consequences; the content sharing and the archive functions, leads to a strong psychosocia consequence, namely to walk down memory lane among informants. As the informant HS states:

Often it is fun to go back in time. It could be see what I listened to during a trip to the U.S., I think it makes it easy to recall good memories, and in this way use the list as a time capsule (...). I think that is fun and interesting as it helps me connect with the past. (HS, m, 26)

Similarly, the informant RJ explains how he, through his music archive, can go back to previous versions of himself:

I feel like I can get to know myself better. The person I was when I was 16. To see, oh!, there I broke up with my first girlfriend’, and ‘there I've been sitting and listening to some whining music for 14 days’, and ‘there I must have been at a party’, and ‘there I've heard the same song 37 times in a row’. (RJ, m, 29)

Such psychosocial consequences can be positively linked to the higher end value of establishing a sense of self Although the sense of self is not a basic psychological need, according to the BPN theory, individuals' perception of their senses of self is tightly knit with the feeling of competence (Ryan & Deci, 2017)

Finally, as in the user-activity-type of eWOB, we also here find a functional consequence of the image-buildin tool. As one user reflects when he talks about his experience with Spotify automatically choosing to feature a specific artist as his top artist on his profile:

I guess it's also a kind of – what do you want to be associated with? (HS, m, 26)

This automatic selection and display of one's ‘top artist’, however, had a negative psychosocial consequence for the user who was left feeling exposed:

What you don't see here is what you use the music for. There was a time when I listened a lot to Taylor Swift and then suddenly somewhere it was stated that Taylor Swift is tour top artist! . I don't mind sharing my activity, but when it is put forward in this manner as ‘top artist’ then I feel like – arrr

– that's just not right. (HS, m, 26)

Similarly, to what we saw in the self-activity-type of eWOB, this negative feeling of exposure can challenge th basic psychological need for autonomy. A tenet of autonomy is that behaviours are self-endorsed and congruent wit one's authentic interests (Ryan & Deci, 2017). In this particular case we argue that whereas the informant had indee listened to the artist Taylor Swift, it takes quite some inference to conclude that a user is comfortable with Spotif automatically, and publicly, highlighting a user's most recently listened to the artist (in a given period) as the user' ‘top artist’. Especially given music's longstanding status as identity-signifier (Franken et al., 2017; Frith, 1981). Therefore, Spotify's aggregation of recent songs into a ‘top artist’ is found to subvert the user's ability to satisfy his/he need for autonomy.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that the use of and value gained from eWOB go far beyond what ha hitherto been presented in existing literature, where eWOB has mainly been portrayed as a tool that guides users Moreover, our findings shed light on both positive and negative impacts on users when faced with eWOB informa tion of their own past behaviours.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

In the following section, we first discuss the key findings of how users interpret and act upon eWOB, and relate the findings to the existing literature and theory. We then outline this study's contributions to existing literature followed by the contributions to practice, and implications for design.

## 5.1 | Discussion of key findings

## 5.1.1 | The importance of reference groups

The empirical findings show that users, to a large degree, indeed rely on the popularity information type of eWOB to follow the lead of others, in line with extant literature (e.g., Thies et al., 2016; Tucker & Zhang, 2011). However, our study also shows that some – perhaps niche-oriented – users actually perform oppositional uses of eWOB, as some actively steer away from following the lead of others. We interpret this finding as being rooted in difference in the user's reference group(s) (Shibutani, 1955). The idea that the impact of observing someone's behaviour wil vary depending on the interrelations between the person performing the behaviour (the model) and the observer of that behaviour is not new. Rather, it is a basic theoretical premise of observational learning theory (cf. Bandura, 1986 and social impact theory (Latané, 1981). Consequently, our findings challenge the dominant assumption that fre quent behaviour should only be viewed as a quality indicator. In other words, more might not always be merrie when it comes to eWOB.

## 5.1.2 | Unwanted exposure

As described in Section 4, we found that eWOB stemming from one's own behaviours could have a negative impact on users, creating a sense of unwanted exposure. Below, we provide three (interwoven) theoretical explanations fo the underlying mechanisms of this discomfort and how it ties to the loss of autonomy. We argue that this is due to a lack of self-endorsement of (public) behaviours, non-transparent audience, and a lack of sharing ‘common ground’.

First, an important tenet of autonomy in the Basic Psychological Needs theory is that behaviours are self endorsed and congruent with informants' authentic interests (Ryan & Deci, 2017). Further, autonomy involves taking ownership of one's actions and taking responsibility for them. In our case study, we assume that the informants music listening behaviours were self-endorsed and congruent with their authentic interests, that is, an autonomy supporting activity. That is, the users were not under pressure to listen to specific songs and chose those of their own volition for functional and/or hedonic purposes. However, individuals' behaviours often differ depending on whether the setting is private or public (Snyder, 1987). Accordingly, when a digital platform makes information about users' privately enacted behaviours publicly observable, these behaviours might no longer be self-endorsed by th user as they were never meant to be public behaviours. This was alluded to by several of our informants as they shared how their behaviours depended on whether the Spotify setting was private or public. When the disclosure is performed by the digital platform without the explicit consent of the user, the digital platform is partly ‘acting’ as the user's proxy, for example, by highlighting a specific artist as one's top artist . As such, our findings provide empirica support for the line of thinking laid out by Kunst and Vatrapu (2019) in their conceptual framework for eWOB where they divide the role of a ‘communicator’ into a source (the model, i.e., the user performing a behaviour) and the plat form (i.e., the agent, who decides to present the behavioural trace in the user interface). Through the lens of BPN theory, we argue that this duality of who is really the sender/communicator of eWOB leads to a sense of loss of ownership and accountability of one's actions, which hinders the sense of autonomy.

Secondly, the negative psychosocial consequence of unwanted exposure relates to a lack of well-defined audiences of one's behaviours when presented as eWOB. Scholars of self-presentation and impression management have long argued that humans adapt their behaviours to specific audiences (Litt, 2012). When our behaviours and communication move to the digital sphere, people then construct imagined audiences described as a person's men tal conceptualization of the people with whom he or she is communicating’ (Litt, 2012, p. 330). However, in today's digital interconnected society the tendency of social media to collapse contexts and audiences means that it can be difficult to determine who is actually one's audience, and how one should digitally cater to people who in the offline realm belong to distinct communities (Marwick & boyd, 2010). This unwanted exposure likely stems from the ambi guity surrounding who is one's audience. In some cases, this led our informants to entirely turn off behavioural disclosures (e.g., by disconnecting their Spotify and Facebook accounts or listening in private mode ). In other cases informants stated that they would alter their behaviours if they were observable by other users, akin to the mecha nisms known from self-monitoring theory (Snyder, 1987). Here, the prospect of having other users observe one' behaviour creates a chilling effect (Marder et al., 2016) that might lead users to modify their behaviours to match the expectations of the observers or to perform impression management (Leary et al., 1990). While such modified or curated behaviours are an expected consequence, when viewed in the light of self-representation theory, it is an important nuance to bring to the field of eWOB. Here, some scholars (e.g., Chen et al., 2011; Cheung et al., 2014) have praised behaviours for their ability to – as the common saying goes – speak louder than words. Based on our findings we stress that when behaviours are self-reported by users – as seen in the work by Cheung et al. (2014) and Bond et al. (2012) – they are not necessarily neutral accounts of what happened, and might disguise behaviours that also happened. In this manner, one can question the trustworthiness of behaviours when these become curated to only disclose those that portray a positive image, similar to the mechanisms of conspicuous consumption (Veblen, 1899).

Third and last, a lack of ‘common ground’ due to missing feedback options can also help us understand th unwanted exposure felt by some of our informants. The concept of ‘common ground’ (Clark & Brennan, 1991) is commonly employed in the Computer Supported Cooperative Work literature, and refers to ‘the recursive knowl edge that each has of the other, that is, ‘I know X’ and ‘You know X’ and ‘I know that you know X’ and ‘You know that I know X’, recursively all the way down’ (Tenenberg et al., 2016, p. 242). Common ground affects how peopl communicate because individuals shape their message under assumptions about the receiver's knowledge or con text. However, not until the ‘receiver’ provides a form of ‘acceptance’ can the ‘sender’ know how the message wa received and understood (Tenenberg et al., 2016). In the case of eWOB, this poses a challenge.

As elaborated upon above, when individual behavioural information is disclosed by the digital platform and not directly by the individual, the user does not have the opportunity to ‘shape’ the message to the audience. In the spe cific case of Spotify, this is further exacerbated as the platform does not provide any easy means for users to respond to eWOB (e.g., with a like ). While some users turn to alternative channels to comment on friends' music lis tening behaviours (e.g., texting or offline conversations), it seems fair to conclude that on a platform like Spotify, it is difficult for users to provide an acceptance , and thus gain the sense of common ground that would make them fee more comfortable and in control when interacting with eWOB. On a broader note, establishing some ways to provide feedback represents an untapped potential to satisfy basic psychological needs for relatedness and competence Prior research has empirically demonstrated that the receipt of positive feedback on one's social media postings can stimulate a feeling of connectedness and a sense of community (Maruyama et al., 2017). Moreover, as the receipt of positive feedback satisfies the basic psychological need for competence (Ryan et al., 2006), we might also expect that a lack of feedback options is likely to thwart competence satisfaction.

## 5.1.3 | The dumbing effect of the algorithm and impact on competence

The findings regarding informants' felt confusion when observing their own past listening behaviours are highly rele vant in today's digitalised and algorithm-driven world. Let us elaborate. One of the key value-adding features of Spotify is its recommendation engine. Specifically, the weekly playlist personalised to each specific user was reg arded as useful by many of our informants. While this use of an algorithm-based playlist arguably is highly conve nient for users, conversely, it can also be viewed as mental outsourcing of one's music competence to an algorithm From a BPN perspective, this can stifle users' sense of competence as they arguably experience less direct impact on their environment – a central component of feeling competent (White, 1959). As such, their music listening behaviours can no longer be attributed solely to their own music competence or as a personal accomplishment because the music was served to the user without the user having to invest any efforts. This will likely be especially prevalent for music enthusiasts, such as the informant CEA highlighted in Section 4, who seemed utterly confused when faced with her own recently listened to overview. For a self-proclaimed music enthusiast such as CEA, this lack of dee knowledge of one's own recent music listening can be regarded as a lack of personal competence and even a per sonal failure. It follows that digital platforms that provide this type of recommendation service (e.g., books, music and movies) risk deemphasizing users' sense of self, and challenge their sense of competence

## 5.1.4 | From digital traces to treasured digital artefacts

The empirical findings revealed that eWOB – when presented as an aggregation of one's own past behaviours – enables a walk down memory lane. This ability is worth highlighting in today's highly digitalised society. Like th observation of others' behaviours, the observation of one's own behaviours can be viewed through the lens of Bandura's observational learning theory. It has been referred to as self-modelling (Bandura, 1986; Dowrick, 1999) for example, when athletes observe their own movements on video to optimise these. In the setting of a digital music platform, Bandura's concept of self-modelling arguably corresponds to using the overview of one's own most lis tened to songs as a shortcut to listening to them again. However, we observe use cases where the focus is less so on (re-)learning by observation of own past behaviours, and, more so, on re-living by observing past behaviours. Specifi cally, the findings clearly demonstrate that being able to dive into an archive of one's aggregated past behaviours (e.g., most listened to songs in the past year) entice feelings of reliving memories and ultimately connecting with a sense of self and one's past. This less functional use of behavioural information is in line with work across scholarl disciplines, such as marketing (Fournier, 1991), anthropology (Strathern, 1990), and consumption theory (McCracken, 1990) where humans have been shown to assign special meanings and values beyond pure utility to personal objects and products. Specifically, products, and our consumption hereof, can be used for creating and sustaining identity, self-expression, and for relating these to oneself and others (Belk, 1988, 2013; Veblen, 1899). Simply put our consumption behaviours tell stories about us and our relations to the world. However, in today's digital society many of the artefacts that have been instrumental in this process of creating a sense of self – music, books, films, an so on – are to a much lesser degree found as physical objects in people's homes. It is becoming increasingly rare to hav one's physical music collection exposed on the shelf providing a sense of self and of one's past. These objects now reside in much less visible ways on digital platforms, such as Spotify. By showcasing to users their own past behaviour in an aggregated format, digital platforms can help turn seemingly elusive and unobservable digital traces of behaviours into digital artefacts with functional as well as high emotional value and meaning. Spotify has in the past few years, for example, increasingly crafted intelligent ways of presenting users with their past listening behaviours; a stellar exampl is its ‘Spotify Wrapped’ feature that lets users know, for example, their topmost listened to song last year and the day the user listened to that song the first time/the most, and so on.

## 5.2 | Contributions to theory and existing literature

This study sheds light on how eWOB is interpreted and acted upon by users of a digital platform. This study – situated in the empirical context of a digital music platform – adds to the current knowledge base about the use of behaviour-based information in digital interfaces – here, referred to as eWOB – in two overall ways. First, it deepen the literature by taking a user perspective and going beyond the functional level of eWOB as a guiding mechanism Second, it broadens the existing literature by investigating types of eWOB hitherto largely left unexplored. In the fol lowing, we detail these contributions.

## 5.2.1 | Deepening existing literature

First, our qualitative findings provide empirical support for the dominant assumption in existing literature, that information about other users' behaviours is indeed interpreted and often used by users as an element meant to provide guidance when presented in aggregated form as ‘popularity information’. This is in line with the works of Chen et al. (2011) and Thies et al. (2016), as well as the theoretical underpinning of observational learning theory (Bandura, 1986) and the related concept of herd behaviour (Banerjee, 1992; Bikhchandani et al., 1998). Relatedly, when observing friends' ‘user activity’, we find that it serves as an inspiration to find new music, akin to the findings by Aral and Walker (2011) and Cheung et al. (2014). However, the findings also provide empirical evidence of a potential inverted effect of eWOB among some users, where songs/artists with a low number of listens are perceived as more interesting for particular users, depending on reference groups. A few scholars have recognised that the effects of ‘popularity information’ might vary depending on its mainstream/niche appeal. For example, Tucker and Zhang (2011) found that niche products benefit more from the display of eWOB (in the form of, popularity information ) than does mainstream products, and Chen et al. (2011) also alluded to such differential impact. We contribute to this understanding by providing qualitative support, showing that presumably niche-oriented users wil interpret and use eWOB to guide them towards less popular content. Consequently, a high count of past behaviours will not equal with high quality for all users, especially so in product categories that are strongly linked to identity (e.g., music) and self-expression (e.g., Tucker and Zhang's (2011) empirical setting of wedding services). It should be noted, however, that in cases where there is risk involved beyond the time spent, such as in Thies et al.’s (2016) study of crowdfunding behaviour, users will likely be more prone to interpret a high eWOB count as high quality.

Second, we empirically demonstrate that users interpret and use eWOB in ways that go much beyond this afore mentioned functional guidance. When presented at the aggregated level, eWOB can provide users with a sense of (imagined) community, feeling special and socially recognised, simply by observing others' behaviours, that is, not necessarily interacting with other users. This, we tie to the stimulation of the need for relatedness and competence. When presented at the individual-specific level, the mere observation of friends' music listening behaviours can also here result in the psychosocial consequence of feeling socially recognised, which we positively tie to the need for compe tence. Moreover, the general presence of other users (presented in aggregate numbers or as specific friends) can also act as social validation of users' choices. Specifically, observing others' behaviours instills comfort and confidence in one's own choices, which we tie to the stimulation of users' need for competence. This can be related to the eWOM phenomenon of studying product ratings and reviews after having purchased a product with the aim of getting con firmation that one has made the correct buying decision (Hennig-Thurau & Walsh, 2003). Finally, eWOB from friends can act as a social facilitator, that is, as an offset for further social interaction outside the Spotify platform, which w tie to a stimulation of the basic psychological needs for relatedness.

## 5.2.2 | Broadening the existing literature

We also contribute to the existing literature by investigating types of eWOB where the displayed behaviours are the users' own past behaviours. To the best of our knowledge, no empirical investigation of these types of eWOB exists Although it thematically borders with the literature about ‘the quantified self’ (Sjöklint, 2014), a key differentiator in th studied case of Spotify, and likely applicable to other content-based digital platforms, is that the primary user motivatio to use the platform is not to self-track, but rather to consume digital content. This is contrary to apps, such as Garmi Connect<sup>6</sup> where one of the main foci is to track one's behaviours and progress within a given sports discipline

From the two types of eWOB coming from oneself (i.e., self-tracking and self-activity ) we identify a numbe of new functional consequences (in addition to the guiding function), namely that the behavioural traces are inter preted (and sometimes used) as content for sharing on social media, as an archive, as well as an image-building tool Interestingly, we found that with the exception of the archive function and the social media content function (bot of which enabled re-living past times down memory lane and ultimately created a stronger sense of self), the two types of eWOB coming from oneself (‘self-tracking’ and ‘self-activity’) have negative psychosocial consequences Specifically, we identified a sense of unwanted exposure resulting in a negative impact on users' autonomy. Thi unwanted exposure is fueled by a lack of disclosure- and audience-control, and lack of feedback options. Finally, con fusion was identified as a negative psychosocial consequence of the ‘self-activity’ type of eWOB in ways that likely impact users' felt competence negatively. This sense of confusion was identified among users, who considered them selves music experts when they were presented with their own algorithmically determined music listening history.

## 5.3 | Implications for design and contributions to practice

From a practice-orientated point of view, our findings are relevant as they indicate that the inclusion of carefull designed elements of eWOB on a digital platform can have positive spill-over effects on user satisfaction, and thu also on user retention. BPN theory posits that the satisfaction of the basic psychological needs is important for the individual's general well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2017). This is demonstrated across a wide range of empirical settings for example, among workers in work-related settings (Deci et al., 2001; Ilardi et al., 1993), among residents in elderl care homes (Kasser & Ryan, 1999), and in friendships (Deci et al., 2006). As such, the successful inclusion of eWOB should also increase the well-being of digital users. Whether this leads to an increased loyalty from users is too premature to conclude, and is an area for further research. However, given the empirically demonstrated close relation ship between customer satisfaction, customer loyalty, and profitability (Hallowell, 1996; Mithas et al., 2005; Shanka et al., 2003) it is reasonable to hypothesize that eWOB can positively affect user satisfaction and retention. How ever, the findings also demonstrated a number of negative consequences, such as confusion and unwanted exposure In the following section, we present design principles (see Table 2) for the application of eWOB in digital content based platforms in manners that tap positively into the satisfaction of basic psychological needs while at the sam time minimising the identified negative outcomes of eWOB on users

First, we find it worth highlighting that even anonymous and aggregated eWOB (such as ‘top XX cities’ for a given behaviour) can provide users with a sense of community and competence through a kind of imagined community (Anderson, 2006). Being able to achieve this positive impact, solely by observing aggregated behavioural data would be valuable for digital platforms, as this type of data is more readily available than behaviours at the individual-specific level. This is especially relevant given the Facebook data controversy where the company Cam bridge Analytica illegally acquired Facebook user data, and subsequently used it for political purposes, which led Facebook to severely restrict digital platforms to gain access to and display behavioural information at the friend level.<sup>7</sup> Second, we suggest that designers should provide finer-grained user control based on to whom and in which context one's behaviour is disclosed. This would lead to increased ownership of the disclosed behaviours, and, as such provide support for users' wish for autonomy. Third, we observe that while eWOB, in general, is interpreted as an indicator of popularity, some users will benefit from being able to easily assess the more detailed sub-groups among whom a given behaviour is popular. This will allow the user to better use eWOB as a guide to finding rel evant content. Fourth, platform designers should provide easy means of responding to eWOB as this increases the sense of common ground among users, and thus reduces discomfort. Further, providing such feedback mech anisms would arguably also increase the felt competence of those receiving feedback as well as heighten th potential opportunities for social interactions between users, and thus stimulate the need for relatedness. Fifth, to address the identified negative impact on users' felt competence, related to their reliance on algorithmic recommendations, we suggest that content-based digital platforms make it possible for users to educate themselves about the content recommended by the algorithm. Thereby, we would expect users to increase awareness and ownership of their consumption choices, which would positively affect users' felt competence. Sixth, to increas users' awareness about and insights into their consumption choices (and thus spur a sense of competence) digita platforms could empower users by giving them more influence over the algorithmic suggestions. For example, by letting the user select which elements of a particular piece of content that the user appreciates, and what (s)he then wants more or less of. This functionality is a key feature in another music streaming service called Moodagent. Here, users get to control the algorithmic suggestions by adjusting the levels of elements such as happiness , tempo and sensuality . Seventh, and last, we argue that in the case of identity-shaping products content, digital platforms should consider presenting users with an archive-like overview of past behaviours Designers should, however, be wary of users' need for privacy, and let this default be private and only public if actively chosen to. Moreover, our findings suggest that this type of information is most meaningful to users whe processed, and not presented in a raw format.

## 6 | LIMITATIONS AND FURTHER RESEARCH

Naturally, this study is not without limitations. It is a single-case study of a digital, content-based platform that operates as a subscription-based ‘all you can eat-service’. The user interpretations and uses of eWOB might work differently on platforms where the digital content is paid for on a per-unit basis. Arguably, there is less tendency to perform spontaneous and/or explorative behaviours when each piece of consumed content (i.e., behaviour) is paid individually. In such cases, behaviours might carry more weight, as users must be assumed to do more research before they engage in consuming. Scholars are thus encouraged to further explore how users interpret and act upon eWOB on platforms that do not operate on the subscriptionbased ‘all you can eat’ model. Second, the findings might be influenced by the larger socio-cultural context, such as individualistic versus collectivistic oriented cultures, where members of the latter culture are likely to pay more attention to the behaviours of others. Third, the use of the BPN framework naturally sets the stage for a focus on the role of eWOB as a means to satisfy basic psychological needs. Other theoretica approaches can help uncover additional relevant aspects of this topic. Fourth, this research provides qualita tive insights which would be useful to further investigate and validate quantitatively. Specifically, the link between eWOB and basic psychologic needs could be quantitatively assessed including an exploration of which of the four types of eWOB are more influential in satisfying basic psychological needs. This would be important knowledge for digital platforms in their efforts to maximise user satisfaction and retention. Fifth and finally, the area of disclosing eWOB to users about their own behaviours warrants further exploration. This is an underexplored topic in the existing literature about eWOB on digital platforms. As alluded to above, we find that the digital traces of one's own behaviours are especially relevant in today's highly digitalised society where physical and identity-building products become less and less visible, replaced by digi tal, and less visible traces. Platform designers should take this into account when designing digital platforms, and future research should explore further how eWOB can strategically be used to contribute to a sense of self and self-expression.

## AUTHOR BIOGRAPHIES

Katrine Kunst is an external lecturer at the Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business Schoo (Denmark) where she lectures on social influence theory and digitalization. She holds a PhD in Information Systems and an MSc in Marketing from Copenhagen Business School. Her research centres around digital behav iours, social influence, and how humans affect each other through behaviours. Her work has been published in Electronic Markets and leading international conferences such as European Conference on Information Systems and the Annual Conference of the European Marketing Academy (EMAC). Katrine Kunst also works as an industry consultant within the field of innovation, helping organisations understand user needs through qualitative and ethnographic techniques and turning user insights into development and design opportunities.

Torsten Ringberg is a professor at the Department of Marketing at Copenhagen Business School (Denmark) Prof. Ringberg publishes across a range of top international academic business journals, such as Journal of Marketing, Journal of Consumer Research, Journal of Consumer Psychology, Journal of Management Studies, Research in the Sociology of Organisations, Annals of Tourism Research, and Journal of Interactive Marketing. Most recently h co-published the management book; Disrupt Your Mindset to Transform Your Business with Big Data. Prof. Ringberg has worked on projects with leading U.S. and European corporations on issues such as consumer conceptualization, subconscious motives, embodied cognition, managerial mindset, and strategic brand positioning. He explores how deeply seated (internalised) mental models and the senses influence consumers' and managers cognition, sense-making, and ultimately behaviour across a variety of contexts. He has won Best in Practice Awards from the American Marketing Association/Milwaukee (AMA), based on efficient and creative consulting approaches and their strategic implications for brands. Prof. Ringberg holds a PhD in Marketing from Penn State

University (U.S.), an M.A. in Psychological Anthropology from University of Hawaii, Manoa, and an Advanced MBA from Chaminade University, Honolulu, Hawaii.

Ravi Vatrapu is the Loretta Rogers Research Chair in Digital Enterprise and professor of computational social science at the School of Information Technology Management, Ted Rogers School of Management, Ryerson University. He was formerly a professor of computational social science at the Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School; professor of applied computing at the Kristiania University College; and the founding director of the Centre for Business Data Analytics (http://bda.cbs.dk). Prof. Vatrapu's current research focus is on business data analytics to design, develop and evaluate a novel set-theoretical approach to computational social science, Social Set Analytics. Prof. Vatrapu holds a PhD in Communication and Information Sciences from the University of Hawaii at Manoa (U.S.), a Graduate Certificate in International Cultural Studies from East– West Centre (U.S.), a Master of Science in Computer Science and Applications from Virginia Tech (U.S.), and a Bachelor of Technology in Computer Science and Systems Engineering from Andhra University (India).
