## Abstract

Most information systems (IS) research takes for granted that consumers' adoption and the use of mobile payment (MP) applications are motivated by generic factors such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Challenging this assumption, we argue that the salient contextual characteristics of MP applications compel a reconsideration and problematization of research on MP adoption and use. Drawing on network effect theory, we examined how contextual network effects and contextual network types determine MP consumer loyalty. Using a mixed methods design, we find that direct network effects (i.e., network size, network centrality, network capability), indirect network effects (i.e., platform–application complementarity, application–service complementarity, service–strategy complementarity) and negative network effects (i.e., general institutional structure, general structural assurance, local institutional structure and local structural assurance) are key determinants of perceived benefits, which further promote MP consumer loyalty. Furthermore, except for general institutional structure and general structural assurance, all of the network effects are important predictors of switching costs, which influence MP consumer loyalty. Finally, the impacts of network effects on MP consumer loyalty differ between consumer- and service-oriented networks. Our study enriches the IS literature by problematizing the core assumption underlying the MP adoption and use research and offering a contextual explanation of MP consumer loyalty. Our work also provides practitioners with insights into how to better leverage network effects on MP consumer loyalty.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Mobile payment (MP) markets have emerged as new drivers of productivity and economic growth in the world economy (Hendershott et al., 2017, 2021). The global MP markets were worth \$1.08 trillion in 2019 alone, attracting nearly 1.06 billion consumers to adopt MP applications for payment activities (eMarketer, 2019a). The huge market potential has made the MP market extremely competitive with the boom of a variety of MP applications, such a WeChat Wallet, Alipay Wallet and Apple Pay. Attracted by various MP applications from competing providers, consumers switch providers frequently, which makes it difficult to build a loyal consumer base (Wang et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2019). Although some MP applications have been successful, most fail outright in establishing long-term rela tionships with their consumers. For instance, WeChat Wallet and Alipay Wallet generated a total of 91% of the MP market share in China by maintaining loyal consumer bases, while more than 95% of the other MP applications accounted for less than 5% of the MP market share in China and struggled to expand their loyal consumer base (eMarketer, 2019b). Therefore, building consumer loyalty is critical for the survival and sustainability of most MP practitioners.

The importance of understanding MP adoption and use of MP applications (including MP consumer loyalty or COL) is also recognized by IS researchers (Kazan et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2009; Mallat, 2007). Our review of the litera ture (see Appendix A) shows that MP researchers have primarily used general technology adoption models— including the technology acceptance model (TAM), the diffusion of innovation model (DIT), and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT)—to examine consumers' adoption and use of MP applications. A centra assumption of these studies is that MP adoption and use is motivated by generic factors such as perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness, which is rooted in a large body of general information technology (IT) adoption and use research in the IS research community (Venkatesh et al., 2003, 2012; Venkatesh, Brown, & Sullivan, 2016 Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, & Hu, 2016; Venkatesh, Thong, & Xu, 2016).

However, the assumption that IT adoption and use result from generic factors has been repeatedl challenged in IS research (Hong et al., 2014), and the limitations of this premise have been acknowledged in IS practice (Chatterjee et al., 2020; Chatterjee, Moody, et al., 2021; Chatterjee, Sarker, et al., 2021). MP researchers and practitioners have also raised the concern that generic factors cannot offer specific guidance to direct the design and practice of innovative MP applications. Critiques of generic factors have pointed to the significance of contextualization in theory development in MP adoption and use research. Avgerou (2019) argued that most the oretical models on IT use are contextually underdeveloped, leaving nearly all of the organizational and environ ment context outside the model’ (p. 977). IS researchers have called for contextual theorising practice in innovative IT adoption and use research (Cheng et al., 2016; Fernández, 2016; Martinsons & Davison, 2016 Sarker, 2016).

Following Alvesson and Sandberg's (2011) problematization methodology and Chatterjee and Davison's (2021 guidance, we problematize and challenge the general technology adoption model assumptions underlying existing MP adoption and use research and propose the contextualization approach to examine MP COL (see Table 1). We argu that in addition to generic factors, an understanding of salient contextual factors is needed in MP adoption and us research. Contextualization provides an effective way to enrich existing IS theories by incorporating the characteris tics and usage context of IT artefacts in theory development (Davison, 2021; Davison & Martinsons, 2016). To under stand the salient contextual factors in the context of MP COL, we first provide an overview of the characteristics of MP applications and then describe how we apply the contextualization approach to examine MP COL

MP applications are innovative financial ITs that serve as intermediaries connecting consumers and service providers to engage in ubiquitous payment activities in the MP technology network. Consumers' MP adoption and use are largely dependent on the development of the MP technology network (Mallat, 2007). MP technology net works are characterized by three categories of network effects: (1) direct network effects—the incremental valu derived from the size and growth of consumers; (2) indirect network effects—the incremental value derived from th size and growth of complementary services and (3) negative network effects—the decreased value derived from the size and growth of transaction and security risks. Furthermore, MP technology networks can be broadly divided into two types: consumer-oriented networks, in which providers subsidize many potential consumers on the consumer side to make the network appealing to the service side; and service-oriented networks, in which providers acquire sources from a large variety of complementary services on the service side to reinforce the size and growth of con sumers on the consumer side. These salient contextual characteristics of MP technology networks call for more con textual richness and practical relevance in the MP adoption and use research.

Hong et al. (2014) proposed two single-context theory contextualization approaches: (1) antecedent contextualiza tion, focused on incorporating contextual factors as antecedents of core variables; and (2) boundary condition contextu alization, centred on examining contextual factors as moderators of relationships. Drawing on network effect theor (Katz & Shapiro, 1985, 1986), we use the antecedent contextualization approach to examine how contextual direct, indi rect and negative network effects affect MP COL. The boundary condition contextualization approach incorporates th network type as a contextual contingency factor to influence the impacts of network effects on MP COL

To test the contextualized network effect model of MP COL, we adopt a mixed methods design. Specifically, we use the mixed methods approach for corroboration (Venkatesh et al., 2013) in that we confirm our findings using quantitative data from an online survey and qualitative data from semi-structured interviews. The use of quantitative and qualitative methods can also leverage the complementary merits and nonoverlapping biases and thus offer a deeper understanding of MP COL under network effects (Venkatesh, Brown, & Sullivan, 2016; Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, & Hu, 2016; Venkatesh, Thong, & Xu, 2016). We believe that our contextualized understanding of MP COL contributes to research and practice. First, we improve the contextual theorization of MP adoption and use researc through Alvesson and Sandberg's (2011) problematization methodology. The contextualization perspective prob lematizes the assumption that MP adoption and use result from generic factors and present an alternative set of assumptions that contextual MP technology network factors motivate MP COL. In addition, the contextualization perspective offers MP practitioners a better understanding of network-building resources, thereby optimising thei return on investment in COL formation in a highly competitive MP market. Second, we advance network effect theory (Katz & Shapiro, 1985, 1986) by identifying contextual network effects and contextual network types. The con textual conceptualizations not only progressively enhance the potential for core concepts of network effect theor to be operationalized but also profoundly extend the boundary condition of relationships in the theory

Network effect theory suggests that a two-sided technology network is characterized by three types of network effects: (1) direct network effects—the size and growth of consumers; (2) indirect network effects—the size and growth of complementary services and (3) negative network effects—the size and growth of transaction an security risks (Katz & Shapiro, 1985, 1986). Network effect theory has two advantages over general technology adoption models for understanding MP COL. First, th conceptualization of direct, indirect and negative network effects is highly relevant to the specific characteristics of MP applications, including various consumer-to-consumer money transfers, various proximity and remote payment scenarios, and various transaction and security risks. Second, the operationalization of the network type effectivel reveals the two-sided market of MP applications, which attracts consumers on one side of the network and servic providers that supply complementary services on the other.

## 2.1 | Direct network effect

The direct network effect refers to the phenomenon that the size and growth of consumers change the value creation/ capture processes in a technology network (Song et al., 2018). Many studies have used network structure factors, such as network size, to conceptualize direct network effects (Lin & Bhattacherjee, 2008). Typical examples include the num ber of consumers in instant message applications (Lu et al., 2010), the number of players in online game platform (Kanat et al., 2020) and the number of users on social networking sites (Lin & Lu, 2011). The assumption is that when more consumers participate in a network, existing members can gain opportunities to benefit equally from transacting with other members. Afuah (2013) suggested that ‘as positions and capabilities are usually not homogeneous across net work members, it is likely that different members of a certain network have different abilities to add or capture valu from it (p. 264). Recent studies have further contended that network size, centrality and capability are all network struc ture factors that should be simultaneously considered in capturing the direct network effect (Peng & Dey, 2013)

The core concept of the direct network effect is the network structure, which captures the number of consumers, the relationships between them, and the heterogeneous capabilities of consumers and their relationships in the technology network (Afuah, 2013). In the context of MP applications, we contextually decompose the network structure into three archetypes: network size, network centrality and network capability (see Figure 1). The network size denotes the total number of consumers and potential payment ties in a MP technology network (Lin & Bhattacherjee 2008). Consumers can gain more potential payment ties to transact with other members when they participate in a MP technology network with a large consumer base. Network centrality refers to the position of focal consumers i their ego network and the number of actual payment ties to other members in a MP technology network (Peng & Dey, 2013). Different consumers have different positions in the MP technology network and therefore should hav different impacts on how much value they can add to or capture from the network. Network capability captures the capability of a MP technology network in supporting irreplaceable strong payment ties between consumers (Katona et al., 2011). Consumers can develop either weak payment ties with the MP technology network, in which they us the network with relatively low frequency and depth, or strong payment ties with the MP technology network, in which they use the network with relatively high frequency and depth.

## 2.2 | Indirect network effect

The indirect network effect refers to the phenomenon by which the size and growth of complementary services change the value creation/capture processes in a technology network (Song et al., 2018). IS researchers frequentl use technology complementarity to capture the indirect network effect (Lin & Lu, 2011). Technology complementar ity highlights the size and growth of lower- and upper-layer complementary services in a technology network. Typica examples are the number of 3/4/5G software platforms for 3/4/5G networks (Hann et al., 2016), the number of add-on services for social networking sites (Zhao & Lu, 2012) and the number of support-communication tools for instant messaging applications (Zhou & Lu, 2011).

The core concept of the indirect network effect is technology complementarity, which demonstrates th synergies across the technology hierarchy that can provide value to consumers through efficient and effective interactions across layers in the technology network (Xu et al., 2010). In the context of MP applications, we con textually decompose technology complementarity into three archetypes: platform–application complementarity, application–service complementarity and service–strategy complementarity (see Figure 2). There are four basi layers in the MP technology hierarchy: (1) platform—the variety of online and offline third-party transaction platforms, such as eBay, Amazon, Starbucks and Walmart; (2) application—the variety of MP applications, such as WeChat Wallet and Alipay Wallet; (3) service—the variety of first-party payment services, such as credit card repayments, phone top-up and utility bills and (4) strategy—the variety of supporting-payment strategies, such as customization, ordering, personalization and search. The four technology lavers further generate three types of technology complementarities. Platform–application complementarity captures the compatibility of a MP applica tion with a large variety of third-party transaction platforms and its smooth operation on these platforms to provide consumers with satisfactory payment performance. Application–service complementarity reflects a MP application having a large variety of first-party payment services and operating these services efficiently on the application to provide consumers with satisfactory payment performance. Service–strategy complementarity refers to a MP application providing a large variety of supporting-payment strategies to help consumers operat payment services efficiently.

## 2.3 | Negative network effect

The negative network effect refers to the self-interested, opportunistic and even criminal behaviours that can gener ate transaction and security risks for network members in a technology network (Strader et al., 2007). Many studie have shown that the increased size and growth of a technology network not only creates positive network effect but also facilitates negative network effects. For instance, Chen et al. (2011) reported that popular security software packages with a large market share and installed consumer base are often vulnerable to security and hacker attacks

Strader et al. (2007) contended that the externality of e-mail systems makes consumers vulnerable to unintended security risks of spim and spam. Similarly, Luo et al. (2010) and Mallat (2007) found that consumers faced high transaction and security risks in a MP technology network when the network size exceeded an optimal point. Therefore, alleviating the negative network effect with institutional mechanisms is regarded as an important means to enhance the value creation/capture processes in a technology network.

The core concept of the negative network effect is institutional mechanism, which is the existence of institu tional safeguards that protect consumers against transaction and security risks in the technology network (Fang et al., 2014). In the context of MP applications, we contextually decompose institutional mechanism into four arche types: (I) general institutional structure, (II) general structural assurance, (III) local institutional structure and (IV) loca structural assurance (see Figure 3). General institutional structure captures application-independent payment safeguards (e.g., MP industry regulations and MP government legislations) on the mobile internet to alleviate risks when using MPs (Huang et al., 2017). General structural assurance denotes application-independent payment safeguard (e.g., MP encryption technologies and MP authentication technologies) on the mobile internet to assure securit when using MPs (McKnight et al., 2002). These application-independent institutional mechanisms protect consumers against potential risks that are rooted in the general MP environment. A local institutional structure highlight application-specific payment safeguards (e.g., monetary loss compensation and informational loss compensation) on a MP application to alleviate risks when using that specific application (Kim et al., 2009). Local structural assurance demonstrates application-specific payment safeguards (e.g., escrow technologies and user profile technologies) on a MP application to assure security when using that specific application (Gefen & Pavlou, 2012). These application specific institutional mechanisms protect consumers against potential risks that are inherent in the transactiona relationship with the MP application.

## 2.4 | Network type

A technology network usually operates as a two-sided network, focusing on attracting consumers on one side of the network and service providers that supply complementary services on the other (Li & Agarwal, 2017). In a two-sided technology network, the size and growth of one side—either the consumer side or service side—is likely to reinforc the size and growth of the other (Chu & Manchanda, 2016). Thus, a technology network can be classified into either a consumer-oriented network, in which the large size of the consumer base helps the network generate more complementary services, or a service-oriented network, in which the large installed base of complementary services helps the network attract more consumers

## 3.1 | Core predictors of consumer loyalty

According to network effect theory, consumers' loyal use of a technology network is determined by two types of value creation/capture processes: dedication- and constraint-based processes (Song et al., 2018). The dedicationbased process centres on the concept of perceived benefits, as consumers' dedication to a long-term relationship with the incumbent MP technology network is usually generated by the prospect of long-term benefits (Kim & Son 2009). Perceived benefits of MP use refer to the perceived effectivity and efficiency associated with making pay ments through the incumbent MP technology network. High perceived benefits of MP use imply that consumers believe that using the incumbent MP technology network helps them perform payment activities effectively and effi ciently (Khalilzadeh et al., 2017). These current beneficial experiences guide consumers to anticipate the long-term benefits of using the incumbent MP technology network (Oliveira et al., 2016). Consequently, consumers are likel to favour a long-term relationship with the incumbent MP technology network and this ultimately manifests as loya use of the network in anticipation of future benefits.

The constraint-based process is based on the concept of switching costs because a costly switching process to new alternatives constrains consumers to a long-term relationship with the incumbent MP technology networ (Kim & Son, 2009). Switching costs of MP use refer to the psychological and physical investments required to switch from the incumbent MP technology network to alternatives (Chang et al., 2014). These investments involve the los of nontransferable assets from the incumbent MP technology network and the unavoidable switching investments necessary to ensure a satisfactory switch to alternatives (Bunduchi, 2005; Maier et al., 2015). As the switching pro cess to alternatives involves the loss of nontransferable assets and unavoidable switching investments, consumers are usually reluctant to terminate their long-term relationship with the incumbent MP technology network (Wang et al., 2019). Consequently, consumers tend to rely on the incumbent MP technology network to perform payment activities and ultimately formulate COL. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1a. Perceived benefits of MP use positively affects MP consumer loyalty.

H1b. Switching costs of MP use positively affects MP consumer loyalty.

## 3.2 | Direct network effect: Network structures

Network structures include the quantity of network consumers, the relationships between them, and the heteroge neous characteristics of consumers and their relationships in a MP technology network (Afuah, 2013). There ar three types of network structures: (1) network size—the total number of consumers and potential payment ties in the MP technology network; (2) network centrality—the position of focal consumers and the number of actual pay ment ties with other consumers in the MP technology network and (3) network capability—the capability of the MP technology network to support irreplaceable strong payment ties between consumers.

MP applications are designed for various consumer-to-consumer money transfers (Cao et al., 2018). High levels of network size denote that a MP technology network has a large number of consumers. Consumers can gain more potential payment ties to benefit from transacting with other consumers when they participate in a MP technology network with a large consumer base (Mallat, 2007). High levels of network centrality mean that a focal consumer ha a central payment position in a MP technology network. That is, a wide range of socially important reference (e.g., friends, families and colleagues) use the MP technology network, which gradually centralizes the position of th focal consumer in the network. The central payment position enables the focal consumer to acquire more actual pay ment ties to benefit from transacting with their socially important references in the MP technology network (Peng & Dey, 2013). High levels of network capability reflect that a MP technology network has the capability to delive superior and irreplaceable payment services between consumers. Such network capability allows consumers to us the MP technology network with high frequency and depth and to benefit from developing strong payment ties with the network. The network capability of providing strong payment ties generates network benefits to consumers in using a MP technology network. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2a. Network size positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

H2b. Network centrality positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

H2c. Network capability positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

Loss of nontransferable assets and unavoidable switching investments are major sources of switching costs (Ra et al., 2012). Network size, network centrality and network capability are all nontransferable assets for a MP technol ogy network (Afuah, 2013). Consumers prefer to participate in a MP technology network with a large consumer bas to gain more potential payment ties in the network (Lee et al., 2019). When switching from the incumbent MP tech nology network to alternatives, consumers will perceive high switching costs due to the loss of potential payment ties with other consumers. Likewise, consumers tend to use a MP technology network that has received widespread usag among their socially important references (Qasim & Abu-Shanab, 2016). In such a MP technology network, consumers have centralized payment positions that allow them to conveniently and efficiently transfer money with their sociall important references. The loss of centralized payment positions in the switching process contributes to switchin costs. In addition, consumers are generally reluctant to make a switching decision because the switching proces involves unavoidable investments in comparing the network capability of the incumbent MP technology network to alternatives (Wang et al., 2019). These unavoidable switching investments of finding alternative MP technolog networks with comparable network capability also contribute to switching costs. Thus, we hypothesize the following

H2d. Network size positively affects switching costs of MP use.

H2e. Network centrality positively affects switching costs of MP use.

H2f. Network capability positively affects switching costs of MP use

## 3.3 | Indirect network effect: Technology complementarities

Technology complementarity refers to the synergies across the platform–application–service–strategy technology hierarchy, which can provide value to consumers through efficient and effective interactions across layers in the MP technology network (Xu et al., 2010). There are three types of technology complementarities: (1) platform– application complementarity—having a variety of third-party transaction platforms available for use with a MP appli cation; (2) application–service complementarity—having a variety of first-party payment services available for use with a MP application and (3) service–strategy complementarity—having a variety of supporting-payment strategie available for use with a MP application.

MP applications are developed for various proximity and remote payment scenarios (Slade, Dwivedi, et al., 2015 Slade, Williams, et al., 2015). The occurrence of various proximity and remote payment scenarios is largely dependent on three types of complementary services surrounding MP applications: third-party transaction platforms, first-party payment services and supporting-payment strategies. High levels of platform–application complementarity mean that a MP application is compatible with a variety of third-party transaction platforms, such as Amazon, eBay, Starbucks and Walmart. Consumers can perform payment activities effectively and efficiently in the MP technology network when transacting with these third-party transaction platforms (Zhou, 2013). High levels of application–service complementar ity denote that a MP application provides a variety of first-party payment services, such as credit card repayments phone top-ups and utility bill payments. These first-party payment services satisfy consumers' various payment need and deliver instrumental benefits to consumers (Xu et al., 2010). As more third-party transaction platforms and first party payment services become available, a MP application should offer a variety of supporting-payment strategies to help consumers effectively reach their payment goals. For example, consumers may feel overwhelmed when processin MP applications with thousands of third-party transaction platforms and first-party payment services. Supporting payment strategies such as searching, ordering and personalization can help consumers to quickly obtain target transac tion platforms and payment services matching their transactional needs (Tan et al., 2013). Collectively, platform–applica tion, application–service and service–strategy complementarities can effectively enable consumers to benefit from effective and efficient payment activities in the MP technology network. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3a. Platform–application complementarity positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

H3b. Application–service complementarity positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

H3c. Service–strategy complementarity positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

Platform–application, application–service and service–strategy complementarities are all nontransferable assets fo a MP technology network. MP applications often spend millions of dollars to collaborate with popular third-party trans action platforms to fulfil consumers' various payment needs (Zhou, 2013). Such platform–application complementarity is generally costly and difficult for competitors to imitate, which increases consumers' potential losses when switchin from the incumbent MP technology network to alternatives. For example, China's largest e-commerce website, TaoBao, is only available for use with Alipay Wallet. When switching from Alipay Wallet to alternatives, consumers will perceiv high levels of switching costs because they cannot make payments on the TaoBao website without Alipay Wallet. MP applications also release numerous first-party payment services (Wang et al., 2019). Consumers' concerns for the loss of these nontransferable first-party payment services in the switching process contribute to the switching costs. Likewise supporting-payment strategies have been regarded as the foundation for a satisfactory payment service delivery proces (Tan et al., 2013). Consumers are unlikely to obtain desirable payment outcomes in the absence of supporting-payment strategies. In making a satisfactory switch from the incumbent MP technology network, consumers must find an alterna tive MP technology network with a comparable level of supporting-payment strategies. These anticipated switching investments will further reinforce consumers' perceived switching costs. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3d. Platform–application complementarity positively affects switching costs of MP use.

H3e. Application–service complementarity positively affects switching costs of MP use.

H3f. Service–strategy complementarity positively affects switching costs of MP use.

## 3.4 | Negative network effect: Institutional mechanisms

Institutional mechanism refers to consumers' perceptions that appropriate payment safeguards exist to protect them from potential transaction and security risks in a MP technology network (Fang et al., 2014). There are four types of institutional mechanisms: (1) general institutional structure—the existence of appropriate payment safeguards on the mobile internet to alleviate risks when performing MP activities; (2) general structural assurance—the existence of appropriate payment safeguards on the mobile internet to assure security when performing MP activities; (3) loca institutional structure—the existence of appropriate payment safeguards on the MP application to alleviate risk when performing MP activities and (4) local structure assurance—the existence of appropriate payment safeguard on the MP application to assure security when performing MP activities.

MP applications are vulnerable to various transaction and security risks (Luo et al., 2010; Zhou, 2013). This negative network effect significantly undermines the value creation/capture process of a MP technology network (Strader et al., 2007). High levels of general institutional structure mean that the risk of loss from using MP is reduced with a variety of application-independent payment safeguards, such as industry regulations and government legislation. High levels of general structural assurance indicate that the safety and security of using MP are assure with a variety of application-independent payment safeguards, such as encryption and authentication technologies These application-independent payment safeguards can alleviate consumers' concerns about transaction and secu rity risks in the general MP environment (Luo et al., 2010). Under these circumstances, consumers will perceive a MP technology network as reliable and beneficial in performing payment activities because a robust general MP environ ment is crucial to the security and safety of the MP technology network (Guo et al., 2018)

High levels of local institutional structure denote that the risks of loss from using a MP application ar reduced with a variety of application-specific payment safeguards, such as compensation policies for monetary and informational loss. Likewise, high levels of local structural assurance highlight that the safety and security of using a MP application are assured with a variety of application-specific payment safeguards, such as rating and feedback technologies, user profile technologies and escrow services. These application-specific payment safe guards protect consumers against transaction and security risks in performing payment activities with a specific MP application (Kim et al., 2009). In this situation, consumers are confident that a MP technology network is reliable and beneficial for performing payment activities (Huang et al., 2017). Collectively, general institutiona structure, general structural assurance, local institutional structure and local structural assurance can effectivel allow consumers to benefit from transacting with a reliable and secure MP technology network. Thus, we hypoth esize the following:

H4a. General institutional structure positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

H4b. General structural assurance positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

H4c. Local institutional structure positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

H4d. Local structural assurance positively affects perceived benefits of MP use.

General institutional structure and general structural assurance are transferable assets for MP technology net works. General institutional structure allows consumers to alleviate risks through legal payment safeguards from industry regulations and government legislation when using MP. General structural assurance assures consumers of their security through technological payment safeguards, such as encryption and authentication technologies, whe using MP. These legal and technological payment safeguards are rooted in the general MP environment and protect consumers against the transaction and security risks from using any MP technology networks (Luo et al., 2010). When switching from the incumbent MP technology network to alternatives, consumers are unlikely to perceiv switching costs because general institutional structure and general structural assurance can be transferable to alter native MP technology networks to promote the safety and security of using MP.

In contrast, local institutional structure and local structural assurance are nontransferable assets for MP technol ogy networks. The local institutional structure protects consumers against transaction and security risks through application-specific payment safeguards, such as monetary loss compensation, financial information loss compensa tion and transactional data loss compensation. Local structural assurance assures consumers that performing pay ment activities is secure and safe with application-specific payment safeguards, such as rating and feedback technologies, user profile technologies and escrow services. The transaction safety and security promised by thes application-specific payment safeguards cannot be transferable to alternative MP technology networks (Kim et al., 2009). The threat of losing nontransferable application-specific payment safeguards increases consumers' perceived switching costs when switching from the incumbent MP technology network to alternatives (Ray et al., 2012). Fur thermore, consumers' efforts of developing technical self-efficacy in learning the use of alternative MP applications with comparable payment safeguards also contribute to the formation of switching costs (Hardin et al., 2014). Con sequently, general institutional structure and general structural assurance have no effect on switching costs, whereas local institutional structure and local structural assurance have positive effects on switching costs. Thus, we hypothe size the following:

H4e. General institutional structure has no effect on switching costs of MP use.

H4f. General structural assurance has no effect on switching costs of MP use.

H4g. Local institutional structure positively affects switching costs of MP use

H4h. Local structural assurance positively affects switching costs of MP use.

## 3.5 | Network type: Consumer- and service-oriented networks

Consumer-oriented networks (e.g., WeChat Wallet, Facebook Pay and Apple Pay) are built on popular social commu nication platforms that have a large consumer base. Consumers often communicate with each other on social com munication platforms and develop network ties to transfer information (Zhao & Lu, 2012). The size and growth of actual network ties centralize the positions of consumers and allow for efficient information transfer (Kane & Alavi, 2008). Consumers are also likely to develop strong network ties with other consumers through depth and frequent information transfer. When developing MP technology networks from social communication platforms, these previ ously established network ties can be easily transformed into payment ties for efficient and effective money transfe among consumers. When switching to alternatives, consumers are likely to experience high levels of switching costs because of the loss of previously established network ties. In contrast, service-oriented networks (e.g., Alipay Wallet PayPal and Amazon Pay) emerge from business transaction platforms featuring limited network ties between con sumers. When transferring money to others through service-oriented networks, consumers are expected to invest time and energy in developing network ties, such as searching payment accounts, sending payment hyperlinks, or adding to their friend lists (Khalilzadeh et al., 2017). The development of network ties requires consumers to invest additional time and effort into the payment process, which may slightly offset consumers' perceived efficacy and effectiveness in using service-oriented networks. Moreover, consumers may not view network ties as non transferable assets of service-oriented networks, given that they can easily find consumer-oriented networks to replace them, and they would thus not perceive very high switching costs in such cases. Thus, we hypothesize th following:

H5a–c. Network type moderates the effects of network size, network centrality and network capability on perceived benefits of MP use such that these effects become stronger for consumer—than for service-oriented networks.

H5d–f. Network type moderates the effects of network size, network centrality and network capability on switching costs of MP use such that these effects become stronger for consumer—than for service-oriented networks

Whereas consumer-oriented networks acquire advantages in terms of network structure, service-oriented networks have advantages in terms of technology complementarity, as they are affiliated with popular business transaction platforms that typically provide a large variety of complementary services to consumers (Chu & Manchanda 2016). These business transaction platforms often attract a large number of service providers to sell products and services. Consumers often purchase products and services on business transaction platforms and develop numerous services ties with service providers. When developing MP technology networks from business transaction platforms, these previously formed service ties allow consumers to conveniently and efficiently pay for products and services When considering switching to alternatives, consumers' concerns about the loss of these previously formed servic ties contribute to the perception of high switching costs. In contrast, consumer-oriented networks are derived from social communication platforms, which generally provide a smaller variety of complementary services compared to business transaction platforms (Lin & Bhattacherjee, 2008). Some complementary services are not even available at the development stage of consumer-oriented networks (Zhao & Lu, 2012). The lack of complementary services undermines consumers' perceptions of payment performance and productivity in using consumer-oriented networks (Qasim & Abu-Shanab, 2016). When switching to alternatives, consumers may not perceive very high switching costs because they can easily replace consumer-oriented networks with service-oriented networks to satisfy their pay ment needs for various complementary services. Thus, we hypothesize the following

H6a–c. Network type moderates the effects of platform–application complementarity, application– service complementarity and service–strategy complementarity on perceived benefits of MP use such that these effects become weaker for consumer- than for service-oriented networks.

H6d–f. Network type moderates the effects of platform–application complementarity, application– service complementarity and service–strategy complementarity on switching costs of MP use such that these effects become weaker for consumer- than for service-oriented networks.

Compared to service-oriented networks, consumers in consumer-oriented networks are more sensitive to insti tutional mechanisms when evaluating perceived benefits and switching costs of MP use. Service-oriented network are developed from business transaction platforms that were originally designed to conduct commercial payment activities (Chu & Manchanda, 2016). These business transaction platforms have established a robust and secure envi ronment for commercial payment activities. Consumer-oriented networks are affiliated with social communication platforms that are primarily used to perform social communication activities (Song et al., 2018). These social commu nication platforms were seldom used to perform commercial payment activities before the integration of MP technology networks (Li & Agarwal, 2017). Consumers are therefore unsure whether consumer-oriented network have the capability to provide as reliable an environment for commercial payment activities as service-oriented net works. Consequently, institutional mechanisms play a more significant role in the formation of the perceived benefit of MP use and switching costs of MP use in consumer-oriented networks than that in service-oriented networks Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H7a–d. Network type moderates the effects of general institutional structure, general structura assurance, local institutional structure and local structural assurance on perceived benefits of MP use such that these effects become stronger for consumer- than for service-oriented networks.

H7e,f. Network type has no moderating effects on the effects of general institutional structure and general structural assurance on switching costs of MP use, but it moderates the effects of local institutional structure and local structural assurance on switching costs of MP use such that these effects become stronger for consumer- than for service-oriented networks.

## 4 | STUDY 1: QUANTITATIVE SURVEY

## 4.1 | Sample and data collection

The population sampled for the quantitative survey was the current consumers of WeChat Wallet and Alipay Wallet in China. These two MP applications were deemed appropriate for three primary reasons. First, WeChat Wallet and Alipay Wallet are the two most popular MP applications in the Chinese MP market. The eMarketer reports that the Chinese MP market amounted to nearly \$450 billion dollars, with 37% and 54% coming from WeChat Wallet and Alipay Wallet, respectively (eMarketer, 2019b). Second, WeChat Wallet and Alipay Wallet are pioneers of implementing network effects in the delivery of MP businesses. These two MP applications spend billions of dollar expanding the size and growth of consumers, complementary services and payment safeguards (Lu et al., 2011) Third, WeChat Wallet is a representative consumer-oriented network, being affiliated with China's largest instant messaging application (WeChat). In contrast, Alipay Wallet is a typical service-oriented network, having originated from within China's largest e-commerce website (TaoBao).

We distributed two sets of questionnaires through a popular online survey site. The generated uniform resource location (URL) of the questionnaires were distributed to potential WeChat/Alipay Wallet consumers through multi ple channels, including (1) group emails to registered online panels of Sojump; (2) postings to the official WeChat Alipay Wallet forum and (3) postings to popular social networking platforms such as WeChat, QQ, Weibo, LinkedIn Facebook and Twitter. The multichannel recruiting practice can acquire opinions from a wide range of WeChat Alipay Wallet consumers and enhance the overall reach of the survey. To ensure that respondents were experienced WeChat/Alipay Wallet consumers, we implemented the following screening questions: ‘Have you used WeChat Alipay Wallet?’, ‘What's your WeChat/Alipay Wallet account name?’, ‘How long have you used WeChat/Alipa Wallet?’ and ‘How often have you used WeChat/Alipay Wallet?’. To encourage participation, we gave \$2 to respon dents who completed the survey. Once the survey was submitted, the incentive money was automatically sent to their WeChat/Alipay Wallet account

Approximately 1000 questionnaire hyperlinks were distributed to WeChat Wallet consumers, and 327 useable responses were collected, for a response rate of 32.7%. Likewise, approximately 1000 questionnaire hyperlinks were distributed to Alipay Wallet consumers and 360 valid responses were gathered, for a response rate of 36.0%. To evaluate nonresponse bias, we compared the early and late respondents to the means of all of the principa constructs for the WeChat Wallet and Alipay Wallet samples (Armstrong & Overton, 1977). We used fashion consciousness as a survey marker variable to assess common method bias. We identified two types of value creation/capture processes at the post-adoption stage: the dedication-based process and the constraint-based process. We operationalized network type as a dummy variable, for which 0 = consumer-oriented network and 1 = service-ori ented network. We included a series of demographic variables, namely age, gender, education, income, usage experience and usage frequency, as control variables in the model. ## 5.2 | Measures

We generated measures for the principal constructs from the interview data using the two-step open coding approach (Venkatesh et al., 2010). Table D2 presents the two-step open coding procedure. First, we performed exploratory coding from the qualitative data by grouping similar sentences into first-order concepts, categorising first-order concepts into second-order themes and aggregating second-order themes into high-order theoretica dimensions (Zhang, 2017). The first-order concepts were extracted from language in the interview transcripts that was closely tied to the principal variables and hypotheses; the second-order themes were theoretical categories of the first-order concepts that describe the same relationships; and the high-order theoretical dimensions were the aggregation of similar second-order themes (Bala & Venkatesh, 2007). For instance, three typical interviewees' com ments are (1) ‘WeChat/Alipay Wallet is compatible with a variety of online and offline transaction platforms, such as Airbnb, KFC, Wal-Mart…using WeChat/Alipay Wallet increases my payment efficacy’; (2) ‘WeChat/Alipay Wallet provides a variety of payment services, such as phone top-up, credit card repay, utility bills…using these payment services improves my payment performance’ and (3) ‘using WeChat/Alipay Wallet improves my payment efficacy because I can quickly find target services by using searching functions’. ## 5.3 | Results

We empirically validated the hypotheses using the hypothetico-deductive logic approach (Chatterjee et al., 2009 2013). Tables D3 and D4 present the hypothesis testing procedure. The hypothetico-deductive logic approach is a sci entific hypothesis testing method that involves starting with an already formulated theoretical hypothesis and using empirical evidence to assess the validity of the hypothesis (Chatterjee et al., 2013). On the one hand, a hypothesis is logically sufficient to be supported when there is are one or more pieces of confirming empirical evidence and there is no disconfirming empirical evidence; on the other hand, a hypothesis is logically sufficient to be refuted when there are one or more instances of disconfirming empirical evidence (Zhang, 2017). Following the hypothetico-deductive logic, we adopted a ✓ mark to display a coding that provides confirming empirical evidence for the hypothesis and an O mark to display a coding that offers disconfirming empirical evidence for the hypothesis. Thus, a hypothesis woul be supported when there were codes for at least one of the cells marked and no codes for any of the cells marked O. On the contrary, a hypothesis would be refuted when there were codes for one or more cells marked O.

## 6 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

This study investigated MP COL. Guided by Chatterjee and Davison (2021), we positioned our article by prob lematizing the core assumption underlying existing MP adoption and use research and presenting an alternative set of contextual assumptions to investigate MP COL. Through the problematization methodology (Alvesson & Sandberg 2011), we developed a contextualized network effect model of MP COL. Specifically, we examined how (1) contextua network effects—that is network structures, technology complementarities and institutional mechanisms—and (2) con textual network types—that is, consumer- and service-oriented networks—influence MP COL. We then used a mixed methods approach comprising both quantitative and qualitative data to validate the contextualized model. From th integrative empirical results (see Appendix E), we can highlight several key findings. First, network size, network cen trality, network capability, platform–application complementarity, application–service complementarity, service– strategy complementarity, general institutional structure, general structural assurance, local institutional structure and local structure assurance were key determinants of PEB of MP use, which facilitated MP COL. Second, network size, network centrality, network capability, platform–application complementarity, application–service complementarity service–strategy complementarity, local institutional structure and local structure assurance were key determinants o switching costs of MP use, which increased MP COL. Third, network centrality, general structural assurance, loca institutional structure and local structure assurance exerted stronger influences on MP COL for a consumer-oriented network, whereas platform–application complementarity and application–service complementarity played stronger roles in MP COL for a service-oriented network. These empirical findings confirmed the direct, indirect and negativ network effects in the implementation of MP applications. Meanwhile, different network effects exerted different influences in formulating COL in different types of MP technology networks.

## 6.1 | Theoretical contributions

We used Corley and Gioia's (2011) framework to illustrate the theoretical contributions of our study (see Table 3). This framework has two dimensions: originality (incremental or revelatory) and utility (scientific or practical). The jux taposition of the two dimensions provides four criteria for evaluating theoretical contributions: (1) incrementa originality—a new theoretical insight progressively advancing our understanding of a field with minor, marginal or even trivial improvements; (2) revelatory originality—a new theoretical insight profoundly advancing our understanding of field with novel, insightful or even interesting findings; (3) scientific utility—a new theoretical insight improving the conceptual rigour of a theory and/or enhancing its potential to be operationalized and tested; and (4) practical utility— a new theoretical insight allowing a theory to be directly applied to the problems faced by practitioners.

## 6.1.1 | Incremental originality and scientific utility (Type I)

This study advances network effect theory by proposing contextual direct, indirect and negative network effects network structures, technology complementarities and institutional mechanisms. We view the antecedent contextualization as a Type I contribution because it improves the conceptual rigour of network effect theory in an incremental way (Corley & Gioia, 2011). Although IS studies have applied network effect theory to examine innovative IT adoption and use, most have conceptualized network effects using generic factors, such as network size and perceived complementarity (Lin & Bhattacherjee, 2008; Zhao & Lu, 2012). Our work identified network structure, technology complementarity and institutional mechanism as the core concepts of network effect theory. Specifically we contextually decompose network structure into network size, network centrality and network capability; technol ogy complementarity into platform–application complementarity, application–service complementarity and service– strategy complementarity; and institutional mechanisms into general institutional structure, general structural assur ance, local institutional structure and local structural assurance. These contextual conceptualizations progressivel enhance the potential of network effect theory to be operationalized and tested (Chatterjee, Moody, et al., 2021 Chatterjee, Sarker, et al., 2021; Rivard, 2014; Weber, 2012).

## 6.1.2 | Revelatory originality and scientific utility (Type II)

This study extends network effect theory by validating network type as a contextual boundary condition of network effect theory. We proposed the boundary condition contextualization as a Type II contribution because it profoundl advances network effect theory and provides insightful findings (Corley & Gioia, 2011). From the two-sided network perspective, MP technology networks are either consumer-oriented, having developed from a large number of potential consumers, or service-oriented, having originated from a large variety of complementary services (Song et al., 2018). However, few studies have examined the moderating effect of the network type. Our results show that network centrality, general structural assurance, local institutional structure and local structure assurance exerted a stronger influence on MP COL for a consumer- than for a service-oriented network, whereas platform–application complementarity and application–service complementarity played stronger roles in MP COL for a service- than for a consumer-oriented network. This interesting contextual boundary condition profoundly advances network effec theory by directing researchers' attention to contextual factors as moderators of relationships in the theory (Hong et al., 2014; Johns, 2006, 2017).

## 6.1.3 | Incremental originality and practical utility (Type III)

This study enriches the MP adoption and use literature by shifting the research focus from generic motives to contextual antecedents of MP COL. We also frame the antecedent contextualization as a Type III contribution becaus it can be directly applied to the problems that MP practitioners face in MP application implementation (Corley & Gioia, 2011). Among the previously examined antecedents, the majority have pertained to generic motives such a perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use (Kapoor et al., 2015; Khalilzadeh et al., 2017). Some IS researcher have argued that generic factors cannot provide specific guidance to direct the design and practice of innovative MP adoption and use (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Wiener et al., 2019). Our work problematizes the assumption tha MP adoption and use result from generic factors and present an alternative set of assumptions that contextual MP technology network factors motivate the loyal use of MP applications. Our empirical findings inform MP practi tioners on how to use contextual network effects to maintain a loyal consumer base in MP implementation.

## 6.1.4 | Revelatory originality and practical utility (Type IV)

This study contributes to the MP adoption and use literature by examining the impact of the network type on MP COL. We also deem the boundary condition contextualization as a Type IV contribution because it enhances the practical utility of network effect theory in guiding the design of MP technology networks in the MP applicatio implementation (Corley & Gioia, 2011). In the global MP market, the two-sided network strategy has been commonly used by MP practitioners to develop their own MP technology networks (Lee et al., 2019; Mallat, 2007; Qasim & Abu-Shanab, 2016). Our empirical findings inform MP practitioners on how to strategically allocate their network building resources for retaining a loyal consumer according to the type of the MP technology network.

## 6.2 | Practical contributions

The global MP market is experiencing rapid technological evolution and intense competition (Wang et al., 2019). A variety of providers have released MP applications, such as WeChat Wallet, Alipay Wallet, PayPal, Apple Pay Android Pay and Samsung Pay (Cao et al., 2018). The variety of MP applications undermines the base of loyal con sumers and results in frequent switching behaviour. In such a dynamic and competitive market, building COL can help MP practitioners gain a competitive advantage in MP implementation. Based on our empirical findings, we recommend harnessing the efficacy of contextual network effects and contextual network types to effectively formu late COL in MP applications.

First, MP practitioners should pay attention to direct, indirect and negative network effects when developing their MP technology networks. For direct network effects, MP practitioners can improve network structures b (1) increasing the size of the consumer base, (2) encouraging existing consumers to frequently transact with their socially important references and (3) offering superior and high-guality payment services (Lin & Bhattacheriee 2008). For indirect network effects, MP practitioners should facilitate technology complementarities by (1) cooperating with a large variety of popular third-party transaction platforms, such as eBay, Amazon, Starbuck and Walmart; (2) providing a large variety of frequently used first-party payment services, such as peer-to-peer money transfer, credit card repayments and utility bill payments; and (3) offering a large variety of supporting-servic strategies, such as searching, ordering, fingerprint identification and authentication (Xu et al., 2010, 2014). To allevi ate negative network effects, MP practitioners can establish application-specific institutional mechanisms to protect consumers against potential transaction risks involved in using MP applications. Typical institutional safeguards include rating and feedback technologies, user profile technologies, escrow services, insurance guarantees and los compensation policies (Guo et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2009). Furthermore, MP practitioners should cooperate with governments and industry regulators to build a more effective application-independent institutiona infrastructure for the entire MP market

Second, MP practitioners should strategically allocate their network-building resources according to the type of MP technology network. Our empirical results show that network centrality, general structural assurance, loca institutional structure and local structure assurance exerted stronger influences on MP COL for a consumer- tha for a service-oriented network, whereas platform–application complementarity and application–service complementarity played stronger roles in MP COL for a service- than for a consumer-oriented network. Therefore, MP practitioners should conduct initial board meetings to assess the type of their MP technology network. If they operate a consumer-oriented network affiliated with social communication platforms, they may wish to strategi cally target facilitating network centrality, general structural assurance, local institutional structure and local structure assurance. Alternatively, if they operate a service-oriented network developed from business transaction platforms, they should commit more resources to reinforce platform–application complementarity and application–service complementarity.

## 6.3 | Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations that offer avenues for future research. First, our work adopted the contextualizatio approach as a problematization methodology to challenge the core assumption underlying existing MP adoption and use research. Specifically, we used Hong et al.'s (2014) single-context theory contextualization approach to contextu alize established theories by incorporating contextual factors as antecedents of core variables or moderators of rela tionships in a single context. Future research should use the cross-context theory contextualization approach to problematize the MP adoption and use research by replicating theoretical models in different contexts. This contex tualization approach will allow researchers to develop context-contingent theories by conducting meta-analyse (Hong et al., 2014). For example, researchers can perform a meta-analysis of MP adoption and use research and iden tify whether user-related factors (e.g., digital divide), technology-related factors (e.g., IT affordances) and poly contextual factors (e.g., culture) are key contextual contingency factors across studies. The integration of single- and cross-context theory contextualization helps to achieve a balance between contextual specificity and generality in I research (Davison, 2021; Davison & Martinsons, 2016).

Second, another direction of problematizing the MP adoption and use research is to develop a nonlinear contex tualized network effect model of MP COL. According to Chatterjee and Davison (2021), the IT adoption and use lit erature has implicitly assumed and investigated only linear relationships between the constructs of interest. Future research should problematize the linear relationship assumption and introduce nonlinear models of MP adoption and use (Chatterjee et al., 2015; Chatterjee, Moody, et al., 2021; Chatterjee, Sarker, et al., 2021). For example, the effect of network size on PEB of MP use may follow an inverted U-shaped curve as the size of the consumer base increases. That is, the increased size of a MP technology network increases the possibility of a negative network effect that can offset the network benefits when the network size exceeds its optimal level. In addition, application– service complementarity may have an inverted U-shaped effect on PEB of MP use because consumers are likely to experience overwhelmed when processing a MP application with thousands of first-party payment services in the application interface. These nonlinear relationships present an opportunity for future research to reconsider and problematize research on MP adoption and use.

## 6.4 | Conclusions

The salient contextual characteristics of MP applications call for a reconsideration and problematization of researc on MP adoption and use. Following this call, this study problematizes MP adoption and use research by adopting the single-context theory contextualization approach and developing a contextualized network effect model of MP COL Congruent with network effect theory, we identified three contextual network effects—that is network structures technology complementarities and institutional mechanisms—and two contextual network types—that is consumer and service-oriented networks—in the formation of MP COL. Using a mixed-methods design, we found that contextual network effects and contextual network types determined MP COL through reinforcing the PEB and switchin costs of MP use. We believe that the results have significant implications for research on network effects and pro vide guidance for building COL in MP applications through network effects.
