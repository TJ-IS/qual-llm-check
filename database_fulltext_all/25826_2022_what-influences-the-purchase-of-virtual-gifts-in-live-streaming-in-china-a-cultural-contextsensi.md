# What influences the purchase of virtual gifts in live streaming in China? A cultural context-sensitive model

Zhengzhi Guan | Fangfang Hou | Boying Li | Chee Wei Phang | Alain Yee-Loong Chong

Nottingham University Business School China, The University of Nottingham Ningbo China, Ningbo, China

Alain Yee-Loong Chong, Department of Entrepreneurship, Marketing and Management System, Nottingham University Business School China, The University of Nottingham Ningbo China, 199 Taikang East Road, Ningbo 315100, China. Email: alain.chong@nottingham.edu.cn

## Abstract

China is one of the largest and fastest-growing markets for live streaming, and the purchase of virtual gifts in live streaming is the core for streamers and live streaming platforms in China to survive and thrive. Compared to western countries, live streaming in China highlights the lively social atmosphere and heated social interactions among streamers and viewers. This study develops a cultural context-sensitive model that contextualises the purchase of virtual gifts in live streaming in China. Specifically, we focus on the viewer's social experience and the social atmosphere in live streaming which have received limited attention yet. We introduce viewers' social perceptions with regard to the streamer and other viewers (ie, perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd) and show how such social perceptions contribute to the development of flow experience, which subsequently leads to purchase intention. This study also reveals how such social perceptions can be shaped by the contextual setting consisting of the IT-related factors of live streaming (ie, responsiveness, two-way communication, social presence, and self-presentation) and the cultural characteristics of China (ie, social orientation and harmony). Our research offers both theoretical guidance for practitioners into cultivating viewers' purchase of virtual gifts in China's live streaming.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Recent advancements in information technology (IT) have revolutionised how people obtain information and seek entertainment. A salient manifestation of IT advancements may be seen in social media, which allows anyone with an internet connection to consume and produce content (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010). More recently, in line with the grassroots and interactive nature of the development of social media, a relatively novel form of social media known as live streaming has gained popularity and is fast becoming one of the most lucrative markets for internet platform providers. The popularity of live streaming is particularly pertinent in China, which hosts the world's largest popula tion of internet users (Q. Chen, 2016; Deloitte, 2017; Yu, 2017; J. Zhao et al., 2017). According to the report of the China Internet Network Information Center (CNNIC), the number of live streaming users in China reached 617 millio in 2020, making up 62.4% of the Chinese internet population (CNNIC, 2021). The market size of live streaming in China reached USD 10.5 billion in 2019 (Thomala, 2020). Douyu.com, a company that focuses on providing live streaming in China, was publicly listed on Nasdaq in July 2019. Given that the live streaming platform market in China is among the largest globally, we chose live streaming in China as the context of our research.

Compared with other forms of social media, live streaming is unique because it can create a live collectiv experience by involving viewers and the streamer, revolving around the streamer's real-time performance A streamer, an ordinary user hosting a live performance session, may decide what to perform during the session, such as sharing personal life experiences, singing, dancing, or chatting with the viewers around particular topics of interest. During the engagement between the streamer and viewers in the live stream session, viewers may rewar the streamer in appreciation of their performance and efforts. This is usually achieved by purchasing virtual gifts that can be converted into monetary value for the streamer. For example, in Douyu.com, viewers may purchase virtua gifts such as a virtual yacht, and reward streamers during a live stream session. In this way, streamers and th platform share the profits from the viewers' purchases. The purchase of virtual gifts can be perceived as a nove monetization model for live streaming. It is the dominant source of revenue for live streaming in China (Xiang, 2016) By contrast, live streaming platforms in Western countries rely more on traditional monetization models (eg subscriptions and advertisements) but have recently started to explore the business potential of virtual gifts. Interestingly, like tipping for busking or street performances, the purchase of virtual gifts in live streaming is voluntary, and viewers can still enjoy the live content without purchasing anything (Hou, Guan, Li, & Chong, 2021; Wan, Lu, Wang, & Zhao, 2017). The purchase of virtual gifts thus has a hybrid nature. On the one hand, the viewe can be seen as a customer who spends money to obtain a desirable live performance from the streamer; on the othe hand, the viewer can be seen as a fan or a friend who shows personal support and appreciation to the streamer b voluntarily purchasing virtual gifts (Wan et al., 2017; X. Zhang, Xiang, & Hao, 2019). Here, live streaming serves more like the ground on which viewers' social experiences are cultivated, which stimulates the purchase of virtual gifts (R. Li, Lu, Ma, & Wang, 2021). This purchases virtual gifts different from the purchase of products in e-commerceorientated live streaming. In the latter, the purpose of the purchase is to make transactions for products, whereas the primary function of live streaming is for product demonstration, which alleviates customers' uncertainties i making transactions (Cenfetelli & Benbasat, 2019). Considering the uniqueness of the emerging phenomenon of purchasing virtual gifts in China, the limited understanding of such behaviour, and the resulting new monetization model in China, this study aims to investigate what drives live streaming viewers to purchase virtual gifts in China This study also intends to offer insights into monetizing live streaming platforms in general and highlight some specific factors characterised by the context of China.

Given the highly social culture of China, viewers' experiences and behaviours are closely related to their perceptions of their social engagement in the live stream session (C. C. Chen & Lin, 2018; Lin & Lu, 2017; X. Zhang et al., 2019; J. Zhou, Zhou, Ding, & Wang, 2019). Studies showed that viewers in China go beyond mere entertaining performances to emphasise the enjoyment derived from having the live interaction with the streamer and other viewers (C. Chan, 2016). Lin and Lu (2017) determined that in China people watch live streaming to satisfy their social needs for digital companionship. Viewers in China tend to seek close social connections with others through the real-time interaction enabled by live streaming (C. Chan, 2016; Hilvert-Bruce, Neill, Sjöblom, & Hamari, 2018 Yang, 2017; X. Zhang et al., 2019; J. Zhou et al., 2019). Hence, we believe that viewers' behaviour during live stream ing can be strongly associated with their collective live social experience. To advance our understanding of the role played by viewers' social experience in driving the purchase of virtual gifts in a social-oriented culture like that in China, we have two research objectives in this study. (a) We introduce viewers' social perceptions concerning the streamer and other viewers and investigate how they influence viewers' flow experience, which is their state of dee involvement with full enjoyment. This may, in turn, promote their purchasing of virtual gifts. (b) We investigate how such social perceptions can be fostered in the contextual environment of live streaming in China.

Specifically, informed by extant literature that notes a high emphasis of Chinese viewers in interacting with the streamer and other viewers in live streaming, we contextualise our research in China by considering viewers perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd as key drivers of creating viewers' flow experience, the desired experience motivating viewers to purchase virtual gifts in a live stream session. On the one hand, viewers' social experiences are essentially linked to the streamer who hosts the ses sion and delivers the performance. On the other hand, viewers will start seeing other viewers participating when entering a live stream session; thus, how they perceive other participants is likely to influence their experience and behaviour as well. Additionally, to comprehensively reveal the salient role of the contextualised external set ting in shaping viewers' social perceptions, we incorporate IT-related factors to capture the characteristics of the immediate technology-mediated environment of live streaming and cultural characteristics to capture th broader social setting of live streaming in China (Choi & Kim, 2004; Finneran & Zhang, 2003; B. Li, Hou, Guan, & Chong, 2018; Obada, 2013).

This study develops a cultural context-sensitive model that contextualises the purchase of virtual gifts within liv streaming in China. It makes the following contributions. First, it enriches the current literature by highlighting the importance of viewers' social perceptions (ie, perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd) in affecting their behaviour in live streaming. Second, it establishes a theoretical link between socia perceptions and flow experience and confirms social perceptions as novel antecedents of flow experience. Third, it extends the understanding of the cultivation of social perceptions by examining the crucial roles of IT-related factor (ie, responsiveness, two-way communication, social presence, and self-presentation) and cultural characteristics (ie social orientation and harmony).

Given the relatively novel nature of live streaming, we start by providing a discussion on live streaming and what could be unique about it in the context of China. We then introduce the extant literature on live streaming viewers behaviour, flow theory, and social perceptions, as well as IT-related factors and cultural characteristics that guide ou research model and hypothesis development. We follow this by describing the methodology we employed in thi study. We also discuss empirical findings and how they contribute to theory and inform practices. Finally, we con clude by discussing the limitations of this study and offers considerations for future studies.

## 2 | LIVE STREAMING IN CHINA

Live streaming allows ordinary users (called ‘streamers’) to broadcast self-produced content to any viewer online with minimal required resources, that is, only a webcam, internet connection, and live streaming software are needed. Yet, in terms of the form and mechanism of how the contents are produced and consumed, it is unique and different from other related forms of social media in two aspects. First, while live streaming involves video content as with conventional video sharing platforms, in the latter the contents are pre-recorded and passively consumed, unlike live streaming videos that are real-time based active consumption. The liveness nature, enabled by th higher internet connection speed today, allows viewers to more closely interact with the streamer compared to the case of pre-recorded videos. Previous research on live performances notes that the ability to see the per former live is what attracts people to such performances (Reason, 2006; Shrader, 2015). This can be attributed to a heightened, intense, and emotive environment arising from the connection it provides for the audience with the performer (Shrader, 2015). Furthermore, in the live streaming context, viewers are able to have reciprocal communication with the performer (or streamer), allowing viewers to feedback to the streamers and potentially influenc their performance. Second, in a live stream session, viewers are temporarily organised around the streamer, shar ing a common interest at the time of the session. Viewers not only can interact with the streamer in real-time but they may also interact with other viewers easily with the help of IT features such as instant messaging. Being in the digital crowd, each viewer is no longer a lonely audience but is able to interact with other viewers in real-time (Lin & Lu, 2017). The psychological closeness with other audience may promote a contagion of emotions as the audience jointly participate in the session and can observe how others react to the performance (Bennett, 1990) The session is not pre-determined or pre-recorded but is co-shaped based on the interactive events delivered organised, and managed by the streamer and viewers together, thus creating a media world characterised by col lective social experience.

The unique characteristics have led to increasing popularity of live streaming worldwide. Given the huge traffic, live streaming in China has developed a new model of monetization, that is, the purchase of virtual gift (X. Zhang et al., 2019). Virtual gifts, such as virtual flowers and rockets, are purchased by viewers for the streamer to show their support and appreciation. Viewers' purchase of virtual gifts in live streaming has con tributed to over 80% of the total live streaming revenue in China with the rest from advertising and other services (Dai, 2019). A top streamer can earn millions of dollars every year because of the virtual gifts sent by viewers (J. Chan, 2019). In 2018, the total value of paid virtual gifts in China amounted to RMB 54.8 billion (around USD 8 billion) (Sohu, 2019). The monetization model of virtual gifts is widely adopted by live streaming platforms in China, while Western live streaming platforms such as Twitch mostly rely on advertising an subscriptions as their primary revenue sources (Dai, 2019). Given the success of the virtual gift model in the East, Western platforms have also started to catch on and incorporated virtual gifts, such as Live.me and Twitch (Q. Chen, 2017; Sway, 2017).

Apart from virtual gifts, compared to Western counterparties, live streaming platforms in China also strive to create a lively social atmosphere for participants (see Figure 1 and Table 1). Research has shown that viewers in China are keen to seek social engagement and digital companionship in live streaming (Hilvert Bruce et al., 2018; Lin & Lu, 2017). Specifically, viewers look for a close social connection with the streame through the ongoing live chat with the streamer and may even value it more than the streamer's performanc (Z. Lu, Xia, Heo, & Wigdor, 2018; Wan et al., 2017; Yang, 2017; X. Zhang et al., 2019). This is in line with what Couldry (2004) notes, … the experience of liveness is not limited to specific performer–audience interactions but to a sense of always being connected to other people, of continuous, technologically mediated co presence with others known and unknown’ (p. 357). In live streaming, interacting with other viewers during the live stream session provides viewers with chances for socialising with peers. They influence and ar influenced by peer viewers in the live stream session, which may entail the feeling of being accompanied by other viewers who watch the streamer's live performance together (Lin & Lu, 2017; Ruether, 2020; Yang, 2017). Although the viewer crowd is a temporary ad-hoc group, they can work together to make the live stream session more enjoyable to all, for example, by being well-mannered in the conversations and giving encouraging words to the streamer (akin to clapping together in a live performance). The ‘hot and noisy’ ambi ence which is valued in the Chinese culture may also play a role in viewer behaviour in live streaming in Chin (Z. Lu et al., 2018).

## 3.2 | Flow theory and social perceptions

Flow theory is widely applied in the online context to explain online user's holistic compelling and enjoyable experience of deep involvement with the internet activities (Hoffman & Novak, 2009). Flow refers to a ‘state in which peo ple are so involved in an activity that nothing else seems to matter; the experience is so enjoyable that people wil continue to do it even at great cost, for the sheer sake of doing it’ (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990, p. 4)

Csikszentmihalyi (1990) proposed nine original dimensions of flow: (a) clear goals, (b) immediate feedback (e) balance between challenge and skill, (d) focused concentration, (e) loss of self-consciousness, (f) sense of control (g) time distortion, (h) merging of action and awareness, and (i) autotelic experience (also referred to as enjoyment) However, the first three dimensions (ie, clear goals, immediate feedback, and balance between challenge and skill) have been widely regarded as the antecedents of flow rather than flow dimensions (Guo et al., 2016). It is also found that the eighth dimension ‘merging of action and awareness’ is least experienced and should not be seen as a separate dimension of flow (H. Chen, 2006; Tenenbaum, Fogarty, & Jackson, 1999). Therefore, instead of utilising all of the nine dimensions of flow from Csikszentmihalyi (1990), prior studies tend to adopt different dimensions of flow depending on the specific contexts and the nature of the activities in which the users engage (Guo et al., 2016; Pelet Ettis, & Cowart, 2017; Su, Chiang, James Lee, & Chang, 2016). For entertaining digital systems, it is believed that th key attributes of flow can be effectively reflected from four key dimensions, namely immersion, perceived control, time distortion, and enjoyment (Suh, Cheung, Ahuja, & Wagner, 2017). Immersion reflects the experience of tota engagement (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). It indicates that people focus all the attentional resources on the activit at hand and tend to lose the consciousness of self (Nah, Eschenbrenner, & DeWester, 2011; Suh et al., 2017). It thus captures focused concentration and loss of self-consciousness in Csikszentmihalyi (1990)'s flow model. Perceived control refers to the user's feeling related to exercising control over the activity (Suh et al., 2017). The sense of control reduces the potential frustration and negative feeling when one is engaging in internet activities (Pelet et al., 2017). Time distortion means that the user is unaware of the time passage when immersed in the activit (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). Enjoyment not only represents one's pleasant feeling but also reflects one's intrinsic interest in the activity (Y. M. Guo & Poole, 2009). We believe these four dimensions of flow can effectively captur people's experience of flow state in the entertaining digital system of live streaming.

Flow theory suggests that one's flow state is a desired psychological state to shape people's behaviour in the contexts of human–computer interaction (HCI) and computer-mediated communication (Choi & Kim, 2004; Y. M Guo & Poole, 2009; Hoffman & Novak, 2009). There is evidence supporting the influence of flow state on driving purchase behaviour and media consumption (Choi & Kim, 2004; Hausman & Siekpe, 2009; L .Y. Huang, Hsieh, & Wu, 2014; H. Liu, Chu, Huang, & Chen, 2016). Given the important role of flow in digital contexts, substantia research efforts have been devoted to disentangling the factors contributing to the flow state. Although a large number of studies have focused on HCI design (eg, interactivity, system quality, and challenge) and user's own characteristics (eg, curiosity, knowledge, and skills) as the antecedents of flow (Ding, Hu, Verma, & Wardell, 2010; M. C Lee, 2009; Pelet et al., 2017; Su et al., 2016; van Noort, Voorveld, & van Reijmersdal, 2012; Zhou, Li, & Liu, 2010), extant flow studies have already noted the importance of social experience (Choi & Kim, 2004; L .Y. Huang et al., 2014). However, many of them have considered social experience and flow experience as two separate factors driving user online behaviour, overlooking the connections between them (C. C. Chen & Lin, 2018). Recently, some studies have started to acknowledge the significance of social factors, such as social interaction and social gratifica tion, in facilitating the development of flow-like experience (L. Y. Huang et al.; M. C. Lee, 2009; H. Liu et al., 2016). Prior literature suggests that close social relationships can elicit an enjoyable experience in the digital context by sat isfying users' social desire, stimulating their interest and hooking in their attention in the activity (Guo et al., 2016 L. Y. Huang et al.; Jiao, Gao, & Yang, 2015; M. C. Lee, 2009; Su et al., 2016). For instance, Su et al. (2016) have exam ined what cultivates the loyalty of mobile game players. They found that social interaction plays an essential role in developing flow experience which leads to mobile players' game loyalty. L. Y. Huang et al. have also validated in their study that social gratification would increase people's intention to continuously use social networking sites (SNS through enhancing online flow experience with the SNS. It thus indicates the potential of users' social experience in cultivating their flow state in the digital environment (C. C. Chang, 2013; L. Y. Huang et al.; Jiao et al., 2015; Pelet et al., 2017). However, limited research has contextualised the effects of social experience on flow development in specific digital environment, such as live streaming. Thus, further study is required

This study uses flow to reflect the viewer's overall experience in the live streaming context since viewer behav iour in this context is strongly related to the hedonic or experiential aspect of live streaming (B. Li et al., 2018)

Furthermore, given the close association of social gratification and flow experience (L. Y. Huang et al., 2014), w believe that in live streaming, viewers' social experience may contribute to their flow state in the environment. A live streaming in China creates a collective live social experience co-shaped by the streamer and the viewers, the social interaction (ie, live chat with the streamer and the viewer crowd) plays a big part in live stream sessions in China (C. Chan, 2016; also see Table 1 and Figure 1). Indeed, it has been suggested that the digital companionship from the streamer and the viewer crowd are highly valued in this context (Lin & Lu, 2017). Thus, in conjunction with the flow theory and live streaming characteristics, we expect that viewers' social perceptions about th streamer and the viewer crowd may play a role in developing the viewers' flow experience and motivating their purchase intention. With regards to the social interaction with the streamer, the viewers' sense of psychologica closeness with the streamer is expected to represent the viewers' social experience with the streamer (X. Zhang et al., 2019). It can be the very reason that viewers take action to support the streamer as a friend in a live stream session. In this study, the perceived proximity to the streamer captures the viewer's psychological closeness to th streamer. In terms of the social interaction among the viewer crowd, viewers with similar tastes or interests get together in a live stream session and mutually influence each other. This would entail a sense of being a part of the ad-hoc virtual group (C.-W. Chen & Lin, 2014; Y. Lu, Wang, Zhao, Zhang, & Chau, 2012). It has been argued that the sense of belonging can explain the behavioural commitment and immersive experience in a virtual com munity (C.-W. Chen & Lin, 2014; Koh & Kim, 2003). Hence, in this study, a sense of belonging to the viewer crowd is introduced to indicate viewers' social experience with the interaction with the viewer crowd

## 3.3 | IT-related factors and cultural characteristics

In this study, the live streaming environment can be seen as a socio-technical system where the development of use experience can be informed by technical and social settings (Chiang, Yang, Koo, & Liao, 2020; Wan et al., 2017). O one hand, viewers can interact with the streamer and the viewer crowd channelled by the live streaming technologi cal setting (Chiang et al., 2020; Morgan-Thomas, Dessart, & Veloutsou, 2020); on the other hand, the heightened social interaction and strong social atmosphere can be the manifestation of the cultural characteristics of the Chines viewers (Hou et al., 2021; Z. Lu et al., 2018; X. Zhang et al., 2019; Q. Zhao, Chen, Cheng, & Wang, 2018; Zhou et al., 2019). Based on it, we believe the viewer's social experience can be facilitated by the technology-mediated liv streaming environment and also shaped by the cultural atmosphere rooted in China (H. Kim, Shin, & Lee, 2015 Kralisch, Eisend, & Berendt, 2005; I. Lee, Choi, Kim, & Hong, 2007). Therefore, as indicated by I. Lee et al. (2007), the IT-related factors of live streaming and the cultural characteristics of China are investigated as the critical contextua setting of the live streaming system in shaping viewers' social perceptions in live streaming in China.

## 3.3.1 | IT-related factors

The virtual world of live streaming consists of a set of technological artefacts which are the foundation and medium fo the interaction and communication among viewers and streamers in live streaming. It is suggested that technologies and social systems evolve together (Ma & Agarwal, 2007). For example, it is pointed out that social technology that supports real-time conversation between individuals and groups allows users to establish new social relationships (Boyd, 2005). In Information Systems (IS) literature, a number of prior studies have shown that user experience of technological feature can influence the social outcomes of interaction in the environment (Ma & Agarwal, 2007; Ou, Pavlou, & Davison, 2014; H. Zhang, Lu, Gupta, & Zhao, 2014). In live streaming, as viewers' interactions are enabled and facilitated by the techno logical environment of live streaming, we expect specific technological features of live streaming, which we term a IT-related factors in this study, to play important roles in their social perceptions of the streamer and the viewer group (ie, perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd).

## 3.3.2 | Cultural characteristics

Culture can be defined as the collective programming of the mind that distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another’ (Hofstede, 1980, p. 260). In the IS literature, people's perceptions related to technology-mediated interactions have been found to be influenced by cultural factors (Avgerou & Li, 2013 Leidner & Kayworth, 2006; Lowry, Cao, & Everard, 2011; Tan, Sutanto, Phang, & Gasimov, 2014). Although the cul tural difference is often discussed at the national level, it is suggested that in the context of the individual-based implementation of the digital system, the cultural trait at the individual level is of more relevance to cultural variation (McCoy, Galletta, & King, 2005). This is because, in the context of information system for personal use, the individua difference within the same national culture is naturally highlighted (I. Lee et al., 2007). Therefore, in this study, w introduce cultural characteristics at the individual level to capture the cultural setting of the context of live streaming in China.

Hofstede's Five Dimensions have been widely accepted. There is a cultural bias when discussing individual cultura traits (F. M. Cheung et al., 2013). It is suggested that the culturally relevant dimensions as individual cultural trait should be incorporated to capture the distinctive cultural characteristics of Chinese people (F. M. Cheung, Fan, & To, 2009). Hence, in this study, we refer to the cultural traits from the personality of Chinese people so as to select the appropriate cultural characteristics that are relevant to the Chinese people's social experience (F. M. Cheung, Kwong, & Zhang, 2003). According to the personality traits of the Chinese people, it is found that the Chinese people are fond of social inclusion in general and also value the collective peace in groups (F. M. Cheung et al.; F. M. Cheung et al., 2009). Thus, viewers in China are expected to be more susceptible to the influence of social bonds and group influence (Lin & Lu, 2017; Z. Lu et al., 2018; Wan et al., 2017). Based on a review of the cultural characteristics inves tigated in the literature in IS, such as Hofstede's five cultural dimensions and high-low context culture (Y. W. Chang, Hsu, Shiau, & Tsai, 2015; Hoehle, Zhang, & Venkatesh, 2015; Q. Huang, Davison, & Gu, 2011; K. H. Lim, Leung, Sia, & Lee Matthew, 2004; Lowry et al., 2011; Rai, Maruping, & Venkatesh, 2009; Richardson & Smith, 2007; Srite & Karahanna, 2006; Würtz, 2006; Xu, Xu, & Li, 2016; Yoon, 2009; Young, Kuo, & Myers, 2012; D. Zhang, Lowry, Zhou, & Fu, 2007) and the Chinese personality traits (Cai, Cohen, & Tribe, 2019; F. M. Cheung et al., 1996, 2009 Filieri & Lin, 2017; He, Kukar-Kinney, & Ridgway, 2018; Hsiao, 2003; Q. Huang et al., 2011; Lin, Luo, Cheng, & Li, 2019; J. Liu, Nandhakumar, & Zachariadis, 2018; Ou et al., 2014; Park & Luo, 2001; Tan et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2016;

Yan, Wang, Chen, & Zhang, 2016; Young et al., 2012), we introduce social orientation and harmony as two key cultural characteristics to reflect the cultural setting of live streaming in China that may shape the viewer's socia perceptions.

Social orientation refers to the extent to which people are involved with each other, and it highlights people's tendency to value social connections and social acceptance from others (D. Kim, Pan, & Park, 1998; Tan et al., 2014) Social orientation is closely related to interpersonal communication and is found influential on how individuals in this culture form perceptions towards others during the interaction (Tan et al., 2014). In a strong social-oriented society such as China, people value intimate inter-relationships and the communications between people tend to be mor personal; in contrast, in a less social-oriented society such as the United States, people are highly individualised and the communications between people are relatively more direct and non-personal. It is argued that social orientation also reflects the Chinese people's inclination to pay special attention to others' views on themselves. They care about what others think of them, whether they are respected by peers and whether they are accepted by the group (F. M. Cheung et al., 2013). We believe that social orientation captures the Chinese culture that values the socia bond and social acceptance from others. It represents the social desire to engage with others. It is relevant to ou context which highlights social experience in the environment (F. M. Cheung et al., 2009). Thus, this study adopt social orientation instead of a related but broader concept called collectivism to emphasise our focus on people's ori entation in valuing other people's views on themselves during interactions.

Harmony is indicated as a China-specific cultural factor (F. M. Cheung et al., 2003) and has been employed to explain the Chinese behaviours that involve interpersonal interactions (F. M. Cheung et al., 2009). Harmony, derived from the Chinese philosopher Confucius (551–479 BC), reflects one's inner peace of mind, contentment, as well as interpersonal harmony (F. M. Cheung et al., 1996, p. 185). It is highly emphasised in the social relationships of th Chinese people and considered as virtue in the Chinese culture (F. M. Cheung et al., 2003). It reflects the Chines mindset of maintaining a peaceful and favourable atmosphere for social interaction. We consider harmony to be more relevant to our study's context compared to other cultural characteristics highlighted to be salient in the context of China, such as guanxi and face. The former serves as the foundation for a peaceful and friendly social interac tion atmosphere in China (Y. B. Zhang, Lin, Nonaka, & Beom, 2005). The latter two may be more relevant for instrumental-based interactions and business transactions rather than social engagement, such as in the contexts of internet shopping (He et al., 2018; Ou et al., 2014), knowledge orchestration of digital entrepreneurs (J. Liu et al., 2018), and knowledge sharing in organisations (Q. Huang et al., 2011). We believe that harmony represents the Chinese mindset of a peaceful atmosphere and conflict avoidance. It is relevant to our context as the Chines viewers tend to keep a friendly social atmosphere where social perceptions are developed. Hence, harmony is also introduced in this study to explain the formation of viewers' perceptions.

## 4 | HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

## 4.1 | Flow and purchase of virtual gifts

Prior studies have stressed that the flow state can encourage users' purchase behaviour (Y. M. Guo & Poole, 2009; Hausman & Siekpe, 2009; E. Huang, 2012). As suggested by Agarwal and Karahanna (2000), flow can be perceived as a self-reinforcing intrinsic motivation which not only reflects one's joy feeling but also indicates the interest in the activity. It can be understood that the interest in the activity stimulated by flow state would encourage the person to commit to corresponding behaviours to enhance the pleasant feeling of flow experience, even at the expense of some forms of high cost (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990). In the context of live streaming, viewers who watch live streaming may experience different levels of flow (C. C. Chen & Lin, 2018). The purchase of virtual gifts can be perceived as the behavioural commitment in exchange for the streamers' compelling performance. Motivated by the flow experi ence, viewers' interest in the live stream session may be increased and they would be self-encouraged to purchas virtual gifts so as to make the live stream session more enjoyable to enhance their pleasant feeling (Pelet et al., 2017). Hence, it can be expected that viewers in the flow state are more likely to purchase virtual gifts. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1. Flow is positively associated with the purchase of virtual gifts.

## 4.2 | Social perceptions on streamer and viewer crowd

## 4.2.1 | Perceived proximity to the streamer

In a live stream session, the streamer plays an important role by providing the performance and stimulating the socia activities in the session. As an important part of viewers' experience with the social interaction in a live stream session, viewers' social perception of the streamer is likely a core factor influencing their media enjoyment and behaviour.

The perceived proximity, in general, captures an individual's perceived closeness with the other party. It is related to one's perception of how close the other party is psychologically (Chae, 2016; Wilson, O'Leary, Metiu, & Jett, 2008). On one hand, it captures one's mental assessment of the distance of another individual; on the othe hand, it reflects one's affective feeling towards this individual. The feeling of being closer to a person is likely to mak the person perceived to be more ‘real’ in the virtual environment (Gunawardena & Zittle, 1997). In live streaming perceived proximity to the streamer refers to a viewer's perceived relational closeness between her or himself and the streamer. It can be expected that when viewers are watching a live stream session, they get the chance to famil iarise with the streamer through the streamer's sharing or performance and the real-time interaction with th streamer. Viewers may develop a close feeling with the streamer which satisfies their social need for interpersona relationships. Such social gratification itself can become the enjoyment experience and felicitate viewers' interest to the streamer (L. Y. Huang et al., 2014), making them more willing to be involved in the session. This also increase the opportunities to pay closer attention to the streamer, which makes viewers susceptible to the streamer and eas ily attracted by the streamer's session. Hence, perceived proximity to the streamer may promote a flow experience in the live stream session. In addition to its potential effects on flow experience, the psychological closeness to the streamer also increases the chance that the viewers will treat the streamer as their friend (R. Li et al., 2021; J. S. Lim, Choe, Zhang, & Noh, 2020). Given that the purchase of virtual gifts is an important form of showing appreciation and support to the streamer, a viewer may have a strong willingness to make their friend-like relational partner fee supported by purchasing virtual gifts (X. Zhang et al., 2019). The preceding discussions lead us to the following hypotheses:

H2a. Perceived proximity to the streamer is positively associated with flow.

H2b. Perceived proximity to the streamer is positively associated with purchase of virtual gifts.

## 4.2.2 | Sense of belonging to the viewer crowd

Apart from the streamer, viewers may also feel connected to the peer viewers and develop a sense of belonging to the viewer crowd, which encourages active participation in group activities in the live stream session (Brown, 2015 Green, 2008). Sense of belonging generally refers to the experience of personal involvement in a system or environ ment so that persons feel themselves to be an integral part of that system or environment (Hagerty, Lynch-Sauer Patusky, Bouwsema, & Collier, 1992). Previous research has shown that a sense of community not only appears in a long-term community but also emerges in temporary groups (Ammeter & Dukerich, 2002; Terrion & Ashforth, 2002). The sense of close feeling towards the group tends to draw an individual member's attention to the group activities, which makes the member more likely to immerse oneself in the group activities and make further community commitment (Koh & Kim, 2003; Ridings & Gefen, 2010). Furthermore, the gratification from the sense of belonging may also become the source of joy encouraging the deep engagement (L. Y. Huang et al., 2014).

In the context of live streaming, viewers who watch the same live stream session tend to have similar tastes and interests. By interacting with such peers, viewers may feel more connected with the viewer crowd. With the live and close interactions with other viewers, an important feeling a viewer may develop is the sense of belonging to the viewer crowd (ie, being an integral part of the viewer crowd watching the same live stream session and sharing simi lar interests). When a viewer has a sense of belonging to the viewer crowd, she or he may more likely develop th fun and joy experienced from the social activities with other peer viewers, and therefore become more willing to concentrate in the live stream session. Similarly, being part of the group gives a viewer a feeling of control over th activities in the live stream session because the viewer can lay influence on others and they can become even more influential as a group (Koh & Kim, 2003), that is, in controlling how the collective live experience develops. The perceived control may increase as the sense of belonging grows. Hence, it can be expected that a sense of belonging to the viewer crowd may lead to a flow experience.

In a similar vein, viewers who have a sense of belonging to the viewer crowd are more likely to engage in socia activities in the live stream session. In a live stream session, sending virtual gifts to the streamer can be seen as an effective way to engage with the live stream session and to contribute to the viewer crowd because virtual gifts can warm up the atmosphere in the live stream session and become an incentive to encourage streamer to generat more interesting content (X. Zhang et al., 2019). This is also in line with previous research that suggests a sense of belonging may enhance people's willingness to engage and contribute more in activities associated with the grou (Y. Lu et al., 2012). In this light, it can be expected that a sense of belonging to the viewer crowd may encourag viewers to purchase virtual gifts. Therefore, we hypothesize

H3a. Sense of belonging to the viewer crowd is positively associated with flow.

H3b. Sense of belonging to the viewer crowd is positively associated with the purchase of virtual gifts.

## 4.3 | IT-related factors and social perceptions

## 4.3.1 | Responsiveness

Responsiveness reflects how a communication medium enables individuals to respond in a synchronic way and contributes to a high level of interactivity (Avidar, 2013). Given the live nature, live streaming affords a high level of responsiveness for interactions between the participants. Prior literature shows that synchronised communication in the online context contributes to one's favourable attitude and relationship development (Ou et al., 2014). We thu expect that responsiveness can lead to the viewer's positive social perceptions in live streaming. First, the liv streaming environment enables the streamer to reply to viewers promptly through the live video session. Instan communication improves communication efficiency and helps viewers achieve a smooth conversation with th streamer. Thus, viewers may feel interconnected with the streamer, leading to a sense of interpersonal closeness (Suntornpithug & Khamalah, 2010). Therefore, vis-à-vis non-live media whereby users have to wait for a long tim to obtain feedback, live streaming's responsiveness feature allows viewers to feel that the streamer is proximat to them.

Second, viewers' ability to engage in instant communication with other viewers enables them to obtain information from peers easily and quickly. When viewers get instant replies from the viewer crowd, it helps create the feeling that they are receiving attention and recognition from peers, and that their participation efforts are worth while (Kuo & Feng, 2013). It makes them more willing to share their opinions and experiences with other viewers in the live stream session, which enhances their engagement in the community (E. S. T. Wang, Chen, & Tsai, 2012). In light of this, they are more likely to consider themselves as a part of the viewer crowd. It may thus cultivate viewer's sense of belonging to the viewer crowd. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H4a. Responsiveness is positively associated with perceived proximity to the streamer.

H4b. Responsiveness is positively associated with sense of belonging to the viewer crowd.

## 4.3.2 | Two-way communication

Two-way communication suggests that reciprocal rather than unidirectional communication is facilitated by the medium (Ou et al., 2014). Reciprocal communication is valued in social networking due to the improved interactivity (C.-W Chen & Lin, 2014). Live streaming provides a convenient venue for information exchange between participants. First viewers can freely deliver messages to the streamer and the streamer is able to give responses. The enabled bidirectiona communication enhances viewers' perception that the streamer is approachable (B. Li et al., 2018). When viewer's ques tions or comments are replied by the streamer, viewers may develop a sense of connection with the streamer (Thorson & Rodgers, 2006). It is found that two-way communication makes one feel intimate towards others on social medi (Labrecque, 2014). Thus, two-way communication may promote viewers' feeling of proximity to the streamer.

Moreover, live streaming allows viewers to exchange ideas and comments with peers in the live stream session. Th enabled two-way communication among viewers boosts the development of mutual understanding within the viewer crowd (Ou et al., 2014). Prior study suggests that reciprocal communication in the online community contributes to the user's percep tion of being a part of the online community (C.-W. Chen & Lin, 2014). Hence, in live streaming, we expect that two-way com munication can enhance a viewer's sense of belonging to the viewer crowd. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H5a. Two-way communication is positively associated with perceived proximity to the streamer.

H5b. Two-way communication is positively associated with sense of belonging to the viewer crowd.

## 4.3.3 | Social presence

The publicness of communication in live streaming facilitates participants' awareness of others in the environment captured by the social presence (Lowry et al., 2010). Social presence reflects one's feeling of human contact, human warmth, and human sensitivity in a media environment (Cyr et al., 2009; Schultze & Brooks, 2018). Prior literatur suggests that the level of social presence in computer-mediated communication is associated with the sociability of media (C. M. K. Cheung et al., 2011). It is also found that people tend to enjoy socialising in platforms with high socia presence (C. M. K. Cheung et al., 2011) and that social presence can lead to positive social outcomes such as high quality relationships (J. Kim & Song, 2016; Lowry et al., 2010; Ou et al., 2014). Social presence is related to the technological capability to convey the contextual cues that are supposed to be available in a face-to-face setting in computer-mediated communication (Schultze & Brooks, 2018). It can be expected that in a live stream session with an enhanced social presence, viewers are more likely to be aware of the presence of others, which is similar to face to-face communication (Cyr et al. 2009; Ou et al., 2014). With the enhanced feeling of the presence of others, we can expect that viewers' feeling of being together with the streamer can be strengthened (J. Kim & Song, 2016) Hence, when perceiving a high social presence, viewers are more likely to feel the streamer to be close to them

Furthermore, prior research has shown that social presence is positively related to online users' community participation (Shen & Khalifa, 2008). In live streaming, a higher level of social presence encourages viewers to have mor social interactions and to participate in joint activities in the viewer crowd (C. M. K. Cheung et al., 2011). With greater opportunities for joint activities, the development of a sense of belonging to the viewer crowd may be pro moted. Conversely, if viewers see interactions in live streaming as impersonal, they will less likely develop a sense of belonging. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H6a. Social presence is positively associated with perceived proximity to the streamer.

H6b. Social presence is positively associated with sense of belonging to the viewer crowd.

## 4.3.4 | Self-presentation

Self-presentation refers to the process of communicating one's desired social identity to others (H. W. Kim, Chan, & Kankanhalli, 2012; Ma & Agarwal, 2007). Studies on self-presentation suggest that people present their identities to others for two reasons: to influence others and to find others who are similar to themselves (H. W. Kim et al., 2012). Social interaction will not be effective if people do not present their identities (Goffman, 1959). In the online environment, presenting identity information as an essential feature of the online platform provides the foundation of social relationship development (Kane, Alavi, Labianca, & Borgatti, 2014; Shin, 2016).

In live streaming, viewers can easily display their self-information such as names and interests, which make information about their traits and value systems available to others (Ma & Agarwal, 2007). The self-presentation b viewers provides an opportunity for viewers to influence the streamer as the streamer can know them better as person (vis-à-vis as just nameless audience). Prior literature suggests that customers' self-disclosed information con tribute to more effective customised service (Moon, 2000). Hence, in live streaming, viewers' self-presentation ma make live content delivered by the streamer more customised and fit viewers' preferences. It in turn makes viewer more involved with the streamer. Therefore, through presenting self-qualities, viewers may perceive greater proxim ity to the streamer.

The presentation of ones' self-information may also help attract similar others (Ma & Agarwal, 2007). Through presenting self-identities, viewers can find those who have similar identities and share similar interests in the viewer crowd, which strengthens the interconnections between the viewers. It increases their level of viewer crowd involvement, and viewers are more likely to follow group behaviours and participate in group activities. Consequently, self-presentation may promote a sense of being part of the view crowd (Shih & Huang, 2014; X. M. Zhang Wang, & Tam, 2010). Together, we hypothesize:

H7a. Self-presentation is positively associated with perceived proximity to the streamer.

H7b. Self-presentation is positively associated with sense of belonging to the viewer crowd.

## 4.4 | Cultural characteristics and social perceptions

## 4.4.1 | Social orientation

The salient characteristic of social orientation of the Chinese viewers may shape their social perceptions in live streaming. It is suggested social orientation leads to the Chinese people's inclination to build up social closeness with others (Q. Huang et al., 2011). Given the central role of the streamer in live streaming, viewers in a high level of social orientation are likely to desire the development of close relationships with the streamer (Q. Huang et al., 2011). Moreover, social orientation emphasises mutual goodwill (D. Kim et al., 1998). In live streaming, as an audience who watches the live performance prepared and provided by the streamer, viewers in this culture tend to be appreciative of the streamer's effort, feel indebted, and consequently perceive a sense of close connection or proximity with the streamer (eg, perceiving the streamer as someone who is willing to ‘brighten up their day’), which shortens the felt distance with the streamer.

Furthermore, viewers with a stronger social-oriented cultural trait are inclined to pay more attention to what peers think of them and whether they are accepted by the viewer group. For example, they are likely to willingly and proactively conform to the requests of others (eg, providing information that is requested or clarifying others' doubts). This is consistent with the study of Tan et al. (2014), which supports the notion that the Chinese people's social orientation promotes their comfort with intimacy in technologymediated communication, and with prior research suggesting that the social orientation culture emphasises conformity and group orientation (D. Kim et al., 1998). It leads to the development of cohesion with others in the viewer crowd (vis-à-vis viewers from a low social-oriented culture), which may in turn contribute to a higher sense of belonging overall.

H8a. Social orientation is positively associated with perceived proximity to the streamer.

H8b. Social orientation is positively associated with sense of belonging to the viewer crowd.

## 4.4.2 | Harmony

The culture of harmony has been found influential in shaping the Chinese social experiences and behaviours (Cai et al., 2019; L. Wang & Juslin, 2009). Therefore, we expect that the cultural characteristic of harmony in China may contribute to the viewers' development of perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd in a live stream session.

The culture of a high emphasis on harmony may lead one to seek friendly and harmonious relationships wit others and attain relationship equilibrium (F. M. Cheung et al., 2009). Thus, it can be expected that in live streaming, viewers in a culture that emphasises harmony may want to develop friendly relationships with and get closer to the streamer, given the latter's favour of delivering performance to and entertaining them. The har monious mindset and inclination to peace tend to create a friendly atmosphere in the live stream session wher the streamer may become more approachable with less psychological distance. This may explain the develop ment of viewers' perceived proximity to the streamer. Moreover, Chinese people with a culture of harmony tend to avoid conflicts and to seek peaceful relationships with peers in interactions such as forming a harmoniou group or community (Leung, Brew, Zhang, & Zhang, 2011). In a live stream session in China, the culture of harmony makes viewers in the same session willing to work together and to jointly create and maintain a favourable and positive social atmosphere in the crowd. It may help cultivate a sense of belonging to a group (in this case the viewer crowd). Therefore, within the culture of harmony, viewers are likely to develop a sense of belongin to the viewer crowd in live streaming. We hypothesize:

H9a. Harmony is positively associated with perceived proximity to the streamer.

H9b. Harmony is positively associated with sense of belonging to the viewer crowd.

## 5.1 | Questionnaire design

A questionnaire survey was designed to test the proposed research model. To increase the validity and reliability of the results, each construct in the model has multiple items adapted from previous studies with minor changes to fit the research context. The measurement items of purchase intention of virtual gifts were adapted from Pavlou (2003); flow from Suh et al. Respondents were asked to provide their responses with respect to the latest streamer they had viewed. A 7-point Likert scale was used to measure all the items with one representing ‘strongly disagree and seven representing ‘strongly agree’, indicating the respondent's level of agreement towards the given statement. In addition, the viewer's age, gender, and education level were included in the questionnaire a control variables. The questionnaire was translated into Chinese by a researcher. The translated question naire was proofread by 9 experienced Chinese live-streaming viewers to ensure the language used was clear and appropriate. After checking the language, the Chinese questionnaire was then translated back to English by another researcher to check translation accuracy. Modifications were made to improve the clar ity and accuracy of the questionnaire. This process was repeated twice until no further modification was needed.

This construct is theoretically unrelated to the constructs of the main survey. The correlations between the marker variable and othe constructs of this study were found to be insignificant. Based on the above measures taken as well as tests con ducted, we are confident that issues related to common method bias should not be a concern in this study.

## 6 | DISCUSSION, IMPLICATIONS, AND FUTURE RESEARCH

## 6.1 | Discussion

In this study, we set out to investigate the role of viewers' social perceptions in flow experiences in regard to driving the purchase of virtual gifts in Chinese live streamers. We also consider the contextual setting of live streaming in China by examining the IT-related factors of live streaming, as well as incorporating the cultural characteristics that are salient in the context of China, leading to the inclusion of the social perceptions related to the streamer an viewer crowd in our model.

We obtained several important findings. We first found that viewers' social perceptions of the streamer and the viewer crowd (ie, perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd) can lead to the purchase of virtual gifts through stimulating flow experience. We confirmed the importance of flow as the desire psychological state in encouraging purchase behaviour in computer-mediated environments by showing how flow states can be developed from viewers' social perceptions of the streamer and viewer crowd in highly social context such as China (Y. M. Guo & Poole, 2009; E. Huang, 2012; H. Liu et al., 2016). Our findings also indicate that, in China, close social connections are of particular importance for viewers in regard to enjoying a live stream session and for streamers to maintain viewers' continuous interest (ie, the flow state). This finding is consistent with prior lit erature, suggesting that social factors are of great importance to Chinese users in shaping their digital media experi ences (H. Liu et al., 2016). This finding is also in line with other studies, which suggest that social experiences can encourage a sense of involvement in activities and encourage inspire immersive participation and behavioural com mitment in virtual communities (Teo, Chan, Wei, & Zhang, 2003; Zhao et al., 2012) (A. R. Lee, Hon, & Won, 2018 O'Leary et al., 2014). This provides new insights to the extant studies about live streaming regarding the user motiva tions that can explain viewers' intention to purchase virtual gifts in live streaming (Sjöblom & Hamari, 2017).

Moreover, by incorporating cultural characteristics into the live streaming context, we also show the significant role of social orientation and harmony in explaining the formation of viewers' social perceptions in live streaming in China. Specifically, the results show that, rooted in a strongly social-oriented culture, viewers are more likely to fee psychologically close with streamers and to feel like an integral part of the viewer crowd. This finding matches with prior research, which stresses that social-oriented people are inclined to positive social engagement, leading to favourable attitudes towards other parties in communicating in entertaining environments (F. M. Cheung et al., 2009; Q. Huang et al., 2011; Tan et al., 2014). Interestingly, our findings show that the culture of harmony in China affects the development of the sense of belonging to the viewer crowd but not the perceived proximity to th streamers in live streaming. A plausible explanation is that, although the cultural characteristics of social orientation and harmony both stress cultivating and maintaining effective interpersonal relations, harmony as a fundamenta concept in Confucianism places greater emphasis on ensuring forbearance in the group to achieve collectivistic peac (Cai et al., 2019). In the context of live streaming in China, viewers within the culture of harmony are inclined to pur sue a harmonious communication climate in the viewer crowd, and each of them makes efforts to help each othe create a close and favourable atmosphere in the live stream session, which contributes to the development of th sense of belonging in the viewer crowd. However, the harmonious perspective of viewers does not help cultivate their perceptions of the streamer. This may be because harmony is usually attached to a group (Cai et al., 2019; Y. Li Wood, & Thomas, 2017). In live streaming, the streamer plays the role of a host while the viewers participate in the session as the audience. Therefore, viewers may not perceive the streamer to be in-group, which makes the effects of harmony not applicable to the streamer. Nonetheless, further investigation is needed in the future to test whethe the in-group and out-group distinction can explain the different effects.

Furthermore, our results show that IT-related factors can facilitate viewers' communication with other partici pants and foster viewers' perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd in liv streaming. Specifically, consistent with prior studies (Lowry, Romano, Jenkins, & Guthrie, 2009; Ma & Agarwal, 2007; Ou et al., 2014), responsiveness, social presence, and self-presentation—which afford and enhanc effective communication—positively affect viewers' social perceptions of streamers and other viewers. They enabl ‘live’ social interactions, which are particularly salient in live streaming. An unexpected finding is that two-way communication does not influence perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd. This may suggest that, although responsiveness and two-way communication can enable effective communication in live streaming, viewers value real-time communication more than reciprocal communication in interpersonal interaction in this context. One plausible reason for this can be that, given that two-way communication has become a standard feature of social media nowadays, it might be taken for granted and by itself may not be able to enhance the two viewers' social perceptions (ie, perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd). For viewers to develop the two social perceptions in live streaming, they may desire highly synchronised communication whereby their messages are responded within a short time (ie, responsiveness) to feel a strong connection with the communication parties

## 6.2 | Theoretical implications

In this study, we developed a comprehensive model to explain the purchase of virtual gifts in live streaming in th Chinese context. Different from the purchase of products in e-commerce-orientated live streaming, the purchase of virtual gifts in live streaming is mainly for obtaining desirable live performance and showing appreciation to the streamers, both of which are closely related to the social experience people develop in the live streaming environment. Our model is unique to the purchase of virtual gifts because it highlights the important role of viewers' socia experience in the environment and contextualises it into viewers' social perceptions towards the streamer and th viewer crowd in live streaming (ie, perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd) This study also delineates how such social perceptions are cultivated in the contextual setting of live streaming in China (ie, IT-related factors and cultural characteristics), as well as how they influence flow development and pur chase intention. The findings from this study thus contribute to the existing body of knowledge in the follow ing ways:

First, our study contributes to the literature on live streaming by contextualising viewers' social perceptions an confirming their importance in boosting the purchase of virtual gifts in live streaming in China. The results show that Chinese viewers' perceived proximity to the streamer and sense of belonging to the viewer crowd can contribute to viewers' purchase intention of virtual gifts via flow. This advances our understanding in terms of how users' socia experiences serve as a driving force of their purchase of virtual gifts. Although prior researchers have often consid ered social bonds as facilitating consumers' decision-making by alleviating uncertainty in consumption (Cenfetelli & Benbasat, 2019; Hu & Chaudhry, 2020), our findings suggest that Chinese viewers are willing to spend money on vir tual gifts for the social experience cultivated in the live streaming environment. Moreover, this study extends th flow theory by showing the significant role of social perceptions in regard to the streamer and the viewer crowd inflow development, enriching our knowledge of the antecedents of flow in digital media. Previous scholars of live streaming have seen social experience and flow as two separate motivations that can lead to viewers' behavioura outcomes (C. C. Chen & Lin, 2018; Hilvert-Bruce et al., 2018; Sjöblom & Hamari, 2017). In our study, we propose that social experience and flow are not necessarily independent; instead, attaining an enjoyable flow experience can mediate the effects of social experiences on user behaviour

In addition, in our study, we offer insights into the cultivation of social perceptions by examining the crucial role of IT-related factors and cultural characteristics. Although prior researchers on live streaming have rarely considered the role of the cultural setting, in this study, we identified social orientation and harmony as key cultural characteris tics that can explain the formation of viewers' social perceptions in live streaming in China. By exploring the effect of IT-related factors on viewer social perceptions in live streaming in China, in our study, we illustrate how social perceptions are enabled by the technology-mediated live streaming environment. This validates the idea that the appro priate technological setting is needed to enable the cultivation of viewer social perceptions and demonstrates wha technological features play a salient role in developing viewers' social perceptions in live streaming in China.

## 6.3 | Practical implications

The findings of this study can be useful for practitioners. For streamers who are interested in the live streaming mar ket in China, they should pay particular attention to cultivating viewers' feeling of their close presence during the liv chat. Even if streamers may not be able to reply to every comment, they should try to frequently engage wit viewers as a whole crowd and respond to the crowd synchronically instead of responding to random individuals Moreover, to enhance responsiveness among viewers, streamers can pay attention to the viewers who actively engage in timely communications and acknowledge those who give prompt responses to other viewers. The prac tices may enhance viewers' perceived proximity to the streamers. Additionally, streamers should strive to encourage interactions among viewers and motivate them to present their own ideas and interests. For example, streamers can encourage the viewer crowd to discuss and decide the subsequent interactive social games or future performanc content together, such as strategically using polling. Paying attention to the potential commonality among the viewers may help streamers to establish a sense of belonging among the viewers in the session. Such practices are likely to be effective in establishing a warm and lively atmosphere in the live stream session in the countries/cultura contexts that are highly socially oriented, as our findings show.

Apart from streamers, platform providers who want to seize the live streaming opportunities in China shoul make use of the importance of social interactions among users. For example, platforms can allow streamers to video call their viewers to encourage viewer self-presentation. Moreover, platforms can enable the streamers to take up the challenge from other streamers. The engagement level of the viewer crowd, reflected from the number of viewe comments and virtual gifts, determines which streamer wins the challenge. Such a mechanism can enhance the lively social atmosphere by encouraging interaction among viewers through setting competition between two viewe crowds. Also, platforms can design group games that require joint participation and reward viewers if the viewe crowd accomplishes certain game tasks. These initiatives may increase viewers' perceived responsiveness and socia presence, which further leads to increasing their willingness to participate in live streaming activities together.

## 6.4 | Limitation and future research

This study has some limitations which provide opportunities for future research. First, while we aim to be compre hensive in our model, there remain other factors that are not incorporated in the current research. For instance viewers' dispositional traits such as social media dependence may have an influence but are not investigated in thi study (B. Li et al., 2018). Future research may consider the effects of other viewers' dispositional traits. Second despite the relatively small effect size of some variables, this study initiates the development and test of a theoretica framework as a starting point to advance the understanding of live streaming usage in the unique context of China According to Fern and Monroe (1996), a small effect size may not indicate that the theory is invalid or bad, and it may still provide theoretical guidance to improve people's understanding of a related phenomenon, especially whe it is not well understood. Therefore, future research can build on our theoretical framework and incorporate some nuanced variables to generate further insights for practitioners to understand viewer experience and behaviour. Fo instance, the design aesthetics of live streaming platforms may have influences on viewers' perceptions and experi ences. Therefore, future research can incorporate design aesthetics when studying viewer experience in live stream ing. Third, live streaming has been combined with e-commerce websites in China to promote the sales of products Future research may extend our model to the context of Chinese e-commerce live streaming by adding e-commerce features. Fourth, this research did not consider the difference between new viewers and viewers with an established tie with the streamer. The longitudinal study is suggested to explore this issue in future studies. In addition, there might be the issue of self-selection bias that people with rich live streaming viewing experience may be interested in responding to this survey. Future research may consider an experimental research design to better deal with this issue.

## Mediation test

We tested flow's mediating effects following Hair et al. (2013)'s instruction on mediation analysis in PLS-SEM. First, we checked whether the indirect effects were significant. The results reveal that the indirect effect from perceived proximity to the streamer (PPS) via flow to purchase intention of virtual gifts (PIV) is significant $( P < 0 . 0 1 )$ , and the indirect effect from the sense of belonging to the viewer crowd (SOB) via flow to PIV is also significant (P < 0.05). We then referred to the significance of the direct effects from PPS to PIV and from SOB to PIV. The results suggest that the direct effect from PPS to PIV is significant $( \beta = 0 . 3 3 0 , P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Given that both the direct and the indirect effects are significant, we concluded that flow partially mediates the relationship between PPS and PIV.
