## Abstract

Organisations across many industries are increasing the use of evidence-based approaches to decision-making through adoption of business analytics. Creative processes and decisions are an area of organisational decision-making which has traditionally been highly intuition-based, and where professional culture and practices are often very different from the engineering disciplines from which data-driven decision approaches originate. Through the case study of the analytics-oriented transformation of creative decisions at Rovio, a leading game development company, this study seeks to understand how organisations can make their creative decision processes more evidence-based, while retaining the best features of artistic intuition and human creativity. The case study highlights several issues that need to be delicately managed and balanced to effectively combine analytics and human creativity, and offers five principles for such “creative analytics”: (1) build shared analytics values but provide tailored BA support; (2) build hybrid teams; (3) balance commercial and creative goals; (4) encourage creative experimentation and learning; and (5) make data-inspired, not data-driven, creative decisions.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Constantly. we have these talks that what does this actually mean and how to choose So I would say it's not data-driven, but maybe data-inspired decision making. (Senior Data Analyst, Rovio)

Many organisations are moving towards more evidence-based approaches to decision-making through increased adoption of business analytics (BA) (Kiron et al., 2014). BA refers to “the extensive use of data, statistical and quanti tative analysis, explanatory and predictive models” (Davenport & Harris, 2007, p. 7) to inform organisational deci sions and actions. Realising value from BA, however, is not a simple process. For example, a study by MIT and SA (Kiron et al., 2014) found that 87% of respondent believed their organisation needs to improve their BA use in decision-making. A key challenge in BA benefit realisation is organisational, for example, EY (2015) found that onl 31% of respondent organisations had successfully restructured their processes to use data to inform all business decisions, and a survey by McKinsey and Company (2016) found that establishment of the appropriate organisationa structure is the key challenge for effective BA efforts.

These issues can be even more challenging to tackle in creative industries, such as architecture, design, digita games, heritage, music, performing arts, publishing, screen production, and visual arts (Australian Trade Commission, n.d.; Bakhshi et al., 2013; O'Connor, 2010). Creative processes and decisions have traditionally bee highly intuition-based, and the backgrounds of creative professionals are usually different from the engineering disci plines from which data-driven decision approaches originate. As such, there can be both higher cultural and knowl edge barriers to the adoption of BA in creative industries.

Creative capital is arguably one of the most important assets for any organisation's (Florida & Goodnight, 2005 Mumford et al., 2002) and such capital is, by definition, at the core of creative industries. While creative organisa tions can benefit from BA, these organisations need to be careful about their adoption approach so as not to negatively affect their creative capabilities. Yet, BA adoption and use in creative organisations have received relatively little research attention. Current studies on analytics adoption and transformations mostly focus on sectors with a predominantly science or engineering tradition, for example, manufacturing (Dremel et al., 2017, 2020), utilities (Gust et al., 2017), or finance (Kettinger et al., 2011). Also, existing research on organisational BA use and benefits rarely distinguishes between different types of BA use and existing BA benefits models seldom distinguish betwee different types of organisational decisions (Clark Jr et al., 2007; Mikalef et al., 2018, 2019; Seddon et al., 2017) Despite the substantial amount of research interest in organisational BA adoption and benefits, there has been littl attention on examining the drivers of effective BA use (Trieu, 2017), particularly for different types of BA use (Tamm et al., 2020) and decision contexts

As such, while the creativity literature suggests that BA adoption and use in creative decisions may face some unique—or at least magnified—challenges, the existing BA literature does not shed much light on how to address such challenges. For example, the tension between balancing artistic intuition with data-based analytics appears to be more pronounced in creative contexts. However, what is an optimal balance between data and intuition and how to achieve it is an important issue that is not yet understood in BA research (Abbasi et al., 2016). Also, as intuitio traditionally plays a prominent role in creative decisions, creative organisations will likely need to undertake consid erable transformation to successfully embed analytics in their decision practices.

This paper seeks to provide further insights into how organisations can make their creative decision processes more evidence-based, while retaining the best features of artistic intuition and human creativity. We use the term “creative analytics” to refer to this considered and balanced fusion between artistic intuition and analytics-base evidence to inform, evaluate, and/or inspire creative decisions

To answer this question, we focus on the analytics transformation case study of Rovio Entertainment Corporation, a leading game development and entertainment company (please refer to Appendix A for details on our research approach). Rovio presents a rich context for studying analytics-enabled creative decisions because of its blend of arts and engineering cultures. The success of digital game development relies on the talent and skills of artists and game designers (typically from arts backgrounds), as well as software engineers (typically from science, tech nology, engineering, and mathematics [STEM] backgrounds). The Rovio case is also revelatory because of the scope and pace of their organisational transformation. Within 4 years, Rovio embedded an analytical approach and culture as a fundamental part of the company's core creative decision processes, which had previously been mainl intuition-based. We wanted to understand how Rovio succeeded to embed a strong analytics-oriented culture in such a short timeframe, while retaining artistic freedom and willingness to take creative risks that remained crucia for the company's success. In particular, we set out to identify the key principles that enabled this transformation.

Our paper begins with an overview of what is known about the unique features of creative organisations an how these features may lead to challenges for effective BA adoption. We then present Rovio's creative process transformation journey, including the key challenges faced, and how progress was achieved. In the second half of the paper, we turn to five creative analytics principles that enabled Rovio to successfully move towards more evidencebased decisions while not only retaining but even enhancing the organisation's creative capabilities. Finally, we reflect on the practical and theoretical implications of our findings and highlight three promising avenues for futur research on creative analytics.

## 2 | ANALYTICS AND CREATIVE DECISIONS

What are “creative decisions”? We adopt the following definition of creativity:

Creativity and innovation at work are the process, outcomes, and products of attempts to develop and introduce new and improved ways of doing things. The creativity stage of this process refers to idea generation, and innovation refers to the subsequent stage of implementing ideas toward better procedures, practices, or products. (Anderson et al., 2014, p. 1298)

Creative decisions, therefore, relate to the choices made during the idea generation stage, as well as the choice about which ideas should proceed to implementation. A key part of the latter involves idea evaluation (Mumford et al., 2002). Such decisions can range from the micro-level (e.g., which shade of colour to use in a design element) to highly strategic choices (e.g., whether to take a particular concept to full-scale production)

There is a substantial body of research on creative industries, including those heavily reliant on digital technolo gies (e.g., Aoyama & Izushi, 2003; Cohendet & Simon, 2007; Tschang, 2007). There has also been a considerable amount of research on organisational BA adoption and use (Dremel et al., 2020; Gust et al., 2017). However, th adoption and use of BA in creative industries—and creative decisions more broadly—appears to have received littl attention. While some of what is known about BA transformation challenges and good practices from other contexts may well be relevant, studies on creative industries suggest such organisations face some unique managerial and organisational challenges (Gotsi et al., 2010; Lampel et al., 2000), which can also affect BA adoption.

## 2.1 | The special features of creative organisations

Organisations in creative industries have been characterised as contracts between art and commerce (Caves, 2000), giving rise to contrasting logics, where the aesthetic is pitted against the efficient, commerce battles with a creative logic, and art for arts sake competes against the exigencies of commercial appropriation (Townley et al., 2009, p. 940). These tensions between the artistic and commercial logics lead to the need for many delicate balancing acts (Cohendet & Simon, 2007; Eikhof & Haunschild, 2007; Lampel et al., 2000), such as between creative autonomy and control (DeFillippi, 2009; Thompson et al., 2007; Tschang, 2007), creativity and productivity (Chang & Birkett, 2004; Gotsi et al., 2010), creative exploration and exploitation (DeFillippi, 2009; Gotsi et al., 2010), familiarity and novelty (Lampel et al., 2000), and the extent to which creative decisions should be artist-driven versus customer-driven (Deuze, 2007). There is also a balancing act between just how creative the creative product should be, as “consumers need familiarity to understand what they are offered, but they need novelty to enjoy it. Finding a successful synthesis of these two opposing elements depends more on art than technique, more on insight than professional judgment” (Lampel et al., 2000, p. 264).

The need to strike the right balance between the artistic and commercial modes is essential, as “attempts to economise creativity and artistic motivation run the risk of damaging these resources (Eikhof & Haunschild, 2007 p. 524), while not paying sufficient attention to commercial considerations can cause high inefficiency (Cohendet & Simon, 2007). Such tensions play out not only on the organisational level but also on an individual level, as artists ar often expected to incorporate business considerations into their creative decisions (DeFillippi, 2009), including empathy towards customer preferences and commercial boundaries (Michlewski, 2008). As part of their professiona identity, each creative professional needs to balance their individuality and artistic independence with organisationa belonging and commercial demands (DeFillippi, 2009; Gotsi et al., 2010).

Leaders of creative organisations, therefore, need “to ensure that the structure of the work environment, the climate and culture, and the human resource practices (e.g., rewards, resources, goals, and expected evaluations) are such that creative outcomes can and do occur” (Shalley & Gilson, 2004, p. 35), while simultaneously ensuring that the creative outcomes also lead to commercial ones. Prior research has identified several factors that are conductive to creativity, e.g., team diversity, participative safety, freedom to take risks and experiment, long-term focus, ope communication, and organisational slack (Amabile, 1998; Anderson et al., 2014; Andriopoulos, 2001).

## 2.2 | Challenges for analytics adoption in creative organisations

The unique features of creative organisations and the conditions conductive of creativity may pose some specia challenges for successful BA adoption and use

First, the high degree of inherent unknowability (Caves, 2000) in how creative products are received by con sumers means that trying to use BA to prescribe or predict creative decisions can be problematic. In addition, crea tive products cannot be conceived or evaluated solely on a rationality-basis, as they deal in expressive or aestheti tastes rather than utilitarian needs; their meaning and significance determined by the consumer's coding and decoding of value” (Townley et al., 2009, p. 940). Hence, it is very difficult to predict in advance which creative idea will be successful (Townley et al., 2009) and judgement plays a central role in both the inception and evaluation of creative work.

Second, analytics approaches and BA tools are often foreign to the work practices of creative professionals, as artistic work traditions can differ greatly from engineering and sciences where BA originates. Sometimes, the use o analytics may even conflict with the core values of artistic freedom and the logic of creating art for art's sake .

Third, the power and benefits of BA use need to be carefully balanced and managed to avoid unintended negative impacts on factors enabling creativity, such as freedom for expression and experimentation, risk taking, participative safety, long-termism, and intrinsic motivation (Anderson et al., 2014; Andriopoulos, 2001; Damanpour, 1991)

## 2.3 | Towards creative analytics

While creative industries have traditionally been viewed as craft-based, low-tech, and far-removed from STEM-based disciplines (Aoyama & Izushi, 2003), this has changed over the last few decades. Not only have digita technologies become an important enhancer and enabler of traditional creative sectors (e.g., museums, visual and performing arts, etc.), but new creative sectors have emerged where creative products and services are fully digital One example of the latter is the digital games industry. These developments lead to two important outcomes that may facilitate BA adoption and overcome some of the earlier-described challenges.

First, digitalization of creative industries inevitably leads to the need to bring arts and engineering cultures close together. For example, in the video games:

As a cultural product, a video game is a complex mix of technology, art, and interactive story-telling. The technological part of the artefact should disappear beyond the magic of the scenario, the smoothness of controls, and the realism of the simulation. Therefore, the generic videogame making involves the development and integration of a scenario, design of interactivity, programming activities, graphic arts, sound design, music, integration and tests/quality assessment. (Cohendet & Simon, 2007, p. 587)

This means that such organisations need to learn to successfully integrate these two different cultures, and to become well-versed with leveraging digital technologies. Both experiences are highly relevant to facilitating successful BA adoption as well.

Second, digitalization can enable profound changes in creative product development. For example, in the mobile game industry, where the creative products are fully digital, there has been a shift from delivering feature-complete products towards continuously refining and extending the product based on user engagement (Davidovici Nora, 2013). This continuous refinement cycle may help address some of the above-discussed challenges for BA adoption in creative decision-making. Regarding “inherent unknowability”, it enables testing and revisiting creativ choices in an iterative design process. Although it does not fully resolve the tension between artistic and commercia logics, it can bring the two closer together (if the artists believe their mission is to provide inspiring and engaging experiences to the consumers of their creations).

The mobile games industry, therefore, presents a valuable context for studying how to combine human intuitio and analytics-based insights for creative analytics, where the tensions between arts and commercial logics, and art and engineering cultures need to be carefully managed. We now turn to a case study of one such organisation, whic successfully completed a journey towards more analytical creative decisions

## 3 | ROVIO'S CREATIVE PROCESS TRANSFORMATION

Rovio Entertainment Corporation is a Finnish entertainment company, founded in 2003 by three students: Niklas Hed, Jarno Väkeväinen, and Kim Dikert. Since then, Rovio has grown to nearly 500 employees, close to €300 million in annual revenues (Rovio Entertainment Corporation, 2020), and authored some of the most successful mobil games of all time. The company is best known for its Angry Birds game franchise, with over 4.5 billion downloads as of late 2019 (England, 2019). The original title in the series, released as Rovio's 52nd game in 2009, has been down loaded more than 3 billion times and led the paid iOS app rankings for longer than any other (Robertson, 2015; Truong, 2015).

## 3.1 | The case for transformation

Rovio originally found success with the “premium game” business model. In this business model, the customer is charged a fixed upfront fee for the game. The game is introduced to the market as feature-complete and highl polished, and minor maintenance releases are provided occasionally thereafter. Many “premium games” are narrative-based, and the designers and developers craft the user journey and experiences. This traditional busines model relied strongly on creative judgement, with high uncertainty about how the final product would be received.

By 2012, the “premium games” business model was being challenged by a new business model called “free-to play” (F2P). As the name suggests, F2P games allow customers to access a significant part of the product functional ity for free. The revenue is earned from a small fraction of users who opt to pay for micro-transactions that enabl additional functionality and experiences. The commercial success of F2P games depends on retention (keeping th customers continuously engaged) and monetization (designing functionality to encourage the micro-transactions (Davidovici-Nora, 2013; Flunger et al., 2019). Repeated micro-transactions are essential for the game to be commercially viable.

In contrast to premium games, where the customers make their purchase decision upfront based on thei expected experience, in F2P customers have the freedom to adjust their purchase decisions based on their actual experience. Therefore, developing successful F2P games requires a deep and fine-grained understanding of customer behaviour and preferences as well as the ability to align design decisions with these. The game is continuously refined following market introduction, turning it more into an evolving service than a finished product. This evolvin nature of F2P games also makes it possible to evaluate and adjust creative decisions, with an evidence-based approach to such decisions becoming critical to success.<sup>1</sup>

[Premium games are] like movies. You make a movie, you get ten dollars or whatever that is when you go to watch the movie. Even if you don't like it, you can walk away from the movie halfway through, you still get the money from that person. But if you are making a free to play game, you are actually operating a shop. […] You, as the game developer, you have to be the shopkeeper. You have to be the one who is marketing and selling the things that you have in the game. (Vice President, Game Development)

The rise of F2P created both a strong market threat and organisational challenge to leading premium game developers, including Rovio. In the words of Rovio's Senior Vice President of Games, F2P “shook the whole system really, and also shook us really a lot”. As F2P games began dominating the charts of most profitable game titles, Rovio's executive team knew the organisation would need to adapt.

However, they also knew that this would not be a simple journey. Competing successfully with “F2P-native” firms would require changing the creative processes and enabling capabilities. Successful F2P product development strongly depends on an evidence-based approach to game design, requiring high-quality data and sophisticated BA capabilities. While Rovio had explored BA adoption a few years prior, at the time the available tools were really lim ited in what they offered, so we couldn't get that much out of that and analyzing the data was hard” (Vice President Game Development). Consequently, BA tools, skills, and awareness within the company remained limited

It doesn't help if you were number one yesterday… We needed to earn our place in this new system. (Senior Vice President, Games)

## 3.2 | Building analytics capabilities

Rovio decided that to succeed with their business model transformation, it was now time to invest in both interna analytics expertise and enabling technology. Rovio began by hiring BA experts to work with the creative teams. Initially, the nascent BA team needed to champion and push their services, as the value of BA was not well known Creative decisions had been largely intuition-based. To encourage acceptance towards analytics, the BA team selected a few key projects where it had the potential to make the greatest impact. They then worked closely wit the creative teams to show the possibilities of BA

That's a huge step change. That don't rely on your gut or your creative thinking, but rather rely on the numbers, which is quite a drastic change of thinking. (Senior Product Manager)

Over time, the awareness and interest in the enabling power of BA grew. The BA team noticed a gradual shift from this “push” engagement model towards a “pull” model, where the creative teams proactively sought their advice. An evidence-based approach was becoming an integral part of creative decisions and development processes. This emerging analytical culture permeated both the work of engineers and artists.

Now everybody is somehow exposed to the data. […] The usage of data has basically entered every area, even the work of artists. (Head of Studio)

The teams that we have right now—they want to make excellent free to play games and they want those [BA experts] to be in the team. Instead of central push, there is now team pull for that knowl edge. (Vice President, Game Development)

Rovio's employees also became more analytics-savvy. Standard reporting was mostly automated and product managers started using “self-service” dashboards and BA tools in their daily work. If deeper and more complex analy sis was needed, they now had access to a dedicated BA expert to assist with bespoke analysis. Employees from engi neering backgrounds leaned towards hands-on use of BA tools, while artists tended to prefer engaging with the BA team for advice. To facilitate closer collaboration between creative teams and analysts, Rovio gradually moved away from a centralised BA team structure to a hybrid structure, where analysts began working as integral members of creative teams.

As much as possible, the regular business intelligence tasks have been automated. So, basically there is a dashboard for all the regular information that people might want for the games. […] What the business intelligence guys are doing most of the time is these more complex queries. (Executive Producer)

Another feature of the new culture was a shift towards increased openness and transparency. Earlier, access to product performance data had usually been limited to managers. Now, game performance reports were shared wit all employees on a weekly basis. Creative teams started holding regular meetings to share insights from their latest projects and to learn about what has worked well (and what not) from each other

If you wanted everyone to be interested about money and about making a business, we had to change that mindset. That everyone can access the data. That everyone can see how your game did yesterday. Did the change that we made actually have an impact on the KPIs of the game? That was a big thing. We had to convince the whole company that yes, we can share data of every game to every employee. (Senior Vice President, Games)

## 3.3 | Embedding analytics in creative processes

Rovio's core creative process—game development—comprises four key phases: Ideation, Prototyping, Production and Launch (illustrated in Figure 1). The Ideation phase is concerned with developing the concept for a new game This phase usually lasts up to a couple of months and involves a small team of 2–3 people. Around 1 out of 20 con cepts from Ideation is considered to have sufficient potential to proceed to the Prototyping phase, which involve building a working prototype to test out the idea in practice. The team size usually doubles at this point. The first prototype can take a couple of months to develop, and additional iterations of a few more months are possible.

Next, the Production phase focuses on developing the fully functional game with the view to release it to the market. The decision to proceed to Production is not taken lightly. First, it involves a significant investment, as th team size often triples at this point with 8–16 people usually dedicated to the project for 6–12 months. Second, i has a substantial associated opportunity cost, as there is limited capacity to pursue other projects in parallel

Following Production, the release to the market occurs in two stages: a “soft-launch” to a limited audience and, if this is successful, a global launch.

The initial concept from Ideation evolves throughout the creative process phases and there can also be substantial iteration and refinement of the concept within each phase. Following the transformation, BA now plays an integral part throughout the four creative process phases, increasing in importance with every phase. The Ideation phase continues to be driven by the culture and capabilitie that existed within Rovio long before the transformation—the employees' creativity and passion for creating excel lent products. However, commercial factors and analysis have become a more significant part of this phase. The tar get customer and their interests are considered, based on a finer and deeper understanding of the different customer segments enabled by BA. Creative teams have become more specialised, focusing on a particular genre and target audience (e.g., puzzle games for 18–24 year old females). Market and competitor analysis has also become an essential component in Ideation, helping not only identify potential threats (e.g., a highly saturated market for particular concept) but also find inspiration and collaboration opportunities.

In the beginning, it's really hard to test anything. You just have an idea. Of course, you try to include everything you know about the market, about the competition that it's in the genre where you are going, you try to have that built into the idea that you are making. […] But all that's still the creative side. (Vice President, Game Development)

In Prototyping, BA is carefully embedded into the game and assumes a much more prominent role. Not only is BA used to gather data on product performance and user experience metrics, but it is also closely integrated wit controls such as game level difficulty and speed of progression. These controls can be used to adjust the user experi ence in real-time based on the analytical insights. The decisions about how and where BA should be embedded need to be made carefully. Otherwise, it can later be difficult to understand the user experience, to do something about it and to make well-informed decisions regarding the commercial viability of the product

It is important to note that while BA use provides a valuable “reality check” for the prototype, the insights are far from prescient. First, the sample size is limited and may not represent broader customer sentiment. Second, while the data helps understand user behaviours, it provides limited insight into the reasons behind these behaviours Therefore, the extensive experience of Rovio's creative professionals remains essential in prototype evaluation. Also the company has not lost the willingness to pursue bold new creative ideas, but such decisions can now be mor thoroughly considered.

From those [internal] tests, we can get data so that we can improve the game, the core gameplay, but there is no way of getting any KPI data, like retention. […] The data that you can get is: Are people interested? Do they come back week after week to play the game? If they do, then you know that you have something good on your hands. (Vice President, Game Development)

During Production, BA is carefully embedded in the game following the design decisions and refinements mad during Prototyping. This allows to continue gathering data about game and to optimise it in real-time. These BA enabled changes have also resulted in a profound shift in Rovio's product development philosophy. The Production phase is no longer focused on developing a feature-complete and fully polished final product. Rather, the aim is to build a strong platform based on a robust core concept that can be extended and refined based on facts, instead of guesses, about customer preferences.

We have the ‘minimum viable product’ […] what we can use to go to market. It's a full working game and doesn't have all the tricks, but it's something that we can still be proud of. The second scope for us is, minimum viable product is the actual term, but the ‘minimum awesome product’ is our own.

(Senior Product Manager)

The role of BA continues post-production. During the soft-launch phase, a key focus of BA is measuring and understanding user retention. If the game performs well on this dimension, monetization and other issues identified through BA can often be resolved. Retention issues, however, tend to come down to fundamentals that ar difficult—or impossible—to address.

As the aim of the first release is now to establish an ever-evolving platform, the work on the game is all but done even after the launch. Informed by BA-based insights, a team of about eight people continues to refine and exten the game, possibly for several years to come. This involves both major updates that require full engagement from the creative team, as well as minor changes which can often be made on the fly using control levers embedded i the game. For example, the game economy configuration sheet allows real-time updates to parameters such as leve difficulty, available rewards, and cost of items.

Then it starts to become data-driven bit by bit until you are in the soft launch phase, when it's exposed to thousands of players or tens of thousands of players. Then, all the data you get is rea data, it's from the real markets. (Vice President, Game Development)

It used to be that we did maybe a few updates to a game after its launch, like four updates a year. Today […] we do bi-weekly updates, and we add new levels. We used to add new levels on a weekly basis, now with the other games we are experimenting adding new levels every day. […] And sometimes it means that you can develop the game with a smaller amount of people, but when you actually put it live, you need to be able to react so that you perhaps need to double or even triple the team size. (Head of Studio)

## 3.4 | Embracing creative analytics

Following the transformation, BA use at Rovio spans all roles and organisational levels and organisation culture ha become highly analytical. At times, the use of BA can still leave some employees a little frustrated because of th extra work required or needing to adapt to the new way of working. However, most employees have either embraced the change or accepted the new evidence-based approach as a useful part of creative decision processes BA is seen as a key enabler of creating products that bring more joy and enjoyment to customers

I think it [the new approach] has enhanced our products a lot. […] Although it can be a painful route to make different variations and it takes time, but it really makes the product better. (Senior Game Artist)

The whole analytic mentality has definitely come through the company. It's a very big win. Leve designers are posting results of how their level performed and why. […] Rather than someone telling them to do that, they actually want to share. (Senior Product Manager)

This does not mean, however, that creativity and artistic values have taken a backseat. They are, and will continue to be, at the core of Rovio's culture and the company's continued success. The role of analytics is not to con strain creativity, but to challenge existing assumptions and to enable evidence-based creative experimentation Indeed, occasionally, the data has encouraged creative decisions that otherwise may have been considered too risky For example, BA played a role in making the “Angry Birds” smile!

Since our game is called Angry Birds, we long thought that we have to picture them always angry. […] But then we noticed through this data that it works best if they are, for example, smiling and really happy. […] This doesn't really perfectly fit our title [Angry Birds], but it just looks best for the people. (Senior Game Artist)

## 4 | FIVE PRINCIPLES FOR CREATIVE ANALYTICS

Fundamental organisational and cultural changes are always difficult. What enabled Rovio to succeed with the major changes, retaining its artistic strengths while enhancing the creative decision processes through analytics? We hav identified five creative analytics principles, summarised in Table 1, that were key to Rovio's transformation success The first and second principle relate to setting up the supporting organisational mechanisms for analytics-informed creative decision-making. The third principle relates to how to approach creative decision content. The fourth and fifth principle relate to how to approach the creative decision process. These five principles are discussed further in the sections that follow

## 4.1 | Build shared analytics values but provide tailored BA support

In many creative organisations, both art and engineering disciplines are critical to the successful achievement of the organisation's mission. This requires interdisciplinary collaboration between very different working traditions and styles towards achieving shared goals. Therefore, when implementing analytics in creative organisations, it is impor tant to build a shared willingness for evidence-based decision-making while carefully tailoring the analytics approaches and tools to the different user backgrounds and capabilities

First, regardless of employee backgrounds, it is important to establish a shared willingness and appreciation for evidence-based decision-making. Building an evidence-based or “data culture” have been found to be an essentia enabler of successful BA adoption in prior research (Davenport & Harris, 2007; Dremel et al., 2017; Gust et al., 2017) and “development of shared understanding, decision-making, and planning should be considered integra parts of BI&A capabilities” (Torres et al., 2018, p. 831).

Our findings suggest that building these shared values is possibly more challenging in creative organisations which bring together employees from diverse professional communities with a wide variety of working style (e.g., within the games industry this includes project managers, scriptwriters, designers, artists, sound designers, and software engineers among others (Cohendet & Simon, 2007)). Therefore, in building these shared values, it is important to be mindful of potential differences in perceptions towards evidence-based decisions. Employees from science and engineering backgrounds may find BA a more natural fit with their ways of working, while employees from art backgrounds may view the approach as “engineerish” and constraining artistic freedom and creativity

## 4.2 | Build hybrid teams

Effective embedding of BA in creative decision processes requires close collaboration between analysts and creativ teams. This helps creative teams better understand the possibilities of BA, helps analysts appreciate the specia nature of creative processes, and enables analysts to provide the required data, tools, and advice to support creativ decisions. Possible organisational arrangements for facilitating this engagement range from fully centralised to dec entralised BA teams, and everything in between (Accenture, 2013; Davenport & Harris, 2007, Chapters 2 and 7)

Creativity research points to several reasons why building hybrid teams can be beneficial for creative decision making. Team diversity and interaction with diverse others (Shalley & Gilson, 2004, p. 43) have consistently bee found to be a major driver of creativity (Amabile, 1998; Mumford et al., 2002). Embedding analysts in creative teams helps facilitate advice seeking, advice giving, and reflective reframing that are three important enablers of team based creativity (Hargadon & Bechky, 2006). As people appear to follow the path of least resistance in establishin contacts and gathering information” (Mumford et al., 2002, p. 729), making analysts as available and close to creative decision-makers as possible is likely to lead to the use of analytics-based insights in informing these decisions. This is also consistent with prior BA research, which has found that multi-disciplinary teams, where analysts work closel with subject-matter experts, enable BA value realisation (Günther et al., 2017; Namvar & Cybulski, 2014; Sharma et al., 2014).

Our findings suggest that creative organisations which are building their first BA capability may benefit from beginning with a predominantly centralised model, then gradually moving towards a more decentralised approach. A mostly centralised structure in the early phases helps establish shared BA resources and know-how that can later b leveraged and reused across creative projects. At Rovio, a large proportion of the BA team was dedicated to shared BA capability and platform building in the early phases of the transformation. The centralised model also made it eas ier for the analysts to learn from each other's experiences and share useful resources

Once a solid BA platform and practices are in place, and the “low-hanging fruit” for BA-enabled improvements have been harvested, close collaboration between the analysts and creative teams becomes increasingly important Such collaboration helps identify more sophisticated and innovative ways to solve business challenges throug BA. At this stage, it may be best for the BA team to transition to a hybrid model. At Rovio, this meant that the ana lysts were still part of BI [function], but rented to the game teams, so to speak (Senior Data Analyst). Some cen tralised capability was maintained, but a large proportion of the BA team started working daily within creative teams. The closer the analysts became to the creative processes, the better they understood day-to-day creative decisions and practices. It also helped analysts to engage faster where appropriate, “since you know who to speak to and you are easy to approach, not locked away somewhere” (Senior Data Analyst).

As the sophistication of BA use continues to increase, close interdisciplinary collaboration becomes more and more important for staying ahead competitors and for continuous improvement. At the time of the case study, Rovio was having early discussions of potentially moving the analysts even closer to the coalface , i.e., to hire them as an integral part of the product development teams.

Rovio develops and produces its own titles using a development process in which a group of creative, production, and technical professionals—including designers, producers, programmers, artists, and sound engineers—in cooperation with marketing, finance, analytics, sales, and other professionals—collaborate in an agile (i.e. iterative and incremental) manner. (Rovio Entertainment Corporation, 2017, p. 156)

Although all employees are likely to become more analytics-savvy over time (Tamm et al., 2020), maintaining dedicated BA specialists may in fact be beneficial for creativity, as expertise-based division of labour has been found to be a driver of innovation and creativity (Damanpour, 1991; Mumford et al., 2002). Furthermore, BA specialists ar likely to continue to be important in supporting BA users with bespoke and sophisticated analytics (Tamm et al., 2020), as well as assisting with interpreting the data-based “answers” and understanding their limitations (Namvar & Cybulski, 2014). While some organisations may still find it more beneficial to build a centralised BA team (e.g., Dremel et al., 2017), they will likely need to pay careful attention to ensuring close and frequent interaction between the analysts and business teams.

## 4.3 | Balance creative and commercial goals

Creativity research suggests that “attempts to manage and market artistic practices following economic logics of practice endanger the resources vital to creative production” (Eikhof & Haunschild, 2007, p. 536). However, this does not mean that economic realities can be disregarded as “in a creative person, just as in a creative company, you have to have both, a creative outlook and one that embodies common sense, side by side, inseparable. If you don't then you get neither art nor commerce” (Michael Eisner, ex-CEO of Disney, quoted in Wetlaufer, 2000, p. 124). Also, a delicate balance needs to be sought to analyze and address existing demand while at the same time using thei imagination to extend and transform the market” (Lampel et al., 2000, p. 263).

Similarly, our findings suggest that in making analytics-informed creative decisions, it is essential to balance th pursuit of commercial goals with creative freedom. Analytics-based mechanisms to evaluate performance of creativ products are often centred on customer perceptions or behaviours and, ultimately, aimed to improve customer experi ence and product sales. Artists are often driven by intrinsic, non-commercial motivations. This requires simultaneously a artistically-savvy approach to commercial decisions and a commercially-savvy approach to artistic decisions

First, commercial decisions need to remain artistically savvy. The adoption of analytics may tempt an organisa tion to shift the focus of creative decisions towards short-term financial benefits. Customer preferences revealed by analytics can provide a reliable source for refinement and exploitation of existing ideas, meaning a lower risk and high likelihood of achieving the intended benefit. However, while such benefits are the easier to measure and con trol, they may be incremental and do not lead to novel concepts and ideas. Often, it is the artistic intuition of creativ experts that is the main source of the latter ideas, which carry a potentially higher risk but also higher rewards Therefore, if the organisation focuses too heavily on evidence-based customer analytics at the expense of artistic intuition of creative experts, it risks jeopardising their long-term competitiveness to short-term revenue. Too muc focus on short-term commercial considerations may also disillusion the artists and can ultimately lead to a loss of ke talent and lower quality and less appealing products.

At Rovio, the ability for the artists to freely create, experiment, and grow was seen as critical for remaining com petitive. Artistic intuition continues to dominate the ideation phase of developing new ideas, while analytics assume an increasingly important role as the game moves towards launch and post-launch refinement

A game needs to have good analysis and design, but the artistic side needs to be good too. Now every part of the game must be top notch. (Senior Game Artist)

Second, for the organisation to benefit from analytics, artistic decisions inevitably need to be commercially savvy While organisational slack is a key enabler of creativity (Castañer & Campos, 2002), there needs to be “just enough” slack—not too much or too little (Amabile, 1998). In other words, although it is essential to allow creative experimentation it is also important to avoid pouring excessive organisational resources into commercially unviable ideas. This requires th artists to become commercially aware and to consider the business perspective in their creative processes.

At Rovio, for many, this required a shift in mindset and work practices. While some artists embraced the opportunit to listen to the consumers of their creations and to be guided by these insights, others had reservations about bein influenced by external perspectives and, particularly, the commercial dimension. Rovio provided ample training and sup port to encourage a more analytical and commercially aware approach to creative decisions, but acknowledged that not all employees were open to joining this journey. Sometimes, it was decided it was best to amicably part ways.

Part of the creativity, as with any fields, for example painting and being a music artist, is that you're doing your art. […] It's not as free anymore and you have to think of the business perspective. Some designers and artists don't like it, for others it doesn't matter. […] There's always been the same discussions about making money with art and is that correct and do I want to make this kind of games. (Executive Producer)

We lost a lot of really good talent back then, but also one of the reasons why we lost good talent was the fact that they realized that this is not their environment. […] They want to make different types of games. So in a way, even though we lost talent, it's better for everyone. (Vice President, Game Development)

## 4.4 | Encourage creative experimentation and learning

Learning through experimentation and data-based evaluation of ideas has been found to be an enabler of BA valu realisation in various industry settings (Viaene & Van den Bunder, 2011), such as finance (Davenport & Harris, 2007 and manufacturing (Dremel et al., 2017). Our findings suggest that in creative organisations too, analytical insights can provide a highly valuable mechanism for learning about the impact of creative choices, and for improving such choices over time. This learning process can be greatly facilitated through frequent creative experimentation, where creative choices can be isolated and tested incrementally. We also found that while the principle is not unique to cre ative organisations, there may be some additional benefits as well as challenges in applying this principle in such organisations.

First, if possible, it is beneficial to shift the purpose of creative processes from producing highly polished and completed creative outputs to a more flexible, iterative, and incremental creative refinement process. Granular crea tive experiments, where feasible, may help to reduce the “inherent unknowability” (Caves, 2000) in creative deci sions. Risk taking and active experimentation are also important drivers of creativity (Andriopoulos, 2001; Mumford et al., 2002).

In the right organisational settings, these features could emerge naturally, as “the spirit of exploration and chal lenge is what designers bring to their workplace" (Michlewski. 2008. p. 385). However, a potential barrier to over come is that data-based experiments often require a targeted and granular approach, whereas creative professionals may be inclined to work with more holistic concepts as part of their creative experimentation. If the creative product is presented to the end-user in its final, polished form, it is difficult to determine how the many different desig choices it comprises affect the overall user perception and satisfaction. Even if it proves possible to ascertain som impacts, there is little that can be done about it.

At Rovio, the move to free-to-play games that were developed as an ever-evolving platform,<sup>2</sup> meant that not only could the impact of individual design decisions be tested through analytics, but the learning can also be immedi ately applied to refine these design decisions. This iterative creative experimentation and refinement cycle allowed to shift the focus from making creative decisions that the organisation thought might delight customers to creativ decisions they knew would delight customers.

Now we can go to soft launch with not quite the full game and then get the initial learnings and develop that based on the feedback or the results. That was unheard of in the premium era, when we just wanted to make a super-polished game and go from that. So that's a huge design thinking change. (Senior Product Manager)

Sometimes, in the game industry, people might say that we want to make games that we want to play ourselves. But with the help of this analysis and numbers, we are more directed towards what people would like to play. […] I think the main reason for the analysis is to listen to people, and I think its basi cally just listening to your audience and fans. (Senior Game Artist)

Second, the shift towards creative experimentation needs to be supported through easy access to required data and organisational mechanisms that encourage reflection and sharing of learning. Timely access to the right data enables listening more carefully to the customers in both making and evaluating creative decisions.

At Rovio, as part of the transformation, the creative teams were trusted with access to all relevant data require to inform design decisions. In turn, creative teams were expected to be accountable for the continuous refinemen of their product. Of course, all of this is only possible if the right data is captured in the first place, which is wh Rovio carefully embedded key performance data capturing mechanisms in each game.

Finally, it is important to encourage the sharing of learning between creative teams, as it can provide valuable inspiration and avoid the need to “reinvent the wheel”. Encouraging information sharing and collaboration has been suggested as one of the most important things management can do to support creativity (Amabile, 1998). A “designers take great pride in breaking rules, subverting accepted norms and refusing to align with something that has already been tested and tried” (Michlewski, 2008, p. 386), some additional encouragement may be needed to ensure creative teams are open to listen to feedback and willing to engage in collective learning.

At Rovio, key performance metrics of each game are now shared weekly with all employees. Creative teams can identify successful design decisions made by other teams and reach out for insights. Regular get-togethers bot within and across the teams focus on sharing recent successes and challenges. These events often lead to new idea and preparation for the events encourages reflection and consolidation of learning within each team

If there is a huge bump in some of the games, then they can chat with them: how did you guys do that there was a big boost last week? (Executive Producer)

On the product lead level, designer level and product manager level, they all have get-togethers with their peers, and also we have product sessions every month, a longer session where different things are shared for the whole company—we did this in our product and it had this much impact. (Executive Producer)

## 4.5 | Make data-inspired, not data-driven, creative decisions

Analytics can be a powerful tool to support and enhance creative decisions, but it needs to be carefully balance with retaining artistic freedom and risk taking. First, creative organisations should be very cautious about excessive measurement and evaluation. It can undermine intrinsic motivation (Amabile, 1998), which is one of the core enablers of creativity (Anderson et al., 2014; Shalley & Gilson, 2004) and a major part of artistic identity and values— a potent way to kill creativity is when “new ideas are met not with open minds but with time-consuming lavers o evaluation” (Amabile, 1998, p. 83). Second, analytics should not be allowed to become a tool for discouraging “failure” or risk taking (Mumford et al., 2002) nor for diverting too much focus to tinkering with existing ideas rathe than pursuing new, bold sources of inspiration (“companies that want to avoid getting stuck in a rut should b especially wary of opinions of customers” (Sutton, 2002, pp. 102–103)). Creativity tends to flourish in environments where management takes a long-term view (Andriopoulos, 2001).

Therefore, while the ultimate goal of an analytics-oriented transformation is often framed as becoming data driven (LaValle et al., 2011; Watson, 2016), for creative decisions a more appropriate goal may be what Rovio's Senior Data Analyst described as “not data-driven, but maybe data-inspired decision-making”. Unquestionably, dat can play an important role in creative decision-making. However, the increasing use of data does not make human expertise any less important. It just refocuses this expertise where it can add the most value. What are these ke areas? The opening quote at the beginning of this paper from Rovio's Senior Data Analyst hints at the answer:

If you don't understand the why? part, then we might make wrong decisions. So it's not trivial. Constantly, we have these talks that what does this actually mean and how to choose. So I would say it's not data-driven, but maybe data-inspired decision-making.

In other words, while analytics can provide reliable answers to what is happening, it does usually not explain wh this is happening. Interpreting the answers provided by BA requires careful consideration of alternative explanation and of the broader context. Also, any one metric is imperfect, and its true meaning often has many possible interpre tations. As such, defining what and how to measure as well as interpreting measurement results requires huma expertise and expert judgement.

Sometimes we make a decision that, OK, we don't trust the numbers. We pick our path because maybe there is something even bigger to be gained later on. Because, sometimes, if you just look at the numbers, it might confuse you in the bigger picture. […] It is still a discussion. Even if numbers point to one direction, it's not yet a decision. (Senior Producer)

Why the metric is so? If it's up or down, okay, it looks better, but do we understand why? And if we don't understand why, then it might be just something that actually, in the long term, is bad for the player. (Senior Data Analyst)

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, asking the right and insightful questions remains a deeply human endeavour. Trying to measure the impact of every minor design detail is unlikely to be a good use of resources Rather, employees should be educated of the various possibilities offered by BA, and then encouraged to constantl look out for important questions in their daily work that could be addressed using BA. In other words, expert knowledge helps the organisation to focus on the highest value-add questions while BA reduces guesswork about the correct answers.

People need to start asking questions. When they start to do that, then we need to supply the answers one way or another. There's many ways to organize getting the answers but if there's no questions, you cannot provide answers. No-one wants to hear an answer if there was no question in the first place. (Senior Data Analyst)

In summary, it is important to balance data-based insights and human judgement. Placing too much trust on intu ition often leads to suboptimal results, but the same is true for allowing data to drive decisions without sufficient expert scrutiny. At Rovio, BA did not replace expert judgement and creative thinking, but supported the creativ teams in gauging how well their ideas were working. Both artistic intuition and analytical insight are now carefull balanced in creative decision-making. Rather than a one size fits all approach, the strengths and weaknesses of bot sources of insight are carefully considered. Ultimately, which pathway to pursue is usually a decision for huma experts. We believe most organisations would benefit from following a similar “data-inspired” approach to creativ decision-making.

Game development is now a marriage of data and creativity. (Head of Studio)

## 5 | REFLECTIONS ON CREATIVE ANALYTICS

Transformation of decision processes and organisational structures is often necessary to fully benefit from BA in an industry (Sharma et al., 2014; Torres et al., 2018). However, navigating the transition can be more challenging in cre ative industries, which need to strike a delicate balance between artistic and commercial logics and where the bene fits of analytics need to be carefully managed to avoid unintended consequences on organisational creativ capabilities. The five principles discussed in the previous section played a central role in helping Rovio balance th best features of analytics and creativity. How relevant may these findings be to understanding and guiding analytics adoption more broadly?

## 5.1 | Practical implications

In considering the degree to which these five principles may apply to other organisations within and outside of crea tive industries, it is important to recognise that (1) Rovio could be described as a “digital native”, (2) its products and services are primarily digital, (3) both artist and engineering cultures were already a key part of its organisational fab ric, (4) the organisation has always had to balance artistic and economic goals, and (5) it operates in the creative sector.

First, as Rovio was founded as a software development company, the organisation had a solid understanding of digital technologies already prior to the transformation. Digitally savvy organisation may be better placed to act on Principle 1, particularly in terms of BA tool implementation. That said, regarding building shared analytics values as part of Principle 1, analytics use in creative processes was new to Rovio and the organisation had to substantiall change its creative processes and culture (as per Principle 4). Therefore, it seems likely that creative organisation with a less digital background can still learn from the approaches and challenges faced by Rovio in relation to Princi ples 1 and 4. However, such organisations may need to be even more mindful of the related challenges that Rovio faced.

Second, it seems likely that organisations with a digital product and/or service portfolio will find the application of Principle 4 to be easier. Digital products can often be more readily and cost effectively tweaked than physica ones, enabling faster iterative experimentation. Incorporating analytics within the product is also more straightfor ward in the digital domain. Principle 4 is likely to be important even if an organisation's products are physical, as prio research has shown the importance of iterative experimentation and learning as an enabler of analytics value in man settings. However, for some physical products and services, the learning cycles may need to be longer (e.g., in archi tecture, where the implementation and feedback loop could be measured in years). In other instances, the physica nature of the products and services may affect the ability to evaluate the results of creative experiments (e.g., in the atre, elements of the play may be easily tweaked from one performance to the next, but it could be difficult to gathe data to gauge audience reactions to a specific change). Sometimes, it may be necessary to combine automated and manual data collection approaches, (e.g., in a museum, embedding digital sensors to understand visitor movements combined with customer surveys and/or observations may be needed to build a full understanding of visitor experi ences and their interactions with the creative space).

Third, as Rovio's products had always required combining arts (i.e., the game narrative and design) with engineer ing (i.e., the programming to bring the game to life), this likely provided a foundation for implementing Principl 2. However, the use of analytics in creative decisions was a new way of doing things for the organisation and required a substantial amount of adjustment and relearning from the creative teams. It was similarly a learning jour ney for the analytics professionals, many of whom were brought in from outside the organisation. We therefor believe the challenges and approaches described in relation to Principle 2 would be similarly relevant for organisa tions within creative industries which have not previously had an engineering or analytics culture as part of thei organisational fabric. However, the learning curve maybe steeper for such organisations and hence it would be wis to dedicate more resources and, if possible, ample time for this change.

Fourth, although operating within the creative industry, Rovio is a commercial organisation. The need to juggle artistic and economic goals was, therefore, not new for the organisation. However, how the balance was achieve changed substantially during the transformation. The artistic and commercial logics had previously been fairly sepa rated through different functional areas, but during the transformation became much more blended and close together. Therefore, the challenges and strategies described in relation to Principle 3 will probably apply to othe types of creative organisations as well—although some may find they have a longer bridge to cross. In addition, it is important to be mindful that in those creative sectors where economic rationale is of secondary priorit (e.g., museums), the desirable balance in the application of Principle 3 may be different. It is likely that customer experiences, perceptions, and interaction with the creative artefacts would still be a key focus in such settings, but the purpose of the analytics may be more focused on improving the hedonic rather than commercial aspects

Fifth, how relevant might Rovio's creative analytics principles be in contexts outside of creative industries? Although our focus in this paper has been on creative industries where the balancing act between creativity and productivity is of central importance, this challenge is also relevant to other industries (e.g., Chang & Birkett, 2004) Therefore, we believe that the five creative analytics principles may be useful for businesses outside of creativ industries as well. However, depending on industry features, not all principles may apply to the same extent or ma need to be adapted. For example, the challenge of balancing creative and commercial objectives may be less pro nounced in industries where the focus of creative processes is highly commercial or utilitarian. As another example outside of creative industries and creative decisions, Principle 2 may still be relevant to blend subject matter and ana lytics expertise, but the challenges may be more focused on balancing analytics with expert judgement (not necessarily creativity).

In summary, we believe the five principles can provide valuable guidance for analytics adoption within creativ industries, and possibly beyond. However, not all principles may apply in the same way or to the same extent. An organisation looking to follow these principles should consider their similarities and differences regarding the fea tures discussed in this section to decide which of these principles to adopt. We believe, though, that the fifth princi ple of data-inspired decision-making as a high-level approach is likely to be beneficial in most creative decisio settings. It is also important to note that the pursuit of creative analytics is best thought of as a journey rather than destination, as maintaining an optimal balance requires continuous reflection and adjustment.

## 5.2 | Theoretical implications

From a theory standpoint, we believe Rovio's creative analytics journey takes a step towards a better understanding of two key issues highlighted at the beginning of the paper.

First, our study addresses a gap in research on BA adoption and use in creative organisations. Our findings show that context does indeed matter and that creative organisations can face special challenges in BA adoption. The need to bring together art and engineering cultures, while carefully preserving the best features of both. They ma also need to substantially shift work practices, particularly in blending analytics into traditionally highly intuition based creative decision processes. With regards to these issues, our study provides a consultable record (Walsham, 1995) of a successful creative analytics transformation, identifies five creative analytics principles which contribute to the emerging understanding of the drivers of effective BA use (Trieu, 2017), and sheds light on how organisational decision processes need to change to benefit from BA (Sharma et al., 2014).

Second, our study provides insights on the balancing of data and intuition, which has been raised as an important but not well-understood issue in BA research (Abbasi et al., 2016). First, our findings suggest that the optimal balance may depend on the nature of the creative decision. Analytics appears to be particularly powerful for incrementa refinements to existing ideas, i.e., meeting existing demand through better understanding of current product perfor mance, how product enhancements are received by end-users, and for ensuring that not too much organisationa resources are poured into commercially unviable ideas. Human judgement and creativity, however, may be a more potent source of new and fundamentally different ideas and concepts, i.e., extending demand beyond what cus tomers may be familiar with. Second, our findings suggest that in creative decision-making, it is likely to be more ben eficial to lean towards a data-inspired rather than a data-driven approach. Analytics can provide highly valuable inpu into creative decisions, but human judgement plays a key role in interpreting and contextualising this guidance. Also an excessive focus on analytics may in fact risk negatively affecting the organisation's creative capabilities

## 5.3 | Avenues for future research

There are many avenues for future research on creative analytics, but considering our findings, we would like to highlight three.

First, the principles for creative analytics would benefit from further study. While we have offered some conjectures on the extent to which the five principles may apply to other organisations within and outside creative industries, furthe empirical work would undoubtedly help validate the principles in other settings, reveal any special considerations in apply ing the principles in such settings, and could reveal additional principles. It may also be valuable to compare the merits of “data-driven” and “data-inspired” approaches to analytics use in other creative decisions and settings. Are there, perhaps, any organisational circumstances in which a data-driven approach gives better creative outcomes? Also, which other types of decisions and/or decision contexts may benefit from the data-inspired approach to analytics?

Second, the Rovio case focuses on using analytics to evaluate the outputs of human creativity. While this is the primary use case for analytics in creative settings for now, the rapid development of artificial intelligence increasingl raises the possibility of using analytics to produce certain creative outputs (e.g., Marks, 2019). Therefore, looking fur ther into the future, there may be a fundamental shift in analytics use as part of creative pursuits. This would open up a raft of new questions and issues in effective creative analytics use (for some examples of these issues and th creative potential of artificial intelligence, please see Amabile, 2020).

Finally, beyond creative analytics, a broader conclusion that can be drawn from the observation that decision context matters is that there is a need to go beyond general models of BA benefit realisation (e.g., Clark Jr et al., 2007; Mikalef et al., 2018, 2019; Seddon et al., 2017). While such models present a valuable foundation for understanding BA benefit realisation processes at a macro-level, the decision type and organisational context (in our case. creative decisions) appear to affect BA benefit realisation at a micro-level. Future research on BA benefit reali sation should therefore also include consideration of different types of organisational decisions and BA use

## 6 | CONCLUSION

As BA investment and impact continues to grow across industries, fewer and fewer organisations can afford to ignore the need to better leverage data in their decision-making. While there has been considerable adoption of analytics in structured and routine decisions, its use continues to expand to other types of decision-making. The Rovio case study provides valuable lessons on the use of analytics for creative decision-making and how to balance dat and human judgement for more evidence-based creative decisions. Carefully managed, this approach—which w refer to as creative analytics—has the potential not only to support better commercial outcomes from creative prod ucts but to spur creativity through experimentation, learning, and growth.

We hope that this paper provides organisations with useful insights to navigate the challenging but rewarding journey towards better informed creative decision-making, and that the paper provides researchers with a usefu stepping stone for further exploring analytics use in creative settings!

## APPENDIX A: RESEARCH METHOD

This study aims to contribute to a better understanding of analytics-enabled transformation in creative organisations and the practices that enable such transformation. As analytics use in this context has received very limited atten tion, our study is largely exploratory and uses qualitative methods. Our primary data is based on a single revelator case study (Yin, 2008) and our analysis follows an interpretive approach (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995)

In terms of our data collection, Rovio can be considered as a revelatory case because of its success in transitioning to an analytics-oriented culture whilst retaining their artistic freedom and willingness to take creativ risks, as well as due to the speed of their organisational transformation (achieving the change in 4 years). The primary data source for this case study were over 12 h of in-depth, face-to-face interviews conducted in 2016. Our inter viewees (see Table A1 for the full list) were from a broad variety of roles, representing the business, artistic, and technology domains that are all critical components in successful game development. All interviews were recorded and conducted onsite at Rovio's headquarters (in Espoo, Finland), allowing us to also gain some firsthand insight into the work environment and culture at Rovio. The interview data was complemented with other relevant sources (such as annual reports, press releases, presentations, and news reports). In particular, the over 400-page Offering Circular (Rovio Entertainment Corporation, 2017) published in preparation for the company's entry to the stock market provided a detailed and highly valuable source for further triangulation and reinforced the importance BA played in th transformation journey. Finally, we reached out to Rovio to seek comment on our preliminary findings, and to fil remaining gaps in our data.

For our data analysis, we followed an interpretive approach (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995). We began by developing narratives to describe the analytics-enabled transformation journey at Rovio. This helped us under stand the key events and decisions related to analytics use at Rovio. We also used the data to develop an outline of Rovio's game development process, and to understand how the various phases of this creative process were shaped by the transformation

These narratives, in turn, helped us develop a deeper understanding of the practices that enabled Rovio to succeed with their transformation. We initially focused our attention on identifying general leadership principles for BA transformation. As part of this analysis, we considered not only deliberate management actions but also organisational circumstances, routines, and culture which enabled transformation. We went back and forth betwee the emerging insights, data, and literature multiple times to continuously deepen our understanding. Initially, thi resulted in a set of six principles, which we later combined into four, and then re-extended into five. However, ou developing understanding of the phenomenon through this iterative reflection process eventually led us to recon sider our overall focus. While the case study presented useful insights regarding general leadership principles for analytics adoption, it became apparent that Rovio's transformation was even more revelatory and novel from th perspective of analytics use in creative decisions. We reanalysed the data again from this perspective and, following a few more iterations, arrived at the set of five principles for creative analytics, as presented in Section 4
