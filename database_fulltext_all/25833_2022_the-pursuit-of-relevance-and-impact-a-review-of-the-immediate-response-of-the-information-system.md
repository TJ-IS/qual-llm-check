# The pursuit of relevance and impact: A review of the immediate response of the information systems field to COVID-19

Stan Karanasios

University of Queensland Business School, Business Information Systems, The University of Queensland, Brisbane, Queensland, Australia

Correspondence

Stan Karanasios, University of Queensland Business School. Business Information Systems, The University of Queensland Brisbane, QLD 4072, Australia. Email: s.karanasios@uq.edu.au

## Abstract

The COVID-19 pandemic demanded an immediate response from the research community, including adjustment to how researchers undertake research. As in many fields, information systems scholars attempted ‘rapid response’ research that addresses the immediate crisis. This paper reviews the state of the immediate research and scholarly discussion in the information systems field. Seventy-one journal papers are reviewed. The review categorises nine thematic areas and one broad area of discussion. The methods by which information systems scholars address immediate research questions are identified. The contributions are discussed, future research directions are outlined and a model is proposed that traces the pathway from initiation of research/ generation of new knowledge to practice impact. A further model is proposed to help information systems scholars clearly establish the link between the study of information systems phenomena and COVID-19.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The COVID-19 pandemic led to a surge in new research in many scholarly fields. It demanded both rapid research and a re-imagining of how we live and work. It also demonstrated that, with concentrated resources and effort breakthroughs are possible in health, medicine and social policy. In line with calls for high-impact research (Díaz Andrade, Techatassanasoontorn, & Ou, 2019; Majchrzak, Markus, & Wareham, 2014; Pan & Pee, 2020; Wiener et al., 2018), the field of information systems (IS) equally attempted to respond with ‘rapid response’ research

This research aligned too with the demand for greater relevance and pathways to impact by business schools around the world (e.g., Stern, 2021).

The pandemic has emphasised the critical role of IS, with many of the challenges that have emerged (e.g., working and learning from home, digital transformation, the design of digital contact tracing applications, interorganisational information sharing and government-to-citizen communication) (Chan, Sadreddin, & Ahuja, 2020; Lillie, Kark, Mossburg, & Tweardy, 2020; McKinsey, 2020; Xiao & Fan, 2020) falling within the area of expertise of I researchers (e.g., Allen, Karanasios, & Norman, 2014; Elbanna, Bunker, Levine, & Sleigh, 2019; Pan, Pan, & Leidner, 2012). Articles in leading medical journals such as The Lancet (Whitelaw, Mamas, Topol, & Van Spall, 2020 and Nature Medicine (Budd et al., 2020) also underscore the critical role of IS in the public health response: ‘the appli cation of digital technologies in pandemic management and response, highlighting ways in which successful countrie have adopted these technologies for pandemic planning, surveillance, testing, contact tracing, quarantine, and healt care’ (Whitelaw et al., 2020, p. 435).

This opinion paper examines the immediate response of the IS field. While there will be a long tail of researc related to COVID-19, and pandemics more broadly, this paper focuses on the immediate research and views publi shed/available online in the year 2020, and subsequently synthesises what immediate knowledge has been contrib uted to addressing and responding to COVID-19. The second objective of this opinion paper is to build on that work to outline further opportunities and present an agenda to guide future research.

To address these objectives, 71 articles, from 14 leading IS journals, are reviewed. This does not capture all the possible ways that IS scholars may contribute their research and ideas. For instance, there was discussion on the Association for Information Systems COVID-19 forum, in conference papers and discussion at conference panels, and dissemination of ideas in outlets such as The Conversation or through institutional forums. However, it does pro vide a snapshot of the immediate peer-reviewed research and ideas. The review identifies nine thematic areas and one broad area of discussion. The immediate contributions are discussed, such as how the new research connects with traditional IS research; how IS research informs the response to COVID-19; and the pathways to impact. Future areas of research are suggested, and a model is proposed that identifies how IS scholars can more clearly establis the relevance of their IS study phenomena to COVID-19

The next section of the paper outlines the process of review. Following this, a synthesis is presented showing the dis tribution of the literature across the thematic areas. A discussion follows outlining the different contributions of the IS community, further areas of connection and considerations for IS scholars undertaking research related to COVID-19

## 2 | THE REVIEW

The purpose of this review is to synthesise the immediate research in the field. The approach followed is rooted in contemporary practice (Okoli & Schabram, 2010; Paré, Trudel, Jaana, & Kitsiou, 2014; Vial, 2019; Webster & Watson, 2002), but is also innovative, as demanded by the purpose of the review. The review can be defined as a qualitative systematic review of a nascent and fluid area of research (Paré et al., 2014). Figure 1 outlines the process followed.

The Scopus database was used to undertake the search and filtering of papers. While systematic literature reviews in IS typically rely on the College of Senior Scholars' basket of eight journals (e.g., Li, Gao, & Mao, 2014), a wider net was cast, encompassing IS journals ranked as ‘A star’ or ‘A’ according to the Australian Business Deans Council (ABDC list) list. This list is consistent with other rankings of IS journals, such as the Chartered Association of Business School (CABS) 3/4 star journals in the UK (see Appendix A for the ABDC list of IS journals). This was don because relying only on the basket of eight journals would have limited the number of papers identified.

An advanced search was undertaken in Scopus by creating a query written to extract papers where (a) the searc terms appeared in the paper title, keywords or abstract, (b) in the selected ABDC IS journals UK (see Appendix A) (c) in the year 2020. The search terms used were ‘COVID’, ‘COVID\*’ (which would capture COVID-19, COVID19)

To validate the search results, a manual search was undertaken for several of the journals. A manual search was also undertaken of the journals in the basket of eight as editorials or discussion papers are not always indexed in Scopus. The manual search located the editorial b Osch, Leidner, and Beath (2020) and the paper by Pietz, McCoy, and Wilck (2020), which was not found in the Scopus search as Scopus had incorrectly indexed it as 2019 (see Appendix C). This round of validation added four to the list of papers and thus confirms the high reliability of the search conducted.

Papers had to fulfil several criteria to be included in the study. For empirical papers, the data must be related to COVID-19. Empirical papers were excluded if they used data from another crisis or if data were from prior to COVID-19. For the non-empirical papers, the goal was to capture the ideas, reflections and strategies from the IS field relevant to COVID-19. Opinion, theory development, editorials and conceptual papers were included on thi basis. Editorials, in particular, give voice to prominent scholars (e.g., Dorothy E Leidner from JAIS, Arun Rai from MISQ and Richard Watson from MISQE). One conceptual paper included 12 contributors, each with a unique contri bution (Dwivedi et al., 2020), while another had five contributors (Thomas et al., 2020). Pure technical papers were excluded (e.g., developing a cloud-based internet of things to monitor disease diagnosis). Table 1 provides a summar of the inclusion and exclusion criteria with examples. Examples of excluded papers are included in Appendix D

After filtering, 71 papers were included in the review. A list of the papers is available in Reference section Table 2 indicates the journals included and the number of papers per journal. Two of the journals, European Journa of Information Systems and International Journal of Information Management had dedicated special issues to th COVID-19 pandemic and used rapid review cycles, as reflected in the number of papers included from those two journals (e.g., Ågerfalk, Conboy, & Myers, 2020; Dwivedi et al., 2020).

Details of each paper were entered into a spreadsheet for analysis. Classification of evidence types (empirical non-empirical) was based on whether the paper used empirical data or was conceptual (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004) For both non-empirical and empirical papers, an inductive analysis was undertaken of the thematic areas in terms of IS research focus and the link to COVID-19 that formed the primary focus of the investigation (e.g., tracing, working from home). Each paper's keywords and abstract were used inductively to produce broad thematic clusters (Paré et al., 2014). Sub-themes were identified within each thematic cluster sub-themes. Because the frame of reference of the sample of papers was narrow, this method made classification straightforward. While earlier keyword an thematic classification schemes for IS exist (e.g., Bacon & Fitzgerald, 2001; Barki, Rivard, & Talbot, 1993; Ein-Dor & Segev, 1993; Mentzas, 1994), they are not appropriate for IS research related to COVID-19 or much of the broader contemporary IS research. More recent attempts (Fallery & Rodhain, 2009) capture high-level themes such as evalua tion, change, IT markets and outsourcing, none of which are relevant. Papers were also analysed based on methodol ogy, using the paper authors' own terminology (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Guo & Sheffield, 2008).

## 3 | FINDINGS

Of the total papers, 21 were empirical and 50 were non-empirical. The percentage of non-empirical papers was higher than other thematic reviews of IS literature (e.g., Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Guo & Sheffield, 2008), which reflects the narrow period between the emergence of COVID-19 and the writing of this paper. To understand the thematic areas of the research, a review of the paper along with an analysis of the keywords and abstract was under taken to produce broad thematic clusters inductively. Table 3 presents the thematic areas. It comprises nine specifi thematic areas and one broad/reflective area of discussion. The thematic areas cover a range of topics such as digita contact tracing systems, information and communication, how to fight COVID-19 and IT management. Thus, the are both new domains of research as well as entrenched topics in existing IS literature. Some of the research cut across these thematic areas. However, this split serves the purpose in first demonstrating the research and progress within each category before connections are drawn across these levels of analysis

Because the review is also interested in how IS researchers do ‘rapid IS research’, Table 4 includes a summary of the methods used in the empirical studies. Each of these studies is unpacked in more detail below as each thematic area is discussed. The methods used in the studies show some consistency with IS research (with some adaptation such as using video conferencing to undertake interviews), but also reflect the challenges in collecting data during lockdown and disruption. As such, there is a reliance on surveys and social media digital traces as well as secondar data such as websites, dashboards and documents, and novel experiments.

The empirical studies delve into how various factors that inform the design of digital contact tracing systems explaining adoption and failure and establishing links to how they fight COVID-19 (Rowe, Ngwenyama, & Richet, 2020; Trang, Trenz, Weiger, Tarafdar, & Cheung, 2020; Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020). A key focus of the research was how the different design specifications and features contribute to their mass acceptance (Rowe et al., 2020; Trang et al., 2020). This raises the question of how to study the design and deployment of tracing apps and their rapid development cycles. In one of the first studies to examine design, an experiment on the social/selfbenefit appeals, privacy designs and two convenience designs with 518 users of a fictitious application found that ‘critics’, ‘advocates’ and ‘undecided’ individuals consider different aspects of the application. This suggests it is imperative that government focus on communicating societal benefits and minimising privacy risks when the major ity of citizens are ‘critics’. This is likely to be a major issue for future plans to encourage mass use of digital contact tracing systems given the lack of benefits and low uptake during 2020.

The challenge of framing digital contact tracing is studied in the case of France's experience with the ‘Stop COVID’ app. Drawing on discourse analysis from the French newspaper Le Monde, the failure of the app is attrib uted to the government's solutionist approach that sidelined important stakeholder groups. It thus lacked transparency and was disconnected from reality (Rowe et al., 2020). Both empirical and non-empirical papers that considered digital contact tracing argued that this solutionist approach—followed in many countries—is a slippery slope that could lead to privacy abuses (Dwivedi et al., 2020; Fahey & Hino, 2020; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Aroles, 2020 Rowe, 2020; Rowe et al., 2020). These studies offer insights into the implications of government framing and imposi tion of digital contact tracing systems

Perhaps the most critical research on digital contact tracing systems attempts to show a link between thei deployment and a reduction in COVID-19 cases (Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020). Based on data from China, Italy, Germany, Singapore, South Korea and USA, an argument is made that the apps were found to be highly significantl correlated with a reduction in the spread of COVID-19 in their countries’ (Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020, p. 405). Thi ambitious claim does come with caveats. If further held up, however, such findings can provide evidence that wil help governments frame the use of digital contact tracing systems so as to encourage mass acceptance (Row et al., 2020; Trang et al., 2020).

The non-empirical papers focused predominantly on the dilemmas facing digital contact tracing systems and suggested ways to better manage and encourage acceptance (Cranor, 2020; Dwivedi et al., 2020; Fahey & Hino, 2020; Riemer, Ciriello, Peter, & Schlagwein, 2020; Rowe et al., 2020; Savage, 2020). Different options for gov ernance (who will govern the roll-out) and accountability enforcement (how strictly will adoption and use be enforced) are examined (Riemer et al., 2020), in line with the concerns around governance and the collective action problem that surround all digital contract tracing systems. As part of this, examples from around the world are used to demonstrate the different pathways that governments have taken. Different features and functions of apps and national experiences are also discussed (e.g., from Nigeria, Singapore, Uruguay) (Dwivedi et al., 2020; Milano Vallespir, & Viola, 2020).

Like any system (e.g., mobile application, health IS, etc.), scholars identify the novel issues of privacy, data protection and surveillance that arise from digital contact tracing (Dwivedi et al., 2020; Fahey & Hino, 2020; Row et al., 2020; Savage, 2020). These contributions underscore the possible negative residual effect and th normalisation of surveillance (Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Aroles, 2020; Rowe, 2020), and suggest that governments have played up how the end justifies the means. For instance, the French government emphasises social good to encourage uptake, while downplaying long-term concerns (Rowe, 2020). This is an important consideration for these systems at a time when many governments are heavily invested in techno-optimism. Combined, these papers revea the tension between ensuring accurate and timely data and maintaining privacy. They further show that government framing of the digital contact tracing systems matters.

## 3.2 | Information and communication

COVID-19 is the first pandemic where social media and digital technology play such an expansive role in communi cation, information dissemination and citizen engagement. While this creates new opportunities for government citizen engagement, it also gives rise to new challenges such as the ‘infodemic’ (Zarocostas, 2020).

Tapping into digital trace data, studies examine social media use and communication by institutional actors (Casalegno, Civera, & Cortese, 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Rao, Vemprala, Akello, & Valecha, 2020), offering instructive findings to inform institutional social media-mediated pandemic communication:

1. Citizens engage more with textual content, with citizens caring more about the textual content of a post, rather than whether it contains a picture or video. Posts presented with multiple dialogic features (e.g., posed as ques tions) had higher citizen engagement (Chen et al., 2020). These findings come from an analysis of 1,411 post from the ‘Healthy China’ Sina Weibo account of the National Health Commission to understand how Chines central government agencies used social media to promote citizen engagement during the COVID-19 crisis

2. Government actors are more reassuring in their communication, whereas non-government actors are more alarmist. The implication of this is possible confusion amongst citizens. This is based on a study of 26,264 tweets from institutions in the USA such as the CDC and Department of Health and Human Services, government actors such as POTUS and Governor of New York, international organisations, such as the WHO, and media outlets such a the Washington Post (Rao et al., 2020).

3. To overcome knowledge-behaviour gaps and the stressful impacts of ‘over’ and ‘under-communication’, govern ment and broadcasters should communicate in ways that: (a) synchronise communication; (b) empower their audi ence by creating tailored knowledge translated for specific groups; and (c) create a stakeholder network tha develops relationships between institutions and communities to reduce the need for erroneous interpretation of government information. These findings were based on broadcaster communication across multiple mediums in Italy in the early months of the pandemic (Casalegno et al., 2020).

Studies also examine individual-level information behaviour (Eachempati, Srivastava, & Zhang, 2021; Laato, Islam, Islam, & Whelan, 2020; Pan, Cui, & Qian, 2020). As with studies that focus on institutional level actors' com munication, these studies draw on digital trace data to examine public sentiment and opinion. In Iran, the USA, India China and Italy, public opinion expressed on YouTube, Twitter and Facebook revealed unified negative sentiment on all platforms, with public opinion in Italy and India showing the most negative sentiment due to the severity of their lockdowns (Eachempati et al., 2021). Negative sentiment focused on impacts on the economy, health, lockdowns and education. While this paper focuses on sentiment and may demonstrate the cathartic role of social media, other studies examine how individuals use social media. Based on a survey of 299 social media users in Bangladesh, study addresses the uncertainty around health information in the early stages of the pandemic. An argument is made that an individual's trust in online information and their perceived information overload are strong predictors that they will share unverified information (Laato et al., 2020). These factors, along with a person's perceived vulnerabilit to COVID-19 and likely severity of illness, influence ‘cyberchondria’ (anxiety about health that is exacerbated by using the internet to search for health-related information). During strict lockdown in China in the early months of the pandemic, individuals (37 people, including 29 people from six families, four community workers, and four onlin emergent individuals) were found to use social media to adjust to new circumstances and maintain normality. Socia media as a tool to continue normality was also found in a survey of 477 social media users. It also revealed that socia distancing has a psychological impact that increased the intensity of use of social media (Chakraborty, Kumar, Upadhyay, & Dwivedi, 2021). This forms part of how individuals combat extreme levels of uncertainty and complex ity during a pandemic (Chakraborty et al., 2021). These studies show that people responded to the stress of COVID 19 and social distancing by adopting coping strategies that used social media for a range of activities such as learning, creating awareness, continuing routines and information dissemination. An interesting change discussed at the individual level was shifts in how individuals self-disclose information on social media. It was found that information changed from private to public knowledge (inside-out) and from readily shared to hidden (outside-in) during COVID 19, highlighting changing behaviour in social media communities (Nabity-Grover, Cheung, & Thatcher, 2020).

Information dissemination and data visualisation became part of everyday activity as part of tracing, modelling analysing and disseminating data about the status and spread of the infectious disease. A survey and ranking of dat and modelling on 25 popular analytics platforms from academia (e.g., MIT), media (e.g., New York Times), government (e.g., New York State department) provides some insights into this new area (Pietz et al., 2020). Such platforms coul be improved to systematically track, analyse and disseminate data about the status and spread of infectious disease and could also offer data on potential actions to reduce the spread of the disease. This could include communicating the timing and stringency of response measures taken in order to give citizens a clear understanding of why compli ance is needed with governmental intervention policies (Pietz et al., 2020).

The non-empirical works raised a related point: the rapidly evolving nature of COVID-19 means the challenge is not to represent things and their states (e.g., national mortality rates), rather to faithfully track changes in states and the events that cause these (Thomas et al., 2020). The information-intensive nature of COVID-19 meant that data, data curation and the visualisation of data on public-facing dashboards (a type of IS typically considered mundane were used to justify policy decisions, which in turn politicised such data to an unprecedented level. These dashboards include collective actions in science and scholarship, such as the CDC's COVID Data Tracker, Nextstrain developed by academics, or NY Time's curation on Excess Deaths, that help to save lives (Shankar, Jeng, Thomer, Weber, & Yoon, 2021). At the same time, as in the high-profile politicisation of the state of Florida's COVID-19 dashboard, they can become a source of controversy and evidence of how data are manipulated to fit certain narratives (Shankar et al., 2021). These dashboards play an important role in communicating to the public. Others suggest that social media could also be used to communicate to targeted populations. An example of this is the suggestion that universities could use Twitter to better inform students of the current situation, follow up on student concerns, and build shared understanding (Edghiem, Abualqumboz, & Mouzughi, 2020). Although as noted in the empirical work social media can exacerbate information challenges (Casalegno et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Rao et al., 2020).

The above themes point to COVID-19 as an information crisis as well as a health crisis (Xie et al., 2020), a fea ture commonly referred to as an infodemic (Zarocostas, 2020). This is an ongoing theme in crisis-related literature. It is argued that the growing role of platforms (e.g., Facebook, Twitter) may contribute to eroding trusted informatio sources (such as government health agencies) (Bunker, 2020). This can lead to the spread of fake news or misinformation, and mounting information overload (Dwivedi et al., 2020). To overcome this, one suggestion is that both government and platform providers collaborate and cooperate on either a legislated or voluntary basis to build on the trust in government to provide information as early and as often as possible in a crisis. This would amplif advice, and ensure algorithmic transparency, information and quality assurance for robust and trusted communica tion and information services overall (Bunker, 2020).

## 3.3 | Remote working and working from home

COVID-19 led to a major shift in how and where work is performed. Empirical studies explore the experience of individuals and organisations in the shift towards mass working from home. One of the biggest questions was how workers can navigate the challenge of working from home and the role of IT personnel, team collaboration and dif ferent organisational strategies to support it.

A study of 29 interviews shows a shift from workplace environmental affordances to technological affordances, with positive and negative effects on team collaboration (Waizenegger, McKenna, Cai, & Bendz, 2020). It demon strated that knowledge workers can use technology to achieve their goals during lockdown. This study also show that for some workers the home environment may not be conducive to work. The home environment matters, but other factors such as professional isolation, increased workload, stress and isolation were also raised (Carillo, Cachat-Rosset, Marsan, Saba, & Klarsfeld, 2021). While organisations attempted to smooth the transition to working from home, a survey of 1,574 workers in France shows that the level of support provided by organisations to hel employees better deal with the work environment change did not have any impact on adjustment (Carill et al., 2021). Focusing on conferencing systems such as Zoom—a specific tool of working from home—a text-mining study of 3.2 million tweets shows that web conferencing systems emerged as a social technology that led to a ‘new virtual togetherness’ (Hacker, Vom Brocke, Handali, Otto, & Schneider, 2020). This notion of togetherness partially conflicts with the other studies that show employees struggled with the transition to working from home (Carillo et al., 2021; Waizenegger et al., 2020). Twitter data may have a positivity bias that misses the nuances and more indepth issues related to working from home.

The non-empirical papers unpack these issues. Some scholars make the argument that the rapid shift to working from home provides an opportunity to embed collaboration systems into the core of organisational IT strategy, not only to transform businesses but also to generate new business models (Kodama, 2020). IS scholars see it as an opportunity and part of a continual process (Kodama, 2020; Richter, 2020) that links to digital transformation (e.g., Carroll & Conboy, 2020). A range of different technologies for future use was discussed such as Zoom and robots (Marks, 2020). This fits into the emerging stream of research on the future of work. A clas between traditional 9-to-5 work and remote work leading towards two possible futures is ruminated on with reference to foundational management scholars and concepts (Taylor, Ford, McGregor). One such option is a digital Taylorism characterised by machine-controlled worker arrangements, prioritisation of work over socia life and normalisation of the gig-economy. The other is ‘worker autonomy’ characterised by creativity, fluidity democratised decision-making and lifelong learning (Wang, Schlagwein, Cecez-Kecmanovic, & Cahalane, 2020) Here, scholars suggest what the impact of COVID-19 will be on the future of work beyond simply wher workers will be located.

## 3.4 | Digital transformation and digitalisation

Digital transformation and digitalisation have been two of the most significant IS research topics in recent years. Here scholars, examine the sociotechnical dynamics, of, how, organisations, educational institutions, hospitals and sporting events are digitalised.

Studies of the education sector in Italy show how institutions grappled with the transition to online and remote teaching (Bolisani, Fedeli, Bierema, & De Marchi, 2020; Iacuzzi, Fedele, & Garlatti, 2020), and the tensions that emerged between staff and parents (Iacuzzi et al., 2020). University lecturers used an existing online community of practice to build knowledge on the transition to online delivery. The community of practice experienced a 469% increase in interaction, which helped staff to transition to online teaching and manage the uncertainty (Bolisan et al., 2020). This suggests a need to share knowledge and learn about how to digitalise teaching, and that peer sup port may be more beneficial than organisational level response efforts (Carillo et al., 2021). A notable finding is a dif ference between institutions that had a strong digital infrastructure and culture prior to COVID-19 and those that did not (Iacuzzi et al., 2020).

Hospitals also took up digital interaction with patients, with many countries pivoting to telemedicine. In Pakistan, a study of patient adoption of telemedicine health services found that patient's perceived vulnerability and perceptions towards COVID-19 are important predictors of telemedicine adoption (Rahi, Khan, & Alghizzawi, 2021 in addition to the typical surrogate measures of adoption (e.g., Davis, 1989; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003). While this reveals context-specific indicators of adoption, these factors also explained the low uptak of telemedicine health services.

Professional sport also suffered unprecedented interruption as a result of COVID-19. While some sports wer postponed in 2020, other events such as professional cycling could continue as virtual events through digitalisation Data from virtual sport events and interviews with athletes show that such events are suitable, realistic and seriou substitutes. Performance in virtual races replicates the experience and performance of specific parts of real-worl sport and contributes to positive perceptions amongst athletes. The main concern raised by athletes was whethe digitalisation of sports could allow cheating through manipulation of data (Westmattelmann, Grotenhermen Sprenger, & Schewe, 2021). The example of professional virtual sports, while still novel, represents a smoother expe rience than the transitions in education and health.

Scholars reflect on how digitally mature organisations have been likely to adapt easily and continue their busi ness operations during COVID-19. The pandemic revealed the fragility of digital immature organisations (Dwived et al., 2020; Fletcher & Griffiths, 2020; Seetharaman, 2020; Willcocks, 2021), including some educational institutions (Iacuzzi et al., 2020). COVID-19 as an opportunity for firms to reimagine their digital strategies and change technology-driven work practices is a common theme (Carroll & Conboy, 2020; Grover & Sabherwal, 2020; Seetharaman, 2020). COVID-19 accelerates digital transformation, acting as a stimulant for organisationa digitalisation through changes such as digital replacement of physical products or identification of how to delive products/service safely with minimal physical contact (Grover & Sabherwal, 2020; Seetharaman, 2020). This is not a universal experience, however. In many instances, COVID-19 has made it harder, not easier, and placed firms under greater pressure (Wade & Shan, 2020). Technology leaders including CIOs will have emerging priorities in the years ahead (Watson, Ives, & Piccoli, 2020) that may complicate digital transformation efforts. This is also true of organisa tions in the public sector where COVID-19 may undo work so far on bridging the digital divide and even deepen it (Iivari, Sharma, & Ventä-Olkkonen, 2020)

The impact, current and future demands of COVID-19 on the future of work are considered through the potential of AI, automation and the roboapocalypse (Coombs, 2020; Willcocks, 2021). AI is positioned as filling the voi of human workers in some sectors (such as health) through intelligent automation (Coombs, 2020). The potential rol of AI and automation speaks to the labour shortage in health and essential industries. Key arguments for intelligent automation include consumer preference for automation, increasing familiarity of intelligent automation technolo gies, and increased business confidence in the technology. At the same time, arguments are made against the hyperbole around AI, robotics and automation, suggesting obstacles to realisation of large-scale automation through digita technology (Willcocks, 2021). Counterarguments include the availability and reliability of big data, the continuing need for human skills, the narrow capabilities of intelligent automation technologies and high availability of human workers (Coombs, 2020).

## 3.5 | IT management and business continuity

Business continuity in the context of COVID-19 is underpinned by organisational resilience and adaptability during ostensibly difficult conditions. The two themes outlined above highlight the challenge of rapid transition at scale Scholars noted the lack of transferable IT management models for organisations to refer to or apply in the context of COVID-19. Extant models are not time critical or are only relevant to short-term rather than prolonged crisis. A stud based on 30 interviews with IT managers, and other managers and staff from three universities in Italy, France and Germany on their response to COVID-19 produced a novel five-phase process model consisting of survival socialisation, normalisation, strategizing and institutionalising. The notion of ‘survival’ as a first phase is new and sug gestive of the impact of COVID-19. Strategising , typically considered at the early phases of management models comes after the survival phase, with organisations experimenting and using IT to continue business operatio (Carugati, Mola, Plé, Lauwers, & Giangreco, 2020).

The notion of ‘survive first, strategise later’ was also present in the non-empirical papers, with the immediate responses and ad hoc activities of organisations referred to as ‘first aid strategies’ (Thomas et al., 2020). The surviv alist orientation is clear in the focus of activities at different times—before the pandemic, the response and lockdow period, and the day after lockdown—with CIO and IT professionals' priorities and concerns changing across thes stages (Papagiannidis, Harris, & Morton, 2020). Reflecting on how organisations could have been better prepared, it is argued that they should have had an IT risk management and business continuity plan in place in the pre-pandemic period, along with the required digital infrastructure. IT strategies can be effective during a pandemic, even whe not designed for it, but part of this is ensuring the digital infrastructure is ready (Papagiannidis et al., 2020). Thi involves not being overly ready to the specific conditions of COVID-19, but rather being flexible enough to adapt and respond to contingencies more broadly (Dwivedi et al., 2020); a trade-off between efficiency and resilience (Vardi, 2020). As an example of better preparation, COVID-19 increased cybersecurity threats, via unanticipated vul nerabilities, for which many firms were not prepared (Naidoo, 2020). Organisational ambidexterity is identified as a strategy that could help organisations, especially small and medium-sized enterprises, through allocation of resource and capabilities to managing daily operations while also being ready for shocks and contingencies such as COVID-19 (Papadopoulos, Baltas, & Balta, 2020).

## 3.6 | Fighting COVID-19

COVID-19 presents a range of new health, social and economic challenges. As noted, IS plays a role in how governments track the spread of disease, but also in how organisations and society adapt. This is implicit in many of the the matic areas covered in the review. A stream of literature dealt directly with the fight against COVID-19 at the leve of citizens and institutions. One area of discussion comprised enforcing social distancing and informing people's decision-making around it. Studies show that giving citizens information on the number of people at a location coul change behaviour around social distancing. An experiment with 360 participants used a website with fictitiou crowdsourced information on the number of people at a medical practice to indicate how crowded it was. It wa found that real-time crowding sourcing of information makes users more likely to select less crowded medical prac tices (Adam, Werner, Wendt, & Benlian, 2020). Applying this knowledge to settings such as supermarkets, shopping centres and other public spaces could help citizens make informed decisions around social distancing

Scholars advocate the role of AI in the fight against COVID-19 (Dwivedi et al., 2020; Sipior, 2020; Thomas et al., 2020; Wang & Wu, 2020). A novel argument suggests that re-purposing existing AI applications could addres the immediacy of needs arising from COVID-19, thus offering rapid availability of applications (Sipior, 2020). Fo instance, an Indian start-up re-purposed an AI system originally developed to detect tuberculosis and used it to assist with diagnosing and assessing COVID-19 cases by checking computer tomography (CT) lung scans for signs of th disease and its severity. Others suggest that AI can and does play a large role in pandemic response, and importantl can help to predict the next pandemic. There is caution, however, that AI is not a silver bullet: questions are raised around the data that AI is built on and the limited information available on how/if AI is making a positive impact on managing COVID-19 (Dwivedi et al., 2020; Thomas et al., 2020; Wang & Wu, 2020)

## 3.7 | Government response

The role of government is implicit in many of the themes covered: developing, governing and framing contact tracing systems (Riemer et al., 2020; Rowe, 2020); information dissemination and communication (Rao et al., 2020); and public-facing dashboards (Janssen & van der Voort, 2020; Shankar et al., 2021).

Looking to other government activities, inefficiencies in IS mediated inter-organisational information sharing have hindered distribution of financial aid in Brazil, leading to eligible individuals missing out on emergency aid. As with other studies in the emergency space, this highlights the importance of integrated data infrastructures and join ing up processes through IS between government at a local and national level (Luciano, 2020). Drawing on the expe rience of the Dutch government, scholars suggest that the ability of governments to respond is stressed by tension between timing (immediacy) of policy measures, the level of decision centralisation, the autonomy of decision making and the need to balance change and stability. For example, governments needed to balance the demands of the medical system, testing and contact tracing, food supply assurance, medical equipment supply, prescription drug supply and funding to keep the economy running. The case of the Dutch government provides a setting to reflect on the concepts of agility (speed of response within given structures) and adaptive governance (system-level change throughout government) to show that existing institutional structures and tools can enable adaptivity and agility even while agility is in tension with adaptability. Bureaucracy, while typically associated with slowness, is put forwar as a key enabler of adaptivity and quick response. Theoretically, this highlights the paradoxical nature of adaptive governance while emphasising the practical role of institutions in the time of crises (Janssen & van der Voort, 2020)

## 3.8 | Cybersecurity and cyber crime

The rise of cybercrime and cybersecurity issues is perhaps unexpected in the context of a pandemic. Specifically however, the shift towards working from home and the removal of the traditional barriers to cybercrime leave many organisations and individuals more vulnerable to cyber threats (Dwivedi et al., 2020).

A quantitative analysis of 185 archival documents related to cybersecurity incidents uncovered novel patterns in the breadth and diversity of cybercrime, showing such crimes are continually evolving in response to situational factors (Naidoo, 2020). These factors include rapidly changing information, remote working, online shopping and unemployment, amongst others, that open up new opportunities for cybercrime (Naidoo, 2020). Analysis of COVID-19 cybercrime attacks shows that cybercriminals tend to follow a dynamic process comprising four broad levels: gather ing information about situational factors; identifying targets; selecting attack methods; and employing social engi neering techniques. As studies on resilience, adaptability and digital transformation suggest, being ready a organisation level for unanticipated cyber threats involves not being ready specifically to the conditions of COVID 19, but rather being flexible enough to adapt and ready to respond to contingencies more broadly (Dwived et al., 2020).

## 3.9 | Digital inequality

Several papers touch on issues of inequality and challenges around access to information and information behaviou (Iivari et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2020; Rai, 2020). A connection is made between digital inequalities and increased vulnerability to COVID-19 (Beaunoyer, Dupéré, & Guitton, 2020), which follows arguments from education tha COVID-19 will deepen digital divides (Iivari et al., 2020). The issue is twofold: digital inequalities can increase vulnerability to the virus itself (through lack of information) and repercussions of the crisis (unemployment). At the sam time, COVID-19 imposed lockdowns exacerbate the digital divide because some individuals do not have access to the necessary digital resources (Beaunoyer et al., 2020)

## 3.10 | Directions, agendas and reflection

Many of the papers spanned a wide range of themes, offering opinion, recommendations and reflection; as such the could be categorised in more than one of the thematic areas. A common theme in these cross-cutting papers is how well placed the IS community is intellectually to respond to COVID-19, and importantly, that it should be seen as an opportunity to demonstrate the field's relevance.

Many of the opinion and editorial papers proposed multiple directions for research or broad agendas and reflections. These drew on the authors' experience working during COVID-19 and the impacts in academia (Davison, 2020; van der Aalst, Hinz, & Weinhardt, 2020), such as offering practical suggestions for moving to teaching online (Dwivedi et al., 2020). Editors reflect on challenges as well as how COVID-19 impacted on differences i authorship and review engagement (Osch et al., 2020). Guidelines on how researchers can continue work during COVID-19 are offered (Prommegger, Bennett Thatcher, Wiesche, & Krcmar, 2021). At the general level, views are expressed on the broad impacts on sectors and firms; the general role of technology (Cerf, 2020; O'Leary, 2020 Rai, 2020); or more specifically on digital contact tracing, big data, AI, analytics and blockchain (Dwivedi et al., 2020 O'Leary, 2020; Rai, 2020).

Scholars suggest different directions in terms of theories, challenges and opportunities for research (Barnes, 2020; De, Pandey, & Pal, 2020; Doyle & Conboy, 2020; Dwivedi et al., 2020; Guitton, 2020; O'Leary, 2020; Rai, 2020; Sein, 2020; Venkatesh, 2020). COVID-19 offers new conditions to test existing theory or develop new theory. Several theoretical lenses are proposed: for instance, normalisation process theory (NPT) (Carroll & Conboy, 2020) and a theory of liquid modernity (Doyle & Conboy, 2020). Research agendas are proposed to gener ate knowledge in the fight against COVID-19 and study its lasting impacts on work and life (Venkatesh, 2020). On instructive suggestion for IS is to redesign the public health system from reactive to proactive. IS is placed at the cen tre of this process because Lags in information flow from hospitals, testing centers, and other organizations to publi health agencies, as well as in and between these agencies, create an information void, thereby increasing uncertaint in estimates’ (Rai, 2020, p. iv). These views see COVID-19 as an opportunity for (re)designing interactions with technologies, explaining the use of technology and understanding connections between new and old concepts (Sein, 2020). One stream in particular posits COVID-19 as an opportunity to turn attention to other grand challenges facing the world such as the achievement of the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) (Pan & Zhang, 2020) This links to the argument that COVID-19 rapidly accelerated predicted trends in IS (Barnes, 2020). Others argue that scholars should learn from previous disasters (Sakurai & Chughtai, 2020; Sein, 2020; Thomas et al., 2020). Argu ments are made for drawing on relevant non-emergency related literature such as that on pluralistic organisations, which can explain why health care professionals respond to IT implementation in different ways (Thoma et al., 2020). A positive aspect of these arguments is that applying learnings from previous crises to COVID-19 sug gests learnings can be applied in the other direction from COVID-19 to future contingencies.

## 4 | DISCUSSION

This review set out to examine the immediate response of the IS field to the COVID-19 pandemic. Thi section expands on the connections between the themes and existing literature, and the relevance and impact of th IS community's response. The discussion aims to be forward-looking as much as reflective, by ruminating and suggesting future research directions it outlines how IS scholars can account for the longer-term implications of COVID-19. Just as the Black Death plague pandemic in 14-century Europe triggered fundamental shifts in people' views of life and the world (Griffin & Denholm, 2020), COVID-19 will have residual impact on how we live and work

## 4.1 | The immediate response: Contributions and looking forward

The review reveals several constitutive streams of research with connection to extant scholarly conversation, for instance on systems design, collaboration and remote work, business continuity and organisational resilience, and information sharing (Powell, 2015). At the same time, new streams of research emerged that will require ongoing enquiry.

Digital contact tracing thrusts the design, (mass) adoption, governance and management of IT into main stream discourse. While scholars have studied these themes, the context of COVID-19 (Riemer et al., 2020; Trang et al., 2020) challenges and extends theory that emerged from and informed prior IS research (e.g., Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Scholars also identify complex and not well-anticipated trade-offs of such systems, such as the normalisation of surveillance and lowering of the threshold for privacy (Fahey & Hino, 2020; Leclercq-Vandelannoitte & Aroles, 2020; Rowe, 2020). These go beyond considerations in tradi tional IS studies. By offering critical perspectives, scholars inject realism and guard against hyperbole an techno-optimism. Combined, these studies are a starting point to advance the conversation on approaches and lessons identified that can inform the development of digital contact tracing 2.0 , encourage rapid diffusion of technology, as well as understand the macro-level impact of IS (Agarwal & Lucas, 2005). This is critical as despite early research (Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020), the success of digital contact tracing applications remains unclear at best or underwhelming at worst.

Re-imagining IS to enable new methods for information sharing, modelling and data visualisation (Pietz et al., 2020) in order to inform individuals will continue. This will help in pandemic response by informing and encour aging social distancing, for instance through real-time information on the number of persons at a venue (Adam et al., 2020), or providing an accurate picture of the current status of a virus in a geographical area (Shankar et al., 2021). IS scholars should inform the development of useful representational systems (Recker, Indulska, Green, Burton-Jones, & Weber, 2019) that can faithfully model and track states and events to understand the pandemic and its future trajectory, and inform behaviour (Pietz et al., 2020; Thomas et al., 2020). Such systems may be critical in learning to live with the virus.

Like other emergencies and contingencies (Elbanna et al., 2019; Vaast, Safadi, Lapointe, & Negoita, 2017), socia media played a central role in the information and communication space. Studies revealed ways that governments and other actors communicate vital public health information to citizens to inform behaviours. The studies also offe actionable insights on how institutional actors should frame their messaging (Casalegno et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Rao et al., 2020). Research should develop a more complete explanation of institutional social media us beyond the early months of the pandemic. One interesting angle would be to explore information fatigue experi enced by individuals and how to counter it.

The papers also illuminate individuals' different patterns of social media use (Laato et al., 2020; Nabity-Grover et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2020). This was found to be demonstrably different to how social media is used during other emergencies, such as storms and wildfires, where the focus is on immediate response and survival. In the case of COVID-19, it was used to maintain a state of normality. It is unclear if individuals' information behaviours will revert to pre-COVID-19 behaviours or if COVID-19 imprints long-term changes in how individuals use social media and digital technology, for example, around how they share personal (Nabity-Grover et al., 2020) and emergency information (Laato et al., 2020). The papers on social media use provide fresh insights into the information landscape of emergencies and build on existing research. They also introduce partially conflicting positions around the role of social media. IS research has mostly argued that social media acts as an information resource and supports individ uals and organisations in times of crisis (Choo & Nadarajah, 2014; Tim, Pan, Ractham, & Kaewkitipong, 2016) COVID-19 sheds light on the flip side of this by illuminating how greater reliance on social media can erode the established command-and-control information paradigm (Bunker, 2020). In this context, the combination of increased points of information and volume of information challenges the traditional top-down approach to informa tion sharing (Karanasios, Cooper, Hayes, & Adrot, 2019). This in turn exacerbates misinformation and uncertainty. As such, this argument demonstrates that social media creates a shift from a context where information load is low and information quality is high, to a context of high information load and lower information quality. This has significan implications for government actors and society. The IS field should be at the forefront of informing and addressing these issues, given its long history of studying social media and crisis (Tim et al., 2016), online communities and their dynamics (Faraj, Jarvenpaa, & Majchrzak, 2011), and recent moves into the study of fake news and misinformation (Schuetz et al., 2021). Scholars are also turning to how COVID-19 is contributing to a rise of polarised communities through and the role of ICT (Parra, Gupta, & Mikalef, 2021). Important questions would include: how organisations and government can treat and correct misinformation? And what role the platforms should play? Similarly, researchers should also consider the multi-level nature of the information landscape (Karanasios et al., 2019). For instance, how does information flow from government organisations to other levels such as NGOs and community groups, which are often closer to individuals, and how is this information re-interpreted and shared?

COVID-19 provides a new context for rapid change in firms and society. Some scholars see the pandemic as a turning point (consistent with the theory of punctuated equilibrium), to usher in a digital transformation that ha hitherto been held back. At the societal level, for instance, it could lead to a re-imagination of the public health sys tem as proactive rather than reactive (Rai. 2020). Or more broadly, turn the focus towards addressing the SDGs (Pan & Zhang, 2020). In this way, COVID-19 may be the starting point for a digital transformation of work and lif that could be largely favourable for society. These papers urge IS scholars to examine the role of IS in addressing other large-scale challenges, such as the SDGs and impacts of climate change, to avoid undesirable futures and help society be better prepared for future shocks. This fits with calls for research to address societal impact (Díaz Andrad et al., 2019; Majchrzak et al., 2014) and recent reflection on the role of the IS field (Leong, Tan, & Ahuja, 2020). This narrative has continued post-review, with scholars prompting researchers ‘to be more aware of futures, to foster the innovative foresight for actively pursuing the preferred future, and to espouse the responsible foresight for con sciously avoiding undesirable futures (Pee et al., 2021, p. 157).

Papers shared a level of techno-optimism in the immediate response phase, evidenced through discussions on using AI for signs of the disease (Sipior, 2020) or consideration of how AI and automation can address the unavailability of human workers in the health sector (Coombs, 2020). Further study of how AI was enacted across different areas in COVID-19 will be useful to the growing literature that positions AI as disruptive technology and at the centre of future research (Collins, Dennehy, Conboy, & Mikalef, 2021; Queiroz & Fosso Wamba, 2021). At th same time, there are partially opposing findings and viewpoints on the role of technology, with examples of technology-mediated responses such as telemedicine that were not as successful as hoped (Rahi et al., 2021), or out lines of the immaturity of AI-based applications (Sipior, 2020; Thomas et al., 2020; Wang & Wu, 2020). There is an opportunity to learn from the initial research and conduct more empirical research on adapting and enacting nove technology in crisis contexts. For instance, findings on the lack of success of telemedicine could be used by health care professionals to encourage future adoption of telemedicine health services by patients and act as a step change towards greater digitalisation of health services (Rahi et al., 2021). Such examples then serve as a learning case. The help scholars to theorise why technology did/did not deliver as expected and importantly guard against hyperbol around technology.

COVID-19 is viewed as a trigger for organisational level transformation by forcing organisations to address ‘deep-structure’ and legacy system challenges that have traditionally constrained full utilisation of digital technology (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Sutcliff, Narsalay, & Sen, 2019). Digitally mature organisations were better positioned to adapt and continue their business operations (Fletcher & Griffiths, 2020; Seetharaman, 2020; Willcocks, 2021) While there is a view that COVID-19 could act as a stimulus for greater digital transformation, with many organisa tions successfully making the digitalisation leap, a conflicting view suggests that organisations continue to be held back by legacy systems and the invisible chains of their deep structure (Fletcher & Griffiths, 2020; Papadopoulo et al., 2020; Wade & Shan, 2020). This points to a digital Darwinism whereby digital-ready firms are better able to compete, survive and adapt to emergent situations and opportunities (Westerman, Tannou, Bonnet, Ferraris, & McAfee, 2017). In this way, COVID-19 revealed the fragility of digitally immature organisations and should be a warning to researchers to pay attention to those organisations that have not successfully adapted.

Linked to this, the papers point to the inadequacy of IT management models in helping to cope with the immediate impact. The notions of survive first, strategise later or first aid strategies (Thomas et al., 2020) ar new and distinct to typical management approaches. The papers suggest a balance is required between being not overly specific to the conditions of COVID-19 (or any other major contingency), but rather being flexible enough to adapt and ready to respond to contingencies more broadly (Dwivedi et al., 2020; Vardi, 2020). More evidence is required to deliver insights into how well current theory explains organisational responses to largescale, disruptive and uncertain contingencies, especially when they are unexpectedly prolonged. This is evident in the case of working from home. While working on the move and remote working were already a growing area of research (Davis, 2002; Spinuzzi, 2012), it took on a sense of urgency as COVID-19 revealed the challenges of doing it rapidly at scale and for an uncertain duration. This pushes the limits of IT management models and knowledge on digital work, as is evident in the papers that outline challenges experienced in the early stages of COIVD-19 related lockdowns. It is also evident in the differing future visions of remote work, ranging from ‘digi tal Taylorism’ to ‘worker autonomy’ (Wang et al., 2020). Future research should examine the different model of working from home used by organisations, the challenges in reversing this trend (Kelly, 2021), and how thes may spur new models of work.

It should be remembered that digital inequality at the individual level exacerbated vulnerability to COVID-19, in both health and economic terms (Beaunoyer et al., 2020). For scholars interested in the digital divide, there is an opportunity to learn how populations that do not have access to digital resources receive information, stay con nected during lockdown, continue working and mitigate health and economic risks. These studies are in line with th research on technology and development with its focus on economic and social divides (Davison, 2005; Lagna & Ravishankar, 2021), in their consideration of how COVID-19 emphasises implications of the digital divide. Future work may also examine how the pandemic ushered in novel use of technology such as mobile payments (Lagna & Ravishankar, 2021) to replace contact with physical money or triggered community-driven technology innovation (Zorina & Karanasios, 2021).

In terms of how scholars answered their research questions in the aforementioned studies, novelty is demon strated in the use of experiments and various secondary data (e.g., Twitter, WeChat, newspapers, dashboards and platforms). These sources provide access to rich and timely data and give voice to various stakeholders that would not be available otherwise due to the unavailability of health agencies, government officials and so on for academic research. Despite challenges, the samples were not trivial. Scholars analysed 3.2 million tweets (Hacker et al., 2020), interviewed 30 practitioners (Carugati et al., 2020) and analysed 355 documents (Rowe et al., 2020). Several nove experiments were performed drawing on large samples (two with 581 and 360 participants) of participants from crowdsourcing platforms, allowing for rapid access to study participants. There will be inescapable compromises in such rapid research, but the relevance trade-off is necessary. It is also worth noting that many of the empirical paper were put through rapid review processes. Future research could examine in more detail the methodological contribu tions on how to do ‘rapid IS research’ to address immediate concerns.

## 4.2 | The pursuit of relevance and impact

Many of the papers make the claim that existing knowledge could have better supported immediate responses to the pandemic. This raises questions for IS scholars. How can IS research be proactive rather than responsive? How relevant is IS research? And, how does it deliver impact in meaningful ways? The question of relevance is encourag ingly straightforward to address, with the papers reviewed demonstrating relevance in two ways.

First, papers aimed to initiate IS research by outlining emerging questions, conceiving research ideas and research agendas, broadly engaging in scholarly conversation related to COVID-19, and connecting to extant research and theory. Second, papers sought to generate new knowledge, establish causal influences and build theory that makes new knowledge contributions. Examples include: seeking links between digital contact tracing and infection transmission rates (Urbaczewski & Lee, 2020); understanding how government can improve information strategies to counter confusion (Casalegno et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Rao et al., 2020); or offering independent analysis and reflection on early shortcomings in government responses (Janssen & van der Voort, 2020; Luciano, 2020). Both these forms of relevance (initiating research and generating knowledge) address immediate concerns and may also lead to longer-term practice impact. Practice impact is concerned with observ able and measurable benefits of research beyond academia such as individuals, organisations, communities industries or governments (Pan & Pee, 2020). It is thus more difficult to measure in a period of several months and often requires systemic programs of research to be achieved (Nunamaker, Twyman, Giboney, & Briggs, 2017). Such impact is not typically achieved directly through academic publications, but rather may be evidenced through events, social media activities, submissions to enquiries, media coverage, contracts, patents, setting standards, broader dissemination and so on (Pan & Pee, 2020; Wiener et al., 2018). An example of broader dissemination is Pan et al. (2020) appearing on the World Health Organisation's (WHO) COVID-19 Global literature on coronavirus disease’ web site,<sup>1</sup> which gathers the latest international multilingual scientific findings and knowledge on COVID-19. Practice impact may also occur as a direct outcome or be achieved unknowingly through external actors' translation of findings into practice impact

## 4.3 | What to do about COVID-19 in IS research?

In the process of composing this paper, it was clear that many of the excluded papers, and others encountered, only refer to COVID-19 in passing or use empirical data collected prior but with some minor connection being made to COVID-19. Some papers include relevant terms such as ‘COVID-19’ or ‘pandemic’ in the title, abstract or keyword even though the COVID-19 context is not engaged with in a meaningful way. For instance, online shopping can b conducted in the context of COVID-19 (at some point in 2020), but authors can fail to explain how COVID-19 influ ences online shopping. This is a challenge for authors, readers and editors. For authors, the pursuit of relevance ma cause temptation to connect everything to COVID-19, or simply to top' and tail' papers with COVID-19-related text. Certainly, there is a need for ongoing research, and to tease out the implications of COVID-19. There is also an empirical need to understand what is and is not related to COVID-19. This is a challenge in the throes of a pandemic but is necessary to ensure the validity of findings.

Figure 3 illustrates four points of interaction that scholars can use to consider how their study connects with COVID-19. In the top left, the figure shows research at the intersection of IS and COVID-19, such as the research included in this review. In the top right, the figure shows IS research undertaken during COVID-19. This research may mentio COVID-19, but there is little attempt to understand its impact on the IS phenomena, or vice-versa. In the bottom left the figure shows research that explicitly applies pre-existing data to the COVID-19 context. For instance, researc on tweets related to Hurricane Harvey in the USA yielded insights into how sense-giving occurs during a large-scale disaster event. It suggests that a more timely and effective communication strategy could have sped up the enact ment of social distancing norms (Mirbabaie, Bunker, Stieglitz, Marx, & Ehnis, 2020). Prior research on remote/mobil working is also applied to COVID-19 as a means to learn about working from home (Hafermalz & Riemer, 2020). Other papers review prior literature on virtual enterprises (Anthony Jnr & Abbas Petersen, 2020) and knowledge management in pandemics (Ammirato, Linzalone, & Felicetti, 2020), and link it to the COVID-19 context. Just as knowledge and theory from other emergencies are applied to the COVID-19 context, so too can the immediate IS research be applied to future contingencies. Going forward, such studies could provide comparative analysis that contrasts insti tutional and cultural differences to explain different responses to pandemics and other crises. Importantly, such stud ies can inform the immediate response to future contingencies.

## 4.4 | Limitations

While this is an opinion paper, the review comes with several precautions and inescapable compromises. Common limitations and challenges related to systematic reviews apply, such as reliance on database searches (e.g., assuming they provide a good coverage of relevant publications) and analysis based on selected keywords (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015). Given that many of the patterns are shifting over time, this review captures the immediate response; it is a checkpoint rather than a normative study. It should be considered as a first step to understanding the response and contribution of the IS field. It should also be acknowledged that there is a time lag between research undertaken and publication.

There are limits to restricting the review to a narrow group of publications. It can be argued therefore that our review privileges and justifies extraction and summation of a particular group of results (Boell & Cecez Kecmanovic, 2015). As shown, a wide range of thematic areas is covered. This may become more problematic for future reviews as the impact of COVID-19 becomes entangled in many aspects of IS, such as work, design of sys tems, information dissemination, information sharing, and so on. Future studies therefore may wish to narrow th sample to specific areas or adopt computational methods to study larger samples (Antons et al., 2021).

## 5 | CONCLUSION

During the early months of COVID-19, there was no rule book for managers, government, workers, teachers, parents and individuals in lockdown. This paper examines the IS community's ‘rapid response’ to COVID-19. While there will be a long tail of research related to COVID-19, and pandemics more broadly, this paper discussed the type of research the IS field produced and the immediate scholarly conversations and knowledge that contributed to fighting COVID-19. The review identifies nine thematic areas and one broad area of discussion. Scholars considered the impact of COVID-19 or a rich array of issues spanning a multitude of social levels from the individual actor to the government, and everything in between. Research presented findings from Bangladesh, Brazil, Taiwan, Uruguay and Iran alongside more common IS research settings such as the USA, Europe and China, offering lessons and cases that may stand in opposition to each other. The immediate contribution from the reviewed papers were discussed, including how the new research connects with traditional IS research, how it spurs new directions and how IS research can inform the response to COVID-19. Th relevance of the papers reviewed and the pathways to practice impact were identified and discussed. Building on th papers reviewed, research directions are proposed to guide future IS research. Four points of interactions between con text and IS were put forward to help IS scholars consider the influence of COVID-19 on their research

## AUTHOR BIOGRAPHY

Dr Stan Karanasios is an Associate Professor of Information Systems at the University of Queensland Business School. Previously he worked at RMIT University and the University of Leeds, where he has a visiting position. His current research focuses on the interaction between new information and communication technologies and society and in particular the impact of digitalisation/digital transformation in a range of settings. He has published in leading information systems journals, including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Journal, European Journal of Information Systems and the Journal of the Association for Information Systems. He has been awarded research grants from a range of government and industry sources. In addition to his research work, he has undertaken a range of consulting work for industry and international bodies. He is a Senior Editor for Information Systems Journal.
