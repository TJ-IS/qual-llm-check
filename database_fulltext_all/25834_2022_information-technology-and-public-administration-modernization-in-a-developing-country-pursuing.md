# Information technology and public administration modernization in a developing country: Pursuing paperless clearance at Ghana customs

Atta Addo

Department of Digital Economy Entrepreneurship and Innovation, Surrey Business School, University of Surrey, Guildford, UK

## Correspondence

Atta Addo, Department of Digital Economy Entrepreneurship and Innovation, Surrey Business School, University of Surrey, Guildford GU2 7XH, UK. Email: a.addo@surrey.ac.uk

## Abstract

Despite significant information technology (IT) implementations in public administrations of developing countries to change their dysfunctional traditional practices towards modern forms, the outcomes are typically disappointing relative to the potential of IT for organizational change. With a case study of the customs clearance process when importing goods through Ghana's main port, the Tema Harbour, we explore why IT struggles to modernize traditional practices of public administration in a developing country context. We focus on explaining the persistence of paper use at Ghana customs despite more than a decade of digitalization and automation to establish paperless processes that eliminate malpractices of traditional clearance. We view modernization as a process of long-term institutional change and therefore draw from the literature on IT and institutional change to focus our investigation on the dynamics of incongruent institutional logics of IT and public administration. We show that in the administration context of a developing country like Ghana, the endurance of patrimonial logics in the state and broader society, coupled with high levels of administrative discretion and ambivalent or weak compliance pressure limit the realization of IT for modernization and allows hybrid practices to emerge.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Traditional public administration in developing countries has been associated with inefficiencies, ineffectiveness malpractices, and corruption that impose significant costs on everyday life. From having to spend an entire day queuing at a government office, to being asked for bribes for even the most basic of services, efficient and effective public services tend to be the exception, rather than the norm in many developing countries (Carr & Jago, 2014; Olken & Pande, 2012). Traditional practices develop under historical conditions that have given them legitimacy and endur ance (Hyden, 2010; Painter & Peters, 2010), and are difficult to change because they are “stable, repetitive and enduring and infused with value beyond the […] task at hand (Oliver, 1992, p. 563; Selznick, 1957, p. 17)

Efforts to improve traditional practices of public administration through IT have typically been presented as modernization (Bhuiyan, 2011; Introna et al., 2010; Margetts et al., 2010), a term popularized by Weber (1968) who used it to describe the process of change from “traditional” administration, characterized by personalized, arbitrary, ad hoc and unfair processes, towards “modern” rule-based, impartial, and service-oriented forms. Computerized processes like payroll, financial management, benefits handling, passport issuing, and vehicle licensing, are now implemented in the public administrations of many developing countries. But while computerization has continue apace, their intended modernization has been less straightforward. Widespread unmet expectations have been reported by development donors who sponsor IT modernization initiatives (Andrews, 2013; World Bank Group, 2011). Although some successes have been reported with IT in terms of project sustainability and stakeholde views (Krishna & Walsham, 2005) as well as improvements in efficiency (De Wulf, 2005b; World Bank, 2005), IT in many instances has been found to reproduce, rather than replace traditional practices (Addo & Avgerou, 2021 Davis, 2004; Heeks, 1999a; Masiero, 2015).

Given the endurance of traditional practices and their institutionalized nature, we consider the potential of IT to enable change in institutionalized practices of public administration in a developing country setting. In the IS litera ture, although technology is known to carry institutions, with the potential to transform practice (Czarniawska, 2009; Gosain, 2004; Scott, 2008), empirical evidence suggests that such IT-driven institutional change is difficult to realize in the context of public administration due to vested interests and lack of political wil (Danziger & Andersen, 2002; Kraemer & King, 2006). Unlike business administration, public administrations tend to lack performance incentive systems, be overcommitted to regulations and rule-following (even if ineffective or poorly designed), and are exposed to political influences that undermine effectiveness (Saxena, 1996).

However, even studies specific to IT in public administration are too general to capture intricacies of IT and modernization in a developing country setting (Avgerou, 2017), or to explain empirical findings of mixed outcomes where improvements in efficiency might occur even as traditional (mal)practices remain (Addo, 2016; Addo & Avgerou, 2021; Addo & Senyo, 2020). Explaining the modernization effects of IT on traditional administration practices in a developing country setting can therefore be usefully advanced by delving into specifics of the technology administration practices, and the broader institutional context of public administration in developing countries

To understand why IT struggles to modernize practices in the public administration of developing countries we study TRADENET, a business-to-government electronic data interchange system that was implemented to reduc paper documentation in import clearance at Ghana customs. We investigate why TRADENET—despite its toute success in Singapore's customs modernization—could not enable a paperless process or eliminate malpractices tradi tionally associated with paper-based processes at Ghana customs.

We chose to study customs clearance because in recent decades that functional domain has experienced exten sive IT modernization in many developing countries (Glenday, 1997; Grainger & Mclinden, 2013; Kituyi, 2013 Portugal-Perez & Wilson, 2009). However, customs clearance in several developing countries still maintained paper work due to officers' puzzling attachment to paper use—what has been described as a “fetishization” of paper (Cantens, 2013, p. 4). In this way, customs clearance resembled other public administration functions in developing countries that remain heavily dependent upon paperwork; with its associated red tape, inefficiency, corruption, and opportunities for malpractices (Hull, 2012a, 2012b). Paper has been an off-shoot of the public administration in developing countries, accepted as “the only medium for carrying messages or transactions, and the electronic form of the same is often seen as something too abstract to be used in practice” (Saxena, 1996, p. 706).

The paper proceeds with a review of relevant literature on IT and public administration modernization in devel oping countries. We then derive from the literature theoretical expectations of IT user responses in such settings A theoretical framework is presented, comprising an institutional logics perspective of IT and practice change unde conditions of administrative discretion and ambivalent or weak compliance pressures found in the public administra tion of developing countries. The TRADENET case is presented and analysed to develop theoretical explanation of IT modernization dynamics in a developing country like Ghana.

## 2 | THEORETICAL FRAMING

IS scholarship has characterized modernization as encompassing varied processes across domains such as nationa development, organizational change, and epistemological shifts (Faik & Walsham, 2013), and as one of the main ways IT's contribution to socio-economic development has been framed (Sein & Harindranath, 2004). In studies of mod ernization as organizational change, two broad approaches have been pursued in the literature—the top-down o macrosocial approach, and the bottom-up or microsocial approach.

The top-down approach reflects the mode of modernization promoted by governments and international development organizations such as the World Bank in their programs to diffuse technologies and best practices acros administrations in target countries (World Bank, 2005; World Bank Group, 2002, 2008). The role of IT in top-down change can be linked to ideologies of modernization, and to IT's institutional potential to legitimize and change insti tutions by inducing coercive, normative, and mimetic pressures (Avgerou, 2003; Avgerou & Bonina, 2020 Park, 2017).

The bottom-up approach considers the role of IT and its associated models of organizational rationality in reorganizing and improving situated practices of actors in various public administration functions (Avgerou, 1990). It might involve changing established administrations towards new models or paradigms of administration (Margetts, 2009; Margetts et al., 2010) by providing new rationalities for action. Such modernization at the organizational level has also been sometimes cast in terms of rationalization (Addo, 2017), reforms (Heeks & Mundy, 2001 Kraemer & King, 2006; Peterson, 1998), transformation (Kim et al., 2007; Weerakkody & Reddick, 2012), re engineering (Saxena, 1996), or restructuring (Chourabi et al., 2009; Heintze & Bretschneider, 2000).

Our study contributes to the bottom-up perspective by explaining the persistence of paper use among street level officers, and broadly, why IT modernization to change institutionalized practices struggles in a developing country setting. An implicit assumption is that the specific administration and broader national context shape IT phenom ena in particular ways that justify further investigation (Avgerou, 2001; Avgerou & Madon, 2004). For example, an immediate aspect of context is the administration type or paradigm that provides norms for the practices of workers Developing countries have progressed through a least three administration paradigms: patrimonial rule, bureaucracy, and public management (Appendix A). In many such countries, endemic malpractices and abuse of office for private gain might be rooted in old patrimonial norms that have not been easily supplanted by bureaucratic or public man agement norms (Clapham, 1985; Erdmann & Engel, 2007).

Research on public administration implementation suggests that government workers, particularly street-leve officers, implement programs in fundamentally different ways from actors in other organizational settings. Rathe than simply implement top-down policies and programs faithfully (as is commonly believed), street-level officers— those government officers who interact with citizens on a daily basis—possess high levels of discretion, understood as the power or the right of deciding according to one's judgment (Bovens & Zouridis, 2002; Langbein, 2004 p. 377; Lipsky, 1980). Such administrative discretion might prove a potential threat to organizational goals such as IT modernization, or encourage the pursuit of private interests despite laid down processes to safeguard the state an public interest (Barrett, 2004; Tummers & Bekkers, 2014).

The implication of administrative discretion for the institutional process of IT-enabled modernization invites attention to the classic structure-agency problem in organizational and management research (Heugens & Lander, 2009). Our theoretical framework therefore considers the interactions between IT, institutions of public administration, and IT user responses when modernization is attempted in a specific developing country context.

## 2.1 | IT and public administration modernization in developing countries

Our starting point is that modernization can be usefully conceived as a process of institutional change where organi zational actors (individuals or groups) are guided by (modern) institutional arrangements and further reproduce thes through everyday situated actions (Powell & DiMaggio, 1991). An institutional approach that allows adequate consideration of such structurational association is the institutional logics perspective (Thornton et al., 2012). An institu tional arrangement such as a given system of public administration, possesses an institutional logic that scripts an action through a set of assumptions, norms, beliefs, or values (Friedland & Alford, 1991). Institutional logics persist through rituals of everyday action that provide actors with meaning, making it difficult for actors to readily discard an incumbent logic in favour of a new one (Thornton et al., 2012). The institutional logics perspective recognizes that actors' mindful behaviour draws from historically formed collective rationalities that further shape institutional logic in a structurational way (Berente & Yoo, 2012; Sahay et al., 2010; Thornton, 2004; Thornton & Ocasio, 2008).

Public administration, like other forms of organizing, does not have a single institutional logic for all actors and activities. Rather, it is an institutionally plural setting comprising various logics that might be congruent or incongruent (Berente et al., 2019; Kraatz & Block, 2008). Such plural institutional logics are embodied through carriers such as IT, rules and procedures, norms, and work arrangements (Gosain, 2004). In developing countries, IT has become an important institutional carrier through its artefacts, techniques, as well as industries, legislation, and the rationa myths about its value (Avgerou, 2003). IT has been further linked to powerful institutions of management and socio economic development that have associated logics, professions, and industries (Hanna & Schware, 1990; Korac Kakabadse et al., 2000; Saint-Martin, 2000).

Public administration modernization in developing countries has primarily involved the reproduction of institutional logics drawn from modern administration types like bureaucracy and variants of public management (Appendix A). These are typically transferred from western contexts as best practice IT implementation and con comitant organizational changes. For example, since the 1950s, bureaucracies have been supported with back office rule-based information systems that translated regulations into effective services (Danziger et al., 1982; Dunleav et al., 2006). New Public Management (NPM) reforms have involved process redesign and cross-agency integrate infrastructures (Aydinli et al., 2009; Kamal et al., 2008; Weerakkody et al., 2011), whereas the Good governance model of administration, congruent with NPM principles, has fostered private-public-partnership for the develop ment of inter-organizational IT infrastructures that streamline multi-stakeholder processes and promote transpar ency (De Wulf, 2005b; Staple, 2002).

## 2.2 | IT user responses in developing country modernization initiatives

By supporting work according to the institutional logics provided by specific administration types, IT has the potential to change traditional practices by reconfiguring work arrangements and how they are carried out. However, IT does not produce change deterministically, independent of actors' choices and actions. In the context of public administration where IT users (street-level officers) have high levels of administrative discretion, Fountain (2001) dis tinguishes between “objective technology” and “enacted technology”: the former relating to systems and artefact and the latter to ways users choose to adapt them to achieve goals in specific contexts

IS studies suggest that the type of user response to IT implementation in an organization with multiple institu tions (i.e., an institutionally plural organizational setting) such as public administration depends on (a) the congruenc or incongruence of institutional logics (between an incumbent/traditional work practice and an implemented IT), and (b) the pressure to comply with a new logic of IT (whether weak or strong) (Berente et al., 2019). These condition lead to an expectation of four possible user responses to IT implementation in the public administration: faithfu appropriation (under conditions of congruent logics and strong pressure to comply), loose-coupling (under conditions of incongruent logics and strong pressure to comply), co-optation (under conditions of congruent logics and weak pressure to comply), and resistance (under conditions of incongruent logics and weak pressure to comply) (Berent et al., 2019, p. 878). In faithful appropriation, users comply with IT and acquiesce to its institutional logics; In loosecoupling, users combine incumbent logics with IT logics; co-optation occurs when users take control of an IT system and steer it in ways that still benefit the organization; resistance occurs when actors reject or undermine IT (Berent et al., 2019).

Historically, in developing countries, faithful appropriation and co-optation have been suggested to be atypica outcomes because incumbent/traditional institutional logics are known to be incongruent with the logics of IT (Hayes & Westrup, 2012; Miscione, 2007). Modes of rationality have been identified to be a fundamental dif ferentiator between modern (western) societies and traditional societies (Fischer-Tiné & Mann, 2004 Touraine, 1995). Developing countries are said to have “alternative rationalities” (Avgerou, 2000) to those of IT, and “design-actuality gaps” are said to exist between the context of developing countries where IT is implemented and used and the typical western contexts from where IT is designed and developed (Heeks, 2002). Consequently, the lit erature has commonly highlighted instances of loose-coupling (Addo, 2016; Asangansi, 2012; Chilundo & Aanestad, 2004) and resistance (Cavalheiro & Joia, 2013; Davis, 2004; Heeks, 1999b; Peterson, 1998) to IT initia tives in the public administration of developing countries.

In this research, we probe the institutional context of customs administration in the developing country of Ghana and how it conditions IT user responses during modernization. We focus on the dynamics between incum bent/traditional logics of practices and IT logics, as well as managerial controls and pressure to comply with IT logics By examining these, we explain the persistence of paper-based practices despite IT's potential to fully digitalize, and we produce a context-specific explanation of the type of practices that result.

## 3 | METHOD

Given our interest in developing explanation from in-depth study of IT modernization, we chose a case stud approach. We conducted an interpretive study of clearance procedures with TRADENET, a business-to government electronic data interchange deployed at Ghana customs. A long-term perspective was taken by also considering evidence from the period prior to TRADENET when a rudimentary customs management system was in use (Appendix C). Such retrospective was needed because change in public administration is known to take long to unfold (Chang, 2001; Schacter, 2000). We sought to understand how the traditionally paper-heavy import clearance process and related practices of customs had been influenced by TRADENET and its associated modernization logics.

## 3.1 | Data collection

The primary research site where interviews were conducted was the customs office at Ghana's largest port, th Tema Harbour. Ghana customs was an ideal research setting because customs administrations in developing coun tries are known to have various malpractices associated with their traditional processes (Cantens, 2012; Hors, 2001 Mclinden & Durrani, 2013; Widdowson, 2013).

We collected data during three research trips to Ghana between 2014 to 2016. Our fieldwork covered 8 months in total and began with a two-month exploration in June and July, 2014, where we identified informants who helped us to better understand the nature and scope of Ghana customs' IT modernization as well as data availability and informants to speak to. From that initial trip, we formed a view of IT modernization as occurring through incongruent logics of modern administration that TRADENET introduced and traditional/incumbent practices that TRADENET was expected to replace. Most of the data were collected between May to September 2015, after further literature review had enabled us to refine the research to focus on the processes of change when institutionalized practices encountered IT. The third round of data collection occurred between August to September 2016, a year after th first round of data had been collected and analysed. ## 3.2 | Data analysis

We adopted an interpretive approach to analysis that privileged the worldview of participants (Klein & Myers, 1999 Walsham, 2006). To better understand the range of TRADENET user responses in the organizational and broader context, we analysed (a) customs imports processes and (mal)practices before and during TRADENET (b) the compli ance pressure and managerial controls in place for effective TRADENET use (c) the organizational and broader national context of TRADENET in Ghana. We first traced the official imports clearance process that comprised 5 mai sub-processes and about 42 activities undertaken between 19 sets of interacting actors (Appendix D). Using the offi cially published process as our starting point, we elicited from informants' accounts of their everyday practices. Thi helped to shed light on how intended modernization through TRADENET was carried out during situated actions o local actors, particularly street-level customs officers who use TRADENET. Our presentation of the sub-processes of imports clearance therefore juxtaposes the official process flow with details of the situated actions and lived experi ences of stakeholders that sustain malpractices (Appendix D).

We further turned our analysis to how and why managerial controls failed to deliver on modernization aspira tions, and supervisory lapses permitted the persistence of paper-based practices as well as widespread malpractice and petty corruption. This focus led us to trace the links between customs and the broader political, social, and insti tutional context of Ghana.

## 3.3 | Empirical setting

Modernization of trade administration was identified as crucial, with the integra tion, automation, and digitalization of customs and other frontline agencies at its core. TRADENET was imported as a best practice modernization program from Singapore, where it had helped increase clearance efficiency and govern ment revenues. Upon the launch of the paperless initiative, the head of Ghana customs laid out some of its broad ambitions and was quoted as saying,

We are looking at implementing the paperless clearing procedure to prevent fraud and ensure a transparent process. The elimination of the paper processes will ensure more revenue, more efficiency, and prevent accusations (Andoh, 2012, p. 2).

TRADENET was therefore intended to integrate, automate, and digitalize customs clearance processes under a customs modernization discourse of paperless clearance through a single window platform. Paperless clearance is an approach to customs clearance that allows officers to perform all required processes via an information management and data interchange system without needing to use paper-based declaration forms and supporting paper documen tation. When done right, paperless clearance could significantly improve the efficiency of clearance operations and eliminate certain malpractices associated with human intervention in paper-based processes.

A single window system, in theory, should allow one submission to fulfil all requirements, in contrast to a “multi window” arrangement where separate submissions are required at a series of windows to complete a single transac tion. However, over a decade after TRADENET implementation, paperless clearance remained a mirage as the singl window was not fully realized. TRADENET was eventually replaced by two subsequent systems that also struggled with realizing paperless clearance.

## 4 | FINDINGS

Traditional clearance had severe challenges that TRADENET was intended to address (Appendix C). However, despite the potential of TRADENET, paper-based and manual activities remained. This allowed human mediation to continue even in activities where it was strictly unnecessary. Such human mediation presented opportunities for street-level officers to abuse their administrative discretion for their personal gain or the benefit of close associates Managerial controls were also compromised in various ways, and overall, pressure to comply with TRADENET logic was ambivalent and often weak.

## 4.1 | TRADENET practices (2004–2015)

Imports clearance with TRADENET was initiated by a self-declaration. By customs regulation, importers were required to clear goods through clearing agents. The process could also involve officers from over 20 other government agen cies involved in various aspects of international trade such as issuing permits, licences, and exemptions. Other interme diaries like shippers and banks were also involved. Private destination inspection companies (DICs) were responsibl for valuation and classification, an outsourced function that was previously performed by customs prior to reforms

Despite digitalization and automation, declarants still printed out paper forms that were physically stamped by officer at different points in the process. This undermined the goal of a single-step process as implied by a single window. The paper import declaration form (IDF) processing hearkened back to the old ways of clearance in the long room during th multi-window scheme before TRADENET (Appendix B). Such manual processing of paper forms increased inefficiency sometimes created opportunities for face-to-face interactions between customs officers and declarants, and consequentl promoted abuses of administrative discretion and coercion by corrupt officers. An understanding of the significance o paper use and the persistence of malpractices requires a closer look at the steps involved in TRADENET imports clearance.

Customs clearance involved numerous complex processes as contained in a 582-page process manual (Ghana Revenue Authority, 2016), but there were 5 main stages (or sub-processes) involved in imports clearance. During th first stage, manifest submission, an importer via an agent (the declarants) submitted documentation via TRADENET to cover the cargo to be cleared. In the second stage, IDF submission/FCVR issue, an agent submitted an import declaration form (IDF) for a final classification and valuation report (FCVR) to be issued by officers of the DICs. In the next stage of compliance, customs officers checked the validity and accuracy of a declaration and supporting documentation such a invoices, receipts, bill of lading, and any special permits required. The fourth stage, physical examination, was where cus toms and other government officers physically checked goods to ensure it met regulatory requirements and all customs requirements in accordance with the declaration. Preventive check was the final stage where customs officers checke goods prior to their exit from the port to prevent violation of revenue laws e.g. through smuggling, contraband goods theft were made to ensure revenue was not lost to the state. The 5 stages were interdependent and reliant on a set of technological and human actors operating at different stations or “swim lanes” (process flow diagrams in Appendix D)

## 4.1.1 | Manifest submission and related malpractices

A key document in the import clearance process was the manifest which described the goods to be cleared as wel as details about the quantity, shipper, consignor, consignee, origination, and value. About 7 activities were required in this sub-process (Figure D1 of Appendix D). Of importance to our analysis, paper copies of the manifest wer required to be submitted in addition to the electronic TRADENET submission. The stated purpose of the paper copies was to cross-check and validate the electronic submission to ensure they tallied. Paper copy submissions were also to help detect late submissions so that penalties could be applied.

However, in practice, manifest entries frequently concealed or misrepresented cargo and the mix of paper and electronic copies—instead of functioning as supposed—enabled negotiations between declarants and opportunisti officers who could be bribed to skip requests for supplementary evidence or overlook dodgy manifests altogether. In addition, reviewing cumbersome paper submissions increased inefficiency in the sub-process thereby causing delay and inducing the solicitation and payment of “speed money” (a bribe paid for quicker service)

## 4.1.2 | Declaration form submission and related malpractices

Crucial to the core customs mandates of revenue collection and regulatory compliance was the submission of the import declaration form (IDF) and the issuance of a final classification and valuation report (FCVR) that detailed how much duty and tax was owed to the state. Declarants and officers both had a keen interest in this sub-process, and it was the locus of much negotiation and corrupt dealing because it determined whether a declarant was to pay high duties and taxes or face heightened scrutiny of their goods. If an importer failed or was unable to pay their assessed duty and taxes, they could by law, have their goods forfeited to the state. This gave officers enormous coerciv power and sway over declarants

Despite the potential to automate this sub-process more effectively, about 18 activities involving paper copies and manual activities remained (diagram II (Figure D2) of Appendix D). Even when entries had to be made in TRADENET the system was misused to further malpractices among networks of officers and declarants. Such TRADENET enabled malpractices included

• Misdescription—declarants entered the wrong item attributes to attract a classification with lower valuation

• Misclassification—officers selected wrong item codes e.g. giving a cheap item code to a more valuable item

• Misinvoicing—declarants supported a claim with a false or wrong invoice to avoid paying fair duties and taxes

• Under-declaration—declarants provided lower quantities or concealed dutiable goods in their submission

• Over-declaration—declarants inflated dutiable goods to generate surplus sums that could be embezzled throug kickbacks by corrupt parties. typically other government officers in charge of procurement

• Data theft—officers took declarants' data such as phone numbers from the system to make unlawful persona demands for cash or favours

• Abuse of exemption codes—officers wrongly applied exemption codes to dutiable goods in violation of protocols

• Override of automatic assignment features—officers disabled or ignored functionalities of TRADENET such as th randomization feature that automatically assigned cases to officers to avoid collusion.

## 4.1.3 | Compliance

Although electronic data on declarations had been lodged in TRADENET by the compliance stage, officers stil checked paper copies of the declaration, and supporting documentation—supposedly for validity (truthfulness) and accuracy (consistency of the electronic submission and documentation). The workflow comprised interactions between officers and declarants (diagram III [Figure D3] of Appendix D). Malpractices that existed at complianc included:

• Officers sent unqualified consignments into the green lane where it avoided extensive physical examination (under inducement of bribes from declarants).

• Officers abused the query feature to create opportunities to solicit or receive bribes

• Officers abused the resubmission function to create opportunities to solicit or receive bribes

• Officers falsified remarks to justify their favourable treatment of a declarant

• Officers held up paperwork to create opportunities to solicit or receive bribes

• Officers created deliberate inefficiencies and redundant activities to induce payment of speed money

## 4.1.4 | Physical examination

Physical examination of goods has been a historical practice of Ghana customs although leading customs jurisdic tions, particularly in advanced economies rarely undertake that step (relying instead on scanning, risk profiling, and other unobtrusive methods). Physical examination involved opening cargo containers to search and examine their content by hand. Its related activities are outlined in diagram IV (Figure D4) of Appendix D. Physical examination provided opportunities for malpractices such as extortion and solicitation for cash and goods, deliberate hold-ups to induce bribes, pilfering of goods, and unlawful seizure of goods

## 4.1.5 | Preventive

Customs preventive was the final stage before goods left the ports. Although it appeared redundant with the work of security agencies at the port and overlapped with aspects of the compliance and physical examination functions it had remained a feature of the ports (diagram V [Figure D5] of Appendix D). Preventive officers, along with officers from other government agencies such as the port authority, national security, and police were a notorious presence at the customs yard where they could be frequently seen holding up trucks with cargo and soliciting for bribes. A common practice was for officers to hold on to the paper documentation of cargo and ask declarants to “come and see” them; a term understood in the port community as a dog whistle for bribes. ## 4.2 | Struggles over modernization with TRADENET

A decade after TRADENET was implemented it had not achieved the vision of paperless clearance and was functioning below the highest potential of a single window as stipulated by international standards (UNESCAP UNECE, 2012). Even for the most rudimentary level 1 single window (paperless customs declaration), TRADENET was some way off. Countries like Singapore that operated the highest level 5 single window systems had fully elec tronic (paperless) configurations that were integrated across all regulatory agencies, parties in the port community as well as other national single windows beyond the country's borders (UNESCAP/UNECE, 2012)

Customs officers continued to exercise significant administrative discretion and could choose to operate by procedures and practices different from what was officially expected. A senior TRADENET specialist noted that,

Even if a declarant sends the declaration form online as we are trying to get them to do, some customs officers at the “long room” will refuse to work until and unless they see hardcopies. Declarants are sent to make paper printouts even when it is clear their record is in the system

As explained by the general manager of the Ghana Community Network (GCNET), the public-private-partnershi company that managed TRADENET, traditional (mal)practices from previous eras of customs administration stil lingered because of the way TRADENET had been implemented and was being used to accommodate vested inter ests who resisted high levels of automation and integration (Eye on the Ports, 2015).

GCNET leadership regularly met with customs officers and stakeholders to encourage full use of the digital func tionalities instead of paper. As reported in the national daily newspaper (Darko, 2012)

In spite of this efficient and world acclaimed system, some stakeholders are reported to be requesting printouts of declarations at the entry points for transactions […] the GCNet team […] was not happy about the use of paper in the clearing of goods at any of the country's ports because doing so defeated the purpose of the electronic platform […]

A senior customs officer further lamented during an interview in August 2015,

the receipt that has been issued and all the other documents are digitized with the TRADENET [...] many of our officers still insist on seeing paper copies although it is quite unnecessary

Officers' fetishization of paper when only an IDF number or universal consignment reference (UCR) sufficed to access and digitally process documents, had questionable justification. Understanding the persistence of traditiona paper-based practices at Ghana customs requires appreciation of the historically-formed context of customs work and the tensions between its original mandate of revenue collection for the state and its later preoccupation wit trade facilitation and service improvements for the business community.

## 4.2.1 | Traditional logic of revenue collection

Ghana customs was fundamentally a rent-seeking organization, meaning it extracted money from importers and exporters (traders and individuals) rather than contributed to the creation of wealth. Officers of customs had enor mous coercive powers to that effect (including the ability to seize goods or make arrests) and had historically devel oped various rent-seeking norms (both for collecting official revenues for the state and unofficial amounts for themselves) rather than norms that promoted value-addition or improvements for service seekers

Ghana's laws mandated customs to collect and protect tax revenues on imports and exports; to expedite clearing processes; to ensure compliance with national laws; to ensure environmental security; and to collect and store trad data for statistical and regulatory purposes and for policy formulation (Ghana Revenue Authority, 2013). Of thes mandates, the revenue collection function was historically the most important for the state

Since the 1800s when Ghana was a British colony, customs enforced indirect taxation on foreign trade, deeme as a cheaper source of revenues compared to direct taxation of colonial subjects that carried the risk of costl revolts, expensive administrative manpower and supporting infrastructure (Gardner, 2012; Waijenburg, 2015). Post independence governments continued to rely on customs as a relatively less costly source of revenue for the state Customs was important in many developing countries for this reason, and the proportion of government revenu they collected in duties and taxes tended to be higher in comparison to developed countries (Cantens, 2012). For example, Ghana customs alone collected about 40% of Ghana's revenues in 2011; the equivalent of all domestic taxes collected in that year (Bank of Ghana, 2012, p. 3).

The government of Ghana, through its finance ministry, therefore measured customs performance by the yard stick of annual revenue targets. Failure to reach targets had serious consequences for officers, particularly senio staff, and this brought pressure on officers to meet their targets at all costs. Importers routinely protested exorbitan duties and taxes collected by customs at the port. Such high amounts were in addition to other payments they wer required to make to other government authorities that also raised money through levies and fees on foreign trade

## 4.2.2 | Modernization logic of trade facilitation

Tension existed between the rent-seeking or revenue collection logics of customs and the trade facilitation agend championed by the World Trade Organization (WTO), World Customs Organization (WCO) and other influentia bodies to improve the costs of doing business across borders and the overall costs of inefficiencies faced by th international business community. The period of TRADENET saw customs no longer viewed as standalone but as an important piece in the broader modernization of international trade administration.

Customs modernization, from the perspective of international organizations and donor bodies, was deemed to require re-engineering according to technologically-supported managerial “best practices.” TRADENET was there fore expected to replicate “best practice” examples with the support of IT consultants and change management experts (De Wulf, 2005a). The redesigned clearance documents processing was guided by the managerial logic captured in the WTO discourse of trade facilitation whereby officers were to adopt a professional and user-centric orientation, and to meet high service levels as set by the international standards organization (ISO)

For example, officers underwent service training in international standards such as ISO 9000 quality manage ment and this allowed them to deliver higher service levels than before. Private sector ethos such as manageria incentives, effective controls, interorganizational collaboration, and competition were also introduced into clearanc by TRADENET and the outsourced private companies such as the DICs. Such measures complemented other mea sures such as process streamlining to produce greater efficiency.

Overall, TRADENET and associated organizational improvements enabled gains in efficiency and effectiveness of the imports clearance process. The World Bank's logistics performance index (LPI), a scorecard that includes mea sures for the efficiency of customs, showed Ghana improving from 129th place among 160 countries in 2007 to 93rd place by 2016 (World Bank, 2016). Improved clearance TRADENET processes also brought a 31% year-on-yea increase in revenue collections (GPHA, 2015; GRA, 2015). These and other metrics were used by TRADENET cham pions as evidence for modernization gains.

Nonetheless, institutional tensions remained as customs officers saw their core focus to be revenue collection rather than improving services for importers. Revenue collection from service seekers was not limited to official rev enues for the state but was also understood by officers to include amounts collected in the form of bribes, spee money, and other illicit gains. A senior officer depicted this view when he observed in our interview,

The state depends on us for revenue mobilization and that is our focus […] I am not denying that some of our officers collect bribes; unfortunately, this is a challenge across the public sector in Ghana and many other countries where government workers view the state as a means to help themselves […]my job is to ensure our revenue targets are met […] unfortunately, we cannot watch every officer all the time. If no one reports an officer [for a malpractice] there is not much I can do […] and mind you, declarants are often the ones that corrupt our officers with inducements because they do not want to follow the laid down procedures (August 2015)

## 4.3 | Compliance pressure and managerial controls

Countervailing pressures at the organizational and national levels often undermined compliance with IT moderniza tion logics instituted by international development organizations like the World Bank. Despite the persistent push b the leadership of GCNET to encourage full use of TRADENET functionalities, the leadership of customs sometime appeared to be committed in rhetoric only and failed to enforce paperless in practice. No punitive sanctions wer pursued as the choice to use paper copies remained at the discretion of street-level officers.

Officers' persistent use of paper was part of a range of strategies that undermined TRADENET modernization of the imports clearance process. Such strategies also included malpractices that required misuse of TRADENET functionalities (Table 3). Although clearance transactions were captured in the TRADENET system, there was weak over sight and deficient managerial controls to flag suspicious transactions and sanction offenders. For example, in th permit regime which granted exceptions or reduced rates of duties and taxes for certain privileged groups like NGOs government agencies, top government officials, and strategically significant companies, abuses by street-level offi cers and their supervisors led to revenue shortfalls of several million US dollars. The permit regime was administered by street-level officers with inadequate controls by superiors (who appeared complicit) (Tiger Eye PI, 2011)

Fraudulent use of exemption codes was linked to political appointees and top government officials at the minis tries, in parliament, and even at the presidency (Tiger Eye PI, 2011). For example, the code “pending parliamentar approval” was for the exclusive use of only certain officials, and under limited conditions. However, it had been sys tematically abused to cover several non-applicable cases and ineligible consignees whose approvals never came. For several years, such exemptions had not been vetted or perfected with the proper approvals from parliament and th total bill was in tens of millions of US dollars. Political appointees and their associates also routinely used the name of the president or links to his office to clear personal goods such as imported fridges and cars. Such transactions could be audited in TRADENET with the help of a designated exemption code. However, such checks did not occur It was estimated that unperfected declarations—those that had not satisfied all formal duties and tax obligations but were cleared—reduced customs revenue by as much as 40% (Tiger Eye PI, 2011)

## 5 | DISCUSSION

We considered public administration modernization with IT as a form of institutional change that might be initiated from international organizations but must be worked out in practices of the local organizational context of developing countries. To better understand such working out of IT modernization, we derived expectations of four broad IT user responses from the literature on IT user responses in institutionally plural organizational setting (Berente et al., 2019). Based on these, key findings from our case study are summarized in Table 4.

As with common findings from studies of IT in developing countries, we do not find evidence of faithful appro priation because logics of IT were incongruent with traditional logics underpinning paper-based practices. Also, pres sure to comply with IT prescriptions and effectively enforce managerial controls were at best ambivalent rather than strong.

Outcomes of faithful appropriation and co-optation have been unfounded in the relevant literature because IT logics are incongruent with the traditional/incumbent logics of organization in developing countries. Examples abound in publications that highlight the modernization benefits of IT in the public administration of developing countries, but upon closer inspection, these often reveal aspirations, debatable or short-lived outcomes rather than long-term institutional transformation. Indeed, the history of computerization at Ghana's customs was highlighted in the promotional rhetoric of international development organizations as a success story of modernization (De Wulf, 2005b), but as our study shows, (mal)practices associated with traditional/incumbent logics were not elimi nated. The modernization logics of TRADENET were not faithfully appropriated to create a fully digitalized single window configuration or a paperless clearance process. Neither were there instances of co-optation where customs officers took control of TRADENET functionalities in ways that improved upon the modernization aspirations of th organization.

However, despite significant evidence of IT resistance in the public sector of developing countries, we do not find evidence of system rejection or abandonment of core functionalities. Although some customs officers expressed reservations about different aspects of TRADENET design, procurement and implementation, the system was implemented and used for many years. IT had clear benefits for efficiency in the core function of revenue collection which was of vital importance to the state, and had outcomes (revenue targets) that were heavily policed by interna tional development partners, senior government officials, and the customs leadership. Nonetheless, for the less foca functions of facilitating trade or eliminating malpractices and corruption, street-level officers retained enormous dis cretion and received ambivalent if any pressure to comply.

Officers' decision-making and discretion remained paramount over IT prescriptions and this was mostly justified because of the numerous international and national imports clearance regulations that required human judgement Consequently, modernizing logics introduced through IT were enacted to create hybrids of context-specific prac tices. Work configurations were formed by combining computer-based with manual procedures, digital storage of data with paper forms, online with face-to-face communication—exemplifying loose coupling arrangements found in the literature.

Persistent paper use was linked to strategies officers used to manipulate or co-opt IT negatively to further thei own interests. Although manipulation—the active take-over of IT resources to influence or control them (Berent et al., 2019, p. 879)—has been linked to co-optation, less is known about instances where manipulation/co-optatio are self-serving for organizational actors and go against the interest of the organization. Studies that examine IT us for petty corruption in developing country public administrations have provided evidence for co-optation as a nega tive, rather than potentially beneficial IT phenomena (Addo & Avgerou, 2021; Davis, 2004). In the context of IT enabled modernization struggles in the public sector of developing countries, the implications of such finding requir further elaboration.

## 5.1 | IT user responses and the context of developing country public administration

Negative co-optation of IT and other unexpected user responses during modernization initiatives are conditioned b patrimonial logics in certain developing countries, and a paradox that exists with administrative discretion. The litera ture on the government and politics of developing countries has noted patrimonialism as an enduring institutiona feature of such contexts. Patrimonialism refers to the persistence of personal rule and community-centred network that involve kinship or lineage, patron-client relations, and other “primary reciprocities” (Hyden, 2006). Although personal rule has existed in many places around the world (Hyden, 1997, p. 25), its prevalence and persistence in devel oping countries has been well noted, particularly in Africa, Latin America and parts of Asia (Clapham, 1982; Eisenstadt, 1973; Erdmann & Engel, 2007; van de Walle, 1994). Features of patrimonial institutional settings includ weak rational-legal authority in favour of patron-client ties and personal networks, as well as the abuse of publi resources for the benefit of public officers and their networks (Diamond, 2008, pp. 145-146).

The association between patrimonialism and underdevelopment has been linked to an absence of institutiona arrangements that guarantee stable revenues to the state to pay its officers adequately and to provide the condition needed for a professional and well-run service (Theobald, 1982). In this view, because of a developing country's numerous failures, public officers and citizens rely on personal relations to pursue their welfare although such rela tions are impossible to institutionalize (Budd, 2004). Other scholars have attributed the persistence of patrimonia logics to the foreignness of state and public administration arrangements imposed through colonialism despite preexisting institutional arrangements of governance (Englebert, 2000; Jackson, 1987). The term neopatrimonial ha often been used to characterize post-independent developing countries like Ghana to emphasize the complex hybri of bureaucratic and patrimonial arrangements that have resulted over time. However, unlike patrimonial administra tions, “the neopatrimonial regime hides behind a façade [of bureaucracy]” (Medard, 1982, p. 181).

## 5.2 | IT and the paradox of administrative discretion

Administrative discretion has been considered as necessary for the effective operation of street-level officers due to the complexity and high degree of contingency in public administration (Lipsky, 1980; Tummers & Bekkers, 2014). However, in the public administration of developing countries, administrative discretion appears to amplify th effects of patrimonial logics. This is because street-level officers in the patrimonial setting are inclined to pursue thei personal interests rather than the state or citizens', and to consider rational-legal/modernization principles as mer suggestions at best and an annoying inconvenience at worst.

This creates a paradox in terms of IT's potential to shape the practices of street-level officers in the public administration of developing countries. On one hand, IT is expected to curb the abuse of discretion by enhancing monitoring and laying down strict procedures and rules for action; but on the other hand, street-level officers requir discretion to remain effective in the complex and dynamic functions of administration where standards might change often, and service-seekers frequently come up with schemes to game government operations. Importantly, officer are generally unwilling to curtail their own discretion through IT, and efforts to that effect are met with pushback

Inefficiencies caused by manual and paper-based processes have been noted as an enabling condition for “para sitic process ecosystems” and the “corruption complex” found to be endemic in the public administrations of many developing countries (Addo & Avgerou, 2021; Blundo & Olivier de Sardan, 2006; Syed & Bandara, 2019). Contrar to modernization expectations for process efficiency, government officers in certain developing country settings view process improvements and efficiency as a threat to their self-enrichment schemes. Therefore, even where radi cal automation and process re-engineering are attempted, government officers might eventually re-intermediate pro cesses after they are disintermediated by IT, or create new opportunities for private gain (Addo & Senyo, 2020)

The challenge for IT modernization is to balance competing tensions between tight controls for officers' compli ance with logics of IT while allowing them flexibility to interpret and apply administrative procedures without bein able to exploit or co-opt them for private ends. So far, in the public administration of developing countries this has proven difficult as IT has not succeeded in curtailing institutionalized practices such as petty corruption (Addo & Avgerou, 2021). One reason is that street-level and senior officers in administration only have partial responsibilit given that patrimonial logics extend across government and society and normalize everyday interactions across orga nizational domains and social strata (Blundo & Olivier de Sardan, 2006). Thus, even though service seekers such as business and citizens might complain about malpractices and corruption in public administration, there tends to be some ambivalence at best and acceptance in many cases (Addo & Avgerou, 2021)

## 6 | CONCLUSION

Our study contributes an explanation of the expectation failures of IT modernization in the context of a developing country by focusing on institutional underpinnings, the articulation of incongruent institutional logics of IT and administration, and the prevalence of ambivalent or weak compliance pressure under patrimonial institutional condi tions. We show that public administration modernization as a process of institutional change might have limited potential when carried out through IT because of the nature of patrimonial institutions and the high levels of admin istrative discretion intrinsic to the work of street-level officers. The paper extends what is known in IS of IT user responses in institutionally plural organizational settings by detailing user responses in the context of public adminis tration modernization in a developing country setting like Ghana's.

Although IS literature has cast doubts on the potential of IT to reform public administration (Danziger & Andersen, 2002; Kraemer & King, 2006), we suggest that IT is not entirely irrelevant to modernization because it might enable the formation of hybrid digital-paper practices that represent significant improvements over traditional paper-only practices. Such improvements do not only promote efficiency but introduce modernization logics tha must be reckoned with in everyday practices, and that over time, might dampen traditional logics if there is sustained pressure to maintain rather than regress improvements. Efficiency improvements enabled by IT might also reduc the scope of opportunities for certain institutionalized malpractices (such as speed money in our case) to thriv (Addo, 2021), although it might not completely eliminate malpractices or corruption.

Our study suggests that modernization as a form of institutionalized practice change might occur gradually to the extent that information system configurations of a (neo)patrimonial administration shift the administration towards more prevalent managerial characteristics. Such theorization of IT modernization in developing countries as a process of institutional hybridization (mixing of logics in practice) implies that IT modernization in such contexts is cumulative rather than substitutive, long-term rather than episodic, emergent rather than plan-determined, an results in context-specific practices rather than expected or universalizable outcomes.

With few notable exceptions like Singapore that, during an earlier period in its development, successfully adopted a “big bang” approach to modernizing its society and public administration with IT (King & Konsynski, 1995; Lee, 2000; National Computer Board, 2009; Teo et al., 1997), developing countries continue to pursue IT modernization as a standalone intervention, with unsuccessful results. In the neopatrimonial context of developing countries administrative discretion of officers might not be entirely curtailed by IT alone. Therefore, IT modernization effort should be pursued in tandem with other vigorous administrative, political, and social interventions for change. For example, broad social and anti-corruption reforms, political will, as well as political continuity and commitment to IT modernization were crucial for Singapore's customs reforms and administration modernization (Civil Service Colleg [Singapore], 2015; Ha, 2013; Lee, 2000; Quah, 2018)

Furthermore, given the significance of administrative discretion in government policy implementation, pro gram designers and administrators, two key protagonists in IT modernization, could be better sensitized to loca conditions in developing country administrations, and in so doing pursue adequate outcome measures that better reflect fundamental institutional improvements rather than only efficiency and effectiveness improvements In the case of Ghana customs, improvements in revenue collection and efficiency (reduction in number of step and turnaround time) occurred without the elimination of paper use and associated malpractices, which only shifted from one form to another. The link between paper use and petty corruption must be better understood to provide an evidence-based approach for effective anti-corruption interventions that do not allow the dis placement of corruption to digital forms.

The case of IT modernization at Ghana customs bears similarity to IT reforms championed through initiatives by international organizations working in developing countries (Barbone et al., 1999; Kulshreshtha, 2008; Staple, 2002; World Bank Group, 2000, 2011). It also reflects dynamics of patrimonial influences that persist in many developing countries (Jackson, 1987; Jackson & Rosberg, 1982), and are manifest as endemic malpractices, corruption, and vested interests confronting IT in public administration (Addo & Avgerou, 2021; Berman & Tettey, 2001 Davis, 2004; Heeks, 1999a, 1999b; Masiero & Prakash, 2015). However, IT modernization of public administration in developing countries is complex and our explanation is not to be generalized across all administration types or developing countries.

We studied public administration practice and IT use as self-contained phenomena and did not adequately consider other important influences in the organizational and broader context such as national culture, politics, or vari ous institutional orders that shape the behaviour of IT users and service seekers. Future studies might consider the multiplicity of traditional and modernizing logics from various institutional orders, and other dynamics of traditio and modernity within developing countries and their various domains of administration.

## APPENDIX A: BRIEF BACKGROUND OF PUBLIC ADMINISTRATION IN DEVELOPING COUNTRIES

Many developing countries in Africa and Asia gained independence from western colonial powers after the second world war when they lacked administration structures to be effective (Jackson & Rosberg, 1982; Mosher et al., 2009). The picture varied, however, with colonial powers like Britain bequeathing relatively viable administra tion set-ups in former colonies like Ghana and India; compared to Belgium that left the Congo without trained administration staff (La Palombara, 1967; Mosher et al., 2009). Development programs have since been considered a remit of the state; and competent administration deemed sine qua non (United Nations Department of Economi and Social Affairs, 2009).

But the bureaucratic model introduced in developing countries was combined with existing traditional regimes of patrimony where elites controlled public office for private gain and without recourse to established rules and pro cedures (Bratton & Van de Walle, 1997). The combination of bureaucracy and patrimony resulted in practices known as neopatrimonial (Bratton & Van de Walle, 1994; Chabal & Daloz, 1999; Eisenstadt, 1973; Erdmann & Engel, 2006;

Van de Walle, 2001), where “officials hold positions in bureaucratic organizations with powers which are formall defined, but exercise those powers as a form of private property” (Clapham, 1985, p. 48). (Van de Walle, 2001). Neo patrimonial administration became a context-specific and dynamic hybrid because states varied in degree between extremes of patrimonial and bureaucratic (Bratton & Van de Walle, 1997, pp. 63–65; Kelsall, 2011, 2012 Mbaku, 1996, 1998).

The bureaucratic model also came under attack around the world for acquiring its own dysfunctions. For exam ple, it was noted for driving barest minimum standards through a culture of acting only according to the rule (Gouldner, 1954). Furthermore, bureaucratic organizations tended to increase in size, became resistant to change, and failed to innovate (Burns & Stalker, 1961). Excessive rule-emphasis fostered “goal displacement” as rules were misapplied at the expense of overall organizational objectives (Merton, 1957). Other dysfunctions included bribery and corruption (Klitgaard, 1991; Mbaku, 1996; Rose-Ackerman, 1999), red tape (Bozeman, 2000; Goodsell, 2000 Kaufman, 1977), incompetence (Hood & Lodge, 2004; Kamoche, 1997), and politicization (Dasandi, 2014 Peters, 2010). Bureaucracy was deemed unfit in practice (Peters, 1981) and not compatible with contexts outside the west (Hyden, 2010; Lynn, 2001)

Failings and distortions of the bureaucratic model motivated efforts to recast public administration (Aucoin, 1990; Hood & Lodge, 2004; Pollitt, 1990). Since the 1980s, variants of results-oriented public management have been tried (Clarke & Newman, 1997; Kouzmin et al., 1997; Pollitt, 1990) and the model usually designated a “new public management” (NPM) began to take hold. NPM placed emphasis on professional management and private sector ethos such as reliance on market mechanisms like performance incentives and efficiency through enter prise (Hood, 1995; McCourt, 2001; Minogue, 2001, p. 6). NPM's underlying thesis of best use of resources in pursuit of organizational goals stood in contrast to administration concerned primarily with regulation and legal enforcement (Dunleavy & Hood, 1994). Many developing countries experimented with items in the NPM bundle (Polidano, 2001, p. 47: Sarker, 2006). Besides NPM, reform of public administration in developing countries was also affected by a model of good governance associated with the Washington consensus approach to development, which focused on economic restructuring based on neoliberal ideas (McCourt, 1998a, 1998b; Stiglitz, 1999; Williamson, 2004). A public administration reform agenda focusing on privatization, unfettered markets and minimal government was championed by international development organizations and promoted as part of donor programs (DFID, 1997; Th World Bank Group, 1994; UNDP, 1998). The NPM model for administration and the Washington model of good governance provided normative influences on public administration in African countries and elsewhere, where the combined with neopatrimonial practices (McCourt, 2001).

## APPENDIX C: TRADITIONAL CLEARANCE BEFORE TRADENET

## Before IT implementation (before mid-1980s)

Before the mid-1980s when IT was first implemented at Ghana customs, import clearance was paper-based. Paper copies were bulky and cumbersome, error-prone, and slow to process. Processing involved face-to-face interactions between customs officers and declarants, and with this, opportunistic behaviours like bribery and extortion that were characteristic of patrimonial administration (rather than a bureaucracy). Manua processing also enabled tightly-linked interactions as paperwork moved from desk to desk, station to station, and one agency to another to be worked on. This allowed severe delays, hold-ups, and the abuse of discre tionary and coercive powers by officers to solicit bribes or to extort from declarants. Significant sums of state revenue were siphoned through malpractices such as account tampering, falsification of paper docu ments, and fraud.

Customs was historically part of Ghana's security services because of their authority of the national borders They were also empowered by the customs regulations to have similar powers as the police. As such, officers histori cally viewed themselves primarily as enforcers (of customs regulations and national laws). In terms of their interac tions with declarants, there was often abuse of their coercive power, and a lack of professionalism and courtesy. Officers claimed they were underpaid and were therefore reluctant to work unless they received bribes. Declarants who were adamant about paying bribes were shaken down or had their documents mysteriously go missing from th pile, only to re-appear once bribes were paid.

Traditional practices were organized and guided by personalization of public office, a lack of standardization, and weak rules that were applied arbitrarily and coercively. In terms of personalization, officers used their office as form of private property and prioritized declarants whom they were personally connected to and those who paid bribes for speedy service. This meant that those who were unable or unwilling to bribe, for example less affluent small business owners and citizens, had to wait an indefinite amount of time to get their declarations processed. Furthermore, the quality of service a declarant received depended almost entirely on whom they knew at the cus toms office, and on the willingness to pay bribes.

Although general guidelines existed, processes were not standardized, and the same type of goods could b cleared under wildly different duties and taxes. This provided officers with opportunities to abuse their discretion b levying arbitrary classifications and valuations of goods, which had the effect of inducing declarants to pay them bribes to get their customs bill reduced or rectified. Officers were also coercive because rules were weak and not strictly enforced. Power was exercised through networks of senior officers who were patrons of junior officers that they had recruited or promoted within the service, and who were therefore beholden to them. So long as an officer had the right links they could bend the rules and coerce declarants for bribes and favours that were then share among the tight-knit network of officers.

## Implementation of a rudimentary data management system (mid-1980s–2004)

The automated system for customs data (ASYCUDA) was a standard data management and statistical package developed in the early 1980s by the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) to “assist customs authorities all over the world to automate and control core processes and obtain timely, accurate and valuable information to support projections and planning (ASYCUDA, 1981; Mansaray, 1992 p. 345). It was intended to usher in system-wide transformation away from patrimonial practices that tradi tionally existed with manual processing, and to generate data for the Ministry of Trade and the Statistica Service in the context of structural reforms to boost bureaucratic capacity, to rationalize revenue collections, and to enhance standardization, hierarchy, monitoring and control across functions (Castonguay, 1999 Tettey, 1997, p. 343).

ASYCUDA's single administrative document (SAD) subsystem enabled declarants to assess their duties and tax obligations with one standard document, saving officers time. Entries were checked against harmonized item code to tally duties and taxes payable. However, declarants still had to submit copies to several agencies involved in trad processing, for example, regulatory agencies, shippers and banks. At customs, SAD was processed in the “long room”—a hallway where officers sat behind rows of glass partitions (multi-window) to interact with declarant (Appendix B).

Paper copies of SAD used at various steps were later entered manually into ASYCUDA. Overall, processing at the long room was tight-linked and rigid, susceptible to coercion and discretion of officers, and prone to errors and inefficiencies. It took 13 to 30 steps depending on various factors and could last weeks to months.

Customs assumed identity as administrators who applied processes such as harmonized classifications. The involvement of international and regional organizations such as UNCTAD with an interest in foreign trade clearance, produced a range of regulations and protocols that had to be administered through ASYCUDA. In short, although officers retained a high degree of discretion in everyday practices, there was now more operational and substantive requirements to be administered through ASYCUDA, which induced a new identity for officers.

ASYCUDA sought to strengthen processes and to reduce personalization of administrative procedures. Every declaration had to be lodged in the computer system instead of in several poorly kept notebooks and this enabled fraudulent transactions to be more easily uncovered. Furthermore, standardization was introduced to reduce incon sistencies in classification and valuation. The harmonization codes supported by ASYCUDA provided a basis to chal lenge unfair or unsubstantiated duties and taxes, and this enhanced clarity in the clearance process. Ultimately, rules were strengthened because malpractices, corruption, and fraudulent dealings were more likely to be uncovered tha before.

ASYCUDA was intended to enhance managerial controls because data was now centralized, easily queried, and easily analysed. Monitoring was also enhanced when transactions were entered into the system and this made it eas ier to fulfil reporting obligations to the government and to take remedial action if revenue targets were not being met. The system was also a means to achieve efficiencies by reducing the number of required steps and automating aspects of the process such as data collation and reporting. As a result, the overall process was to become mor effective than before with reductions in paperwork errors and improvements in revenue collections.
