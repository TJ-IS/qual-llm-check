# That's interesting: An examination of interest theory and self-determination in organisational cybersecurity training

## Abstract

With government and industry experiencing a critical shortage of trained cybersecurity professionals, organisations are spearheading various training programs to cultivate cybersecurity skills. With more people working from home and the existing cybersecurity staff shortages, cybercriminals are increasingly exploiting new and existing vulnerabilities by launching ubiquitous cyberattacks. This study focuses on how to close the gap in cybersecurity skills through interest cultivation and self-determined motivation. Our study shows that situational interest (SI) in cybersecurity along with situational motivational determinants (i.e., perceived learning autonomy and perceived relatedness) engendered self-determined motivation toward cybersecurity training. Consequently, self-determined motivation facilitated actua learning behaviour. Meanwhile, individual interest in cybersecurity created positive moderating effects in the relationships between self-determination and its key antecedents (i.e., perceived relatedness and situational interest). Based on these findings, we provide research implications accordingly.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

While organisations are fraught with ubiquitous cyberattacks, the cybersecurity workforce faces a drastic shortage of skilled workers. In a survey of cybersecurity professionals led by the International Information System Security Certification Consortium $( | \mathsf { S C } ) ^ { 2 }$ , 56 percent of its respondents claimed that a shortage of cybersecurity staff put thei organisations at risk $( ( | \mathsf { S C } ) ^ { 2 }$ Cybersecurity Workforce Study, 2020). The COVID-19 pandemic widens the gap in cybersecurity skills as cyberattacks impair organisations' information technology (IT) systems by exploiting working from-home vulnerabilities (Bennatan, 2021). At this critical time, closing the gap in cybersecurity skills is crucial fo protecting organisations' information assets.

A viable way of resolving the staff shortage is to train current non-cybersecurity workers. Presently, the federa government is encouraging its employees to voluntarily join free cybersecurity training offered by the National Initiative for Cybersecurity Careers and Studies (NICCS) (Stuart, 2020). Moreover, the Federal Cyber Reskilling Academy a public–private collaboration with the SANS Institute, offers cybersecurity compliance training with Executive Orde 13870 (Shaw, 2020). These training options are considered part of security education, training, and awareness (SETA) programs. SETA includes security training, such as skills-based lessons, that instruct trainees on how to perform complex security-based tasks, and security education that delves into the theoretical foundations of cybersecu rity principles (Hu, Hsu, & Zhou, 2021)

The challenge remains that cybersecurity is a cognitively complex field that precludes individuals from pursuing cybersecurity as a viable career option. According to the United States (US) National Initiative for Cybersecurity Education (NICE) framework, cybersecurity includes planning, managing, monitoring, securing, analysing, and gathering IT intelligence, spanning technological and managerial topics (NIST Special Publication 800-181, 2017). Cybersecurity's heterogeneous nature demands greater mental effort to form the connections between knowledge domains (e.g., between software and networking concepts) that subsequently lead to advanced problem-solving skills (Spiro, Col lins, Thota, & Feltovich, 2003). The learning process signifies complex learning that integrates knowledge, skills, and atti tudes across disciplines (van Merriënboer & Paas, 2003). Training programs that construct complex schemas, such as those described above, require considerable training time (Bransford, Brophy, & Williams, 2000), which is not ideal con sidering the imperativeness of filling the cybersecurity skills gap. Training programs that efficiently teach complex con cepts must enrol trainees who have at least a baseline level of background knowledge upon entering the program. IT workers may be the ideal trainees for such a program. IT workers possess the foundational knowledge upon which th cybersecurity discipline is built, thus making it easier to train them to protect IT systems

However, simply having an ideal trainee pool may not be sufficient for developing successful training programs Sustaining complex learning requires interest to promote learning quality (Schiefele, 1991) and learning engagement (Hidi, 2006). Interested individuals are motivated to learn despite challenges (Hidi, 2006). Organisations may use interest-based training to resolve the staff shortage. However, despite the potential for interest to positively impact learning outcomes (Krapp, 2002a, 2002b), information systems (IS) studies have neglected interest-based training Presently, IS researchers have built a wide-ranging research stream analysing the impact of SETA programs. Recent examples include investigations into SETA's role in reducing security incidents (Kweon, Lee, Chai, & Yoo, 2019), in mitigating phishing attacks via a mindfulness training approach (Jensen, Dinger, Wright, & Thatcher, 2017), and in improving employees' security behaviours through an emphasis on the training's relevance (He, Zhang (Justin), 2019) and on employees' accountability (Yaokumah, Walker, & Kumah, 2019). Moreover, several SETA studies examined security training through technological implementation (Beuran et al., 2018; Yamin, Katt, & Gkioulos, 2020) and training design (Jenkins, Durcikova, & Burns, 2013; Silic & Lowry, 2020). Despite the numerous prior studies related to SETA, the results are often confounding or contradictory in the broader research scope. Additionally, despite studying other variables that may influence users' security behaviours through SETA programs (Lowry, Posey, Bennett, Becky, & Roberts, 2015), SETA researchers have not yet explored critical cybersecurity staff shortages and how cultivating employees' interest in cybersecurity can address the ongoing shortage, even though interest may impact motivation. To fill this gap, we ask:

RQ1. How can an interest-based SETA program promote improved cybersecurity skills of IT workers?

Moreover, prior studies have posited that SETA was non-cognitive but persuasive (Karjalainen & Siponen, 2011). SETA tried to alter users' security behaviours through persuasive communication. However, w argue that SETA can be both persuasive and cognitive. To support this argument, persuasion theory contends that specially crafted messages persuade individuals by evoking their systematic cognitive processing (Wood, 2000). An effective training program draws trainees to cybersecurity (i.e., persuasion) through interest development, whic gradually propels them to grasp cybersecurity's characteristically esoteric knowledge (i.e., cognitive). This progression results from interest motivating individuals to learn by enhancing individuals' focus, cognition, and persistenc toward learning (Hidi, 2006). To examine how interest affects individuals' desire to learn cybersecurity, we form th following research question:

## RQ2. How does interest in cybersecurity entice individuals to learn complex knowledge?

We base our theoretical framework on self-determination theory (SDT) (Deci & Ryan, 1980, 1985) and Interest Theory (Hidi, 2006; Hidi & Renninger, 2006; Krapp, 2002b). Since SDT was used to understand the effect of interest on motivation (Durik & Harackiewicz, 2007; Schraw, Flowerday, & Lehman, 2001), integrating both theories revea the impact of interest on cybersecurity trainees' motivation and critical outcomes of cybersecurity training. Our find ings show that individuals' innate interest in cybersecurity produces minor moderating effects on the relationships between self-determination and its key antecedents. Conversely, situational interest (SI) developed during training directly fosters motivation toward cybersecurity training. We found that self-determined motivation was a focal con struct, bolstered by autonomy and relatedness within the training program and significantly affecting both effort and performance. In total, our research highlights the interplay between interest and self-determination, which is novel in the SETA research stream. Capturing trainees' interest during SETA is possible and has positive direct and indirect effects on motivation and behaviour. Based on these findings, we suggest that training programs need user interfac designs that incorporate our model's motivational and interest-based principles

The remainder of our study is structured as follows. First, we highlight fundamental studies that inform the hypothesized relationships between self-determined motivation and human interest, resulting in a theoretical mode that adapts interest and motivation regarding cybersecurity training. Second, we present our methods for testing th theoretical model and discuss the results. Finally, we share our research limitations, implications, and conclusion

## 2 | CONCEPTUAL FOUNDATIONS AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

## 2.1 | Knowledge domain of cybersecurity

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) (2021) defined cybersecurity as “prevention of damage to protection of, and restoration of computers, electronic communications systems, electronic communications services, wire communication, and electronic communication.” Alternatively, information security (InfoSec) is a subset of cybersecu rity, underlying the processes of preventing organisations' sensitive information from unauthorised access, modifica tion, and destruction (CISCO, n.d.). This study covers training in a cybersecurity context, including a broader notion of information assets protection. Conceptually, we adopted ethical hacking as part of our research design to highlight system vulnerabilities that could be mitigated through information assets protection.

The foundation of cybersecurity training in the United States is built on the NICE framework. NICE, directed by NIST under the US Department of Commerce, defined knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs) related to cybersecu rity (NIST Special Publication 800–181, 2017). The NICE framework proposed seven core categories of cybersecu rity knowledge: Securely Provision (SP) (e.g., designing and building IT systems), Operate and Maintai (OM) (e.g., supporting and managing IT systems for performance), Oversee and Govern (OV) (e.g., security gover nance and leadership for organisational effectiveness). Protect and Defend (PR) (e.g., identifving. analysing. and mitigating cyber threats), Analyse (AN) (e.g., analysing IT systems to gather cyber intelligence), Collect and Operat (CO) (e.g., collecting cyber intelligence via deceptive operations), and Investigate (IN) (e.g., uncovering cybercrimes through incident response and digital forensic investigations) (NIST Special Publication 800–181, 2017). Our stud incorporates a training program focused on teaching parameter tampering techniques, which feature elements of SP and PR from the NICE framework (see Figure 1).

Some individuals may find cybersecurity interesting due to its multifaceted nature. However, such a multifaceted nature suggests that cybersecurity training requires high cognition to grasp the complexity of software and network security, involves analytical skills to assess big data, and requires organisational skills to apply security knowledge in managerial decision-making. In short, cybersecurity training embodies complex learning characterised by high inter activities between knowledge structures (van Merriënboer & Sweller, 2005). “Complexity” refers to element interac tivity (Sweller, 2010), where an element is a concept someone learns, and high element-interactivity signifies concepts that cannot be learned in isolation. Therefore, attaining a good understanding of cybersecurity requires trainees to grasp these interactivities

To cultivate complex skills, learners need to develop declarative (knowing what to do) and procedural knowledge (knowing how to do it) (Anderson, 1996, 2013). Complex concepts cannot be learned in isolation (Darejeh, Marcus, & Sweller, 2021). Doing so requires domain-specific instruction (Tricot & Sweller, 2014), which SETA programs imple ment through awareness (learning the what ) and training (learning the how ) programs (Guttman & Roback, 1995). For example, to identify a cyberattack, trainees must grasp the interrelations between software and network architectures and then connect to the other technology services implemented in an organisation. Therefore complex learning integrates knowledge, skills, and attitudes (van Merriënboer & Paas, 2003), requiring the use of substantial mental effort (Paas & van Merriënboer, 1994). This integration enables trainees to identify the links within knowledge structures (i.e., pattern searching) and adapt the acquired knowledge for problem solvin (i.e., flexible adaptation) (Spiro et al., 2003). With such complexity, understanding how to best facilitate cybersecurit training becomes even more critical. Researchers have responded by developing a wide-ranging stream of studie focused on SETA

## 2.2 | Security education, training, and awareness (SETA) research

SETA programs comprise education, training, and awareness efforts that underline an organisation's InfoSec polic (Guttman & Roback, 1995). SETA programs can be operationalised in various ways and emphasise delivering broad information about the security environment along with the skills required to perform any necessary securit protocols (Lee & Lee, 2002; Whitman, Townsend, & Alberts, 2001). Awareness, training, and education programs are distinct categories of SETA and satisfy specific organisational goals (Cram, D'Arcy, & Proudfoot, 2019; Crossler & Bélanger, 2014; Hu et al., 2021). An awareness program involves distributing information about specific threats and countermeasures to make employees recognise threats in the corporate environment and remind employees of stan dard security practices. However, education programs typically fall outside the scope of organisational SETA pro grams and provide deeper insight into why users should adopt security countermeasures. Usually reserved for thos whose jobs require security expertise, this education often occurs externally (i.e., certification or specialised degre programs).

Before specifically studying SETA, IS literature primarily focused on general IS training. IS training research has long history of studying training techniques (Bolt, Killough, & Koh, 2001; Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Davis & Bostrom, 1993; Davis & Davis, 1990; Santhanam & Sein, 1994; Simon, Grover, Teng, & Whitcomb, 1996), technology-mediated learning (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Gupta & Bostrom, 2012; Piccoli, Ahmad, & Ives, 2001; Santhanam, Liu, & Shen, 2016; Santhanam, Sasidharan, & Webster, 2008; Söllner, Bitzer, Janson, & Leimeister, 2018), and corporate training (Gallivan, Spitler, & Koufaris, 2003; Kang & Santhanam, 2003; Lee, Kim, & Lee, 1995; Sharma & Yetton, 2007). By comparison, SETA is relatively new. The first academic study related to SETA proposed using SETA to prevent computer abuse (Straub, 1990). Nearly a decade later, Straub and Welke (1998) surveyed Fortune 500 companies and proposed risk mitigation via SETA. Gradually, more SETA studies emerged Puhakainen and Siponen (2010) studied how to refine and validate cybersecurity training, proposing that SETA should motivate employees and address employees' prior knowledge of a related subject. Karjalainen and Siponen (2011) also proposed a framework for SETA assessment and posited that SETA differed from IS training because SETA embodied non-cognitivism and persuasion that fostered employees' security behaviours

Several SETA studies have focused on persuading employees to engage in security behaviours. Specifically, Yoo Sanders, and Cerveny (2018) proposed that, during security awareness training, flow experience (i.e., immersion in one's activity) propelled employees to follow security policies (i.e., persuasion). Yaokumah et al. (2019) also posite that SETA altered employees' security behaviour (i.e., persuasion) by educating employees about their accountability Conversely, IS training mainly underlined cognitive learning, probably because IS training cultivated IS knowledge for job performance (Nelson & Cheney, 1987). In this context, Santhanam and Sein (1994) posited that mental model formed by IS trainees (i.e., cognition) affected their learning performance. Moreover, Sharma and Yetton (2007) pro posed that IS training taught employees critical skills (i.e., cognition) for system implementation

Most SETA studies have not focused precisely on training to resolve the critical issues of cybersecurity staff shortages. Instead, many SETA studies have focused only on security awareness programs that altered employees security behaviours through persuasion, rather than examining the cognitive perspective focal to training programs (Karjalainen & Siponen, 2011) (see Table 1). To fill in the research gap, we examine both the persuasion and cognition aspects of SETA by investigating how interest-based training affects individuals learning cybersecurity (i.e., cognition and encourages individuals to sustain cybersecurity training (i.e., persuasion).

## 2.3 | Self-determination theory (SDT)

SDT (Deci & Ryan, 1985) emerged in response to prior motivational research that classified intrinsic and extrinsic motivation as dichotomous constructs (Ryan & Deci, 2000). This stream of research draws a sharp contrast to learn ing motivation in some organisational studies. For example, Noe and Schmitt (1986) posited that employees wer motivated to join on-the-job training as they believed it would bring career advancement. Although Noe an Schmitt (1986) discussed psychological identification with one's job as a source of internalisation that fostered intrinsic motivation (Deci & Ryan, 1980), most motivational factors led to extrinsic motivation. Since cybersecurity training involves complex learning, one must have some intrinsic motivation to sustain learning in the long run. Thus, w chose SDT for a more granular view of motivation.

An individual's extrinsic motivation differs based on the degree of internalisation (self-determination) exhibite within the motivational type (Deci & Ryan, 1980). On the self-determined spectrum, the specific types of motivation are (ordered from least internalised) external, introjected, identified, and integrated regulations (Vallerand, 1997). External regulation refers to an individual being motivated purely by rewards or sanctions (Vallerand, 1997) Introjected regulation refers to an individual's motivation to be driven by important others' opinions, such as th attainment of pride associated with verbal praise or the avoidance of shame associated with verbal reprimands (Vallerand, 1997). Identified regulation is the motivation to perform some behaviour that is not self-determined but may lead to attaining some other internalised goal (i.e., an externalised means to an internalised end) (Vallerand, 1997). Integrated regulation is the motivation to perform a behaviour as an extension of oneself (Vallerand, 1997).

Several prior IS security studies have adopted SDT to examine users' security behaviour. Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) has been studied extensively as a key theory to predict security behaviour through the use of fea appeals. Prior research has further examined the impact of these fear appeals in an SDT context. For example, Wall, Palvia, and Lowry (2013) noted that motivation drove users to follow security policies via response efficacy (i.e., perceptions on how one's compliance would enhance InfoSec). Menard, Bott, and Crossler (2017) also posite that understanding users' motivation to protect information assets enabled a safer computing environment. Using SDT, Gangire, Da Veiga, and Herselman (2021) noted that users' autonomy and competency in operating a securit task shaped users' security motivation. Similarly, we assert that SDT could also explain users' motivation toward cybersecurity training.

We adopt situational motivation or motivation experienced by individuals when performing a task (Guay, Vallerand, & Blanchard, 2000; Vallerand, 1997). Situational motivation means that motivation can be momentary and dynamic. Domain knowledge (e.g., nature of a subject), task specificity, and environmental factors (e.g., support offered during training) can alter one's motivation toward activities in a specific situation (Murphy & Alexander, 2000). Thus, interactions between individuals and the environment affect situational motivation (Gagne, 1985).

According to SDT, individuals are motivated to learn when they believe they are competent (Ryan & Deci, 2000), meaning that individuals internalise their motivation to learn through knowledge acquisition. For cyber security training, perceived skills competency refers to individuals' perceived abilities in acquiring cybersecurit knowledge. Colquitt, LePine, and Noe (2000) posited that one's belief about his or her ability to learn fosters learning motivation in organisational training. Similarly, cybersecurity trainees who perceive a high level of competence in knowledge acquisition will be intrinsically motivated to learn. Thus, we propose:

H1. Perceived skills competency fosters self-determined motivation toward cybersecurity training.

Perceived learning autonomy pertains to individuals' perceived control over various tasks (e.g., topic areas and learning styles). In educational contexts, Patall, Cooper, and Wynn (2010) examined the impact of providing choices in homework assignments on students' motivation and subsequent academic performance, finding that students fel more competent and performed better on exams when choices were provided to them. This relationship is applicabl in work-based training situations as well. Prior studies have also shown that employees given the autonomy to selec their training programs showed learning motivation (Baldwin, Magjuka, & Loher, 1991; Wlodkowski, 1985). Individ uals who feel that they have the freedom to manage their learning will internalise the learning process (Gagné & Deci, 2005), resulting in greater intrinsic motivation to learn. Pelletier, Fortier, Vallerand, and Brière (2001) and Deci, Schwartz, Sheinman, and Ryan (1981) have empirically established that autonomy leads to self-determined motiva tion. We then propose:

## H2. Perceived learning autonomy fosters self-determined motivation toward cybersecurity training.

The third antecedent of SDT is perceived relatedness, which refers to individuals' perceived emotional connection to other related individuals or objects (Ryan & Deci, 2000). When individuals have an elevated perception of relatedness to a particular task, they exhibit a higher degree of internalisation (Gagné & Deci, 2005). Noe (1986 empirically established that employees who have formed a psychological identification (i.e., perceived relatedness with their jobs will be motivated to learn during training. Hence, we argue that when trainees feel that they have an emotional connection to cybersecurity (e.g., believe that their cybersecurity skills could protect a nation's critica infrastructure), they will be motivated to learn. We then propose:

H3. Perceived relatedness fosters self-determined motivation toward cybersecurity training

## 2.4 | Interest theory

SDT emphasises innate psychological needs of feeling competence, autonomy in learning, and a sense of relatedness to the knowledge domain (Deci & Ryan, 1985). Alternatively, interest signifies a psychological state during interactions between individuals and their objects of interest (Krapp, 2002b). Overall, SDT has a cognitive focus, which ma have ignored the affective aspect (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002) of cybersecurity training. Because interest theor embodies an affective component (Hidi & Renninger, 2006; Krapp, 2002a), integrating both theories would allow u to examine SETA from affective and cognitive perspectives

Deci and Ryan (1985) linked self-determination to interest by describing interest as “a directive role in intrinsicall motivated behavior in that people naturally approach activities that interest them” (p. 34). Moreover, Müller and Louw (2004) integrated both theories to examine the relationship between learning environments and interest devel opment. Krapp (2002a, 2005) also presented the interactions between SDT and interest development as a new theoretical foundation. However, interest and motivation are two different psychological states (Schiefele, 2001). The former embodies individuals' values stored in long-term memory, but the latter signifies the intention to do something. SDT does not indicate a cognitive link between an object of interest and motivation (Schiefele, 2001), but interest addresses how an object of interest affects motivation (Krapp, 2005)

Not all motivational outcomes are based on interest (Deci, 1992). Some individuals are motivated to engage in activities that they are not interested in to obtain external rewards. For example, employees may be motivated to learn cybersecurity to earn a job promotion within their organisation, but they may not be necessarily interested i the subject. Moreover, interest does not equate to intrinsic motivation but rather enhances the quality of intrinsic motivation (Deci, 1992). For instance, interest-based learning is not equivalent to but is related to self-determined motivation toward learning (Krapp, 2005) in such a way that interested individuals do not easily give up despite fail ures and frustrations (Renninger, 2000).

Prior literature on organisational training does not precisely address the role of interest. Noe (1986) proposed that interest fosters learning motivation in organisational training but did not examine the role of interest further Conversely, educational psychology literature has widely studied interest theory. O'Keefe and Linnenbrink Garcia (2014) posited that interest motivated individuals to attain challenging learning goals, and Katz, Assor, Kanat-Maymon, and Bereby-Meyer (2006) suggested that interest helped individuals cope with a non-optima learning environment. Therefore, we adopt interest theory to examine how individuals learn in an interest based SETA.

Overall, interest is a concept that describes a relationship between someone and their object of interest (Krapp, 2002b). Individuals decide their development through the self-determined interaction of activities that innately pique their interest in their physical and social environments (Krapp, 2002b). When an object of interest emerges in the environment, a person may integrate it into their self-structure. In general, the extant literatur divides interest into individual interest and situational interest. Prior studies have established that classifying interest into these two categories offered more precise results (Alexander, Jetton, & Kulikowich, 1995; Murphy & Alexander, 2000). Individual interest signifies “personality-specific orientation, reference valuations, or an awareness of possibilities for actions” (Krapp, Hidi, & Renninger, 1992, p. 6) such that individuals find meaning in activities that identify with their innate dispositions. In addition, individual interest tends to be durable (Renninger. 2000) and embodies preexisting values, in which feeling-related valence refers to positive emotion when interacting with an object of interest, whereas value-related valence refers to the belief that an object of interest is meaningfu (Schiefele, 1991).

In contrast, situational interest (SI) is developed in an environment (Hidi & Renninger, 2006) and aroused by an external stimulus (Hidi, 2006). SI is “elicited by aspects of a situation, such as novelty or intensity, and by the presence of a variety of human interest factors contributing to the attractiveness of different types of content” (Tobias, 1994, p. 38). For example, individuals may form SI in ethical hacking in an exciting learning environment. Unlike individua interest. SI will dissipate over time (Hidi & Renninger. 2006). SI comprises triggered-SI and maintained-SI (Hidi & Baird, 1986). Triggered-SI (i.e., catch) is an ephemeral state where interest is first aroused in an environment, stimu lating affective experiences and grabbing an individual's attention (Mitchell, 1993). Conversely, maintained-S (i.e., hold) is a more involved state where individuals find meaning in the object of interest to sustain interest over time (Mitchell, 1993).

When external stimuli cognitively arouse individuals (i.e., catch), they will be attentive in learning more about their objects of interest (Krapp, 2002a). Regarding interest theory, a program that successfully cultivates learning wil hold participants' interest (Mitchell, 1993). For example, when cybersecurity trainees are attracted to Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) attacks (i.e., an ethical hacking method) presented in a lesson, they experience catch. After realisin that this attack has been widely used to exploit web application vulnerabilities, trainees would want to learn more about XSS and how to prevent an XSS attack (i.e., hold). This example illustrates the process of catch leading to hold in a training environment (Hidi & Renninger, 2006), which we believe will be applicable in SETA programs. We then propose:

H4. Perceptions of triggered situational interest (catch) are positively associated with perceptions of maintained situational interest (hold).

As both extrinsic and intrinsic motivation involve forming intentions (Vansteenkiste, Simons, Lens, Sheldon, & Deci, 2004) to attain a goal, motivation entails a level of commitment that does not correspond to the fleeting natur of catch. While catch is ephemeral, hold lasts longer because it embodies a higher degree of self-initiation (Dewey, 1913; Mitchell, 1993). Hold is a more involved state of SI because it empowers individuals to achieve thei learning goals (Mitchell, 1993). Once individuals are empowered, they will engage in learning. For example, when cybersecurity trainees' experience holds, their learning is empowered by the suitable lessons that present the hacking techniques. Gradually, they will engage in the training that they find meaningful. Since hold exerts a more profound effect on learning than catch does (Mitchell, 1993), we assert that hold significantly affects self-determined motiva tion toward cybersecurity training. We then propose:

H5. Perceptions of maintained situational interest (hold) are positively associated with self-determined motivation toward cybersecurity training.

## 2.5 | Individual interest in cybersecurity

Individual interest signifies an internalisation process through which individuals identify with their objects of interest (Renninger, Ewen, & Lasher, 2002). Gradually, individual interest becomes a stable personal characteristic (Schiefele, 2001). Individual interest is an inner state that interacts with one's innate psychological needs (Krapp, 2005), including competency, autonomy, and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 1980). Because individual interest embodies ingrained values about an object of interest (Schiefele, 2001), individual interest may enhance the inter nalisation of that object (Durik & Harackiewicz, 2007). Gradually, this may engender a greater appreciation for the fulfilment of one's basic psychological needs, which individuals attain by interacting with an object of interest.

Our inclusion of a trait-based measure of individual interest representing one's inherent interest in cybersecurity as a subject may influence a trainee's motivation toward learning about a specific cybersecurity skill Indeed, researchers posit that motivation may be modelled hierarchically, with motivation occurring globally contextually, and situationally and each level connected only by direct proximal effects between motivation (Vallerand, 1997). For example, a contextual level factor (like interest in a subject area) would not directly influ ence situational level motivation; its influence on the situational level would only occur indirectly through contextual level motivation or would be exerted as a moderating effect on relationships within the situationa level. In our case, interest in cybersecurity would occur at the contextual level, and motivation to learn about a specific cybersecurity skill would occur at the situational level. This theoretical framework suggests that indi vidual interest may moderate innate psychological needs. In other words, individual interest in cybersecurit should act as a positive moderator on the relationships between training-based motivational antecedents (com petence, autonomy, and relatedness) and motivation to learn within a training program. For example, suppose someone who is not generally interested in cybersecurity is enrolled in a SETA program teaching a specifi cybersecurity skill. This trainee will not likely be motivated during the program, regardless of interventions bol stering competence, autonomy, and relatedness. Conversely, if someone who possesses an innate interest in cybersecurity is enrolled in SETA, this trainee will be more receptive to such self-determined interventions with each motivational antecedent able to influence motivation in its intended fashion. We argue that when one's general interest in cybersecurity grows, the effects of autonomy, competency, and relatedness on self determined motivation will be accentuated within a SETA program, resulting in stronger self-determined moti vation to learn a specific cybersecurity skill. We then theorise:

H6a. Individual Interest in Cybersecurity positively moderates between perceived learning competencies and self-determined motivation toward cybersecurity training.

H6b. Individual Interest in Cybersecurity positively moderates between perceived learning autonomy and self-determined motivation toward cybersecurity training.

H6c. Individual Interest in Cybersecurity positively moderates between perceived relatedness and selfdetermined motivation toward cybersecurity training.

Individual interest represents one's predisposition about an object of interest (Schiefele, 1991). Overall, individ uals display a high level of curiosity and personal attention about their objects of interest. For example, individuals with a preexisting interest in cybersecurity will be more willing to explore and learn the subject matter. In cybersecu rity training that arouses SI, trainees with high individual interest will likely get excited about cybersecurity and find deeper meaning (Ainley, Hidi, & Berndorff, 2002) in cybersecurity training to further engage in the training program They have already integrated cybersecurity into a sense of self, enabling them to espouse situational factors more profoundly, resulting in elevated self-determined motivation. Accordingly, we assert that individual interest moderates the relationship between SI (hold) and self-determined motivation. Following prior studies that have empiricall established that individual interest generated a moderating effect on SI (Durik & Harackiewicz, 2007; Høgheim & Reber, 2015), we propose:

H7. Individual interest in cybersecurity positively moderates the relationship between hold and selfdetermined motivation toward cybersecurity training.

## 2.6 | Actual learning behaviours

Motivation has demonstrated positive effects on attitude, cognition, and behaviour in various research contexts including education and training (Vallerand, 1997). When individuals are motivated to learn, they will exhibit behav iours indicative of continued learning. Motivation is a critical step for individuals to continue learning, as it increases positive affect and self-esteem (Chan & Ahern, 1999). Following prior motivational studies (Vansteenkist et al., 2004), motivated employees who participate in cybersecurity training will put more effort into their training leading to successfully completing the program. We then hypothesize:

H8. Self-determined motivation is positively associated with the actual learning efforts toward cybersecurity training.

Motivated individuals usually realise the strategic use of their learning efforts (Pintrich & De Groot, 1990) resulting in better learning performance (Alexander, Murphy, Woods, Duhon, & Parker, 1997). These findings follow the notion from Cerasoli, Nicklin, and Ford (2014) that intrinsic motivation fosters learning performance. In our short training program, motivated trainees may use a different tool (such as a browser extension) other than the built-in default features in a browser to run parameter tampering (i.e., an ethical hacking technique that alters the web inter face). These tools might make parameter tampering attacks more effective. Overall, we argue that motivated trainee will strategically think of a better way to run parameter tampering, resulting in better performance. Thus, we argue that motivation toward cybersecurity training empowers trainees to perform well. Hence, we propose:

H9. Self-determined motivation is positively related to the actual learning performance toward cybersecurity training.

## 3 | RESEARCH DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY

To study the effects of interest and motivation on participants' cybersecurity learning outcomes, we built a web based training program that incorporated motivational and interest-based features. Additionally, we embedded survey items to measure our trainees' perceptions about the program. Prior studies have measured subjects' perception of SI based on naturally occurring environmental factors (i.e., the novelty of a given lesson) rather than an experimen tal manipulation of SI (Knogler, Harackiewicz, Gegenfurtner, & Lewalter, 2015; Linnenbrink-Garcia, Patall, & Messersmith, 2013). Similarly, we designed our study to measure our participants' SI without explicit manipulation in the training program. For data analysis, we used covariance-based structural equation modelling (SEM) to determin the validity of our latent constructs, and used partial least squares (PLS-SEM) (Hair, Risher, Sarstedt, & Ringle, 2019) to analyse the structural model. PLS-SEM eases the normal distribution assumptions (i.e., maximum likelihood) use to estimate covariance-based SEM, offers a better estimation of complex models with smaller sample sizes, an allows for a more accurate estimation of moderating effects (Gefen, Rigdon, & Straub, 2011; Hair et al., 2019).

## 3.1 | Student sample

We initially ran a pilot study (N = 40) using an online training program and an online survey to determine each sub ject's SI and level of motivation for cybersecurity training. After receiving feedback from our respondents, cybersecurity instructors, we modified our measurement items and altered the training program's design. We excluded thes respondents from our main study and used a different web link to distribute our study to our main participants. We conducted a power analysis of our proposed structural model, which accounts for the number of latent construct and observable variables for those constructs along with effect size, power, and alpha. Using values of alpha = 0.05, power = 0.8, observable variables = 22, latent variables = 7, and estimated effect size = 0.25, we determined that we would need a minimum sample of 180 to achieve adequate power for our model's structure. Our final sample siz (n = 202) met this requirement.

Our subjects were college students from two private universities in the United States. Most of them had good grades (A's or B's) at the time of participation. Using student samples may trigger concerns of external validity. Com peau, Marcolin, Kelley, and Higgins (2012) questioned using students to study organisations' IT usage, as the power structure within an organisation differed from that in a classroom. However, our study examined subjects' perceptio of SI that relied on our training's interestingness (Hidi & Baird, 1986) independent of any settings. We also argue that our sample represented non-cybersecurity employees who wanted to develop cybersecurity skills to operate withi an organisation. First, our subjects majored in IT-related fields, which gave them some basic IT skills. Thus, they were like non-cybersecurity employees who had some understanding of IT. Our subjects were also appropriate for our training program because it required only basic IT skills rather than high-level skills that our participants lacked. Also cybersecurity is a relatively new field, thus making our training relatively novel. When confronted with novelty, indi viduals will feel curious or anxious (Kashdan & Silvia, 2009) and cognitively process novel information (va Merriënboer & Sweller, 2005). We then assert that our participants and non-cybersecurity employees may share some common human cognitions and emotions (Druckman & Kam, 2011) during cybersecurity training

We invited our subjects to participate in this study via email. We incentivised students to participate by offering a nominal amount of extra credit to their course grades. Participation was entirely voluntary, with 144 male and 58 female participants averaging 24 years of age. Our participants were predominantly male (71%), reflecting th current state of recent cybersecurity hires as only 11% to 24% female (Epstein, 2019). Our subjects had little to no knowledge of cybersecurity concepts before they participated in our study.

## 3.2 | Training program

We first presented subjects with an electronic consent form—those who chose to participate answered items related to individual interest in cybersecurity. Next, we redirected them to a short training program built using PHP con nected to a MySQL database server. We designed this training program based on prior studies of IS training becaus our study conceptually aligned with IS training research in several ways. First, consistent with technology-mediate learning adopted in IS training research (Alavi & Leidner, 2001), we used online technology in our training program Second, like most IS training, our participants engaged in learning for knowledge acquisition in support of task per formance. Finally, similar to prior studies that examined self-regulation in IS training (Santhanam et al., 2008; Wan Compeau, & Haggerty, 2012), we studied participants' motivation toward cybersecurity training.

Our training program explicitly focused on parameter tampering, a process of manipulating parameters in a we form to compromise an application (Trott, 2016). This vulnerability is caused by validating form entry on the client side rather than the server-side. Parameter tampering is typically not easy for novices but is also not difficult to master, mak ing this concept ideal for inclusion in our experiment. We designed three exercises by referring to the parameter tam pering concepts presented by the Open Web Application Security Project (OWASP) (OWASP, 2020). First, we determined the difficulty level for each exercise from previous college-level assignments and examinations on simila material. Second, we based the order on the number of students in the past who could complete similar exercises. Th preliminary study then tested a small sample of subjects to determine the outcomes before running a larger-scale train ing. We believed this set of exercises was sufficient to assess motivation, as it entailed cybersecurity learning.

To introduce optimal challenges, we implemented exercises with a difficulty level slightly above the subjects capabilities (Keller & Bless, 2008). Prior IS training research revealed that optimal challenges were an essential factor in encouraging individuals to engage in an activity (Jung, Schneider, & Valacich, 2010). We also implemented handson demonstrations (i.e., online videos) to show some ethical hacking techniques. Thus, subjects could access the embedded training videos to complete an exercise during the training; however, they were not required to watch any of the videos. These videos' availability aligns with the behaviour modelling (i.e., hands-on demonstration) approach adopted in IS training (Bolt et al., 2001; Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Simon et al., 1996). Also, consistent with Santhanam et al.’s (2008) notion that feedback improved IS trainees' experiences, our training program offered feedback to keep our subjects engaged. Hence, after each attempt, subjects were given feedback to rework the same problem or viewed a message indicating that they successfully finished the exercise. A subject could also click the “I Give Up! button to end the training. We presented this feature because autonomy was crucial in cybersecurity training (Chau et al., 2013; Kam et al., 2020). Accordingly, the button offered our subjects decision-making freedom in their training experiences (Sailer, Hense, Mayr, & Mandl, 2017), allowing them to end their training anytime. ## 3.3 | Online survey

After receiving feedback from our pilot study, we refined our measurement items to remove ambiguities. We then finalised our items and designed our survey instrument using best practices related to instruction wording and question order, as Dillman, Smyth, and Christian (2014) advocated. After that, we used a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 4 neutral, and 7 strongly agree) and adopted the pre-validated measurement items from the prior stud ies (Harackiewicz, Durik, Barron, Linnenbrink-Garcia, & Tauer, 2008; Vallerand, 1997; Wilson, Rogers, Rodgers, & Wild, 2006) (see Appendix A (Table A1)). The reflective constructs are perceived skill competency (PCOMP), per ceived relatedness (PREL), perceived learning autonomy (PAUT), catch (CATCH), hold (HOLD), and individual interest in cybersecurity (IIC). We randomised all the measurement items to reduce order effects (Podsakoff et al., 2012). To minimise common method bias (CMB), we adopted the best practices related to the proximal separation of the measurement items used to represent our independent and dependent variables (Podsakoff et al., 2012). We conducted a post-hoc CMB analysis and found that CMB did not significantly impact the fit of our measurement model (se Appendix D for further details).

We incorporated situational self-determined motivation (MOV) in our model as a single composite index. In general, situational motivation comprises four types of motivation along the self-determined spectrum—intrinsic motiva tion, identified regulation, external regulation, and amotivation. Researchers can use the values of these four type of motivation to compute a single composite index (Vallerand, 1997), in which a high value leans toward intrinsi motivation through an aggregation of the different degrees of self-determination. Vallerand (1997, p. 287) offered his perspective on using a single composite index:

“… [T]he use of a single score, such as the self-determination index, may at times be useful. For instance, the index may be used when researchers want to select individuals who display either a self-determined or a non-self-determined motivational profile.”

Because a single composite index represents the degree of motivation in a continuum of self-determination, we can examine the intensity of motivation toward an exogenous factor. For example, what degree of self-determination i produced by an interest in cybersecurity (i.e., exogenous factor)? We argue that using a single composite index help reveal the degree of self-determined motivation without including too many indicator variables (related to motiva tion) in a structural equation model (Vallerand, 1997). As a result, this facilitates model parsimony.

We conceptualised actual learning effort (EFF) as a formative construct measured by two attributes: the tota number of attempts in all the exercises and the total number of times a subject watched the online video (i.e., tutorials) in our training program. These attributes do not create a unidimensional construct with a common theme (Petter, Straub, & Rai, 2007). Nevertheless, they also denote one's commitment to a specific task, especially when the challenge intensifies (Meyer, Becker, & Vandenberghe, 2004). Therefore, EFF measures an individual's persistence on a task. The user may attempt an exercise for an extended period of time but ultimately may decide to give up. Although our conceptualization is valid based on prior research on formative indicators and training pro grams, we statistically validate our formative conceptualization (see Construct Validity and Reliability subsection) Moreover, we applied min-max normalisation (Han, Kamber, & Pei, 2011) to convert data collected into the 7-Likert scale for these attributes. We captured learning performance (PERF) by tracking the number of exercises completed successfully by our sub jects. Our training program gave participants the foundational knowledge to solve each exercise, but we did not offer guidelines on how to solve specific exercises. ## 4 | DATA ANALYSIS

We ran bootstrapping with 3000 resampling iterations, which is adequate for estimating standard errors and con fidence intervals (Hesterberg, 2011). We also conducted a one-tailed test because each hypothesis articulated a directionality between two variables (Kock, 2015). Using PLS-SEM, we assessed the effect size (F<sup>2</sup>) of each hypothesized relationship. It is considered a good practice to use effect size along with null-hypothesis signifi cance testing (NHST) in IS studies (Aguirre-Urreta & Rönkkö, 2015). A fundamental limitation of NHST is sensitiv ity to sample size (Ferguson, 2009). Because effect size is independent of sample size, it delivers an accurate measure of the magnitude of the effect between an exogenous variable and an endogenous variable (Ferguson, 2009). Effect sizes larger than 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 signify small, medium, and large effect sizes, respec tively (Cohen, 1977).

## 5 | DISCUSSION

## 5.1 | Summary of analysis results

Our results revealed that maintained situational interest (SI) (i.e., hold) and situational motivation determinants (except for perceived skills competency) facilitated self-determined motivation toward cybersecurity training. W believe that the complexity of cybersecurity as a subject matter could explain an insignificant relationship betwee perceived skills competency and motivation. Given cybersecurity's complexity, some subjects might not accuratel view their technical competencies but were nonetheless motivated to learn. Conversely, our post-hoc analysis noted that a higher degree of perceived skills competency increased learning performance. Thus, we assert that high perceived skills competency fostered positive learning outcomes.

Notably, individual interest in cybersecurity (IIC) did not directly affect self-determined motivation. ## 5.2 | Research findings

Our results revealed that individual interest exhibited a significant positive but small moderating effect between hold and motivation and between perceived relevance and motivation. These results indicate that, while individual inter est somewhat strengthens the effects on motivation, one's lack of individual interest would not preclude the effec tiveness of SI boosting motivation. Alternatively, catch and hold can be incorporated into training programs and stil be effective at bolstering motivation, even for subjects not generally interested in cybersecurity before entering the training program. With this finding, we could then address our first research question—how does an interest-based SETA program increase cybersecurity skills? When trainees show self-determination facilitated by SI, they will be mor persistent and more likely to complete their training programs.

Furthermore, SI coupled with perceived relatedness motivates trainees to engage in learning. Meanwhile individual interest in cybersecurity training augments the effects of SI and perceived relatedness on selfdetermined motivation. This finding suggests that subjects may be first captivated by our training lessons (i.e., catch). They may later realise that our lessons have real-world relevance (i.e., perceived relatedness), pro pelling them to sustain their interest (i.e., hold). Thus, with predispositions in cybersecurity (i.e., individual interest), subjects will be more willing to engage in cybersecurity training. In fact, an interplay between SDT (i.e., perceived relatedness) and interest theory (i.e., individual interest) along with an interaction between SI and individual interest helps address our second research question—“How does interest in cybersecurity entic individuals to learn complex knowledge?”

## 6 | RESEARCH IMPLICATIONS

## 6.1 | Theoretical implications

As we noted in our literature review, several SETA studies have leveraged some form of persuasive communication to convince users to adopt safe computing behaviours (see Table 1). Our study contributes to this stream of researc by examining persuasive SETA programs through the lens of SI. One theory that has been adapted in prior SETA research is persuasion theory (D'Arcy & Lowry, 2019), which posits that both affect and cognition alter individuals attitudes (Wood, 2000). Thus, messages that appeal to positive moods would be more persuasive to some individ uals. In contrast, specific messages persuade individuals by evoking their systematic cognitive processing, such as making a deliberate judgement (Wood, 2000). Similarly, SI comprises catch and hold toward an object of interest, in which catch corresponds to affect and hold corresponds to cognition (Mitchell, 1993). These theoretical similarities indicate that interest theory aligns with persuasion theory (D'Arcy & Lowry, 2019) but provides additional nuanc through its explanation of psychological processes that capture and maintain someone's attention. Our findings offe an initial empirical basis for this link and point to future research opportunities on determining optimal affective an cognitive conditions for SETA program success.

Personal relevance also affects the effectiveness of a persuasive message (Petty, Cacioppo, & Goldman, 1981) Individuals who perceive that a message is highly relevant are more likely to carefully consider the quality of th arguments presented in the message. Conversely, those who view a message as less personally relevant are mor likely to pay attention to factors external to the message itself, such as the message's source (Petty et al., 1981) Along these lines, security researchers have examined the importance of personal relevance on users' adoption of secure behaviours, studying both the use of sanctioning rhetoric within fear appeals proposed by protection motivation theory (PMT) (Johnston, Warkentin, & Siponen, 2015) and the development of SDT-based appeals that explicitl feature relatedness (Menard et al., 2017). In these examples, researchers leveraged relevance to emphasise th importance of cybersecurity to those who might not otherwise think that it personally affects their lives. In our work we offer additional evidence that relevance is not only crucial for the general adoption of safe computing practices but also for cultivating individuals' motivation to learn about cybersecurity more deeply. The respondents in ou study were already aware of cybersecurity's relevance, yet relatedness still significantly impacted motivation. Addi tionally, although researchers have conducted numerous PMT-based studies in the cybersecurity domain, our find ings highlight opportunities to explore further the role of individual and SI on fear appeal effectiveness, offering more nuance to the applicability of PMT for security-based situations. Further, individual interest in cybersecurit enhanced the relationship between relatedness and motivation. This finding indicates that persuasive techniques that bolster personal relevance may be acutely effective on trainees exhibiting a high general interest in cybersecu rity before entering the training program.

Our post-hoc analysis demonstrated that perceived skills competency positively affected learning performance This finding could be related to work by Crossler and Bélanger (2019), who suggested promoting smartphone users privacy by closing the knowledge-belief gap. In this research, knowledge refers to one's awareness of privacy protec tions available in smartphones, whereas belief refers to one's self-efficacy in using a smartphone's privacy features The authors observed an interaction effect where individuals with more knowledge about information privacy used less restrictive privacy settings when their privacy self-efficacy was low; as their self-efficacy increased, they wer more likely to utilise more restrictive settings. Similarly, we may be observing a competence-interest gap in our results. We observed an interaction effect such that as individual interest in cybersecurity increased, the impact of perceived competence on motivation was weakened. We believe that our respondents viewed cybersecurity as a novel, promising field. However, these perceptions coincided with a lack of experience, resulting in such respondent not possessing confidence in their cybersecurity capabilities during the training program. It seems paradoxical that someone interested in a topic would not also possess a higher level of confidence in their ability to learn more about the topic. However, we believe this speaks to the high barrier of entry into dense fields, like cybersecurity. Thi paradox warrants further investigation in cybersecurity research and other domains that feature a high barrier of entry due to the complexity of the domain. Determining the factors that close the competence-interest gap may b crucial in improving participants' motivation during SETA programs.

Our work contributes to the research streams of both SDT and interest theory, particularly highlighting how self-determination and interest interact. Researchers have long considered motivation to be hierarchical in nature meaning that motivation occurs at general, contextual, and situational levels with top-down and bottom-up effect (Vallerand, 1997). Our study examined motivation and interest at the situational level, which captured individuals interest and motivation toward a specific activity (in our case, cybersecurity training on parameter tampering). The contextual level would encompass individuals' perceptions of cybersecurity, including perceptions of individual inter est in cybersecurity. Our work demonstrates that individual interest is a critical contextual-level construct that should be included in models that integrate situational-level interest and motivation for examining SETA program effectiveness. In addition, our results showed that individual interest might moderate these relationships in unex pected ways, as illustrated by the competence-interest gap. Researchers should also test these moderating effects i other contexts to further gauge how individual interest operates as a moderator.

## 6.2 | Practical implications

Our results showed that perceived relatedness generated a sizable impact on motivation. To sustain trainees' inter est, we suggest that the development of training programs explicitly highlight the real-world relevance of learnin cybersecurity skills. Training creators can achieve this relevance by using a “serious fun” approach, which entices current employees to receive cybersecurity training by combining relevant subject matter (real-world security impli cations) with a user interface (UI) design that fosters hedonic perceptions. Since SI begins with triggered-SI (catch) we assert that a training program should start with a good UI design that promotes usability and attracts trainees attention. Prior studies have empirically demonstrated that flow affects security training success (Kam et al., 2020 and that UI design facilitates security training (Baxter et al., 2016; Beuran et al., 2018; Cone et al., 2007; Jenkin et al., 2013; Silic & Lowry, 2020). Thus, we believe an attractive UI could keep trainees engaged. For example, whil trainees are still intrigued by the fun introduced in the early training stage, instructors could link a current event (e.g., ransomware attack) to an existing lesson (e.g., server hardening) and then present that current event and lesso on the same UI. This will help trainees realise the relevance of their lessons, gradually finding deeper meanings in their training to enable a maintained SI (hold).

Furthermore, suppose existing employees are being re-trained for cybersecurity positions within their organisations. In that case, security managers may want to avoid repurposing traditional security modules they may have used for periodical compliance training. Rather than creating training modules that are inherently interesting to employees, security professionals typically structure their organisation's cybersecurity training with efficiency an scalability in mind. This approach is ultimately manifested as SETA programs that attempt to make employees aware of cybersecurity issues in as little time as possible while being efficiently distributed to as many employees as possi ble, resulting in one-size-fits-all programs that are not inherently interesting to participants and do not promot long-lasting impacts on employees' cybersecurity knowledge and abilities. Our research demonstrates tha employees' interest in cybersecurity matters in determining their motivation and behaviour within a training program.

Moreover, SI has both direct and indirect effects on motivation and training behaviour. Therefore, security man agers should develop or adopt re-training programs that catch and hold employees' interest. Doing so should result in employees being more prepared for their new cybersecurity duties and a better understanding of the pertinen threats posed to the organisation, the critical goal of organisational security training. Our findings also demonstrat that training programs may not need to be specifically catered to the level of the individual trainee. Rather, organisa tions may find training to be more scalable by targeting a cohort of people who share a similar background. In ou study, we targeted our training toward people with IT experience. This type of training is likely to intrigue similarl profiled participants, thus motivating those participants through increased interest

## 6.3 | Limitations and future research

Although we designed our study to minimise weaknesses to the greatest extent possible, our work is not without limitations. Trainability (i.e., ability to learn cybersecurity) is considered one of the key factors facilitating training program success (Noe, 1986; Noe & Schmitt, 1986). Most of our subjects were seniors in college who possessed som background in IT; before participating in this study, they had already been exposed to complex IT-related concepts in their programs of study. Thus, the variance in our respondents' trainability would likely be narrow. Future researc should replicate our study but with a broader spectrum of trainability among respondents

Our research examines the effectiveness of SI and self-determination within a training program in a cross sectional manner. However, in prior psychology research, both interest and motivation have exhibited positive long term effects on outcomes such as attitude, behaviour, cognition, and others (Krapp, 2002a). Although our research captured certain key variables over a short period (e.g., motivation, effort, and performance), SI and selfdetermination may have had longitudinal impacts on our subjects that we could not measure in this study. Addition ally, our study design was executed to tightly control the training environment and accurately capture the effects of interest and motivation. Our participants were not exposed to certain factors from a real-world workplace that would interact with the variables contained in our model, such as balancing their training requirements and typica daily tasks or facing pressure from upper management to achieve adequate training performance. Future researchers may want to study our model longitudinally within an organisation to assess the long-term effects of interest and motivation on trainees' security knowledge and learning persistence

Similarly, as a cross-sectional study, we could not account for the effect of the forgetting curve, which demon strates that learning retention decreases quickly after training (Ebbinghaus, 2013). Future research may build on the similar concepts we explore here (situational interest's catch and hold concepts) to incorporate a measure of reten tion decay over time and the ways in which the forgetting curve can be thwarted in SETA situations (Dunlosky Rawson, Marsh, Nathan, & Willingham, 2013). Additionally, we presented all respondents with the same training program. Future researchers may also want to examine the effects of interest and motivation using differential SETA to assess other conditions under which interest and motivation are maximised.

Our study was also limited to capturing the perceptions of our respondents using validated survey-based measurement scales. To further understand the underlying psychology behind how interest and motivation emerge bio logically, neuro-IS researchers may be interested in replicating this research while measuring participants' brai activity (Hidi, 2006). Because neuro-IS research has effectively addressed users' security behaviour (Anderson Vance, Kirwan, Jenkins, & Eargle, 2016; Vance, Anderson, Kirwan, & Eargle, 2014; Vance, Jenkins, Anderson, Bjornn, & Kirwan, 2018), researchers could pinpoint which moments within training programs trigger the brain activ ity associated with motivation and cognitive effort. Neuroscience measurement techniques may also allow researchers to more accurately capture variables that correspond with effort, potentially explaining more variance i effort than the factors and methods we used in this study.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

Organisations have consistently been at risk of attacks on their information assets. The shortage of employee trained in cybersecurity skills exacerbates this problem. Our study examines the interplay between situational moti vational determinants (i.e., perceived skill competency, perceived learning autonomy, and perceived relatedness) and interest regarding cybersecurity training to understand better how future cybersecurity workforce may be cultivated

We have empirically established that SI can be cultivated in a training environment that fosters key situational motivational determinants—feelings of competency, autonomy, and relatedness to training. Furthermore, our findings empirically highlight how organisations can help facilitate employee interest and motivation and hopefully lead to more robust workforce and ultimately a safer environment for organisational information assets

## APPENDIX D

Common method variance (CMV) refers to spurious correlations occurring in a dataset due to systematic error (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003). This can manifest when a common method is used to measure al items in a survey instrument. Because the present study includes the collection of measurement items using a com mon data collection mechanism, common method bias is a potential area of concern. The presence of commo method bias indicates that common method used is contributing to some of the correlations present in latent variables. Researchers who detect common method bias cannot state that observed correlations are fully attributable to the underlying relationships present in the studied phenomenon. This can severely impair data interpretation

To ensure that correlations were not falsely inflated or deflated due to common method bias, researchers can employ a variety of techniques in the instrument development phase to potentially reduce the likelihood of common method bias manifesting. Some procedural remedies include conducting expert panels for purifying measurement items, randomization of items within the instrument, and temporally spacing measurement items as they are pres ented to the respondent (Podsakoff et al., 2003). We utilised each of these techniques in the present study.

Although we took preventative measures to mitigate the potential effects of common method variance, we also conducted a post hoc examination of the potential influence of CMV on the data (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Including an unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC) in the measurement model allows researchers to determine if there is a significant change in model fit due to the inclusion of the ULMC. This technique is appropriate for measurement models calculated using maximum likelihood (Marsh & Hocevar, 1988; Straub, Boudreau, & Gefen, 2004; Woszczynski & Whitman, 2004). The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference test assesses the degree of difference in model fit between competing models. These models differ by one degree of freedom, which means that a significant difference between models at an alpha level of 0.05 can be demonstrated by a difference in $\chi ^ { 2 }$ values greater than or equal to 3.84 (Collier, 2020). The difference in $\chi ^ { 2 }$ values between the standard measurement model and the ULMC model indi cated that common-method variance did not have a significant impact on the main study's datase $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 3 6 . 2 7 4$ with common-method factor included; $\chi ^ { 2 } = 2 3 9 . 2 7 4$ without common-method factor; $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference = 3.4).
