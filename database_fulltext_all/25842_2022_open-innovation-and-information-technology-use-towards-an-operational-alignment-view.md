## Abstract

A centralised open innovation search approach refers to setting up a dedicated and specialised sourcing unit to search external sources for knowledge, while a decentralised open innovation search approach refers to empowering employees in various functional units to search external sources for knowledge during their routine research and development work. This research examines the operational alignment of the open innovation search approach with information technology (IT) use given that open innovation search can be significantly enhanced by IT. We theorise that organisations that have a high extent of employing a centralised (decentralised) open innovation approach and a high IT use intensity for external (internal) information flows are likely to have a high extent of exploratory (exploitative) innovation. We further posit that harnessing exploratory and exploitative innovation can lead to organisational innovation performance. This theory was validated through a field survey involving 186 organisations. The findings from this study advance the IT and open innovation literature by gaining an understanding of the interplay among different open innovation search approaches and IT use, and the resulting variances in innovation performance in terms of new product sales and patents.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Organisations that aim to be innovative need to actively access and acquire knowledge that goes beyond their insti tutional boundaries, which is termed open innovation search (Appleyard & Chesbrough, 2017; West & Bogers, 2014) Although the benefits of engaging in open innovation search are discussed in several prior studies (Berchicci, 2013; Chesbrough, Vanhaverbeke, & West, 2006; Gomez, Salazar, & Vargas, 2017; Huston & Sakkab, 2006), it is still challenging for practitioners to effectively implement open innovation search for the desired innovation outcome. For example, an organisation could set up a dedicated and specialised organisational unit to perform the open innovation search (ie, centralised approach) or empower existing employees in various functional units to search externa sources for knowledge during, for example, their routine research and development (R&D) work (ie, decentralised approach). However, it remains unclear in the existing literature which of the two approaches would be mor rewarding and in which conditions it would be

Seeking such an understanding requires recognition that open innovation search is significantly enhanced by information technology (IT) (Cui, Ye, Teo, & Li, 2015; Gomez et al., 2017; Trantopoulos, Von Krogh, Wallin, & Woerter, 2017). Explicitly, an open innovation search is supported by IT tools, such as knowledge management tools and enterprise applications, to source knowledge external to the firm and share it with employees easily and quickly (Lioukas, Reuer, & Zollo, 2016; Ravichandran, Han, & Mithas, 2017).<sup>1</sup> Prior alignment studies (eg, Croteau & Bergeron, 2001; Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007) have predominantly focused on intellectual alignment. However, there is a gap between the organisational strategy (what organisations plan to do) and the actions taken to realise the strategy (what organisations do) (Mintzberg, 1987). Emerging research calls for studies to examine operational alignment for greater realism (eg, Gerow, Thatcher, & Grover, 2014; Queiroz, 2017). Hence, this research fills a void in the cur rent understanding by opening the black box of open innovation search and IT use (to support external or interna information flow) at the operational level. It raises the fundamental question of whether alignment would be achieved at the operational level, as opposed to the strategic level according to the business–IT strategy alignment.

Anchoring on the alignment perspective and based on the open innovation literature, we theorise that an opera tionally aligned open innovation search approach and IT use (eg, a centralised open innovation search approach wit high IT use intensity for external information flows and a decentralised open innovation search approach with high IT use intensity for internal information flows) can achieve better innovation performance, as mediated by explor atory and exploitative innovation. We further consider and theorise the joint influence of exploratory and exploit ative innovation on innovation performance. A field survey involving 186 organisations was conducted to validat our conjectures. Based on the empirical results, this study contributes to the extant work on alignment by highlighting the importance of operational alignment between open innovation search approaches and IT use for information flow, which could lead to improved innovation performance. It further advances prior discussion in the open innova tion and IT literature (Cui, Wu, & Tong, 2017; Dodgson, Gann, & Salter, 2006; Trantopoulos et al., 2017) by discover ing how organisations can develop internal practices to source and assimilate external knowledge. Furthermore, th findings contribute to the IT and innovation literature by identifying the distinct impacts of IT use and developing a holistic framework for exploratory and exploitative innovation.

## 2 | THEORETICAL FOUNDATION

Zynga (2013, p.2) pointed out, “just as biological organisms have antibodies whose job is to combat intruding organisms, every organization has human versions of antibodies who make it their business to fend off foreign ideas. Sim ilarly, open innovation antagonises the long-term, well-defined boundary of organisational innovation. To this end the approach that open innovation uses to access external knowledge matters (Fey & Birkinshaw, 2005). Indeed engaging in open innovation is a delicate task that requires an organisation to manage its open innovation searches (Laursen & Salter, 2006).

## 2.1 | Open innovation search approaches: Centralised and decentralised

Existing literature on open innovation search focuses customarily on where to search in a given search space (eg, Dong & Netten, 2017; Garriga, von Krogh, & Spaeth, 2013; Piezunka & Dahlander, 2015). For example, early work on open innovation search reported that organisations engaged in open innovation initiatives by having a small num ber of individuals in managerial positions who tapped into their personal networks for knowledge acquisition (eg Allen, 1977; Katz & Tushman, 1981). Despite the consensus on the importance of search heuristics in realising open innovation, limited attention has been paid to how open innovation search takes place (eg, Felin & Zenger, 2014 Martini, Neirotti, & Appio, 2017). Our in-depth review of the extant literature suggests that there are two plausibl open innovation search approaches.

The first approach is the centralised open innovation search approach, which has dedicated core employees being responsible for the open innovation search (Edler, Meyer-Krahmer, & Reger, 2001; Walsh, 2015). The stream of works centred on this approach has examined the roles of specialised technologi cal sourcing employees in the R&D department (eg, Harada, 2003; Lopez-Vega, Tell, & Vanhaverbeke, 2016; Martini et al., 2017; Whelan, Golden, & Donnellan, 2013). It is suggested that dedicated employees with high research capabilities and sourcing expertise can be gathered to form an organisational unit that takes on the task of external searching. They regularly scan and search the external environment to identify relevant tech nological and new scientific knowledge for the organisation (Martini et al., 2017; Walsh, 2015; Whelan, Teigland, Donnellan, & Golden, 2010). Leading from this, in this study, we define the centralised open innovation search approach as the approach in which an organisation has a specialised sourcing unit in the R&D department to search external sources for knowledge. The specialised sourcing unit closely focuses on tech nological development, aggressively brokers knowledge fusion, and builds external ties (Fernald, Pennings, & Claassen, 2015). By setting up a dedicated organisational unit to serve the open innovation search purpose, a centrally managed open innovation expedition is institutionalised. The centralised approach can potentiall enhance organisational capability as specialists can accumulate and master relevant skills and abilities within their specific fields (Ihl, Frank, & Wagner, 2012; Pertusa-Ortega, Zaragoza-Sáez, & Claver-Cortés, 2010). Wellsituated sourcing employees often help connect distinct parties and advocate for innovation. They also enabl the transmission of information essential to innovation (Hung, 2017). This helps the organisation to activel accumulate optimum technological developments to enable a new locus of emerging technology ties (Hung, 2017; Walsh, 2015).

The second approach, which we term a decentralised open innovation search approach, does not requir an organisation to have a specialised sourcing unit. Rather, the approach builds on the supposition tha external knowledge search activities can be performed by individual employees (hence, decentralised approach) (Hung, 2017; Whelan, Teigland, et al., 2010). In other words, ordinary employees in various functional units can be empowered to perform open innovation searches (eg, Dodgson et al., 2006; Lopez-Vega et al., 2016; Martini et al., 2017). For instance, as a pioneer in open innovation, Procter & Gamble empowered its employees in various functional units to initiate new ideas and make new connections with exter nal partners (Dodgson et al., 2006). Empowered employees in various functional units can better search the external environment, identify potential opportunities for performance enhancement through product inno vation, and enable internal and external collaboration for innovative ideas (Martini et al., 2017). Essentially, the decentralised open innovation search approach captures the movement of an organisation to have employees in various functional units empowered to search external sources for knowledge during their routine R&D work. Proponents of this approach argue that the decentralised approach increases the number of potential receivers of external knowledge, hence cultivating immediate absorptive capacity, and reducing possible technological lock-out (Walsh, 2015). Additionally, a decentralised approach can enhance the adop tion of new attitudes and behaviours toward external knowledge use (Ihl et al., 2012; Lopez-Veg et al., 2016).

## 2.2 | IT use intensity for information flows

Open innovation searches are heavily supported by IT (Teigland & Wasko, 2003). Using IT, an organisa tion can engage in open, collaborative and network-centred innovation practices (Cui et al., 2017; Trantopoulos et al., 2017). IT is thereby used as a facilitator of primary organisational operational activi ties (Nambisan, Lyytinen, Majchrzak, & Song, 2017). As reviewed in Appendix A (Table A1), which summa rises prior studies on open innovation and IT, most (if not all) prior studies focused on open innovation at the strategic level while overlooking the operational details of open innovation activities and processes. For instance, prior studies have discussed the impacts of open innovation competency and strategy (eg, Ettlie & Pavlou, 2006; Gomez et al., 2017) as well as the open innovation scope (ie, open search breadth and depth) on organisational performance (eg, Cui et al., 2015; Dong & Netten, 2017; Trantopoulos et al., 2017).

While seemingly hopeful, it is cautioned that the actual use of a multitude of IT tools, such as open innovation platforms and data mining techniques, and managing the resultant work changes helps an organisation with limited resources make a wise investment (Dong & Netten, 2017; Kleis, Chwelos, Ramirez, & Cockburn, 2012). IT tool impose new requirements on dedicated external search personnel (Teigland & Wasko, 2003). Without building suffi cient knowledge about leveraging new IT tools in open innovation search activities and processes, organisations can suffer from unnecessary organisational resource waste and chaos in open innovation searches, all of which could lead to identifying inappropriate external partners and entering a vicious cycle that undermines the success of open innovation (Chiaroni, Chiesa, & Frattini, 2011)

Previous literature shows that the use of IT to support open innovation search can differ, depending on how IT is used to support information flow (Dong & Netten, 2017; Nambisan et al., 2017). To this end, IT could be used to support external and internal information flows, which we explain below.

The first way is to recognise that IT enables the amplification of external knowledge via various sources, includ ing online platforms and communities, crowdsourcing or broadcast search, and data analytics. For instance, data min ing specialists can use sophisticated data mining technologies to analyse billions of web pages, scientific literature, and patent databases (Gomez et al., 2017). This initiative allows organisations to find potential technology or collabo rators across a wider area and then link them to their business needs (Dong & Netten, 2017). With the support of open innovation and crowdsourcing platforms, organisations can publish their collaboration needs to a broad array of potential collaborators (Dodgson et al., 2006; Schlagwein & Bjørn-Andersen, 2014). They can also create their open innovation portal and use third-party open innovation platforms, all of which help connect externally source solutions to internal problems (Cui et al., 2017). We call the extent of use of this set of IT tools IT use intensity for external information flows in the open innovation search.

The second way IT can be used is to support the transfer of idiosyncratic and privileged information and enable organisations to better understand their value chain partners and create innovation opportunities (Nambisan, 2013; Nambisan et al., 2017). Organisations can employ knowledge and resource management IT solutions (eg, innovation process management software, knowledge management tools, and enterprise applica tions) to effectively facilitate their open innovation search. For example, organisations can create specialised electronic collaborative workplaces to enable their employees to exchange information with supplier (Ravichandran et al., 2017). This system effectively increases the assimilation capabilities of organisation engaged in these partnerships, thus stimulating added innovation opportunities between these organisations and their external value chain partners (Trantopoulos et al., 2017). IT thereby enables organisations with connected partners over IT-mediated networks to create and deliver value. It focuses on absorbing external knowl edge; thus, IT can be seen as enabling organisational operations and innovation (Amit & Han, 2017). Th deployment and leverage extent of these IT tools is termed IT use intensity for internal information flows in th open innovation search, which stimulates in-depth knowledge exchange and coordination among employees and with value chain partners.

## 2.3 | Operational alignment

Alignment refers to “the degree to which the needs, demands, goals, objectives, and/or structures of one component are consistent with the needs, demands, goals, objectives, and/or structures of another component (Nadler & Tushman 1980, p.119). According to Henderson (1999) (as illustrated in Figure 1), alignment has been examined specifically as (a) the fit between the business and IT strategies as intellectual alignment (eg, Chan, Huff, Barclay, & Copeland, 1997; Chan & Reich, 2007) and (b) the fit between business and IT infrastructures and processes as operational alignment (eg Tallon, 2008). Appendix B (Table B1) summarises prior empirical research on business and IT alignment

As reviewed, prior studies on alignment have predominantly focused on intellectual alignment (eg, Croteau & Bergeron. 2001: Oh & Pinsonneault. 2007) although recently scholars have suggested that operational alignment offers greater realism by providing insights on the performance implications of alignment (Gerow et al., 2014 Queiroz, 2017; Tallon, 2008, 2012). This new perspective is built on the assumption that gaps exist between th organisational strategy (what organisations plan to do) and actions taken to realise strategy (what organisations do (Mintzberg, 1987; Mintzberg & Waters, 1985). The actions are taken to realise strategy enacted through business activities and processes where the influence of IT is most likely to create value. The evolving literature also indicate that the operational alignment perspective could produce a better understanding of the role of IT alignment on performance and calls for more research in this promising avenue (Queiroz, 2017; Tallon, 2008, 2012; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011).

We argue that operationa alignment can guide the organisational use of IT to facilitate the implementation of open innovation searc approaches and encourage collaboration to reach a common goal (Tallon, 2010). By achieving a balance among business and IT infrastructure and processes, operational IT alignment can effectively allocate organisational attention, and consequently improve organisational innovation per formance (Croteau & Bergeron, 2001; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). It is suggested that organisations that are keen to engage in open innovation look inward to evaluate the interplay of the two main conditions, namely organisational open innovation search approaches and prevailing IT use, to support operational execution.

## 2.4 | Innovation pursuits: Exploratory and exploitative innovation

To understand the theoretical development fundamental to the relationship between the operational alignment of open innovation search with IT use and organisational innovation performance, we leverage the explorationexploitation framework of organisational learning. March (1991, p.85) proposed that the essence of exploration i experimentation with new alternatives,” while the “essence of exploitation is the refinement and extension of exis ting competencies.” Exploratory innovation is radical innovation and is used to meet the demands of customers and markets (Benner & Tushman, 2003; Danneels, 2002). With exploratory innovation, new designs, new markets, an new channels of distribution can be created (Abernathy & Clark, 1985). Conversely, exploitative innovation is incre mental and is used to meet the demands of existing customers and markets (Benner & Tushman, 2003; Danneels, 2002). It broadens existing knowledge and expertise, improves established designs, expands existing prod ucts or services, and increases the effectiveness of existing distribution channels (Levinthal & March, 1993). Explora tion and exploitation are not mutually exclusive and an organisation may harness both exploratory and exploitative innovation (Benner & Tushman, 2003; O'Reilly & Tushman, 2008).

A stream of research advocates that excelling at both exploratory and exploitative innovation is critical for firms long-term success (eg, Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; He & Wong, 2004; Sirén, Kohtamäki, & Kuckertz, 2012). Exploratory and exploitative innovation allows organisations to avoid traps related to favouring only a particular type of innovation (Jansen, Van Den Bosch, & Volberda, 2006; Kang & Snell, 2009). Organisations focusing on explorator innovation may not completely capture the value of commercialising existing capabilities (Gupta, Smith, & Shalley, 2006). On the other hand, organisations only focusing on exploitative innovation may enjoy short-term returns and may bear the risk of not being able to adapt sufficiently to environmental changes (Levinthal & March, 1993). Hence, balancing both exploratory and exploitative innovation not only supports organisations to avoid structural inertia through a focus on exploitation, but also stops organisations from accelerating exploration without deriving benefits from their innovation practices (Levinthal & March, 1993)

## 2.5 | Addressing the research gap

In summary, the literature on open innovation suggests where to search in a given search space (eg, Garrig et al., 2013; Piezunka & Dahlander, 2015) as well as how to facilitate open innovation strategy and capability with IT support (eg, Ettlie & Pavlou, 2006; Gomez et al., 2017). However, limited studies examine ways to address ope innovation challenges by considering aligning IT use with the adopted open innovation search approach

Furthermore, prior literature in the area of alignment mainly focused on strategic alignment and largely overlooked operational alignment which needs a deeper understanding of open innovation at the more executable operationa level (eg, Schlagwein & Bjørn-Andersen, 2014; Whelan et al., 2013).

Anchoring on the alignment perspective and the open innovation literature, this study theorises that an aligned open innovation search approach and IT use can achieve better innovation performance. This is also mediated by exploratory and exploitative innovation to influence innovation performance. By examining the open innovation sea rch approach and IT use, we can push the boundary of knowledge on operational alignment by examining how adopting both open innovation search approaches could lead to exploratory and exploitative innovation. It also answers the calls for more research in this promising avenue (eg, Queiroz, 2017; Tallon, 2012). Furthermore, it advances our understanding of open innovation search literature by identifying the theoretical mechanisms and empirical evidence on how organisations can develop internal practices to source and assimilate external knowledge It also contributes to IT and innovation literature by distinguishing the roles of IT in open innovation searc approaches, consequently leading to differentiated innovation outcomes.

## 3 | RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

Figure 2 presents the research model. We first propose the main effects of both the centralised open innovation search approach (H1) and the decentralised open innovation search approach (H2) on innovation performance. We next theorise the operational alignment of the open innovation search approach and IT use, which could lead to the pur suit of either exploratory (H3) or exploitative (H4) innovation, which in turn could lead to better innovation perfor mance (H5 and H6). We further propose that a combination of exploratory and exploitative innovation is positivel associated with innovation performance (H7). Two important and observable measures of innovation performance, which are new product sales and patents, are considered. The selection of these innovation performance measures i in line with prior innovation literature (Cooper & Kleinschmidt, 1995; Nerkar & Roberts, 2004; Petersen, Handfield, & Ragatz, 2005; Tsai, 2009).

## 3.1 | Main effect of open innovation search approach on innovation pursuit

We posit a positive relationship between the open innovation search approach (as manifested by the centralised approach and the decentralised approach) and innovation pursuit (consisting of exploratory and exploitative innovation).

The centralised open innovation search approach emphasises a regular and dedicated search of a wide range of external sources through setting up a specialised sourcing unit in the R&D department. This approach enables the orga nisation to reach a wide range of external sources (Harada, 2003; Whelan et al., 2013). Furthermore, it enables an orga nisation to institutionalise an infrastructure for facilitating fluid communication and knowledge flows from outside t inside (Dhanaraj, Lyles, Steensma, & Tihanyi, 2004). The concerted effort could improve the innovation pursuit (Appleyard & Chesbrough, 2017; West & Bogers, 2014). In more detail, exploratory innovation seeks novel knowledge and is associated with an organisation's questions on traditional methods-in-use and mental models, and challenges organisational routines and existing procedures (Argyris & Schön, 1997). Through a centralised open innovation search approach, an organisation can connect different sources of knowledge and strengthen lateral interactions between fields of technological expertise for more exploratory innovation (Jansen et al., 2006). Conversely, the acquired knowl edge from outside can be disseminated within the organisation leading to the cultivation of exploitative innovation which is often used to meet the demands of existing customers and markets (Benner & Tushman, 2003 Danneels, 2002). Prior works on open innovation suggest that an external search atmosphere in the front-end of innovation becomes institutionalised when the organisation assigns dedicated task forces to scan external ideas (Martini et al., 2017). It can further enable knowledge recombination and the emergence of new ideas (Walsh, 2015) Hence, we expect a centralised open innovation search approach which leads to exploratory and exploitativ innovation.

H1. A centralised open innovation search approach is positively associated with innovation pursuits, namely (a) exploratory innovation, and (b) exploitative innovation.

A decentralised open innovation search approach empowers external knowledge sourcing and inter organisational collaboration through routine R&D work (Whelan et al., 2013). This approach advocates having a larg number of potential recipients of external knowledge and points of interface with the external environment (Hung, 2017). Employees at all levels are supported to regularly attend technological development fairs, conferences and workshops to obtain new ideas from other areas of interest (Dahlander, O'Mahony, & Gann, 2016; Salter, Criscuolo, & Ter Wal, 2014). Also, they are encouraged to work on side projects of exploration and experimentation (Dodgson et al., 2006). By doing so, the decentralised open innovation search approach enables the employees at various functional units to become more proficient in producing new ideas through collaboration (Lopez-Veg et al., 2016), as well as knowledge integration and knowledge sharing by allocating the decision rights to employees (Foss et al., 2011). This facilitates exploitative innovation, which needs a deep understanding of specific knowledge instead of a wide grasp of general knowledge (Rowley, Behrens, & Krackhardt, 2000) and can be performed by employees who are well versed in it. At the same time, we believe that a decentralised open innovation search approach could also have a positive bearing on exploratory innovation. This is because, through the approach, nove knowledge that is often unavailable in an organisation's existing technological trajectories could also be acquired leading to challenges to organisational routines, procedures, and operations. As suggested by prior work, a dec entralised open innovation search approach facilitates communication among interdependent domains and enhance information processing capability (Lopez-Vega et al., 2016). It enables organisations to connect scattered signals, as well as integrate knowledge resources across various domains to discover more creative solutions for exploratory innovation (Jansen, Tempelaar, Van den Bosch, & Volberda, 2009). Therefore, we propose that a decentralised open innovation search approach can contribute to exploratory and exploitative innovation.

H2. A decentralised open innovation search approach is positively associated with innovation pursuits, namely (a) exploratory innovation, and (b) exploitative innovation.

## 3.2 | Operational alignment of open innovation search approach and IT use

In H1 and H2, we posit that open innovation search approaches (centralised and decentralised) are positive for th pursuit of innovation (ie, exploratory and exploitative innovation). Here, we further develop the conjectures by con sidering the interplay of IT use intensity for information flows in affecting the relationship between the approac and pursuit. Specifically, we examine the operational alignment of the open innovation search approach and IT use on innovation pursuit by positing that there is a differentiated bearing of IT use intensity depending on whether it i for external or internal information flow.

## 3.2.1 | Operational alignment of centralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensity for external information flows

The use of IT to support external information flows enables an organisation to explore external knowledge and resource across a wide area to link them to the needs of the business (Dong & Netten, 2017). We posit that the IT use intensit for external information flows has a differentiated bearing on the relationship between the centralised open inno vation search approach and innovation pursuit. Specifically, the moderating effect is more salient for the relation ship with exploratory innovation but less so for the relationship with exploitative innovation, as explained below.

As elucidated earlier, a centralised open innovation search approach is a concerted external search to aggressively identify relevant external knowledge among numerous sources (Fernald et al., 2015). This search approach requires IT tools to amplify external sourcing channels and ease sourcing efforts. This is possible when the IT use is for externa information flows, which could facilitate the amplification of external sourcing channels and ease sourcing efforts (Kroh, Luetien. Globocnik, & Schultz. 2018). For instance. IT tools used for external information flows can support the sourcing staff's knowledge searching capacity by increasing the speed, intensity, and directionality of external knowledge discov ery from wide and distant external sources using data mining technologies to efficiently search billions of web pages, sci entific literature, and patent databases (Gomez et al., 2017). IT use intensity for external information flows also supports sophisticated data structuring, indexing, and tagging, which can help sourcing specialists identify trends and usefu knowledge in the right direction (Whelan, Teigland, et al., 2010). Internet-based open innovation platforms, such as InnoCentive, Yet2.com, and NineSigma online communities, help sourcing specialists link externally sourced solutions to internal problems (Dahlander & Wallin, 2006; Di Gangi & Wasko, 2009). They are also instrumental in identifying distant new product ideas and technologies (Jeppesen & Lakhani, 2010). Hence, there is an operational alignment of the centralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensity for external information flow. However, the operationa alignment with the centralised open innovation search approach is less so for the IT use intensity for internal information flow. IT use for internal information flow focuses on supporting rich and efficient information sharing among intraorganisational units and inter-organisational partnerships in interlinked processes (Dodgson et al., 2006), which would be more efficient if the employees at large are involved in the open innovation search to facilitate dissemination

Leading from here, we conceive that the operational alignment between the centralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensity for external information flow contributes to the pursuit of explorator innovation more than exploitative innovation. Exploratory innovation seeks novel knowledge that is often unavailabl in an organisation's existing technological trajectories. It is associated with an organisation's questions on traditiona methods-in-use and mental models and challenges organisational routines and existing procedures (Argyris & Schön, 1997). To do so, an organisation needs to gain access to a wide range of external knowledge sources, such as research institutes or individual innovators (Lettl, 2007). It requires an emphasis on access to unique capabilities and expertise to create exploratory breakthrough innovations, which are possessed by a few specific types of part ners or unequally distributed in distant technological areas (Phelps, 2010). With this operational alignment, dedi cated sourcing specialists can use various data mining and analytics tools to effectively conduct a regular and specialised open innovation search. This enables an organisation to reach a wide range of external source (Harada, 2003; Whelan et al., 2013). Consequently, an organisation may be more likely to acquire external knowl edge inputs from a wide range of sources, which is necessary for exploratory innovation (Whelan, Teigland et al., 2010). In other words, through a centralised open innovation search approach and IT use for external information flows, organisations can connect different sources of knowledge and strengthen lateral interaction between fields of technological expertise for more exploratory innovation (Jansen et al., 2006). As a result, novelty appears from the flexible mobilisation of high knowledge variation as a way to produce new knowledge, therefore enhancing exploratory innovation (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000)

H3. IT use intensity for external information flow positively moderates the relationship between a centralised open innovation search approach and exploratory innovation but not the relationship between a centralised open innovation search approach and exploitative innovation.

## 3.2.2 | Operational alignment of decentralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensity for internal information flows

The use of IT for internal information flows focuses on supporting the transfer of idiosyncratic and privileged information and enables organisations to develop in-depth knowledge about their external partners and create innovation opportunities (Nambisan, 2013; Nambisan et al., 2017) as explained earlier. We further develop that IT use for internal information flows is more suitable to support the decentralised open innovation search approach, which can effectively result in exploitative innovation, rather than exploratory innovation.

IT tools such as knowledge management tools and enterprise applications can enhance employees' under standing of both internal and external knowledge and deepen their connections with external collaborators fo more exploitative innovation opportunities. IT tools such as innovation process management software and elec tronic collaborative workplaces stimulate employees in the decentralised open innovation search strategy to leverage inter-organisational partnerships in interlinked processes that enable rich information sharing. The tools allow employees within interconnected networks to efficiently gain knowledge access and share knowledge not onl internally, but also externally with valued network partners. They also facilitate employees in various sourcing units to synthesise and share information from multiple sources to integrate knowledge and ideas coherently which accelerates knowledge discovery and innovation. Inter-organisational relationships may go beyond opera tional tasks and are structured to pursue high-order goals, such as understanding new market dynamics and learn ing from partners to provide great customer value together. Favourable contexts are hence created for firms to exchange and transmit valuable knowledge with diverse external partners to encourage constant innovations and continuous improvements (Maritan and Brush, 2003)

However, the operational alignment with the decentralised open innovation search approach is less so for the IT use intensity for external information flow. This is because the use of sophisticated IT sourcing tools (eg, data mining tools and data analytics) in external search requires high IT literacy and efficacy, which may go beyond the capabilit of R&D employees as the sourcing staff in the decentralised open innovation search strategy (Harada, 2003; Whelan Teigland, et al., 2010). Moreover, IT use intensity for external information flows strengthens knowledge discover from organizationally unfamiliar domains, which does not intentionally search for specific external knowledge to address employees' innovation needs for their routine R&D work

Following the above theoretical arguments, we further develop that the operational alignment between the dec entralised open innovation search approach and IT use for internal information flow is more evident in the pursuit of exploitative innovation than exploratory innovation. Exploitative innovation is when an organisation refines an strengthens its existing product base, which needs specific and detailed external knowledge (Henderson, 1999). In contrast to exploratory innovation, exploitative innovation requires a deep understanding of specific knowledge rathe than a wide grasp of general knowledge (Rowley et al., 2000), which could be best performed by employees who are well versed in it. A decentralised open innovation search approach and IT use for internal information flows can enable an organisation to effectively source specific and detailed external knowledge to refine and strengthen its exis ting organisational product base (Harada, 2003). By using this operational alignment, employees in various functiona units as sourcing staff can actively use various IT tools for knowledge exchange and collaboration during their routin R&D work. Since they are most familiar with the existing products, they can openly search for specific and detailed external knowledge to address their existing innovation problems (Whelan, Collings, & Donnellan, 2010). The sourced external knowledge hence builds on and broadens employees' existing knowledge and provides refinement for exis ting product innovation (Ettlie & Elsenbach, 2007; Fichter, 2009). Consequently, an organisation can use the collected knowledge to uncover important approaches in customer preferences, competitor movements, and sectorial tenden cies, thereby leading to exploitative innovation (Akgün et al. 2006)

H4. IT use intensity for internal information flow positively moderates the relationship between a decentralised open innovation search approach and exploitative innovation but not the relationship between a decentralised open innovation search approach and exploratory innovation.

## 3.3 | Innovation pursuit and innovation performance

Innovation offers organisations a novel method of positioning the business ahead of its competition and the poten tial to achieve a competitive advantage in the market (Ahuja, 2000). We posit that exploratory innovation and exploitative innovation could individually and jointly lead to higher innovation performance, as measured by new product sales and patents.

## 3.3.1 | Exploratory innovation on innovation performance

Exploratory innovation produces value with organisational development of new fields or shifts to various domains to adopt or create new products or services (Ozer & Zhang, 2015). It enables organisations with a new approach to manag ing business to compete with others and offers opportunities to create a competitive advantage (Ahuja, 2000). Katil and Ahuja (2002) stated that existing knowledge can only lead to limited innovation. R&D departments hence depend on exploratory innovation to improve their capability to add new components to their knowledge inventory as it enable and supports the creation of new knowledge (Atuahene-Gima, Slater, & Olson, 2005; Levinthal & March, 1993). In thi way, they can improve opportunities for discovering a new and useful repository, and for building an innovativ problem-solving approach through R&D projects. In other words, a direction of new products or services empowers an organisation to diversify, modify, and redevelop itself to meet new market needs (Brown and Eisenhardt, 1995). It offers the organisation an innovative approach to meeting customer requirements through new products. Hence, new product sales and patents are expected to increase accordingly. Thus, we expect exploratory innovation to be positively associ ated with organisational innovation performance.

H5. Exploratory innovation is positively associated with innovation performance, which consists of (a) new product sales and (b) patents.

## 3.3.2 | Exploitative innovation on innovation performance

Exploitative innovation strengthens the existing knowledge base of an organisation through improving its existin products or processes (Ozer & Zhang, 2015). It buffers efficiency through continuous reinforcement and improve ment of existing organisational operations and product knowledge (Jansen et al., 2006; Ozer & Zhang, 2015) Hence, it reduces ambiguity and mistakes in problem-solving and provides incremental development of new prod uct performance. It may further enhance new product quality, save cost and effort, and improve productivit (Baer & Frese, 2003; Klomp & Van Leeuwen, 2001). Such an effect creates the necessary environment for achieving greater new product sales, patents, and a steady stream of cash flow for performance. Furthermore, exploit ative innovation not only helps organisations be responsive to the current market with excellent production competence and lower operational costs but empowers them to meet the demands of the future market more pre cisely through the identification of the right solutions (Lichtenthaler, 2009). It stimulates new investment or inter mediate goods in an existing production process (Edquist, Hommen, & McKelvey, 2001) and provides deepe knowledge and more skillful competencies in the implementation of product development (Atuahene-Gima & Murray, 2007; Rowley et al., 2000). Therefore, we expect exploitative innovation to be positively associated with organisational innovation performance, as posited below.

H6. Exploitative innovation is positively associated with innovation performance, which consists of (a) new product sales and (b) patents.

## 3.3.3 | Interaction effect of exploratory and exploitative innovation on innovation performance

Exploratory innovation identifies new customers through the use of new and emerging technologies, whil exploitative innovation leverages current competencies to develop products or services to meet demands from existing customers more effectively (He & Wong, 2004). Organisations need to integrate both types, rather tha make a trade-off between them (Cantarello, Martini, & Nosella, 2012; O'Reilly & Tushman, 2008). Combining exploratory and exploitative innovation indicates more comprehensive synchronicity for the development and commercialisation of successful new products (He & Wong, 2004). First, exploratory innovation brings organisations potential for long-term competitive advantage, while exploitative innovation facilitates organisations to strengthen existing capabilities. Hence, combining the two innovation pursuits simultaneously may create an optimal balance (Kauppila, 2010). Second, the unique combination of diverse knowledge and resources throug both innovations enhances the complexity of organisational activities, which can increase organisational perfor mance (Atuahene-Gima, 2005), especially in dynamic and competitive markets (Kriz, Voola, & Yuksel, 2014). Fo instance, a prior study suggests that a business's capability to concurrently realise alignment and adaptability can lead to performance improvement (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). He and Wong (2004) found that organisation that combine exploratory and exploitative innovation strategies obtain higher sales growth. Thus, we expect th combination of exploratory and exploitative innovation to be positively associated with organisational innovation performance.

H7. The interaction effect between exploratory and exploitative innovation is positively associated with innovation performance, which consists of (a) new product sales and (b) patents

## 4 | RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## 4.1 | Construct measurement and questionnaire development

We used the survey method to test the seven hypotheses. To adapt the measurements with contextualised informa tion of open innovation search approaches, we interviewed 26 R&D managers or employees in four organisations (see Appendix C for interview details and Table C1 for the interview organisation details). We first identified a list of specific descriptions as the measurements of centralised and decentralised open innovation approaches based on prior open innovation search literature (Harada, 2003; Whelan et al., 2013; Whelan, Collings, & Donnellan, 2010) Second, we discussed with our interviewees about the list of descriptions of their open innovation search in their respective R&D departments to contextualise and refine the measurements. The items were measured using seven-point Likert scale ranging from never to always.

For the measurement adaptation of IT use, we initially listed a set of specific IT tools used as internal information flows IT and external information flows IT for open innovation search based on prior literature (Dodgson et al., 2006; Kroh et al., 2018; Trantopoulos et al., 2017). Through discussions with our interviewees, we reconfirmed and refined the list of specific IT tools used in open innovation search to support external and internal information flows. Specifi cally, as suggested by our interviewees, the items of open innovation online platforms were added and the item of data analytics was used to replace data retrieval technology as a more popular term. Then, we asked the inter viewees to report the extent of their use of the listed IT tools during their open innovation search approaches. Th items for these variables were measured using a seven-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “always.” The mea surements of exploratory and exploitative innovation were adapted from existing validated scales (see Table C2 i Appendix C).

Organisational performance was measured by using objective data. New product sales were measured throug the annual sales of newly introduced products, following prior studies (eg, Nerkar & Roberts, 2004; Tsai, 2009) Because it may take several years for a patent to be granted and the time can also be influenced by the patent office's processing time, the number of patents granted in the past 5 years was used as a measure of patents in this study (eg, Ahuja & Katila, 2001). The issue of common method variance can be reduced through objective measures which builds the validity of the findings (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003).

According to previous studies, organisational and R&D characteristics may have impacts on organisational innovation activities. Hence, the control variables in the model included the age, size, industry, and R&D intensity of the organisation (He & Wong, 2004; Rai & Tang, 2010)

A two-stage Q-sorting was conducted to refine the survey instrument and to enhance the conceptual validit (Moore & Benbasat, 1991). The unlabelled (93% accuracy) and labelled (95% accuracy) results indicated good mea surement quality. Several colleagues reviewed the items and refined the framing and phrasing of the questions which helped identify and rectify potential problems. Then, the questionnaire was translated from English into Chi nese. The back-translation method was adopted to ensure consistency in meaning (Brislin, 1970). R&D managers, who are in charge of their organisations' open innovation search participated in a pre-test to evaluate the Chines questionnaire for face and content validity, clarity of instructions, and formats. Minor changes were made to resolv issues raised by them.

## 5.4 | Post hoc analysis

Although our hypotheses were drawn from the open innovation search literature and alignment perspective, we conducted several post hoc analyses to obtain a more comprehensive view of the phenomenon. First, we examined the alternative hypotheses: (a) the interaction effect of centralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensit for external information flows on exploitative innovation was not significant $( \beta = 0 . 1 0 5 ; P > . 0 5 ) ;$ ; and (b) dec entralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensity for internal information flows also did not signifi cantly influence exploratory innovation $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 3 ; P > . 0 5 )$ ). Second, we further investigated the non-hypothesised relationships: (a) the misalignment of centralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensity for interna information flows did not have a significant impact on either exploratory innovation $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 4 ; P > . 0 5 )$ or exploitative innovation $( \beta = - 0 . 0 6 4 ; P > . 0 5 )$ ; and (b) decentralised open innovation search approach and IT use for externa information flows also did not lead to exploratory innovation $( \beta = 0 . 0 4 7 ; P > . 0 5 )$ or exploitative innovation $\beta =$ $0 . 1 0 4 ; P > . 0 5 )$ . Hence, the post hoc analysis results indicated that these alternative hypotheses could not provid positive organisational innovation outcomes. Instead, the operational alignment of centralised open innovation sea rch approach and IT use intensity for external information flows as well as decentralised open innovation searc approach and IT use intensity for internal information flows were more suitable models for organisational open inno vation search, as we hypothesised.

## 6 | DISCUSSION

Our survey findings generally support our theory on the operational alignment of the open innovation search approach with supporting IT use intensity for information flow. Specifically, our findings indicate that open innovation search approaches (centralised and decentralised) have positive bearings on the pursuit of explorator innovation, rather than exploitative innovation. The non-significant findings for exploitative innovation point to the importance of considering the alignment between the open innovation search approach and IT use. Mor importantly, the operational alignment of a centralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensity for external information flow leads to exploratory innovation, while a decentralised open innovation searc approach and IT use intensity for internal information flow contribute to exploitative innovation. Furthermore exploratory innovation, exploitative innovation, and the combination increase organisational new product sale and patents.

## 6.1 | Limitations and future research

Before we discuss how our empirical investigation contributes new insights to the investigation of open innovation and IT, we must recognise the following limitations when interpreting the findings

First, the four industries, which the survey data were collected from, were all in the manufacturing sector. Although they had a broad presence in the Chinese economy, our model should be evaluated in other industries from the service sector. This can further evaluate the generalizability of our findings

Second, as the focus of this study is the open innovation search approach, we did not examine how other external search properties, such as external network density and organisation centrality, may lead to companies' innova tion performance. Hence, it provides a valuable direction for future studies. Enhancing and testing our model wit data collected in different institutional contexts could also be useful for future research.

Third, we used cross-sectional data to empirically evaluate our research model. We derived our proposed research hypotheses theoretically to achieve causality, however, the results may still involve associations. There is potential for future research to collect time-lagged performance data to have a temporal separation of the indepen dent and dependent variables or adopt a longitudinal focus to understand the nature of open innovation approaches how they develop and evolve, and how they can be leveraged for improved innovation performance. Additionally, to validate the respondent organisations' self-reported information of new product sales, we collected the annua reports of the 27 organisations in our sample from the public listed company database and confirmed the data accu racy. However, not all firms in our sample have filed IPOs. Future studies may consider using a full sample of publi listed companies for analysis. We also acknowledge desirability bias and the vested interest of respondents as a gen eral limitation of the survey method. We encourage a qualitative method to confirm the causality between con structs as well as collecting data from multiple informants in the future.

Lastly, as suggested by Antons and Piller (2015), external knowledge can be conceptualised in three different ways: the disciplinary context of the knowledge; the functional boundaries the knowledge needs to cross; and th spatial distance between the knowledge source and its recipient. The conceptualisation of external knowledge in thi study is restricted to the organisational boundary. Furthermore, our centralised open innovation search approac focuses on a specialised sourcing unit in the R&D department for external knowledge sourcing. We encourage futur studies to explore other ways, which our study does not, as well as other open innovation search implementations to identify more theoretical insights.

## 6.2 | Theoretical contributions

Notwithstanding the limitations, this study is one of the first to develop a deeper and holistic understanding of the operational alignment of the open search innovation approach with IT use in several ways.

First, we contribute to the literature on open innovation searches (eg, Mason, Beltramo, & Paul, 2004; Whelan et al., 2013) by focusing on open innovation search approaches and prevailing IT use. As open innovation is gaining importance in both research and practice, new challenges and questions increasingly arise that need a deeper understanding of these phenomena at the more executable operational level (Schlagwein & Bjørn-Andersen, 2014; Whela et al., 2013). This study enriches the discussion by discovering the theoretical mechanisms and empirical evidence on how organisations can develop internal practices to source and assimilate external knowledge. By examining th operational details of open innovation approaches and IT tools in supporting open innovation searches, our study addresses the issues around organisational innovation management beyond institutional boundaries (Rothaermel 2015). Furthermore, while innovation scholars have previously observed that organisations increasingly search outside their boundaries (eg, Reichstein & Salter, 2006; Terjesen & Patel, 2017), they have thus far downplayed the rol of IT in pursuing such open innovation searches (Chesbrough et al., 2006; Garriga et al., 2013; Laursen & Salter 2006). Our findings add to this discussion by explaining how IT allows organisations to leverage external knowledge for innovation. In so doing, it contributes to exploring the microfoundations of open innovation search more compre hensively (Laursen, 2012; Martini et al., 2017; West & Bogers, 2017).

Second, this study extends the perspective of alignment. This perspective has been applied to explain organisational performance, including competitive advantage (Kearns & Lederer, 2003) and sales growth and innovation (Chan et al., 1997; Croteau & Bergeron, 2001). Our study productively extends this field of work by iden tifying the route to a profound understanding and appreciation of strategic IT alignment in open innovation. Consid ering the features of open innovation search approaches and their applicability to the context of open innovation widens the theoretical lens. Furthermore, this study responds to the recent call for research on the operational align ment of business and IT infrastructure and processes, rather than the traditional intellectual alignment (Gerow et al. 2014; Queiroz, 2017; Tallon, 2008, 2012). By investigating operational alignment, this study contributes to greater realism by exploring the performance implications of alignment (ie, operational alignment of the adopted open inno vation search approach with IT use may have an impact on organisational innovation). The findings provide insight into how the operational perspective of strategic IT alignment can be amended for application to the context of open innovation (Powell, Koput, & Smith-Doerr, 1996).

Third, this study advances IT and innovation literature by explicating the role of IT in open innovation searc approaches and consequently leading to differentiated innovation outcomes. Prior IT and innovation literature highlighted the importance of IT in facilitating the exchange of knowledge among organisations and external sources (Hopkins & Brynjolfsson, 2010; Nambisan & Sawhney, 2007; Yoo, Boland Jr., Lyytinen, & Majchrzak, 2012). How ever, our literature review on IT use for open innovation searches revealed a lack of in-depth discussion on the supporting role of specific IT tools for open innovation search approaches at the operational level. Thus, we filled the gap and broadened the theoretical understanding of IT use and its alignment with open innovation search approaches to enable and determine different organisational innovation outcomes (Ettlie & Pavlou, 2006; Roberts Galluch, Dinger, & Grover, 2012). We theorised that IT use could support different open innovation search approaches. Specifically, IT use supports the centralised open innovation search approach to reach a broader technological area, in which new innovative ideas emerge and are selected for exploratory innovation. IT use also facilitate the decentralised open innovation search approach to stimulate in-depth knowledge exchange and coordinatio among employees in various functional units with network partners for exploitative innovation. These results sup port an important stream of research arguing that IT fundamentally changes organisational innovation processe (Nambisan et al., 2017; Roberts et al., 2012; Tambe, Hitt, & Brynjolfsson, 2012). We contributed to the theoretica base of IT use and its relationship with external knowledge absorption for organisational learning and innovation (Ettlie & Pavlou, 2006; Roberts et al., 2012)

Fourth, this study adds to the emergent dialogue on organisational ambidexterity (eg, Gibson & Birkinshaw 2004; He & Wong, 2004; Sirén et al., 2012). Combining exploration and exploitation within an organisation ha considerable benefits for organisational innovation (O'Reilly & Tushman, 2004). Organisations can enjoy shortterm efficiency and profits while accelerating their knowledge accumulation to prepare for future technologica and customer demand changes (Jansen et al., 2006). We examine the two-way interaction effect between exploratory and exploitative innovation on organisational innovation performance. In so doing, this stud deepens our understanding of organisations simultaneously pursuing exploratory and exploitative innovation.

## 6.3 | Practical contributions

Despite the widespread belief that external searches are changing because of IT-induced open innovation, limited practical guidance is available to support practitioners to recognise and achieve the alignment of the open searc innovation approach with IT use. Our findings provide managers with the conceptual clarity to mindfully implement open innovation search approaches and use specific supporting IT tools to effectively pursue optimal organisationa innovation.

First, managers need to be aware of the importance of the operational alignment of open search innovation approaches with IT use. Our findings show a successful engagement in open innovation relies on the interplay of two main conditions: open innovation search approaches and prevailing IT use. To pursue the exploratory innovation of expanding their technology and breakthrough innovations, organisations can build an alignment of a centralised open innovation search approach supported by IT use intensity for external information flows. They can set up a dedicated and specialised unit to take a centralised view of company-wide open innovation search needs. In thi case, this unit can drive and conduct open innovation search activities for various business units. Together, using IT such as data analytics and open innovation platforms can further significantly enhance companies' open inno vation search to reach a wide range of external sources and distant technological areas. Organisations with a decentralised open innovation search approach and IT use intensity for internal information flow alignment can pursue exploitative innovation by significantly improving their key technology through open innovation. The can encourage and empower employees in various functional units to proactively search for a potential partner or useful knowledge externally as well as set up different external contact points in various functional units Together, employing IT, such as knowledge management tools and innovation process management systems enables the real-time exchange of rich information between focal organisations and their partners, thereby exac erbating in-depth knowledge of partners and innovation opportunities. Managers must pay attention to design ing and deploying appropriate operational alignment of the open innovation search approach with IT use to achieve their specific organisational innovation.

Second, serving as an important enabler for each open innovation search approach, the business value of IT i open innovation must be fully recognised. Managers need to consider IT when formulating and executing open inno vation search activities. On the one hand, IT managers in charge of IT infrastructure in companies must be involved in the formulation phase of open innovation search approaches. Thus, the efficient allocation and deployment of IT resources can be planned. On the other hand, relevant employees must be equipped with sufficient IT resources Specialised IT skills such as data mining must be sought when recruiting employees in specialised sourcing units. IT training should be provided to relevant employees to support the shift towards open, collaborative, and network centred innovation practices.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

Open innovation flourishes in an increasingly competitive and fast-paced environment precipitated by new technological development. Open innovation search is fundamental to an organisation's success. Understanding the appro priate search approach considering IT tools, as well as the consequences on innovation, can critically influence an organisation's innovation performance. This study provides a foundation to gain such an understanding and contrib ute insights of instrumental value to organisations

We validated and adapted the measurements of open innovation search approach and IT use through interviews with key stakeholders. We carried out the interviews by visiting four organisations (out of the ten organisations tha were contacted) that agreed to participate. These organisations had active open innovation search practices. Eac interview lasted 45–60 min and was taped and transcribed. We conducted these interviews in an open-ended fash ion. Close attention was made to interpret and make sense of the experts' responses.
