## Abstract

Are centralised or decentralised strategies more suitable to address a developing nation's socio-economic challenges through information and communication technology (ICT)? We respond to this long-standing question by conceptualising ICT-enabled national development as a multilevel social process and by drawing on empirical findings from a natural experiment set in the context of health information system projects in Indonesia. Our study demonstrates that successful ICT-enabled national development is not contingent on pursuing one strategy or the other but on how micro-level actors interpret, and subsequently respond to, these strategies and the local changes they trigger. Our findings indicate that centralisation and decentralisation are complementary rather than competing strategies to ICTenabled national development because, if integrated into a hybrid strategy, decentralisation enables local communities to achieve national development outcomes commonly attributed to centralisation. As such, our work provides empirical evidence, explanations and new theoretical insight into the wider ‘centralisation versus decentralisation’ debate, while also outlining avenues for future research and guidelines for policymakers.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Information and communication technologies (ICTs) can contribute to the development of nations by improving healthcare, education and commercial opportunities (Walsham et al., 2007). The Information Systems (IS) disciplin therefore has a long history investigating how ICTs should be used to foster social, economic or political progress (Ganju et al., 2016). Specifically, work ranges from Walsham et al.'s (1988) seminal piece exploring the implications of IS for developing nations to studies investigating ICT implementations in India (Madon, 1992), the introduction of satellite communication in Africa (Unwin, 2009) or rural e-commerce (Cui et al., 2019). However, notwithstandin these important contributions, one of the most fundamental and long-standing questions in the domain remain contested.

To date, a debate persists as to whether decentralised or centralised strategies are more suitable when attempting to address a developing nation's societal, economic or political challenges through ICT. For one, decentralised strate gies have been promoted by supra-national bodies like the United Nations (UN) as a type of national policy intende to enable local communities to initiate and control their own self-directed ICT-enabled development processe (UNDP, 1999). Conversely, centralised strategies promote the idea that international donors, non-governmenta organisations (NGOs) or national governments are best suited to manage the introduction of ICTs in national development contexts. However, empirical work provided largely inconclusive evidence for either approach. For example, IS studies repeatedly report high failure rates of centralised strategies (i.e., Dodson et al., 2013), concluding that ICT enabled national development should be decentralised (Chaudhuri, 2012). On the contrary, decentralised strategies have been linked to political conflict of local elites (Khaleghian, 2004), may fail due to lacking technical skills (Mauro et al., 2017) or promote inequality across regions (Sumah et al., 2016). Most importantly, the notion that ICT-enabled national development takes place in a centralised/decentralised dichotomy only has been challenged (Suma et al., 2016). Today, hybrid strategies that combine both approaches are emerging (Paris et al., 2010) but have received little attention in the discipline (Hanseth & Bygstad, 2015). Ultimately, the centralisation vs. decentralisation’ debate is unresolved, with theoretical insights and policy guidelines remaining inconclusive

Here, we address these gaps in knowledge. To do this, we first argue that the lack of consensus pertaining to the ‘centralisation versus decentralisation’ debate can be attributed to a well-known challenge in our discipline understanding how individuals attempt to benefit from any ICT requires investigating how they link macro-leve strategies imposed on them to their own actions (Avgerou, 2010; Foss & Pedersen, 2016). We therefore ask: How d hybrid strategies for ICT-enabled national development (the macro level) impact subsequent actions local actors mak (the micro level)?

Second, to address this research question, we conceptualise ICT-enabled national development as a multi-leve social process, using Coleman's (1990) work on social systems (the bathtub model). This provides the theoretica scaffold needed to empirically investigate the interplay of centralisation and decentralisation as the two macro-leve strategies underlying hybrid ICT-enabled national development and the execution of subsequent programs by loca actors. We thereby respond to calls for IS research to consider the interplay of macro-level processes and micro-leve actions when investigating ICT-enabled national development (Njihia & Merali, 2013; Ramadani et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2018) and achieve this by introducing an analytical lens in the wider ICT-enabled national development litera ture with a well-established precedent in IS research (Racherla & Mandviwalla, 2013; Williams & Karahanna, 2013).

Third, we subsequently present empirical findings from a case study set in the context of health information sys tem (HIS) projects in Indonesia, a suitable instantiation of the more general phenomenon of ICT-enabled nationa development underlying our work (Bernardi et al., 2017; Holeman & Barrett, 2017). Specifically, we draw on a natu ral experiment with two polar cases (Barrett et al., 2015), thus comparing a successful with an unsuccessful instance of HIS projects across two local districts exposed to Indonesia's hybrid ICT-enabled national development strategy. Our research design enables us to explain how micro-level actors (i.e., healthcare professionals) respond and act according to macro-level social conditions (i.e., centralisation and decentralisation) and how this interplay affects th success of ICT-enabled national development initiatives. We demonstrate how centralisation and decentralisation can be effectively integrated into a hybrid ICT-enabled development strategy and explain the mechanisms through which this macro-level strategy yields micro-level actions that, consequently, advance ICT-enabled national development objectives through a multilevel social process. Our study thereby extends prior work that investigated centralisation (i.e., Shih et al., 2008) or decentralisation (i.e., Wiredu, 2012) in isolation and advances the emerging discourse associated with hybrid ICT-enabled national development in the IS literature (Paris et al., 2010) through, to best of our knowledge, the first multi-level empirical inquiry therein

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Towards hybrid ICT-enabled national development strategies

Attempts to create a better world through ICTs have existed for decades, with international donors, NGOs and national governments all supporting efforts to solve societal, economic and political challenges faced by developing nations (Unwin, 2009). However, the current body of knowledge pertaining to ICT-enabled national development is characterised by an inherent and persistent disagreement as to how to best accelerate national development throug technology. Specifically, two competing literature streams aim to dominate the narrative, with each advocating a fun damentally different strategy.

The first literature stream explores, emphasises and promotes the engagement of international donors, NGOs and national governments in ICT-enabled national development, arguing these actors are best suited to foster the diffusion of ICTs within developing nations. Concepts such as top-down (Dodson et al., 2013), technology transfer (Braa et al., 1995) and ICT diffusion (Shih et al., 2008) are synonymously used to describe and support centralised strategies for ICT-enabled national development (Avgerou, 2008). Specifically, studies that promote centralisation highlight improvements in national technology readiness (Lee et al., 2011), enhanced business climate (Dedric et al., 2013) and higher socio-economic development (World Bank Group, 2011) as its key advantages. However centralised strategies for ICT-enabled national development have also been criticised. Specifically, the inability of national governments or NGOs to understand local conditions when managing the diffusion of technology is seen as the root cause for the failure of centralised ICT-enabled national development initiatives (Diniz et al., 2014).

Conversely, the second literature stream explores, emphasises and advocates the engagement of local actors in ICT-enabled national development, arguing that their ability to understand local needs is key to success (Brown & Thompson, 2011). Concepts such as bottom-up (Dodson et al., 2013), social-embeddedness (Avgerou, 2008) and contextualism (Wiredu, 2012) are synonymously used to describe decentralised initiatives as the more successful and sustainable strategy. However, empirical studies have demonstrated that decentralised ICT-enabled national devel opment often produces outcomes that contradict what was initially promised. This is because local authorities may not possess the necessary managerial capacity, financial resources or technology skills to successfully foster the implementation, adoption and diffusion of new technologies (Chongsuvivatwong et al., 2011). In addition, conflict between central and local authorities can render national development programs unsuccessful (Collins & Green, 1994), and decentralised strategies may be too attached to local expectations, which may contradict nationa goals (Khaleghian, 2004).

While the debate persists, the argument has been put forward that any ICT-enabled national development pro gram rarely occurs in such an isolated dichotomy (Sumah et al., 2016). For example, Paris et al. (2010) suggest that centralisation and decentralisation strategies could be combined in a hybrid approach. By doing so, nations may dis tribute development tasks across centralised and local authorities. However, IS research has only begun to explor this phenomenon (e.g., Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013), and we highlight the need to understand the interpla between centralisation and decentralisation in hybrid settings, as well as their impact, rather than focusing on eithe strategy in isolation. This viewpoint aligns with calls in our discipline that have repeatedly highlighted the need to employ more holistic lenses that incorporate multiple levels of analysis when studying ICT-enabled nationa development (Avgerou, 2010; Njihia & Merali, 2013; Zheng et al., 2018). Put differently, investigating ICT-enabled national development implies considering both nation-level processes and strategies (i.e., centralisation and decen tralisation) and the resulting local-level actions. We now establish the theoretical foundations for this approach

## 2.2 | Conceptualising ICT-enabled national development as a multilevel social process

We use Coleman's (1990) work on multi-level social systems (the bathtub model) as a theoretical scaffold through which to examine ICT-enabled national development. Coleman's (1990) model has previously been applied in management (Kim et al., 2016), marketing (Storbacka et al., 2016) and IS research, where it helped explore how universal access to information infrastructures can be achieved (Racherla & Mandviwalla, 2013) or how centralised IT governance structures can meet customer needs (Williams & Karahanna, 2013). Coleman (1990) suggests that, in order to understand how any social system changes, one needs to understand the interdependencies with which the system's macro and micro levels influenc one another. This is because macro- or macro-level influence in social systems (i.e., the impact of centralisation or decentralisation as national ICT-enabled development strategies on social, economic, or political outcomes) cannot be observed directly due to the complexity all social systems exhibit. Coleman (1990) therefore suggests that, to understand macro- and macro-level influence, it is necessar first to explore how macro-level social conditions affect micro-level social environments and how these, in turn influence actions performed by individuals, which then aggregate into macro-level outcomes. Conceptualising ICT-enabled national development as a multi-level social process using Coleman's (1990) model is therefore particularly useful for our objective to advance the centralisation versus decentralisation debate becaus this approach takes both macro-level conditions and the subsequent micro-level actions into consideration, as called for in our discipline (Avgerou, 2010; Foss & Pedersen, 2016). However, Coleman (1990) also emphasises that the actions by micro-level actors in a social system are not determined solely by individua motivation, but rather by the dependency of micro-level actors on and degree of control over, availabl resources. Again, in our illustrative example, resources may include new technologies (i.e., number of laptops available) that exist in each micro-level social environment (i.e., a local school district). ## 3 | METHODS

## 3.1 | Research design

Empirically investigating ICT-enabled national development as a multi-level social process is an emerging research area Theory building through case studies is therefore an appropriate research design, and we used Yin (2018), Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007), and Miles et al. (2014) to inform our work. For one, case selection relied on a criterion-based theoretica sampling approach. Following Yin (2018), we developed a protocol to screen potentially suitable cases prior to data collec tion. Here, we ensured that we (1) conducted our study in a developing country that relied on a hybrid approach to ICT enabled national development, thus allowing us to study and compare the interplay of both centralised and decentralised strategies; (2) employed a maximum variation strategy (Flyvbjerg, 2006) by selecting both successful and unsuccessfu instances of ICT-enabled national development, as this allowed us to compare findings with known outcomes and (3) identi fied polar cases that were comparable in terms of demography (i.e., ethnicity, life expectancy), development (i.e., human development index) and economy (i.e., income per capita), thus addressing Barrett et al.'s (2015) suggestion to exploit nat ural experiments’ (p. 150) when studying phenomena like ICT-enabled national development, as these provide more com prehensive insights (Lapointe & Rivard, 2011).

## 3.2 | Research context

We conducted our study in Indonesia, a developing country with 265 million inhabitants scattered acros 17 744 islands. Indonesia uses a hybrid strategy for ICT-enabled national development (Agustina et al., 2019), thus providing us with a suitable context to examine the interplay of both centralisation and decentralisation. Specifically, we focus on how Indonesia implemented and used HISs, as these represent a suitable instantiation of the general phenomenon of ICT-enabled national development underlying our present work (Bernardi et al., 2017; Holeman & Barrett, 2017). HIS encompass ICTs that are used in healthcar settings to acquire, store and analyse medical data (Haux, 2006) and that can improve policy decisions related to public health indicators like maternal health or be used to manage epidemic diseases like malari more effectively (World Health Organization, 2018). As such, HIS are used by developing nations such as Indonesia to achieve the sustainable development goals (SDGs) (World Health Organization, 2020) and to better lives for the poor (Walsham, 2010, p. 3). We conducted screening interviews with officials at Indonesia's Ministry of Health, HIS vendors and government consultants, which helped us identify and select District Anakranti (pseudonym) as a successful case and District Cintolamo (pseudonym) as an unsuc cessful case. ## 3.3 | Data collection

Data collection in Indonesia took place from November 2017 to March 2018. All fieldwork was completed by on author, an Indonesian citizen familiar with local customs and language. The primary data collected include semi structured interviews; observations at PHCs,<sup>1</sup> government offices and training facilities and documentation provided Utilising multiple sources of evidence was appropriate, given the use of the case study method (Yin, 2018). It enabled us to understand the complex interactions among the macro-level social conditions, the micro-level social environ ment in both districts and subsequent actions taken by local actors, their resources, underlying rules of the game and ways these translated into macro-level outcomes (Coleman, 1990).<sup>2</sup>

We developed initial interview questions using existing literature, ensuring participants expressed ideas in their words (Schultze & Avital, 2011). Online Appendix B provides the interview protocol. In line with best practices for case study research, our questions evolved as new insights emerged (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007). Each semi-structured interview lasted approximately 60 min, took place face-to-face and was audio-recorded. We followed Merriam and Tisdell's (2015) advice to select participants able to provid specialty rather than average opinion (p. 96) while recruiting participants from different hierarchical levels functional areas, groups […] who view the focal phenomenon from diverse perspectives’ (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007, p. 28). This included representatives from supra-national organisations (e.g., WHO), Indonesian government officials and health workers, as well as local government officials in both districts Overall, we completed 24 in-depth interviews.

Observations of formal meetings (e.g., weekly staff meetings) and informal interactions (e.g., workshops provided both complementary insights (Klein & Myers, 1999) and contextual metadata (Trauth, 1997). We followed Trauth's (1997) suggestion to act as a ‘complete observer’ (i.e., outsider), as well as Mays and Pope's (1995) recommendations for conducting observations in health settings when identifying settings that provided information about (1) how the rules of the game were disseminated (e.g., seminars), (2) the resources available (e.g., ICT infrastructure in PHCs) and (3) the behaviour of individuals in their natural setting (e.g., health workers in PHCs). Once we selected the most appropriate sites and events, we scheduled visits for observation. Following Trauth (1997), we also spent time ‘wandering’ each site, which helped u develop a deeper understanding of the setting. ## 3.4 | Data analysis

Data analysis followed recommendations and processes outlined by Yin (2018), Miles et al. (2014) and Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) and was separated into within- and cross-case stages, with our within-case analysis following Yin's (2018) stages of compiling, disassembling and reassembling

First, compiling involved the cleaning, verification and organisation of data in nVivo 11. We familiarised ourselves with the data again by re-reading all transcripts and created contact summary sheets to highlight key findings for each interview. In addition, memoing helped us summarise emerging findings, a technique that we used throughout the entire study.

Second, disassembling involved the coding of our interview data using descriptive codes. Following Strauss and Corbin (1998), we ensured that this process was data-driven, using the terminology of our participants, as this generates richer insights. Overall, we created 329 descriptive codes, thus disassembling the data in a meaningful and rigorous manner.

Third, we reassembled clusters of descriptive codes into more abstract categories using Miles et al. (2014) interpretive and pattern coding and triangulated interview data with field notes and case documentation. As such we consolidated 329 descriptive codes into 89 interpretive codes, which enabled us, for example, to explain the interplay between social facts (e.g., government regulation) and their impact on micro-level actions (e.g., healt workers' use of technology). In a subsequent step, we used Miles et al. (2014) pattern coding to identify emergent themes. This process involved grouping 89 interpretive codes into 36 pattern codes. Finally, our cross-cas analysis followed a variable-oriented strategy (Miles et al., 2014) to identify similar themes and key difference across the two cases (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007), and we used Coleman's (1990) model as a scaffold to struc ture our narrative.<sup>3</sup>

## 4 | FINDINGS

We now present the empirical findings of our study, using Coleman's (1990) model to frame the narrative and Davison's (2016) suggestion to provide a rich story and contextual details about our research setting. We explain how the macro-level social conditions of ICT-enabled national development in Indonesia affected the micro-leve social environment and how this shaped local actions that, in turn, led to macro-level outcomes for Indonesia's healt sector.

## 4.1 | Macro-level social conditions: Indonesia's ICT-enabled national development strategy

Like other developing nations, Indonesia aims to advance its social and economic progress through ICTs. However, Indonesia is one of the few nations using a hybrid strategy and therefore relies on both centralisation and decentralisation—the macro-level social conditions shaping Indonesia's ICT-enabled national development (Coleman, 1990).

Centralisation is a strategy used whenever Indonesia's government intends to achieve prestigious, high-profile or politically sensitive objectives for its health sector. For example, Indonesia's Ministr of Health procured multiple custom-made HIS to digitise operations across its PHCs (Ramadani et al., 2022) Indonesia's Ministry of Health thereby aimed to achieve the UN SDGs through more accurate and timely monitoring and reporting of national health data and was supported by Indonesia's government through new laws and regulations<sup>4</sup>:

The central government strengthens the foundations through laws and regulations. […]. For example, when we made “SIKDA Generik” [a HIS for PHCs] that we offered to [districts Cintolamo and Anakranti]. [Official, Ministry of Health]

Conversely, decentralisation is a strategy for ICT-enabled national development Indonesia's government uses to achieve health sector objectives that are neither prestigious nor politically sensitive. For example, in 2014 Indonesia's Ministry of Health implemented the necessary laws and administrative processes for local districts to pursue decentralised HIS initiatives. This included the offer to instigate, undertake and manage the acquisition of standard software and hardware or to hire IT staff:

Health Information Systems are a top-level policy, yet needs efforts at the bottom, from district leadership, program managers, data officers. […] Decentralization allows regions to have their own strategy. [Official, Ministry of Health]

## 4.2.1 | Altering the configuration of available HIS

Indonesia's Ministry of Health imposed two centralised HIS initiatives on District Anakranti and District Cintolamo Collectively, these aimed to (1) enable consistent, nation-level documenting and reporting of epidemic diseases and maternal health indicators and (2) provide consistent, nation-level data to monitor and evaluate PHC programs and their performance. Indonesia's Ministry of Health aimed to achieve these objectives by deploying HIS into District Anakranti and District Cintolamo, thus altering the configuration of available HIS. Specifically, SIHA, SITT, SISMAL PWS, SIKDA Generik and SP2TP HIS intended to alter the way health and PHC performance data could be collected processed and exchanged:

The initiatives [centralized HIS] from the Ministry of Health are a good thing. Rather than keeping al records on paper [at the district level], the initiatives [centralized HIS] enable us to optimize the exchange and use of the data. [Representative, Ministry of Health]

However, despite the ministry's best intentions, its HIS were not distributed equally across both districts. For exam ple, while the SIKDA Generik system was offered to both, District Anakranti insisted on procuring its own HIS e-Puskemas, while District Cintolamo attempted to implement the SIKDA Generik system but eventually reverted to paper-based records instead. Across both districts, we found that lacking technical compatibility with existing technical resources, integration challenges and a perceived oversupply of technical resources (i.e., legacy HIS previousl deployed by the ministry) were the main challenges associated with centralised HIS. We now elaborate

The ministry's intention behind any centralised HIS initiative like SIKDA Generik or SP2TP is to provide all dis tricts in Indonesia with the same HIS; thus, systems like SIKDA Generik or SP2TP should emerge as the primary source of data for Indonesia's central government. However, every health division within every PHC in both district (i.e., maternal health and immunisation are individual health divisions) already uses different technical resources and processes and is constrained by varying policy requirements (i.e., the requirements for recording immunisation dat differ from those related to maternal health data). As one representative of the WHO noted, even prior to the launch of SIKDA Generik or SP2TP, technical resources in both districts were heterogeneous, and individual HIS, if in place were not integrated with one another:

They [HIS] exist but are dispersed. [Representative, WHO]

Consequently, integrating new technical resources like SIKDA Generik or SP2TP within the existing configuration of HIS and processes is challenging. One government representative in District Anakranti explained:

Our immunisation tool [Excel] is good. Unfortunately, it cannot be combined with “SIKDA Generik”.

[…] “SIKDA Generik” also has an immunisation [module], but the sequence [procedural requirement

to record data] is different. [Government official, District Anakranti]

Ultimately, our participants suggested that many centralised HIS initiatives are non-essential to their day-to-day work, a perception that was further amplified by the oversupply of HIS across Indonesia at the time<sup>5</sup>:

The ministry launched too many systems. In the end, they [health workers] become overwhelmed.

[Government official, District Cintolamo]

## 4.2.2 | Formation of local rules

Deploying centralised HIS initiatives in District Anakranti and District Cintolamo led to the formation of new local rules. Across both districts, these manifested by imposing new language on health workers, altering organisational structures in PHCs and enforcing compliance with rules and procedures defined by Indonesia's Ministry of Health.

First, centralised HIS initiatives by Indonesia's Ministry of Health imposed the use of new language in both dis tricts. This finding is significant because the development and use of appropriate language are important wheneve any new technology is adopted in social systems, as language regulates and homogenises complex technical terms thus providing a standardised vocabulary for all actors using that technology (Pop et al., 2018). In Indonesia, public health practices are standardised through Operational Definitions (OD), which outline operating procedures fo health workers. For example, prior to the launch of SP2TP, maternal health workers in District Cintolamo schedule two check-up visits for pregnant women (‘Kunjungan’, each coded as ‘K1’ and ‘K4’).<sup>6</sup> However, we found that SP2TP deviates from the OD used in District Cintolamo to standardise data collected across all Indonesian districts Midwives in District Cintolamo therefore shared their concern that using SP2TP requires them to document prenata visits in a manner that deviates from their local OD:

The Operational Definition is different in SP2TP. Here is K1, but there are K2, K3, K4 [points to screen], while we only use K1 and K4 […] The [form] in SP2TP is not consistent with our OD. [Midwife, District Cintolamo]

Second, centralised HIS initiatives by Indonesia's Ministry of Health altered organisational structures within PHC across both districts. In the context of Indonesian public health, organisational structures are defined by local areas Prior to the deployment of PWS, a HIS used in the context of maternal health, each PHC assigned to each village a human coordinator, who would then establish a health service facility there (‘Posyandu’), managing and monitoring it with the village's local administrator (‘Rukun Warga’). However, the introduction of PWS enabled district govern ments and PHCs to directly manage and monitor maternal health services in local villages:

[PWS] was introduced so they [PHCs] can now monitor every village and the on-going immunization

programs for expectant mothers. [Representative, District Cintolamo]

Third, centralised HIS initiatives enforce compliance with rules and procedures defined by Indonesia's Ministry of Health. Across both districts, the use of centrally imposed HIS required health workers to follow standardised rules of conduct and procedures when providing health services. For example, because all tuberculosis treatments are recorded in the SITT HIS, health workers necessarily had to follow relevant WHO guidelines throughout the treat ment, as these were implemented within SITT. This HIS therefore enforces compliance with WHO rules and procedures because it monitors and records all activities health workers perform. The resulting data are verified and validated by the Ministry of Health, who then assesses and accredits the PHC using that data:

All services [provided by PHCs] need to be recorded. There is a verification and validation by the end of the month, and besides, PHC accreditation is also based on those records. [Representative, District Anakranti]

## 4.2.3 | Local actor hermeneutics

Health workers (i.e., nurses, midwives) and local government officials in both districts eventually ‘made sense’ of the macro-level social conditions imposed on them, using their own value systems (Coleman, 1990). While participant in both districts understood and supported centralised HIS initiatives as a means to standardise national healt services, participants in District Anakranti associated centralisation with a loss of control, perceiving it as an attempt by the Ministry of Health to exercise power over the district:

The problem is that central [Ministry of Health] insists on us using their [HIS] system, [but] we don't want to rely on them anymore. [IT staff, District Anakranti]

Unsurprisingly, participants in District Anakranti viewed decentralisation as an opportunity to detach from Indonesia's central bureaucracy, to regain control over their health services and to ultimately accelerate the socia development of the district:

We saw it [decentralization] as an opportunity for us, particularly for PHCs. [Data & Information Offi cial, District Anakranti]

Conversely, participants in District Cintolamo saw centralisation, and the subsequent guidance and control by Indonesia's Ministry of Health, as a valuable contribution to the district. This is because the capabilities of some loca leaders were considered inadequate:

The Heads of PHCs have an important role in local health management […] We have some who are really capable and [some] who are not. Hierarchical direction [centralized guidance by the MoH] is stil best here. [Consultant, District Cintolamo]

Consequently, decentralisation was perceived unfavourably by actors in District Cintolamo. For example, a representative of District Cintolamo's local government highlighted the district's standpoint that publi health is a matter of national importance and, as such, should be managed by Indonesia's Ministry of Health only:

The health sector should be managed from the center [Ministry of Health]. It cannot be transferred to the districts. This is a national responsibility. [Official, District Cintolamo]

## 4.3 | Micro-level actions performed in district Anakranti and district Cintolamo

Individuals act according to the conditions defined by the micro-level social environment they are embedded in (Coleman, 1990; Schatzki et al., 2001). We identified five distinct actions, each performed in response to the micro-level social environment in Districts Anakranti and Cintolamo. Specifically individuals here were (1) enacting formal rules, (2) complying with local rules, (3) developing local human capital, (4) pursuing decentralised HIS initiatives and (5) addressing local health objectives. We discuss each in turn.

## 4.3.1 | Enacting formal rules

Individuals in both districts enacted the formal rules underpinning Indonesia's hybrid strategy for ICTenabled national development. However, while the rules were identical (each district was exposed to centralisation and offered decentralisation), the extent to which individuals enacted these rules varied.

Starting in 2008, District Anakranti enacted the formal rules underlying Indonesia's hybrid strategy for ICT-enabled national development by pursuing a decentralisation strategy. This initially involved establishing a local Center of Data and Information (CDI), which managed district-specific IT procedures and systems, procured new hard and software and provided ICT training to local health workers and administrators. In addition, District Anakranti restructured its PHCs into Badan Layanan Umum Daerah (BLUD), a decentralised health service largely independent in decision making and resources management. Pursuing a decentralisation strategy was viewed as the key enabler by participants in District Anakranti that acceler ated the district's ability to benefit from HIS more strongly than other comparable districts (i.e., District Cintolamo):

While other districts were still waiting for a [government] decision on which [HIS] to adopt, we made our own decisions. [Official, District Anakranti]

On the contrary, District Cintolamo enacted the formal rules underlying Indonesia's hybrid strategy for ICT-enabled national development by not pursuing a decentralisation strategy, even though all districts had been encouraged b the Ministry of Health to do so. As such, District Cintolamo never established a standalone ICT unit like Distric Anakranti's CDI nor did the district establish BLUD to increase the autonomy of its PHCs. The district formally justi fied its decision with concerns that decentralisation might favour local elites:

I discussed with the head of the district why [Cintolamo] doesn't want to establish BLUD. He does not want to […] prioritize certain elite groups. That is what they are worried about. [Consultant, District Cintolamo]

## 4.3.2 | Complying with local rules

Health workers in both districts perceived newly established local rules that resulted from centralised HIS initiatives as disruptive to their roles. However, the extent to which they subsequently complied with them varied considerably In District Anakranti, health workers eventually accepted these rules, decided to learn about new HIS and provided each other with mutual support to offset the disruptive impact that centralised HIS initiatives had on their roles. Fo example, they collectively and consciously pledged to each other to learn and use new systems and to support each other regardless of age or educational background:

There is even a staff-member who only has a high school degree, who is willing to learn. He always asks me how to use the application. [Nurse, District Anakranti]

There are many applications, but that is okay, I will learn, with God's willing, I will be able to do it. [Midwife, District Anakranti]

Conversely, health workers in District Cintolamo complied with new rules to a much lesser extent, for example, b purposefully entering incorrect data or by falsifying the number of patients treated during a shift—thus improving the district's perception of their performance or ‘making up’ for the time ‘lost’ using the HIS:

I am a nurse, [but am] instructed to become an IT technician. When creating a report, I must record how long my shift was […] I can cheat, can't I? I can write something I did not do. [Nurse, District Cintolamo]

## 4.3.3 | Developing local human capital

District Anakranti actively fostered the development of local human capital to further its vision of a technologicall advanced local health sector that was largely independent of Indonesia's Ministry of Health. Its leaders understood that adequate training for health workers is needed whenever new HIS were introduced. Furthermore, District Anakranti also involved health workers in the rollout of new HIS (i.e., by using their feedback), thus ensuring thes systems align with their roles:

When we introduce a new system [HIS], we need to pay attention to their [health workers] needs […] we can't be ignorant. [District Official, District Anakranti]

We prepare annual training as well as the on-the-job training. If there are some [health workers] who still don't understand it [HIS], we visit and coach them. [Data and Information Officer, District Anakranti]

Developing human capital in District Anakranti also meant involving stakeholders such as technology providers, patients, or other government representatives. Our participants described this as fostering an inclusive culture of innovation by the district's leadership.

We invited all stakeholders, so they feel involved in healthcare. [District Official, District Anakranti]

District Anakranti's approach to developing human capital thereby stands in stark contrast to Indonesian publi healthcare more generally, which has been described to us as ‘rigid’ and ‘difficult to change’:

That kind of spirit from our leader is difficult to find in other government [agencies]. He has a lot of innovative ideas […] He wants to achieve many goals and is not satisfied with simple outcomes. [District Official, District Anakranti]

On the contrary, District Cintolamo did not provide adequate ICT training for its health workers. Our participant associated this with the district leadership's aversion to change:

Civil servants are accustomed to existing ways. So, when you try to introduce change, it will take them a really long time to get used to it. They are already inside their comfort zone. [Consultant, District Cintolamo]

District Cintolamo's leadership operated in isolation from other stakeholders. Unlike in District Anakranti, meetings in District Cintolamo rarely involved multiple stakeholders and typically did not foster actions to advance public health services. In fact, during our field study, a local consultant invited us to attend a rally held by local healt workers protesting District Cintolamo's local government:

Their [local government's] commitment is not well-aligned with health stakeholders. There is even a rally from the health workers. [Consultant, District Cintolamo]

Consequently, health workers in District Cintolamo were hardly exposed to HIS or ICT training and involved in any decision-making processes related to the rollout of HIS. One nurse in one of District Cintolamo's PHC shared with us how s/he received a short text message instructing him/her to adopt a new HIS, yet without training provided:

Via WhatsApp - ‘Download this!’, they said. There was no explanation about how to fill-in [the HIS report]! [PHC Staff, District Cintolamo]

## 4.3.4 | Pursuing decentralised HIS initiatives

The fourth action we identified describes the extent to which District Anakranti and District Cintolamo pursued dec entralised HIS initiatives. District Anakranti pursued multiple decentralised HIS initiatives from 2008 onwards and was ultimately able to do so because it had local support (i.e., actor hermeneutics), institutions (i.e., the CDI) and sufficiently trained workforce (i.e., local human capital). One notable decentralised HIS initiative pursued in District Anakranti includes the implementation of e-Puskesmas, a HIS that enabled the use of electronic medical records interorganisational exchange of electronic data and automated reporting of data for health workers in remote village and that emerged as a prime example for decentralised HIS initiatives across Indonesia (see also Table 2):

Our district uses e-Puskesmas and its derivative product namely e-Posyandu. The systems simplify our work, like for me, it shows the status of a patient's nutrition across nice charts. [Nutritionist, District Anakranti]

There are a lot of innovations that have happened in our PHC. Many external parties have visited and regarded us as role model. [Nutritionist, District Anakranti]

Conversely, District Cintolamo did not develop any decentralised HIS. District Cintolamo was unable to do so because of its overall stance towards decentralisation more generally, as well as the absence of a dedicated ICT unit and any other form of human capital able to implement such initiatives. Over time, our participants in District Cin tolamo had eventually accepted their dependence on central government interventions. For example, technical staf habitually requested support from the Ministry of Health whenever technical problems arose and adhered to centra processes when ordering technical equipment:

When PHCs demand technical support or infrastructure, [they] need to submit a Government Activity Plan (Rencana Kerja Pemerintah), which can be submitted and assessed annually. [District Official, District Cintolamo]

Whenever there is a network problem, we need external [Ministry of Health] support. We don't do it [solve the problem] ourselves. [Technical Staff 2, District Cintolamo]

## 4.3.5 | Addressing local health objectives

The fifth action we identified delineates how the two districts addressed local health objectives. First, District Ana kranti was well positioned to do so because of its decentralised HIS initiatives. For example, District Anakrant aligned technology with the needs and objectives of its health workers by installing stable WiFi connectivity acros all PHCs, a rare achievement in Indonesia, and by providing phone credit to health workers, thus enabling them to access or enter data from home. Similarly, nutritionists were provided with tablets, thus allowing for data to be recorded on site in remote areas.

The nutritionists have tablet so they can input the data directly from Posyandu [remote facility for maternal health]. [Official, District Anakranti]

District Anakranti furthermore ensured technology met the needs of patients and its local community. For example, PHCs in District Anakranti had a long-standing reputation for being disorganised, which was largely attributed to the lack of a patient management system. In response, District Anakranti introduced a digital queuing system to improve patient experiences and scheduling of appointments:

In the PHC, the registration process uses a new queuing system, so I do not have to worry about being preceded by other patients anymore. [Patient, District Anakranti]

District Cintolamo was also aware of both the problems in its PHCs and the improvements the local community desired. However, the district could not deliver any solutions. District Cintolamo's persistent unwillingness to imple ment a decentralisation strategy eventually culminated in the district's inability to address its local healthcare objectives:

They [district government] want to change but have no idea how to start. There are always excuses: ‘Who would do it?’, or ‘we still lack infrastructure.’ When I introduced them to [technical training program], they refused to participate. [Consultant, District Cintolamo]

Health service provision in District Cintolamo's PHCs therefore met neither patient nor health worker needs. For example, during a group discussion that we observed, a staff member responsible for patient administration in a PHC explained how paper-based medical records frequently delayed response to patient queries and how this decrease patient satisfaction:

These are all [medical records] we have [points to shelf]. If we had a computer, we could find it easier [patient data]. Now, when they [patients] do not bring the card [national ID], it is difficult to find their records. [PHC Staff, District Cintolamo]

In what follows, we outline the extent to which Indonesia achieved its national development objectives related to healthcare.

## 4.4 | Macro-level outcome: National development objectives achieved in Indonesia

Indonesia achieved two key development objectives. First, District Anakranti's adoption of centralised and decentralised HIS initiatives paved the way for data-driven public health management, which helped Indonesia implement WHO guidelines for evidence-based decision-making in healthcare (World Health Organization, 2020), monitor epi demic diseases, and improve maternal health and nutrition – prerequisites to achieving the UN SDGs (World Healt Organization, 2018). Specifically, reports from District Anakranti provided the central government with timely data for health indicators (e.g., malnutrition) and epidemic diseases (e.g., cases of dengue fever). Throughout Indonesia, District Anakranti excelled in implementing data-driven public health management initiatives, which led to multipl national awards for electronic government and to the district becoming a national ‘blueprint’ for ICT-enabled national development:

The central government (MoH) sees us as one of the best [on data and information]. [District official,

District Anakranti]

Second, District Anakranti was able to increase health service efficiency and patient experience that, subsequently, translated to improved public health outcomes. Specifically, the district's data-driven approach to public health man agement paved the way for targeted interventions and preventative measures, such as vaccination programs in vil lages experiencing a surge in epidemic diseases. The coordinator of a local TB program explained:

I ensure which areas need a little sprint to achieve their targets. For example, here [points to name of village on map] the indicator of TB in the first quarter was 100. We can now predict whether our target is achievable. [PHC Staff, District Anakranti]

Similarly, electronic medical records enabled health workers to make better clinical decisions based on a patient's medical history, ultimately reducing the total service time needed to treat patients—a key performance indicator to measure health service efficiency:

Handling patients is now electronic. The new system makes our PHC feel like the airport. [Nutritionist, District Anakranti]

Consequently, the public health outcomes in District Anakranti directly contribute to, and address, national develop ment objectives set by Indonesia's government. For example, higher immunisation rates for infants (81.29% in Dis trict Anakranti vs. 57% nationally), lower prevalence of infectious diseases in adults (1.44% of residents in District Anakranti contract pneumonia annually vs. 2.0% nationally) or access to neonatal care (65.46% of children born in District Anakranti vs. 43.5% nationally).

Conversely, District Cintolamo's public health system contributed less to Indonesia's national developmen obiectives. While the district was able to supply performance and health data. it was ultimately unable to benefi from any data-driven public health management initiatives. However, by the end of our fieldwork in District

Cintolamo, we observed the awareness of health data and reporting had improved. One of our participants explained to us:

They [District Cintolamo's government] know about the state their data is in. Planning should be based on actual data, not based on what they had last year […] That is the thing [that has changed] their awareness of data. [Consultant, District Cintolamo]

## 5 | DISCUSSION

Our study was motivated by the long-standing question of whether centralised or decentralised strategies are more suitable to help developing nations foster social, economic or political progress through ICTs (Avgerou, 2008). We conceptualised ICT-enabled national development as a multi-level social process (Coleman, 1990), thus responding to calls for IS research to consider the interplay of both macro-level processes and micro-level actions when investi gating this phenomenon (Avgerou, 2010; Njihia & Merali, 2013; Ramadani et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2018). Our research design furthermore built on Barrett et al.'s (2015) suggestion to conduct natural experiments, and we thus selected and contrasted successful District Anakranti with unsuccessful District Cintolamo from our empirical stud set in Indonesia.

Comparing how centralisation and decentralisation, the macro-level social conditions underlying Indonesia' hybrid strategy to ICT-enabled national development, affected the micro-level social environment in districts Ana kranti and Cintolamo, as well as macro-level outcomes associated with Indonesia's national development objectives enabled us to extend prior work that investigated centralisation (Braa et al., 1995; Shih et al., 2008) and decentralisa tion (Avgerou, 2008; Wiredu, 2012) strategies in isolation. It furthermore advances the emerging discourse associ ated with hybrid ICT-enabled national development strategies in the IS literature (Paris et al., 2010) through, to th best of our knowledge, the first empirical multilevel inquiry therein.

Proponents of both centralisation and decentralisation advocate that the success of an ICT-enabled nationa development initiative can be attributed to a policymaker, NGO or supra-national organisation selecting and implementing ‘their’ respective alternative (Ganju et al., 2016; World Bank Group, 2011). Our empirical findings chal lenge this notion, demonstrating that successful ICT-enabled national development is not merely a matter of pursuing one strategy or the other but is contingent on how micro-level actors interpret, and subsequently respond to, the ICT-enabled national development strategy imposed on them—irrespective of the strategy chosen. ## 5.1 | Theoretical implications

Two theoretical implications arise from our work that contribute to our understanding of the phenomenon of ICT enabled national development:

First, micro-level actors are not passive objects that respond according to the premises underlying centralisation and decentralisation as macro-level social conditions but are ‘agents of change’, capable of autonomously adapting to either strategy offered (e.g., decentralisation) or imposed on (e.g., centralisation) them. However, as we have seen from contrasting District Anakranti with District Cintolamo, the willingness of micro-level actors to do so is contin gent on actor hermeneutics—their varying interpretations of the changes to the micro-level social environment in which they operate. Consequently, we challenge the previous assumption that factors like lacking technology skill (Chongsuvivatwong et al., 2011) or ethnic differences (Baker et al., 2010) represent hindrances affecting the success of decentralised ICT-enabled national development initiatives. Instead, we argue these factors are not causal mecha nisms but merely symptoms resulting from the conscious actions micro-level actors perform in response to their interpretation of the micro-level social environment—which previous work may have been unable to recognise due to the macro-level analytical lenses typically employed (Njihia & Merali, 2013). By the same token, we challenge the assumption that centralised ICT-enabled national development initiatives fail because national governments are unable to understand local conditions—the micro-level social environment. Instead, the micro-level social environ ment itself can be unfit for a technology transfer (Braa et al., 1995) or ICT diffusion (Shih et al., 2008), not becaus of the inability of a central government to apprehend it but because the micro-level actors therein consciously alter it in response to their interpretation of the macro-level social conditions.

Second, decentralisation represents a catalyst, which can offset the negative impact centralisation has on micro level social environments. Therefore, centralisation and decentralisation should be viewed as complementary, rathe than competing, strategies to ICT-enabled national development. In both successful District Anakranti and unsuccessful District Cintolamo, centralisation altered the configuration of available HIS and imposed new local rules, which adversely affected day-to-day operations in PHCs. However, only District Anakranti coped with these chal lenges, while also translating centralised HIS initiatives into nationally beneficial outcomes. The district was able to do so because it had pursued a decentralisation strategy, which enabled actors here to better comply with new loca rules, develop local human capital and, eventually, address local health objectives, which then helped actualise national development objectives. Conversely, by not pursuing decentralisation, District Cintolamo reduced its abilit to translate centralised HIS initiatives into national development outcomes.

Prior contributions argued decentralisation may contradict national strategic long-term goals (Khaleghian, 2004 or result in conflict between local and central authorities (Collins & Green, 1994). Our empirical evidence points into the exact opposite—namely that decentralisation enables local communities to effectively implement, and adhere to the national development objectives that centralised ICT-enabled national development initiatives aim to achieve We thus challenge the prevailing argument that decentralisation increases inequality between regions in developing nations (Sumah et al., 2016) by suggesting that this would be the case only if decentralisation is not utilised holistically across a nation or if decentralisation is implemented without any centralised initiatives. As such, we explai how and why centralisation and decentralisation strategies can be combined in a hybrid approach.

Ultimately, ICT-enabled national development is a multi-level social process wherein human actions, rather than a specific strategy chosen by a macro-level actor, affect a nation's ability to advance social, political or economic goals through ICT. This perspective does, in our opinion, uniquely complement our discipline's current understandin of the phenomenon. By shifting the narrative away from what we perceive to be an ideology-laden dichotom centered around a choice of ‘centralisation vs. decentralisation’, our work may stimulate future research supporting Walsham's vision for the IS discipline to ‘making a better world with ICTs’ (Walsham, 2012, p. 89)—as exemplified through this Special Issue and beyond (Díaz Andrade et al. 2019).

## 5.2 | Implications for policy and practice

Our study provides important guidance for those pursuing ICT-enabled national development initiatives. First, w suggest ICT-enabled development initiatives should always be initiated by taking both centralisation and decentrali sation as complementary strategies into consideration. The question should never be about choosing one strateg over the other. Instead, it is necessary to determine how to best combine these two in each local context. To hel answer this question, local experimentation may be beneficial. Specifically, new ICT systems can be deliberatel trialled within a given community, even if the ICT system, process or community may not yet be mature enough for its adoption or use. We believe this approach can provide new insights into the technical capabilities and readines of a community to be exposed to new ICT initiatives before committing scarce resources at scale. Put differently, early local experimentation with a ‘minimum viable product’, not dissimilar to the ‘lean startup’ methodolog Blank (2013) introduced in the context of innovation management, could enable policymakers to determine whethe local communities need additional support from a central government (i.e., an ICT-enabled development strateg emphasising centralisation) or if they can be given authority over ICT-enabled development initiatives (i.e., an ICT enabled development strategy emphasising decentralisation). Local experimentation should therefore be viewed as a desirable first step—not only for policymakers but also for local communities. Once a community understands its abil ity and limitations, any allocation or sharing of tasks and decisions can be reassessed.

Second, we encourage national policymakers to ensure dedicated organisational units responsible for selecting developing and managing IS are put in place in local communities. We use this opportunity to reemphasise that Dis trict Anakranti's success can be attributed to the fact that it had already established its Center for Data and Information in 2008, while District Cintolamo and other districts across Indonesia decided to manage all IT decisions from within their local governments. Having a dedicated IT unit on the ground enables strategic planning and use of ICTs, ensures the availability and adequacy of supporting resources, and aids the overall alignment of ICTs and stra tegic objectives. Such a unit could also help initiate a shift in perspective of the role of technology and the responsi bilities of local vis-à-vis central governments in this context.

## 5.3 | Limitations and future research

We conducted this study retrospectively. Our participants therefore had to rely on their memory and may have attempted to impress the interviewer. We mitigated this potential limitation by following Eisenhardt and Graebner's (2007) suggestions to interview participants from (1) different hierarchies (e.g., medical doctors vs. nurses), (2) different groups (e.g., senior officials from Indonesia's Ministry of Health vs. ICT consultants) and (3) different geographies (e.g., Districts Anakranti and Cintolamo as well as Indonesia's central government in Jakarta). As with all qualitative studies, our data were subject to interpretation. Following Miles et al. (2014), we minimised researcher bias by selecting descriptive, interpretive and pattern codes, as these are known to ensure close relationship of findings with data. We ultimately view our work as an important avenue for future research.

First, future research could extend the conceptual foundation of ICT-enabled national development as a multi level social process. Like all social processes, ICT-enabled national development is affected by its settings (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). This provides future researchers with the opportunity to examine the boundary conditions per taining to ICT-enabled national development. For instance, future research could focus on constructs such as trust social capital, or stereotypes (Huang et al., 2017; Huetten et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2009) multiple stakeholders hold towards digital technologies (Someh et al., 2019).

Second, future research could provide additional contextual insights to extend our work. Beyond replication studies in other countries, cross-national or cross-sectoral analyses might be of interest. Institutions in other coun tries will likely differ, as will interdependencies between macro-level social conditions and micro-level social environ ments therein. Future research could, for example, study healthcare systems of nations with more complex institutional arrangements (Breidbach et al., 2016) or expand ICT4D knowledge beyond healthcare into education, political participation, or economic development (Ramadani et al., 2018). In addition, the centralisation vs. decentralisation debate is equally applicable to contexts beyond HIS in ICT-enabled national development. For example, future researchers could investigate the role and success of centralised vs. decentralised financial service (Breidbach et al., 2020), including cryptocurrencies (Breidbach & Tana, 2021), which have been introduced as lega tender in developing nation El Salvador.

Finally, future research could benefit from new methodological and theoretical avenues that expand our knowl edge about ICT-enabled national development as a multi-level social process. For example, we see potential to focu on the social mechanisms (Avgerou, 2013; Hedström & Swedberg, 1998) underlying ICT-enabled national develop ment or to adopt critical realism (Mingers, 2004), which could help further articulate the multi-level social and gener ative mechanisms therein. Finally, future research could use action design research (Sein et al., 2011) to full immerse in and directly support developing nations improving their healthcare, education or commercial opportuni ties with ICTs (Walsham et al., 2007) but also shift towards new sources of ‘big’ textual data like government record that would require advanced machine learning methods to analyse (Antons et al., 2021)

## 6 | CONCLUSION

IS researchers and practitioners attempted to create a better world through ICTs for some time. However, th question of how this objective may be best achieved remained contested, with centralisation and decentralisation representing two competing strategies. Our work approached the phenomenon of ICT-enabled national develop ment from a multi-level social process perspective, while empirically exploring Indonesia's endeavour to integrate both centralisation and decentralisation in a hybrid strategy. We found that centralisation and decentralisation can, in fact, complement one another, and we have shown how these strategies may be integrated to yield valu able ICT-enabled national development outcomes. Ultimately, we like to think that the multi-level social proces lens on ICT-enabled national development we put forward here will enable others to generate new knowledge about the phenomenon, while our empirical insights can further guide those aiming to create a better worl through ICTs.

## AUTHOR BIOGRAPHIES

Luthfi Ramadani received bachelor and master degrees in informatics from Institut Teknologi Bandung, Indonesia, and a PhD degree in Computing and Information Systems from The University of Melbourne, Australia. He is currently a Lecturer in Information Systems at Telkom University, Indonesia. His research interests include ICT in developing countries, health information systems and enterprise architecture and governance.

Christoph F. Breidbach is senior lecturer in business information systems and co-lead of the UQ Service Innovation Alliance at The University of Queensland. He previously held positions at The University of Melbourne and the University of California, Merced, and was a visiting researcher at IBM's Almaden Research Center. His empirical and conceptual research addresses fundamental questions of digital transformation and appears consistently in leading journals and conference proceedings. He serves on the editorial boards of the Journal of Service Research, Journal of Service Theory and Practice, as well as the Journal of Business Research and led the AIS Specia Interest Group Services as elected president from 2018–2021.

Sherah Kurnia is an Associate Professor at the School of Computing and Information Systems, the University of Melbourne, Australia. Her research areas include electronic commerce, inter-organisational information systems, supply chain management, sustainability, strategic IT decision-making and enterprise architecture. She has over 180 publications and have published in leading journals including Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Information and Management, International Journal of Production Economics and International Journal of Supply Chain Management. She has won six Best Paper Awards from leading Information Systems conferences. She is currently an Associate Editor for Information and Management Journal.
