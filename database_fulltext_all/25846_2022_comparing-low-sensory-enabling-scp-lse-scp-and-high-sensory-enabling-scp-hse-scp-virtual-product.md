# Comparing low sensory enabling (LSE) and high sensory enabling (HSE) virtual product presentation modes in e-commerce

## Abstract

Online shops have become increasingly interactive, using different technologies to create virtual experiences that attempt to simulate a realistic product experience. We explore the impact of high sensory enabling (HSE) virtual product presentation modes using state-of-the-art virtual reality (VR) technology that allows consumers to imitate natural movement and interactions via head-mounted displays (HMD) and dual hand VR controllers. This will compare the HSE virtual product presentation mode with a typical low sensory enabling (LSE) virtual product presentation mode that utilises conventional computer screens, along with mouse and keyboard inputs, on a desktop computer. For the HSE virtual product presentation mode, the results show significantly higher values for the studied variables, including presence, perceived diagnosticity, attitude towards product, and purchase intention. Shopping frequency has a moderating effect on the significant differences of presence between presentation modes. Our research contributes to theory by building on attitude theory, cue summation theory, as well as repetitive learning and memory to explore and explain the effects of HSE virtual product presentation modes on the constructs considered. For managers and industry leaders, this study identifies the importance of using state-of-the-art technology when creating HSE virtual experiences for their products.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

To gain market share in an increasingly competitive online retail market, online shop designers use novel technolog to stimulate the various senses of consumers, achieving a more direct product experience (Chin et al., 2005). Thi aims to help consumers form a clearer understanding of complex products, increase their appeal, and subsequentl facilitate purchase intention.

Research considers perceived diagnosticity to be an important indicator of cognitive consumer learning (Kempf & Smith, 1998). The main idea behind this concept is to determine the extent to which an experience with a product is helpful for evaluating its quality and performance (Kempf & Smith, 1998). As with perceived diagnosticity presence can be seen “as the psychological foundation of virtual experiences” (Biocca et al., 2001, p. 8), and is a central cognitive variable and point of analysis in our study (Nah et al., 2011; Suh & Chang, 2006; Verhagen et al., 2014) Presence describes the subjective sense of “being there” in a virtual world and perceiving oneself as physically pre sent (Bowman & McMahan, 2007); it is positively influenced by stimulating visual, haptic, and auditory senses.

Previous studies indicate that the presentation mode of a product addressing various sensual cues (e.g., visual, tactile, and auditory) has an impact on consumer learning with respect to better understanding (cognitive dimension and/or liking (affective dimension) a product (Jiang & Benbasat, 2003, 2004, 2007b; Verhagen et al., 2016). Researc related to cue summation theory (Moore et al., 2004) shows that direct experiences through all senses, that is, multi sensory interactions simulating a direct experience with the product (Overmars & Poels, 2015), are superior for eliciting consumer learning processes. This kind of exposure ideally generates an intention to purchase the product or complete a purchase (conative dimension) (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007b; Li et al., 2001).

Consequently, virtual product presentation modes typically are enhanced with novel interaction or display methods (Chin et al., 2005) that allow consumers to interact with products in real time, and thus attempt to simulate more senses to achieve a direct product experience. In past years, a number of studies focused on the impact of vir tual product presentation modes on consumer learning using a classic desktop scenario (Baker et al., 2019; Jiang & Benbasat, 2003; Suh & Chang, 2006; Suh & Lee, 2005; Van Kerrebroeck et al., 2017). Only limited research has investigated virtual product presentation modes (Martínez-Navarro et al., 2019; Peukert et al., 2019) using immersive technologies that are able to block visual and auditory cues from the users' physical environment, teleporting them into a simulated virtual world by using head-mounted display (HMD) technology (Slater & Wilbur, 1997). These immersive technologies allow a targeted stimulation of specific senses, serving as sophisticated sensory enablin technology. We take inspiration from Kim and Forsythe (2008) to define sensory enabling experiences, which “includes both product visualization technologies (visual support) and haptic interfaces (tactile support)” by the use of special devices such as a pen, glove, or joystick (p. 902).

Overmars and Poels (2015) determined that online buyers with a high shopping frequency have a better understanding of complex online presentation formats and interfaces because they may be more frequently exposed to novel technologies used for shopping experiences, that is, those that are more helpful for them for evaluating prod ucts. Drawing on repetitive learning and memory (Hintzman, 1971; Ward, 1974), research shows that the more ofte online consumers are exposed to online product stimuli, the better the presented product is understood (Jiang & Benbasat, 2003, 2004, 2007b; Verhagen et al., 2016). Hence, shopping frequency may be a moderating factor for perceived diagnosticity and presence.

The main goal of this paper is to investigate the effects of different levels of sensory enabling virtual product pre sentation modes on perceived diagnosticity and presence, attitude towards product, and purchase intention A traditional low sensory enabling (LSE) virtual product presentation mode is compared with a state-of-the-art high sen sory enabling (HSE) virtual product presentation mode. The LSE virtual product presentation mode consists of a tradi tional interactive 3D online shop experience (visual support) presented in a web browser utilising standard mouse and keyboard interactions (tactile support). The HSE virtual product presentation mode is an immersive virtual reality versior of this same online shop. It places users in an immersive virtual world to present products, imitating a more natural expe rience by allowing free viewpoint changes using head motion (visual support), along with spatial controllers that enabl users to manipulate products with their hands (tactile support). In contrast to previous studies that have focused on LSE virtual product presentation modes, we carefully design the shopping experiences to reuse best practices and existing knowledge of online shop design. Here, we integrate the 3D modes utilised in HSE and LSE as natural extensions of the shopping experience, combining well-known online shop designs with the HSE virtual product presentation mode to tap into previous knowledge of user online shopping experiences, minimising the obstacles of transitioning to an HSE experi ence. In doing this, we streamline the investigated LSE and HSE virtual product presentation modes to account for th differences that affect consumer learning. This is relevant, most notably in light of research by Peukert et al. (2019) who show that contrary to their assumptions, HSE virtual product presentation modes had lower perceived diagnosticity fo a food product than the typical LSE virtual product presentation modes. This would suggest that the results from LSE vir tual product presentation modes are not necessarily transferable to HSE virtual product presentation modes

Our study makes two key contributions. First, it investigates the effects of the two virtual product presentation modes (HSE and LSE) on cognition (perceived diagnosticity and presence), and subsequently on the affective (atti tude towards product) and conative (intention to purchase) dimensions. As Peukert et al. (2019) show, a system' sensory enabling qualities require cognitive processing to explore and learn about product features, which is why we follow the traditional consumer learning process of cognitive, affective, and conative sequences based on the hierarchy levels as identified by Ajzen and Fishbein (1975). Second, this study reveals moderating effects of shopping fre quency on perceived diagnosticity and presence, an issue that to date has not been investigated in empirical studies

## 2 | THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

## 2.1 | Theoretical foundations: Integrating attitude theory, cue-summation theory, and repetitive learning and memory

## 2.1.1 | Attitude theory and sequence of consumer learning

Attitude theory, often referred to as tripartite theory, typically measures three response variables of consumer learn ing: cognitive, affective, and conative outcomes (Ajzen, 2001; Ajzen & Fishbein, 1975). However, while their role in attitude formation or change is generally accepted, there is still debate on the sequence of these three response vari ables (Suh & Lee, 2005): the higher the financial, psychological, or social risks associated with a product purchase, the more intense the cognitive processing of information tends to be (Ajzen, 2005). In contrast, the less risky a prod uct purchase is perceived, the more likely the purchase decision is made ad-hoc, or based on emotional triggers (“ like the product, I have to have it”). Along with product type, we argue that the sensory enabling character of a vir tual product presentation mode requires cognitive processing, measured in terms of the constructs of perceived diagnosticity and presence as described in the following line of argumentation.

As technology increases, so does the ability of consumers to interact with virtual products, allowing them to experience increasing levels of visual and haptic functionality (Jahng et al., 2007). These imitation and interactio possibilities require cognitive processing, which is why we align ourselves with Jiang and Benbasat (2004), who conceptualise perceived diagnosticity as a cognitive outcome. A key element of virtual product experiences is their sense of consumer presence, that is, the perception of users being located in the mediated virtual world (Verhagen et al., 2014). We argue that presence is a construct expressing the perception of users as being physically present in a virtual world. Here, processing of visual stimuli through vivid imagery, the ability to grab a product and explore its features, and freely move around in real time enable users to perceive the environment as real. According to Shih (1998), the central determinant of presence is the quantity and quality of information dimensions. He argues, “in any environment we are operating in, we perceive environmental cues with all of our senses, and to the extent that cyber technology can reproduce these sensory data, we feel present in the computer-generated environment (p. 658). The awareness of different cues is strongly linked to the cognitive component as developed by Lavidge an Steiner (1961). Following this, we argue that presence is a cognitive variable (Schuemie et al., 2001) expressing the perception of users as being physically present in a virtual world. Presence is a response to the immersive quality of a virtual experience, and a direct function of the system's visual and haptic support. Cummings and Bailenson (2016) provided a meta-analysis of experiments regarding presence and systems' immersive qualities. They determined that in terms of visual support, increased levels of field of view and stereoscopic vision, as well as increased levels of use tracking, have a medium to strong effect on increasing presence. Increasing levels of user tracking also lead to th users' ability to interact more naturally with the virtual world and its products, thereby improving tactile support

Following attitude theory related to a high-risk product evaluation (Edwards, 1990), cognitive outcomes such as perceived diagnosticity and presence influence affective responses, which are often expressed as measures of atti tudes themselves, for example, attitudes towards products or brands as part of attitude formation (Ajzen, 2001; Kim et al., 1998). We furthermore align with Ajzen and Fishbein's research (1975) which explains that attitude towards product influences intentions (Petty et al., 1997), following their retrieval or activation as stored in the user's mind (Bagozzi et al., 1992; Davis et al., 1992).

## 2.1.2 | Cue-summation theory

Central to the cue-summation theory is the understanding that consumer learning increases with the number of available and relevant cues or stimuli of a product (Moore et al., 2004; Severin, 1967). The effects of learning in vir tual reality settings are influenced largely by the stimulation of multiple senses to various degrees through visual o tactile support that allow for direct interactions with virtual objects. For instance, in HSE virtual product presentation modes, head-mounted display (HMD) technology teleports users into a computer-generated virtual world (Slater & Wilbur, 1997). Tactile support is enabled with state-of-the-art technology by utilising dual hand VR controllers. This kind of technology makes it possible to more accurately simulate haptic touch from direct experiences with the prod uct (Riedel & Mulcahy, 2019), supporting HSE virtual product experiences as a result

HSE virtual product presentation modes convey multisensory information to users, allowing for direct interactions while imitating natural movements. All these cues relate to product knowledge, and consequently enhance presence and perceived diagnosticity (Daugherty et al., 2008).

## 2.1.3 | Repetitive learning and memory

Several studies in the learning literature have suggested that meaningful learning occurs the more frequently learners are exposed to presented information (Hintzman, 1971). The more this kind of experience learning take place, and the more often this kind of relevant information is integrated with itself and relevant prior knowledge, th stronger the learning effect (multiple trace effect). Hence, repetition in a vivid environment takes the form of cycle of expansive learning (Engeström, 2001, 2010). Learning to move and interact in HSE virtual experience settings is new skill, highlighting the importance of repeated practice because of its task complexity. Each time a user visits virtual environment, it is an event; every occurrence of an event is a unique episode. Hence, with every virtual expe rience, a separate, independent memory trace is formed, storing information about the time and situation in which it happened. The more frequently this kind of virtual experience occurs, the more traces of that event are deposited into memory. Repetitive learning therefore occurs because finding at least one trace of an event becomes easie when there are more traces of it within memory (Ward, 1974). In line with this theory, Overmars and Poels (2015 identify a relation between online shopping frequency and the “perceived diagnosticity of experience attributes” (p. 276), concluding that “because experienced internet users and frequent online shoppers probably have a bette understanding of complex online interfaces, they are likely to consider mediated product experiences more helpfu than do inexperienced internet users/shoppers (p. 277).

In summary, based on the consumer learning theories of attitude formation, cue-summation, and repetitiv learning and memory, HSE virtual product presentation modes facilitate consumers' product understanding by pro viding multiple sensory cues and allowing for more direct interactions than LSE modes. The more frequently con sumers engage in such virtual experiences, the more effective product learning is because of growing familiarity with technological newness.

## 2.2 | Perceived diagnosticity

Online presentations should support consumer learning in forming a clear understanding of products (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007b). Creating this kind of understanding means online product presentations should reflect th diagnosticity of the presentation in the offline world (Algharabat, 2014; Algharabat & Shatnawi, 2014). According to Jiang and Benbasat (2004), the ability to view products from various angles and distances (visual control), and explore and experience different features and functions of products (functional control) are the main determinants of perceived diagnosticity. Jiang and Benbasat (2007b) further show that, when interacting with a mouse and key board, videos and virtual product experiences lead to higher perceived diagnosticity than static pictures. Lee (2012 tested the influence of 3D presentations on perceived diagnosticity, confirming their expectation that virtual experi ences will increase the perceived diagnosticity of participants in comparison to indirect experiences. They also con firmed that perceived diagnosticity can be increased with a virtual product experience in comparison to indirect experiences when using a traditional desktop PC.

Research shows that perceived diagnosticity also appears to be influenced by product type and how the product can be investigated. For instance, Visinescu et al. (2015) compared a 2D online bookstore to a brick-and-morta bookstore recreated as an LSE VR world in a non-immersive browser setup. Even though previous work identified the benefits of using 3D representations for products (Jiang & Benbasat, 2003; Park et al., 2008; Verhagen et al., 2016), in this scenario, the use of a complete virtual store representation to search for products was generally not positively received. The complexity of the 3D shop was regarded as high, which negatively impacted the per ceived ease of use and purchase intention. In a recently published study, Peukert et al. (2019) found that a HSE vir tual product presentation mode had a lower perceived diagnosticity of the presented food product (muesli) than an LSE virtual experience. The authors argue that this result was likely affected by the poor resolution of the HMD glasses used in their study, and assume that the result would change with HMD glasses having better resolutio (Peukert et al., 2019). This relates to cue summation theory indicating that a cue needs to elicit compatible responses to ensure consumer learning (Miller, 1957, p. 78). In other words, if a product description is not readable because o bad HMD glasses resolution, low perceived diagnosticity is the result. In addition, books and cereals as used in th two studies of Visinescu et al. (2015) and Peukert et al. (2019) are less complex products, for which simpler presenta tion modes may be sufficient. We instead expect that the perceived diagnosticity for explanatory products with higher level of functionality may strongly benefit from an HSE virtual product presentation compared to an LSE vir tual experience.

There is evidence that when cues from different modalities (or different cues within the same modality) are used simultaneously (Miller, 1957), they may only facilitate each other if an interaction with the product comes as alread learned, for example, in an online shop scenario. If users can naturally move through space and use their hands to interact with products (tactile support), we expect that this increase in interaction fidelity with the virtual produc will also lead to an increase in perceived diagnosticity in comparison to the LSE virtual product presentation mod that allows the view of the product to be moved using indirect mouse and keyboard manipulations, albeit with no sophisticated natural interaction. Hence, we postulate:

Hypothesis 1a. Compared to an LSE virtual product presentation mode, an HSE virtual product pre sentation mode significantly increases perceived diagnosticity.

## 2.3 | Presence

Presence describes the subjective sense of being there in a virtual world. With a high sense of presence, users for get that they are immersed in a different, virtual world through a human-computer interface; they perceive them selves as physically on-location (Bowman & McMahan, 2007). Supported by cue summation theory in the context o multimedia or hypermedia environments (Moore et al., 2004), presence is positively influenced by stimulating visua channels and enabling interactivity; more specific, stereoscopic vision to perceive the environment; and increased levels of tracking (hand and head motion) for exploration and interaction (Cummings & Bailenson, 2016), thu improving visual and tactile support. With respect to virtual product presentation modes, presence is a main contributing factor in facilitating consumer learning, that is, better understanding the product and creating a positive attitude towards it. This can be generated either through interacting with the virtual environment or the virtual product (Li et al., 2001). Presence can even be seen “as the psychological foundation of virtual experiences” (Biocc et al., 2001, p. 8), and plays a central role as a direct dependent variable of virtual product presentation modes (Nah et al., 2011; Suh & Chang, 2006; Verhagen et al., 2014).

To create realistic virtual experiences, it is generally important that the visceral reactions in the virtual scenarios be tantamount to physical experiences. This requires a strong perception of being present in the virtual world (Cowan & Ketron, 2019). A useful framework for this is provided by Sheridan (1992), who conceptualises three determinants of presence (p. 121): 1) Approximating the real world stimuli, for example, processing of visual stimul through vivid imagery and auditory stimuli, 2) the ability to control the product (e.g. the ability to grab a product and explore its features), and 3) the ability to modify the stimuli (e.g., opening a product or changing its colour). Lee an Chung (2013) identified the freedom of movement (motion tracking) in the environment as a further enabler of presence. Hence, the quality and quantity of information, the person-product interaction, and the person-environmen interaction that allow for natural movements are crucial for eliciting presence.

Research looking into the quantity and quality of information include Coyle and Thorson (2001) who investigated different versions of web sites, varying the level of used stimuli, including additional animations and audio information. They found that the level of presence increases as the quantity and quality of information increases Fiore et al. (2005) investigated person-product interaction and compared different levels of interactivity for a website, showing that an increased level of interactivity positively affects presence.

Research examining the person-environment interaction and effect on presence includes, for example, Nah et al. (2011), who compared an LSE 3D world (Second Life) where participants could move around freely with their avatar, and a 2D version of the same world using static images. The authors found that the LSE 3D world enabled a higher sense of presence than the 2D version. Also, Martínez-Navarro et al. (2019) found that presence can be increased when beer, wine, and water areas of a supermarket are presented using an HMD (HSE virtual product pre sentation mode) instead of a big screen. Schnack et al. (2019) transferred a supermarket scenario to a 2D desktop and an HSE virtual product presentation mode, looking at the different levels of presence. Interestingly, the authors found that not all aspects of presence can be improved by simply presenting the supermarket scenario using an HMD. They argue that “the results indicated that the desktop system performed slightly better in enabling partici pants to examine objects from different angles (Schnack et al., 2019, p. 45). In an extensive meta-analysis, Cum mings and Bailenson (2016) determined that increased overall technological immersion, for example, using an HMD compared to showing virtual reality on a monitor, indeed has a medium-sized effect on presence, with strong con tributing factors being stereoscopic vision as well as increased levels of tracking of hands and head to enable natura interactions in virtual reality. While research clearly shows a significant increase of presence in an LSE virtual product presentation mode compared to a desktop scenario, there is only limited research examining whether an HSE virtua product presentation mode can elicit a level of presence that is more highly perceived than in an LSE environment. Leaning on Sheridan (1992), we expect that the integration of real-world stimuli, the ability to control and modif the product and the freedom of moving around should enhance the feeling of being in the computer-generate world. We therefore hypothesize the following as a result:

Hypothesis 2a. Compared to an LSE virtual product presentation mode, an HSE virtual product presentation mode significantly increases the sense of presence.

## 2.4 | Shopping frequency as a moderator

Drawing on Hintzman (1971) and Ward (1974), each exposure to informational stimuli leaves traces in the user's mind. Hence, the more traces a user has, the easier s/he can handle the virtual reality encounter. To explain th moderating effect of buying frequency on presence and perceived diagnosticity, it's important to distinguis between already existing and ad-hoc stored knowledge structures when exposed to stimuli. Following Fabriga et al. (2005), people quickly and easily access already-existing associated knowledge structures such as a web sho environment. Knowledge structures are also temporarily created at the moment of stimuli exposure, albeit with wea ker linkages, and are harder to retrieve. Consumers who often use web shops as a result have stronger knowledg structures, which are easier to access than the weaker ones of consumers who only occasionally shop online

Furthermore, literature on cognitive learning theory distinguishes between procedural and factual knowledge (Dacin & Mitchell, 1986; McCormick, 1997). Procedural knowledge refers to “performative knowledge” or “know ing-how” to for example move around a virtual reality shopping environment (McCormick, 1997). Factual knowledg on the other hand, also known as “declarative knowledge” or “knowing-that,” involves knowledge of specific facts or propositions, for example, about a product (Dacin & Mitchell, 1986).

Strong factual knowledge structures are characterised by inherently linked, consistent knowledge that is evalu ated as highly reliable. For instance, Sebastianelli et al. (2007) analysed in detail the difference between consumers with low online shopping frequency and those with high online shopping frequency, applying seven e-tailing quality dimensions. According to their results, for frequent shoppers, the importance of reliability (consistency of websit performance) and product content were significantly higher than for infrequent shoppers. This indicates that th selection and presentation of the product is more important for consumers with high online shopping frequency The more frequently consumers are exposed to virtual visual, haptic, and auditory stimuli, the stronger factual knowl edge structures are anchored in their minds (Bleier et al., 2019). Therefore, we expect that consumers with hig online shopping frequency will profit more from product presentation that uses HMDs and dual hand controller (HSE virtual product presentation mode) to achieve a natural experience in terms of evaluating the quality of the presented product (perceived diagnosticity). Hence,

Hypothesis 1b. The effect of HSE virtual product presentation modes on perceived diagnosticity wil be stronger for people with high online shopping frequency than for people with low online shopping frequency.

In the context of moving within a virtual environment, procedural knowledge—or the “know-how” attributed to technology—is particularly relevant (McCormick, 1997). Procedural knowledge structures are formed through repeated exposure which may strengthen presence: coherent associations exist between objects and evaluations increasing the ease of retrieval of the evaluation from memory (Fazio et al., 1982; Powell & Fazio, 1984). This kind of “know how to do it” knowledge helps frequent online shoppers who already know common web shop interfaces when an HSE component is added, they only have to learn the HSE component, not the entire web shop experience.

Consumers may already be familiar with LSE virtual presentation formats because the media used is frequently encountered when shopping online. This familiarity should facilitate the retrieval of cues already stored in memory, that is, the knowledge of navigating the virtual world and of using haptic and visual support to explore the product As research in this area is not yet existent, we propose:

Hypothesis 2b. The effect of HSE virtual product presentation modes on presence will be stronger for people with high online shopping frequency than for people with low online shopping frequency.

## 2.5 | The impact of perceived diagnosticity and presence on attitude towards product

Attitude towards a product can be described as the overall evaluation of a product (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007a). Atti tude is especially formed when a direct interaction with the product is included (Fazio & Zanna, 1978). Directly trying out the product increases self-motivation, leading to higher consumer confidence (Kempf & Smith, 1998). In th context of online shopping, natural movements and interactions with the product are strongly linked to the sense of being there (presence), and are a very important enabler of attitude towards product (Eroglu et al., 2001; Suh & Chang, 2006). In this context, Suh and Chang (2006) compared a video clip and a multiple-picture version with an LSE virtual product presentation mode, the latter of which allowed an interaction with the product in real time. In their study, presence had a significant effect on attitude towards product, and explained 56% of the total variance.

The more relevant cues or stimuli the consumer is exposed to, the more likely an attitude will be formed (Jahng et al., 2007). Because presence is strongly dependent on the quantity and quality of information (Coyle & Thorson, 2001), we assume presence has a direct effect on the attitude towards product in an online shopping envi ronment. In the HSE virtual product presentation mode, haptic and visual support, including direct and imitating interaction, enhance this effect. We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. The sense of presence positively impacts consumer attitude towards a product.

Fazio (1995) posits that sensory information about the object, or direct experience with it, are information that are commonly viewed as highly diagnostic. Also, knowledge can be particularly accessible when based on information a person considers to be highly diagnostic (i.e., credible evaluative information) (Fazio, 1995). In the context of onlin product evaluation, higher perceived diagnosticity means that consumers have a better product understanding and can make more informed and wiser purchase decisions (Jiang & Benbasat, 2003, 2007a), leading to positive onlin experiences. Attitude is formed by this kind of positive online experience if it is considered relevant and helpful, and diagnostic as a result (Fazio, 1995)

Studies by Lee (2012), Lee et al. (2012), and Jiang and Benbasat (2007a) confirmed that perceived diagnosticit is a determinant of attitude towards product. According to the study by Jiang and Benbasat (2007a), perceive diagnosticity can explain 20% of the variance in attitude towards product. Therefore, we conjecture:

Hypothesis 4. Perceived diagnosticity positively impacts consumer attitude towards a product.

## 2.6 | The impact of HSE virtual product presentation on attitude towards product and purchase intention

Previous research has provided evidence that LSE virtual product presentation modes have a positive impact on the attitude towards product (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007a; Lee, 2012; Lee et al., 2012; Suh & Chang, 2006) and purchase intention (Algharabat, 2014; Fiore et al., 2005) compared to standard non-virtual presentation modes. However, when considering HSE virtual product presentation modes, there is a lack of research about their impact on both atti tude towards product and purchase intention.

The impact of HSE virtual product presentation on attitude towards product and purchase intention can be explained by referring to research that shows that consumers' haptic experiences often serve as an information source for forming a judgement about a product (Krishna & Schwarz, 2014). Consumers become more self-motivated and confident in their attitudes while trying out and evaluating products directly (Kempf & Smith, 1998)

Haptic experiences that are enabled by devices such as a touch screen appear to improve the affective and conative dimensions of consumer learning (Brasel & Gips, 2014) compared to the traditional interaction using a mouse. Hence, consumers' haptic experiences appear to affect attitude towards products, and are important and helpful to rely upon when making a purchase decision (Schwarz, 2011).

Along with the additional haptic support of HSE virtual product presentation modes, visual control—if perceived as useful—also induces consumers to form positive attitudes towards products (Kim & Biocca, 1997). According to Schwarz (2011), bodily sensations are an essential factor to rely upon when making a buying decision. In this context he argues that arm flexion and extension appear to have an influence on information evaluation and processing styl (Schwarz, 2011). This is why we also argue that the ability to move around freely and relocate within HSE virtua product presentation modes influence attitudes towards product as well as purchase intention.

Because of the additional visual and haptic support of the HSE virtual product presentation mode, we assume that both attitude towards product and purchase intention can be increased, leading to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 5. Compared to an LSE virtual product presentation mode, an HSE virtual product presentation mode significantly increases the attitude towards product.

Hypothesis 6. Compared to an LSE virtual product presentation mode, an HSE virtual product presentation mode significantly increases purchase intention.

## 3 | RESEARCH METHOD

## 3.1 | Study setting

We tested our hypotheses in a laboratory experiment with two types of virtual product presentation modes and a between-subjects study design. One web shop with two different presentation modes was designed for this study, providing the independent variable of presentation mode: an LSE virtual product presentation mode for desktop com puters, and an HSE virtual product presentation mode for VR HMDs. In both modes the same kitchen appliances were presented, with the same product information maintained across the two conditions. Similar to the study by Suh and Lee (2005), the web shop consisted of an introduction page, a page with product lists, and a page with detailed informa tion about the product. A fictitious brand for the web shop was created to eliminate any effects resulting from previou experiences or attitudes (Algharabat, 2014; Fiore et al., 2005). In contrast to Martínez-Navarro et al. (2019), we did not investigate different shopping scenarios, but instead examined the differences in interaction with a product. We refrained from transferring a brick-and-mortar store to the VR environment, creating a 3D environment matching the typical user context of the kitchen appliance product type in an online shop scenario. A typical user context in product images wa the application context of the product, which in our case was a kitchen. We created a 3D world matching the product type instead of a real-world store experience, reducing the differences between the desktop-based LSE virtual product presentation web shop and the HSE virtual product presentation web shop. Here, the main difference between the pre sentation modes was the degrees of visual and tactile support. The content of the presentation was the same across both conditions; each contained a website to select products and finalise a purchase, and both contained the same 3D model and respective context to investigate. This allowed us to present a familiar HSE virtual product presentation mode to participants that closely resembled typical online shopping interfaces.

For our study, we chose a product type that fits the technological capabilities and limitations of state-of-the-art immersive VR hardware. Regarding the virtual presentation of products, the literature distinguishes between virtuall high experiential and virtually low experiential products (Suh & Lee, 2005). Virtually low experiential products are those whose most prominent and important attributes for making a buying decision cannot be virtually presented or imitated with the latest technology. With current VR technology, these limitations include a lack of access to taste and smell (e.g., food products), as well as difficulties reading textual descriptions or product aspects due to limite resolution (e.g., books). These presentation issues may be a reason why previous work has not found advantage with the virtual presentation of food products in an immersive VR environment (Peukert et al., 2019). Virtually high experiential products refer to products whose most important attributes can be imitated well within a virtual product presentation. For our study, we chose the product category of kitchen appliances because they are well-suited fo the current technological specifications of HSE-immersive VR technologies. We also chose kitchen appliances for their product type: due to perceived risks resulting from higher acquisition costs, participants engage in cognitive decision-making efforts when shopping for them. Consumers can examine their visual design (visual support) and manipulate the product directly to examine its details (tactile support). We took care to ensure that all textua descriptions were legible at the available VR HMD resolution.

In the HSE virtual product presentation mode, the HMD HTC VIVE Pro was used to show a virtual room with a table and kitchenette, creating a realistic, typical application scenario for a kitchen appliance. It was possible for th participants to move around freely within this VR (a four-meter by four-meter area) and relocate themselves to the front of the kitchenette via a teleport locomotion technique (visual support). When participants were at the front of the kitchen, they could see the kitchen appliance standing in front of them, and were able to interact with it using the HTC VIVE Pro handheld controller (haptic support) (see Figure 2). In the background of the virtual scenario, a version of the browser-based web shop with the same information as the LSE version was available. We deliberatel avoided sophisticated interactions with the virtual kitchen appliances, for example, switching the machine on/off because as Yi et al. (2015) suggest, with online product presentation, restricted interaction is more enticing than ful interaction, and is as effective as a full interaction when it comes to engaging users.

In the LSE virtual product presentation mode, the same products were presented using a 3D view, similar to Suh and Lee (2005), Lee (2012), and Algharabat et al. (2017). Here the participants could manipulate the size (zoom in and out) and the rotation of the different products, allowing them to see the product from different angles. The 3D presentation integrated into the web shop also included the virtual room of the HSE product presentation mode to provide the same virtual context for the appliances (visual support) (see Figure 3). The interaction possibilities and information shown in the web shop were the same as in the HSE version, albeit using the indirect mouse and key board inputs (haptic support) instead of head tracking and spatial controllers to manipulate the viewpoint and object

## 4 | DATA ANALYSIS

A total of 212 students were recruited from 12 academic disciplines at a European university. They came from very diverse backgrounds. 105 were male (49.5%) and 107 female (50.5%). The average age was 23.45, the median 22 years. Approximately 60% of the university students had a job along with their studies (i.e. they were part-tim students), helping explain the high average age. There were no significant differences in terms of age, how often th participants had bought products online in the previous month, and how often they worked with computer $( p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . This implies that the two groups—99 participants in the LSE condition and 113 participants in the HSE condition—were two homogeneous groups whose individual backgrounds did not differ. We focused on college stu dents because students are a major online shopping target market (Ha & Stoel, 2009), and are comfortable with new technologies (Hindi et al., 2002), as a result making them optimal candidates for studying the effects of VR in the context of online shopping. Moreover, students are very typically computer-literate, are more likely to try out new features (Lee et al., 2006), and are often the main target in HCI-focused research

## 4.1 | Missing values and manipulation

Of the 19 relevant measurement items used for this study, 10 were complete, and nine were incomplete data with missing values. However, the maximum of missing values per measurement item was only 2. The missing completely-at-random test showed that the missing data was completely random $( p > 0 . 0 5 )$ ). Following Hair et al. (2016), we replaced missing values using multiple imputation (regression method) in SPSS.

To ensure an effective manipulation, we were careful to offer the same information and interaction methods in both web shop modes. Our goal was to create scenarios close to real-world environments to generate useful result and practical implications. Mouse and keyboard inputs are currently state-of-the-art in the context of e-commerc when it comes to creating an LSE virtual product experience (Papagiannidis et al., 2017). Dual hand controllers hav become the standard device in virtual reality, achieving a natural interaction that can imitate a direct product experi ence (Riedel & Mulcahy, 2019), allowing for an HSE virtual product experience. The differences between the two tested scenarios do not lie in the information content or interaction possibilities, but in the input devices (mouse and keyboard vs. spatial controller) allowing for haptic support, and their associated visual output (desktop screen vs. HMD), enabling visual support. We limited the real-time product interaction to viewpoint changes in both modes allowing users to view products in different sizes and from different viewpoints. These interaction options are often used in online shops (Vonkeman et al., 2017). Although the interactions in both presentation modes were identical the perception of the subjects' interacting with the products should be increased when using a more direct HSE vir tual product presentation mode that allows natural navigation through the immersive virtual shop environment (visual support) and more natural interactions with products (haptic support). This is why we added the question of whether it would be possible to interact with the kitchen appliances (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007a) as a manipulation check. As expected, because of the limited interaction possibilities of size and rotation, the values of both groups were spread around the centre of a six-point Likert scale: $M = 3 . 5 3 , 5 \mathsf { D } = 1 . 5 1$ for the HSE virtual product presenta tion mode, and $M = 2 . 9 2 , 5 \mathsf { D } = 1 . 3 5$ for the LSE virtual product presentation mode. A univariate analysis of variance showed significant differences between the two groups $( F \left[ 1 , 2 1 1 \right] = 9 . 5 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), indicating that the HSE virtua product presentation mode was, as expected, perceived as more interactive.

## 5 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

## 5.1 | Theoretical contributions

This study developed an HSE virtual product presentation mode offering identical functionality to the LSE mode, and visually matching the application context of the chosen product category. Importantly, HSE and LSE modes differed in their visual and haptic support of the product presentation; the HSE mode utilised immersive virtual reality tech nologies (visual support) and allowed for a more natural interactivity via spatial controls (haptic support), while the LSE setting comprised a traditional desktop computer showing a web shop on a 2D screen (visual support) allowin interaction via mouse and keyboard (haptic support). Our study procedure expands upon previous studies comparing LSE virtual product presentation modes for web shops using a traditional desktop computer with non-immersive 2D screens (Jiang & Benbasat, 2003; Lee et al., 2012). Furthermore, in contrast to previous research that investigated HSE virtual product presentation modes for shopping (Martínez-Navarro et al., 2019; Peukert et al., 2019), we did not replicate a typical brick-and-mortar shopping scenario in the HSE mode, instead creating a natural extension of the online shop presentation. Our HSE virtual product presentation mode using the natural application context of products shows that it is not necessary to create a brick-and-mortar representation of a shop to harness the positiv outcomes of immersive technologies for consumers. Transferring the product to a representative consumer living environment, for example, a representative apartment setting, appears to have beneficial effects on the variables we investigated.

Our results confirm that the chosen variables of presence, perceived diagnosticity, and attitude towards product turn out significantly higher for the HSE shop design compared to an LSE shop design and consequently, also score higher on the intention to purchase. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study investigating all of these variables within a single model in the context of an HSE virtual product presentation mode that integrates attitude theory, cue summation theory, and repetitive learning and memory. We extend attitude theory to the context of virtua product presentation modes, defining perceived diagnosticity and presence as cognitive variables. Also, we use cu summation theory to explain why HSE virtual product presentation modes with their interactive, natural, and imitat ing functionality elicit higher effects on the variables studied. Repetitive learning and memory are used to add insights into the moderating role of shopping frequency.

Supporting previous findings that attitude towards product positively affects the intention to purchase withi LSE virtual product presentation modes (Park et al., 2008; Suh & Chang, 2006), this study found that the same is also valid for HSE virtual product presentation modes. This is highly relevant, because previous research results on HSE virtual product presentation modes (Peukert et al., 2019) suggest that this relationship may not always be transferra ble to these kinds of presentation modes. This finding is further relevant for information systems research, and con firms that building a positive attitude towards a presented product is essential for HSE virtual product presentation modes as well.

As discussed in Section 4.3, both perceived diagnosticity and presence turned out to be significantly higher for participants in the HSE condition compared to the LSE condition. In addition, we found that shopping frequenc played a moderating role regarding the increase in presence due to the use of an HSE virtual presentation mode (se Appendix D). Our findings show that the increase in presence that results from using an HSE virtual product presentation mode is significantly higher for subjects with a high online shopping frequency than for those with a low online shopping frequency. Even though this increase in presence is higher for frequent online shoppers, presence in the HSE condition generally increases for both groups (high and low shopping frequency) compared to the LSE con dition. The descriptive statistics provided in Appendix D indicate that presence, perceived diagnosticity, attitud towards product and purchase intention increase for both low and high frequent shoppers when using HSE virtua experiences. This suggests that both types of online shoppers could be potential targets as their perception is mor favourable in high sensory enabling environments

In the e-commerce sector, the frequency of online purchases—expressed in number of online purchases mad within a certain period—has become particularly popular in determining different segments of consumers (Che et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2017; Sebastianelli et al., 2007). Research shows that frequent shoppers may be more loyal and accordingly provide higher revenue and profit than infrequent shoppers (Min et al., 2012). According to ou results, targeting frequent online shoppers with HSE virtual product presentation modes may be a feasible tool fo enhancing customer loyalty.

Our analysis shows that presence and perceived diagnosticity are antecedents of attitude towards product in both the HSE as well as the LSE virtual product presentation modes. Improving perceived diagnosticity may be mor feasible than improving presence, because further improving presence using current state-of-the-art VR technolog as used in this study is challenging. Optimization potential for presence mainly lies in the visual realism of th scenario and auditory feedback, because other factors such as head and hand tracking that have a stronger effect on presence are already integral to this technology (Cummings & Bailenson, 2016). Algharabat (2018) also showed that a high level of presence can be generated through colour vividness, 3D authenticity, and control. Perceived diagnosticity for HSE experiences could be increased by integrating different ways of exploring the product, as foun in real life product usage. And to evaluate the cost–benefit ratio, the optimal level of natural imitation of different product functions should also be determined.

## 5.2 | Practical contributions

Based on our results, we recommend a focus on HSE virtual product presentation modes for virtually high experiential products including kitchen appliances, electronic devices such as cameras, or cars that are a suitable match for the sensory channels of current virtual reality technology. HSE virtual product presentation features help explain how/why these product types are typically explored visually and tactilely.

Online store designers can use existing online shop designs and interaction concepts for websites because they can be transferred well to VR interactions when moving the 2D browser window into VR. Users here do not have to learn new interaction concepts in addition to 3D interaction, and can utilise their existing knowledge of online shops to browse products and make purchases. For the 3D world, online shop designers can use a representative applica tion context for the chosen product category, such as a kitchen for kitchen appliances (as seen in our study). W designed an apartment which could be reused for other products such as furniture, or different electric appliance such as TVs or washing machines. This indicates the flexibility of 3D environments: using them, designers can show products under ideal conditions, and optimise their presentations, making them increasingly attractive. Attractive HSE virtual product presentation modes may even convince consumers that do not have a strong affinity towards VR technology to use or purchase it. Currently, we see retailers as the main target group for HSE virtual product pre sentation modes because of the relatively high costs involved in designing a VR environment for a large number of products. For retailers, this may be an enticing selling and communication tool. Online retailers should complement their existing LSE web shops with HSE product presentation modes for virtually high experiential product types, all owing consumers to choose the option that fits their shopping needs and wants best

An estimated 171 million people had used VR hardware by the end of 2018 (Pizzi et al., 2019), with a growing market and a considerable push for it resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic (Schiopu et al., 2021). Nevertheless the willingness to pay for VR hardware is rather low. Results from Manis and Choi (2019) show that only 30% of their participants would pay more than 200 dollars for proper VR equipment. When looking at private households, the target group for VR could perhaps be gaming-savvy consumers (Disztinger et al., 2017) who typically own high end VR hardware.

## 5.3 | Limitations and future work

This study tested its hypotheses using an experiment in a controlled laboratory environment focusing on a between subject design with two different groups. It has several limitations:

First, its lab environment may suffer from limited external validity. Participants here did not find themselves in natural surroundings, possibly prohibiting normal usage and decision-making behaviour. Second, focusing on onl two conditions may constitute a limited representation of reality. Integrating other conditions with different levels of interactivity or comparing an HSE virtual product presentation mode to a digital recreation of a classical brick-and mortar shopping scenario might provide an even better understanding of the effects of HSE virtual product presen tation modes on consumer learning. Third, students as participants are not representative of any overall population Future research could replicate the study by setting up a representative sample of real consumers. Fourth, in contrast to previous work investigating HSE virtual product presentation modes (Peukert et al., 2019), we analysed a virtuall high experiential product category that is well-suited for the sensory channels available with current state-of-the-art VR technology. Different product types should additionally be considered to obtain an even clearer understanding of the effect of HSE virtual product presentation modes.

Future studies should also investigate variations of the presented study scenario. We tailored the virtual environment to the product, i.e. a kitchen appliance in a kitchen. Future research could assess whether this more sophisticated and expensive presentation mode actually has advantages over a more traditional presentation without a product-specific con text, which could have implications regarding the costs involved for generating virtual environments

Our study considered the variables of presence and perceived diagnosticity as being independent of each other to estimate their effects on attitude and consumer learning. The development of procedural knowledge (represented by presence) could be linked to the development of factual knowledge (represented by perceived diagnosticity), see for example, Brucks (1986). Future studies could therefore include a possible relationship between presence and per ceived diagnosticity in the context of novel HSE virtual product presentation modes by assuming not only a direct effect of presence on attitude towards product, but also an indirect effect via perceived diagnosticity. Independent of this, another indication that additional influencing factors (e.g., related to the technological novelty) might be at work with the HSE virtual product presentation mode is the comparatively lower explained variance obtained fo attitude towards product compared to the LSE scenario.

Finally, the lack of a significant moderating effect of shopping frequency on perceived diagnosticity in our study requires further investigation. Confirming the effect that there is no difference between frequent and infrequent online shoppers could be interesting as far as it relates to capitalising on its potential to attract new online shoppers

## APPENDIX A: MEASURES OF VARIABLES

An abbreviated version of the ITC Sense of Presence Inventory scale was employed to measure presence (Lessiter et al., 2001). Following the work of Li et al. (2002) and Suh and Chang (2006), we removed all questions that could not be meaningfully answered in our two studies (e.g. questions about smell and temperature), measuring presence via the following eight questions from the section “spatial presence”: B4, B9, B12, B18, B25, B28, B34, B38.

Perceived diagnosticity was adapted from Kempf and Smith (1998) and Jiang and Benbasat (2003), and measured using two items based on a six-point Likert scale. Adapted to the context of the experiment, we used the fol lowing two statements:

• This website is helpful for me to understand the performance of the kitchen appliance.

• This website is helpful in familiarising me with the kitchen appliance.

Attitude towards product was measured using three items from Kempf and Smith (1998):

• The kitchen appliance that I've just examined is good.

• I have formed a favourable impression towards the kitchen appliance that I've just examined.

• I like the kitchen appliance that I've just examined.

We borrowed the three-item measurement from Jiang and Benbasat (2003) to measure purchase intention (this research dropped one item from the scale of Coyle and Thorson (2001) due to the thinking that trying a product does not automatically mean purchasing it).

• It is very likely that I will buy the kitchen appliance.

• I will purchase the kitchen appliance the next time I need a kitchen appliance.

• Suppose that a friend called you last night to get your advice about his/her search for a kitchen appliance. Would you recommend buying this kitchen appliance?

All items mentioned above were measured using a six-point Likert scale (“strongly disagree” = 1 to “strongly agree” = 6). Finally, we measured online shopping frequency, referring to Overmars and Poels (2015) and Schultz (2019), and using one question with four different answer possibilities (1–3 times; 4–6 times; 7–9 times; and more than 10 times)

• How often do you purchase products/services online in a typical month?
