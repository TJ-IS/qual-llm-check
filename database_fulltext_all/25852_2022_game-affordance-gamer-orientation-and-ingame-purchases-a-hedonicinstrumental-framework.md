## Abstract

Despite extensive research on user behaviours in free-to-play games, what motivates users to purchase in-game items is still not well understood. We classify game affordances, gamer orientations, and in-game items into two dimensions according to the instrumental–hedonic dichotomy. Utilising the affordance theory, we propose that the fit between game affordances and gamer orientation determines game identification, whereas the fit between game identification and gamer orientations determines in-game purchases. We test the proposed model using a two-wave longitudinal survey. The results suggest that instrumental gamer orientation strengthens the relationship between instrumental game affordances and game identification as well as the relationship between game identification and instrumental in-game purchase. A hedonic gamer orientation strengthens the relationship between hedonic game affordances and game identification as well as the relationship between game identification and hedonic in-game purchases. This paper identifies different types of game affordances and gamer orientations and examines the interactions between the two, advancing the theoretical understanding of proactive behaviours in free-toplay games.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

With the extensive usage of mobile devices, mobile multiplayer online battle arena (MOBA) games have become increasingly popular. For example, League of Legends, a typical MOBA game, had a player base of over 100 million worldwide as of August 2017 (Gough, 2017). This huge player base makes MOBA games one of the most profitable technology markets. Clement (2021) estimated that the mobile MOBA gaming market generated an income of over 77 billion U.S. dollars in 2020. The freemium business model, which refers to free-to-play games, has become ubiqui tous among MOBA games (Koch & Benlian, 2017; Rietveld, 2018; Shi et al., 2019). In free-to-play games, game operators offer their services for free, while revenue is generated through the sale of digital in-game items (Civelek et al., 2018; Mantymaki et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). However, less than 5% of registered players of free-to-play games have ever purchased in-game items (Fang et al., 2019; Koch & Benlian, 2017). The low purchase rate challenges the profitability of free-to-play games (Hamari et al., 2020; Mantymaki et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2021). Research shows that a large number of free-to-play games fail to survive because most players spend nothing on the games (Meng et al., 2021; Sheng et al., 2020). Therefore, converting free players to paying customers is of increasing importance in both research and practice.

Social identity theory suggests that identification with a social referent motivates individuals to contribute to that social referent (Moon et al., 2013; Mueller et al., 2019). Free-to-play games provide players with opportunities for self definition and identification. Drawing on social identity theory, when players develop a sense of identification towards a free-to-play game, they are likely to contribute to that game by purchasing in-game items. A fruitful line of empirical work has validated the role of game identification in shaping in-game purchase behaviours and explored the antecedents of game identification from different perspectives, especially from the perspective of game affordances (Badrinarayanan et al., 2015; Farivar et al., 2018; Konijn et al., 2007; Park & Lee, 2011). Generalising affordances theory to free-to-play games, we define game affordances as the action possibilities available in the mobile gaming environment (Jonasd ottir & Müller, 2020). Empirical evidence shows that game identification occurs when game affordances satisfy players' self definitional needs, which in turn motivates players to contribute to the game (Karahanna et al., 2018; Leonardi, 2011) Although the logic chain of affordances–identification–purchase is clear, there are several gaps to be addressed

First, although prior studies have classified in-game items into a functional–non-functional dichotomy, the moti vational differences between purchasing the two types of in-game items remain open (Hamari & Keronen, 2017a; Wang & Hang, 2021). Functional items offer in-game advantages by enhancing the functional attributes of a game character. Non-functional items alter the appearance of game characters and offer no in-game advantages. Sinc functional and non-functional items are different in nature, the antecedents of functional item purchases should dif fer from those of non-functional item purchases. Prior studies treated in-game purchase as an overall concept with out differentiating between different types of in-game purchase behaviours (Hamari et al., 2020; Park & Lee, 2011; Shukla & Drennan, 2018). As Hamari and Keronen (2017a) stated, few studies clearly reported the type of virtua goods, preventing us from analysing differences between the attractiveness of functional and appearance (non-func tional) based virtual goods (p. 67) . Although prior studies have examined the relationship between game identifica tion and in-game purchases, they have not differentiated between the effects of game identification on functiona and non-functional item purchases (Badrinarayanan et al., 2015; Choi et al., 2018; Park & Lee, 2011). Recently, Wang and Hang (2021) suggested that functional and non-functional item consumption play distinct roles in satisfying players' psychological needs. They focus explicitly on the consequential differences between purchasing functiona and non-functional items. A notable research avenue in gaming research is to examine the motivational difference between purchasing the two types of in-game items.

Second, prior studies that examined the relationship between game identification and in-game purchase behaviour neglected to consider the effect of gamer orientations on in-game purchases. Gamer orientation describes a player's moti vation to play a game. Gamers approach games with different psychological orientations, and there are different kinds o games that cater to those orientations (Bonina et al., 2020; Goh et al., 2012). Playing games has commonly been seen as a leisure activity (Wu et al., 2010). We identified play-for-fun as a primary orientation for gamers. Moreover, games are increasingly used for instrumental purposes, such as playing to win (Hamari & Keronen, 2017b). This is especially true in MOBA games, which are characterised by intense competition. MOBA games are a subgenre of real-time strategy games in which two teams, typically consisting of five players each, compete against each other with a clear winner or loser (Mora-Cantallops & Sicilia, 2018). We therefore identified play-to-win as an important orientation for MOBA gamers, as they may enjoy the process of beating their opponents. Because functional and non-functional items are developed to satisfy different psychological needs of gamers, the relationship between game identification and different types of ingame purchase behaviours may be contingent on game orientation. It might be interesting to examine whether play-to win players are likelier to purchase functional items that make winning easier, or whether play-for-fun players are likelie to purchase non-functional items that boost self-presentation (Hamari, Alha, et al., 2017)

Third, previous studies offer mixed conclusions on the relationship between game affordances and in-game pur chases. For example, Rong et al. (2018) suggest that game balance is positively related to in-game purchase behaviour while Palmeira (2021) suggests that the relationship between game balance and in-game item purchase is not significant Game operators afford different technological features to satisfy the different psychological orientations of players. Thes inconsistent conclusions may derive from the fact that game affordances failed to match gamer orientations. These con tradictory perspectives resonate with recent calls in premier information systems journals for in-depth investigations int the antecedents of in-game item purchase behaviours. Thus, we propose a theory-based research model that explores how the interactions between game affordances, gamer orientations, and game identification jointly predict in-game pur chase behaviours. We raise the following general research questions to guide our theoretical and empirical inquiries

RQ1. What are the relationships between game identification and different types of in-game purchases? How do gamer orientations moderate those relationships?

## RQ2. How do game affordances and gamer orientations jointly predict the formation of game identification?

To address these questions, we first identify two primary gamer orientations that are highly relevant to the com petitive nature of free-to-play MOBA games: play-to-win and play-for-fun. We then identify four typical affordances in free-to-play MOBA games: game balance, game fairness, game novelty, and design aesthetic. Studies have long suggested that hedonic and instrumental rationales determine human behaviours (Chiu et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2021; van der Heijden, 2004). Free-to-play MOBA games develop different types of in-game items and affordances to cater to different gamer orientations. Prior studies have established an instrumental–hedonic continuum to under stand the underlying motivation for playing a game. Following this logic, we classify game affordances, gamer orien tation, and in-game purchase behaviours based on the instrumental–hedonic framework. Instrumental game affordances, such as game balance and game fairness, are aspects of game design that primarily aim to fulfil instru mental gamer orientations. Hedonic game affordances, such as game novelty and design aesthetic, are aspects of game design that attempt to satisfy hedonic gamer orientations. We propose that a strategic fit between gam affordances and gamer orientations (i.e., instrumental affordances interacting with an instrumental gamer orientation may lead to enhanced game identification. We also suggest that the influence of game identification on in-game item purchases can be amplified if gamer orientations match the type of in-game items

This study contributes to theory and practice in several ways. First, this study classifies game affordances, gamer ori entation, and in-game purchase behaviour based on the instrumental–hedonic framework. Second, unlike prior studies that treated in-game purchases as an overall concept, this study differentiates the purchase of functional items from th purchase of non-functional items. This study elaborates that the effects of game identification on in-game purchases ar contingent on the fit between gamer orientation and the types of in-game items offered. Third, while prior studies hav investigated the formation of identification from the perspective of game affordances, this study introduces gamer orien tation into the conversation and examines the synergistic effects of game affordances and gamer orientation.

From a practical point of view, the moderating role of gamer orientation on the logic chain of affordances–iden tification–purchase provides operable recommendations to facilitate in-game purchases. A large number of game affordances, such as fairness, balance, novelty, and design aesthetics, have been found to promote in-game consumption (Badrinarayanan et al., 2015; Palmeira, 2021; Teng, 2018). However, the relative importance of these gam affordances has been underexplored, which challenges the operability of these findings. Game development typicall suffers from time and financial constraints due to marketing and budget limitations. Under time and resource con straints, it is impossible for a game vendor to satisfy all the affordances discussed above. Our results suggest that game affordances should have an emphasis, and that this emphasis depends on the orientations of potential gamers

## 2 | THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

## 2.1 | Antecedents and consequences of game identification

Identification is a cognitive state of psychological attachment characterised by the perception of belongingness to a social referent (Ashforth & Mael, 1989). Social identity theory suggests that people define their positions through associations with social referents, such as communities, workgroups, and organisations (Tajfel, 1982). We therefor define game identification as the degree to which a player regards themselves as a part of the game (Badrinarayanan et al., 2015; Farivar et al., 2018; Jones & Volpe, 2011; Teng, 2017). Identification occurs when an entity satisfies one's self-definitional needs. which in turn motivates the individual to contribute to that entity (Ogbanufe & Gerhart, 2020). Game identification thus motivates players to contribute to a game by purchasing in-game items.

Given the positive role of game identification in predicting in-game purchase behaviour, studies have explore the formation of game identification from the perspective of technological affordances. The theory of affordance provides an analytical link between the technological features of mobile games and the gamer experienc (Chatterjee et al., 2021; Mettler & Wulf, 2019). Affordances are neither properties of players nor game technologies rather, they should be viewed as potential interactions between players and game technologies (Jonasd ottir & Müller, 2020). An affordances perspective represents a relational approach to understanding how gamers interact with the technological features of free-to-play games (Evans et al., 2017; Leonardi, 2011). According to Suh et al. (2017), the central tenet of affordance theory is that technological capabilities do not exist in a specific techno logical function or feature, but rather exist as part of the relationship between a user and technological artifacts in specific situation (p. 273)’. When a player takes advantage of one or more game affordances—by playing the game— to achieve a specific goal, game affordances are actualized (Lee et al., 2021). Thus, the usability of a game's affordances is determined by what the game affords and whether the affordances fit the gamer's orientation. It is important, therefore, to evaluate the synergetic effect between game affordances and gamer orientation and diag nose whether a specific affordance has the appropriate function for gamers to achieve their goals. Such synergetic effects influence whether the game affordances are actualized fully, partially, or not at all.

## 2.2 | Gamer orientations, game affordances, and in-game items under the hedonic– instrumental framework

According to self-determination theory, both hedonic and instrumental rationales determine an individual's attitudes and behaviour (Chiu et al., 2014; Chu et al., 2015; Soliman & Tuunainen, 2021). Drawing on the instrumental– hedonic framework, gamer orientation can be classified into instrumental and hedonic orientations. The instrumenta gamer orientation refers to the fact that people play mobile games to achieve task-oriented, cognitive, and non emotional goals (Chiu et al., 2014). Hedonic orientation is more subjective and personal than instrumental orienta tion. We define hedonic gamer orientation as the fact that people play mobile games to acquire personal emotive experiences, such as fun and enjoyment (Venkatesh et al., 2012).

This paper situated itself in the context of mobile games, particularly free-to-play MOBA games. We identified gamer orientations and game affordances that capture the unique characteristics of free-to-play MOBA games. Compared to computer games, mobile games are played on portable devices at anytime and anywhere. Mobile games are typically played as a pastime and are mostly played in a piece of time, such as on the move or while doing other things concurrently. Mobile games therefore serve a specific purpose: to make the player feel good and relax whe playing them. In their review paper, Hamari and Keronen (2017b) investigate the motivations for playing mobile games and suggest that ‘Gamers just want to have fun (p. 126)’. Several studies have proposed that one of the most important reasons for playing mobile games is to have fun (Goh et al., 2012; Wei & Lu, 2014; Wu et al., 2010). We therefore identified play-for-fun as a typical hedonic gamer orientation

Different games cater to different gamer orientations. For example, social games focus on social interactions within the game, while strategy games deploy predefined plots and tasks (Bayrak et al., 2021; Wang, 2012). Unlik other subgenres of games, MOBA games do not provide predefined plots and tasks. In MOBA games, each player controls a game character, and two teams of five players compete against each other (Mora-Cantallops & Sicilia, 2018). As in e-sports, the ultimate goal of a MOBA gamer is to defeat their opponents. Some play games to enjoy the process of beating others or ‘winning’ the game (Kaye & Pennington, 2016). In the absence of other press ing obligations, some people play games to relax and make the time pass more enjoyably, and some play games to enjoy the process of beating others or winning the game (Kaye & Pennington, 2016). Therefore, we identified play for-fun as a hedonic orientation to playing mobile games and play-to-win as an instrumental orientation

MOBA games provide different kinds of affordances. Similarly, we classified game affordances into a hedonic– instrumental dichotomy. Instrumental game affordance is defined as game designs that primarily aim to fulfil th instrumental goals of gamers (e.g., play-to-win), while hedonic game affordance refers to game designs that primaril satisfy gamers' hedonic goals (play-for-fun).<sup>1</sup>

We suggest that game fairness and balance are two important game affordances that help players achieve their instrumental goals in free-to-play MOBA games. Game fairness refers to the game vendor's assertion of justice in the game context, which enables players to feel they have a fair environment in which they can achieve their goal (Rong et al., 2018). MOBA gamers compete directly against opponents, with a clear winner and loser. Like e-sports MOBA games are most associated with competition (typical MOBA games, such as Arena of Valor™ and League of Legends™, have professional leagues and tournaments). The competitive nature of MOBA games encourages game operators to guarantee fair competition. MOBA gamers thus care about game fairness because they expect a fair match with their opponents. Because most game players expect (or even require) fairness in a gaming environment game operators should strive to provide this affordance (Hamari, Hanner, & Koivisto, 2017; Lin & Sun, 2011; Wu et al., 2016). Game balance refers to the match between the challenges offered by a game and the gaming skills of the players. In MOBA games, each player controls an in-game character and fights with the other team. Players ar likely to be interested in the game only when it allows for a balance between skills and challenges. An imbalance between skills and challenges is likely to result in boredom (i.e., when skills surpass challenges) or anxiety (i.e., when challenges surpass skills) (Hoffman & Novak, 1996). Therefore, we identified fairness and balance as two critica instrumental game affordances of free-to-play MOBA games.

As discussed above, we identified play-for-fun as a hedonic gamer orientation. Hedonic game affordances should aim at satisfying players' psychological needs for fun. We suggest that game novelty and design aesthetic are salient game affordances that help players achieve their hedonic goals in free-to-play MOBA games. Game novelty refers to game design features that users perceive as surprising and unfamiliar (Huang, 2003). Design aesthetic measures the degree to which a player believes a game is aesthetically pleasing to the eyes (Hekkert, 2006). Free-to-play games normally suffer from a high turnover rate since players can switch to alternatives freely. Game novelty and desig aesthetics stand out in free-to-play games, as they were found to help the game ‘lock in’ newly adopted players and encourage players to continuously use the game (Merikivi et al., 2017; Vashisht, 2021). Moreover, game novelty and design aesthetics are important antecedents of game enjoyment value (Merikivi et al., 2017; Teng, 2018). Game novelty generates exploratory behaviour and positive feelings of interest, which make the game enjoyable. The design aesthetic has also been shown to have a positive effect on users' perception of enjoyment (Cyr et al., 2006). There fore, we propose game novelty and design aesthetic as two hedonic game affordances that drive players to have fun in free-to-play games.

Accordingly, free-to-play games develop different in-game items to satisfy heterogeneous gamer orientations In-game items largely fall into two categories: non-functional and functional items. Functional items enhance th functional attributes of a game character and satisfy players' instrumental need to win. Non-functional items are designed to portray game characters in a more fashionable way and fulfil players' hedonic need for fun. We therefor theorise non-functional items as the hedonic products in free-to-play games and functional items as instrumenta products.

## 3 | RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

By differentiating instrumental in-game purchases from hedonic in-game purchases, we specify the effects of game identification on different types of in-game purchase behaviours (H1a and H1b). We suggest that a supplemen tary fit between game affordances and gamer orientation leads to an enhanced perception of game identification (H6a, H6b, H7a, and H7b). ## 3.1 | Effect of game identification on functional and non-functional item purchases

Since functional and non-functional in-game items are different in nature, we would like to specify the unique effects of game identification on different types of in-game purchase behaviours. Functional items grant players instrumental value, which enhances the functional attributes of a game character. According to the basic utility principle of perceived value, a brand with a stronger identity tends to enhance value perception (Teng, 2017). He et al. (2012 further confirmed this argument and suggested that a strong brand identity relates to higher brand value. We applied this logic here to suggest that when players feel they are part of a game with which they strongly affiliate (i.e., gam identification for the purposes of this study), they are likelier to acknowledge the instrumental value of functional in game items and likelier to purchase those items.

Game identification allows players to regard their in-game characters as part of themselves in the gaming world (Klimmt et al., 2009). Once game identification forms, in-game characters and the personal images of gamers are emotionally and cognitively connected (Ewell et al., 2018). Studies have found that gamers tend to build online identities that are close to their ideal aesthetic image (e.g., thinner, younger, and more fashionable) (You et al., 2017) Because gamers view in-game characters as part of themselves, they are likelier to purchase non-functional items to make their in-game characters more fashionable or attractive. For the above reasons, we may expect the following:

H1a. Game identification positively predicts functional in-game item purchase behaviours.

H1b. Game identification positively predicts non-functional in-game item purchase behaviours.

## 3.2 | Game affordances and game identification

We expect the balance between gamer skills and game challenges to positively predict the formation of game identi fication. Hoffman and Novak (1996) noted that a mismatch between user skills and the challenge afforded by infor mation technology is likely to result in either boredom (i.e., when gamer skills surpass game challenge) or anxiety (i.e., when game challenges surpass gamer skills). Therefore, players tend to spend a great deal of time on a gam only when it allows for a balance between skills and challenges. The greater the time spent on an interface, such a with mobile games, the greater the likelihood that a user will develop a social identification with that interfac (Amaral & Monteiro, 2002). D. S. Choi and Kim (2004) offered a more specific conclusion, suggesting that game enjoyment is endorsed if players possess the necessary skills and if the game provides adequate opportunities fo exercising such skills. Taken together, we posit the following:

H2. Game balance is positively associated with game identification.

Fairness is important in our society as well as in games. The fairness literature shows that people care about being treated fairly and are willing to punish unfair behaviours (Kahneman et al., 1986). Without fairness, games often lose their charm. Game fairness encourages players to form positive attitudes towards the game (Lin & Sun, 2011). These positive attitudes are likely to enhance identification with that game. Therefore, we formulate th following:

## H3. Game fairness is positively associated with game identification.

Game novelty describes the extent to which games regenerate themselves. Karanasios and Slavova (2019) argued that novelty facilitates exploratory behaviours and positive feelings of interest. Novelty offers thrills through previously unknown sources and encourages players to become deeply immersed in a game (Chen et al., 2018). Nov elty makes the experience of playing mobile games interesting, thrilling, absorbing, and enjoyable. The critical role of positive attributes (e.g., ‘interesting’, ‘thrilling’, and ‘absorbing’) in developing identification has been established in previous studies (Hou et al., 2012; Konijn et al., 2007). We thus formulate the following:

H4. Perceived game novelty is positively associated with game identification.

Game design aesthetic refers to the degree to which a player believes that a game is aesthetically pleasing. Th design aesthetic construct comprises three perspectives: overall attractiveness, layout, and colour. According to both economics and sociology literature, attractive job applicants are regarded as more impressive and are likelier to catch the eye of potential employers (Chiang & Saw, 2018; Patacchini et al., 2015; Ruffle & Shtudiner, 2015). In the same vein, mobile games with aesthetically pleasing designs (i.e., attractive game characters and a smooth operation inter face) are likelier to obtain positive evaluations from players. Moreover, studies have found that design aesthetics positively predict users' perceptions of a mobile system's ease of use and enjoyability (Cyr et al., 2006), both of which contribute to the development of game identification. We thus propose the following:

H5. Design aesthetic is positively associated with game identification.

## 3.3 | The moderating role of gamer orientation

This section attempts to explore the interaction between game affordances and gamer orientations. A supplementar fit is formed when instrumental gamer orientation intersects with instrumental game affordances. For play-to-wi gamers, game fairness and balance afford a reliable gaming environment to accomplish their goals. A counterexampl is that in some free-to-play games, premium players may have superior advantages over free players, which erode game fairness (Mantymaki & Salo, 2015). Wealthier players can purchase more powerful weapons and in-game char acters, which undermines game balance (Lin & Sun, 2011). In this case, play-to-win gamers show less identification with the game, as their orientation does not fit with the game's affordances. Therefore, we expect that the positiv effect of game fairness and balance on game identification would be more prominent among play-to-win gamers Thus, we hypothesize:

H6a. A play-to-win gamer orientation strengthens the positive relationship between game balance and game identification.

H6b. A play-to-win gamer orientation strengthens the positive relationship between game fairness and game identification.

A supplementary fit is formed when hedonic gamer orientation interacts with hedonic game affordances. Play for-fun gamers seek to immerse themselves in games that make time pass more enjoyably. The novelty and design aesthetic of technology have been widely acknowledged as critical attributes that can result in a distorted sense of time (Guo et al., 2016; Merikivi et al., 2017; Pelet et al., 2017; Rose et al., 2012). This sense of time distortion indi cates that the consumer is unaware of time passing, so time appears to pass more quickly and enjoyably (Novak et al., 2000). We expect that a mobile game that prioritises novelty and design aesthetic is likelier to make th player's time pass enjoyably, which will further enhance the player's identity with the game. Therefore, the positiv effects of novelty and design aesthetic on game identification would be more prominent for those gamers who pla for fun. This leads to the following hypotheses:

H7a. A play-for-fun gamer orientation strengthens the positive relationship between game novelty and game identification.

H7b. A play-for-fun gamer orientation strengthens the positive relationship between design aesthetic and game identification.

If a player shows high identification with a game, they are likelier to acknowledge the value of in-game items which in turn stimulates the player's purchase behaviours (Farivar et al., 2018; He et al., 2012; Parasurama et al., 1988). A supplementary fit between gamer orientation and in-game items reinforces the above mechanism Play-to-win gamers play games with explicit goals. Functional items enhance the functional attributes of a gam character, which helps the player achieve their goals more efficiently. For example, a player can gain superior advan tages by equipping characters with functional items to beat opponents more easily. Players can decorate their in game characters in a more fashionable way using non-functional items. Non-functional items are aestheticall appealing, which makes time pass enjoyably. In the same vein, we expect that play-for-fun gamers are likelier to b aware of and pay more attention to the value of non-functional items. Taken together, game identification enable players to acknowledge the values of both functional and non-functional items; this value identification proces would be leveraged if a gamer's orientation fits with the in-game item offerings. Thus, we posit the following:

H6c. A play-to-win orientation strengthens the positive relationship between game identification and functional in-game item purchases.

H7c. A play-for-fun orientation strengthens the positive relationship between game identification and nonfunctional in-game item purchases.

## 4 | METHODOLOGY

## 4.1 | Research settings and data collection

This paper focuses on free-to-play mobile games. Arena of Valor was selected to test the proposed model, as it is one of the most profitable mobile games worldwide (Pocket Gamer, 2018). We tested the model using a two-wave longitudinal survey. In the first wave of the survey, we posted links to our questionnaire on several Arena of Valor forums and communities and invited visitors to participate in the survey. Subjects were notified that participation was volun tary and that only aggregate data that contained no personally identifiable information would be used. Subjects who provided valid responses would receive a ¥10 (approximately \$2) cash reward. When a respondent visited a particu lar forum, the survey URL was made available either on the first page or in the form of a pop-up that prompted th respondent to participate in the study. The first survey was left open for 4 weeks, and 1456 gamers completed th survey. All respondents were asked to disclose their email addresses for the subsequent study. To increase the valid ity of the data, criteria for determining invalid responses were set. Of the responses, 21 were not useable becaus they did not meet the age criterion (above 18 years old), 17 were dropped because they used duplicate IP addresses and nine were dropped because the participant reported playing the game for more than 168 h per week (which i impossible). Based on preliminary testing, the survey was expected to take 15–20 min to complete. Therefore, to increase response quality, 127 responses were dropped because they were completed in less than 15 min. In total 1282 valid responses were received for the first survey.

We conducted the second wave of our survey 3 months later. The second wave was also left open for 4 weeks. For our second survey, a link was sent via email to the 1282 qualified respondents from the first survey. A friendly reminder was sent to all non-respondents on the 15th day of the second survey. After eliminating incomplete o rushed responses, 233 valid responses were received for the second survey.

We further examined the sample for potential self-selection bias by comparing the demographics of the first survey respondents with those of the second survey. The results of a Chi-square test showed that the demographics of the first survey respondents did not differ significantly from those of the second survey. For both first- and second wave respondents, we compared the demographics of early respondents (those who completed the survey in the first 2 weeks) and late respondents (those who completed the survey in the last 2 weeks) and found no statisticall significant differences between the two groups. ## 4.2 | Questionnaire development

To ensure construct validity, we used items from existing scales wherever possible. All survey items were measured on a 7-point Likert-type scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). All the measurement scales were drawn from literature written in English. Because our survey was conducted in China, we followed the standard translation approach put forward by Van de Vijver et al. We asked a professional translator who was unfamiliar with our study to translate the original English questionnaire into Chinese. ## 4.3 | Common method bias

Several recommended procedures were used to eliminate common method bias (CMB) ex ante. First, the indepen dent and dependent variables came from two separate surveys, which significantly reduces such bias. According to Podsakoff et al. (2003), one remedy for controlling for common method biases is to collect data with a time lag between the measurement of the predictor and the outcome variable. Second, we followed a systematic question naire and item development process to achieve clarity on the measurement scales. Third, we guaranteed the ano nymity of all respondents, along with the confidentiality of the data, to reduce evaluation apprehension. Fourth, w randomised the order of the measurement items to limit the respondents' ability to detect patterns between the con ceptual scales. Finally, we placed filler tasks (demographic questions) between the measurement of the dependent variables and independent variable to create a psychological separation, which helps minimise CMB (Fulme et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2017, 2020).

While these procedural remedies may help to minimise CMB, they may not eliminate it entirely. We adopted th confirmative factor analysis (CFA）marker technique to ensure that our findings were not skewed by CMB threats We used ‘life–work balance’ as the marker variable since Schaller et al. (2015) found that ‘life–work balance’ is virtu ally neutral to any theories, and its items elicit similar cognitive processes to those of the constructs studied in this paper.

Table 3 shows that life–work balance is statistically unrelated to other scales, satisfying the criterion for an idea marker variable (Schaller et al., 2015). Following the procedures established by Williams et al. (2010), we tested th following four models to check for CMB: the CFA model, the baseline model, the method-constrained model, and the method-unconstrained model. In the CFA model, we loaded all the indicators on their latent constructs, and all th coefficients were estimated freely. In the baseline model, we fixed the loadings and errors of the marker variable wit the value obtained in the CFA model and fixed the structural path of the marker variable to the other focal latent constructs to zero. In the method-constrained model, the marker was loaded on every other latent construct with fixed loading (constrained). Finally, the method-unconstrained model is identical to the method-constrained model, except that the cross-loadings of marker variable items on other latent constructs are estimated freely (unconstrained). Th results are presented in Table 2. The comparative fit index statistics among the four models do not show significant dif ferences. The model inequivalence test between the baseline and method-constrained models is not significant, indi cating that method variance does not significantly alter the results. The insignificant inequivalence between th method-constrained and method-unconstrained models further supports that neither congeneric nor non-congeneric method variance poses a significant threat to our results. Hence, we can conclude that CMB is not a significant threat

## 4.4 | Tests for non-response bias

To further establish data quality, we tested for potential non-response bias for the two-wave longitudinal survey. In the first wave, we received 1282 qualified responses. We compared the demographics of early respondents (who completed the survey in the first 2 weeks) with those of late respondents (who completed the survey in the last 2 weeks) and found no significant differences between the two groups (age: $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 1 . 3 3$ ; income: $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 0 . 9 4 ;$ gender: $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 1 . 2 5 ;$ ; education: $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 1 . 5 0 ;$ ; game experience: $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 0 . 8 7 )$ . In the second wave, 233 valid responses were collected, indicating that there were 1049 non-responses. We compared the demographics of th valid responses with those of the non-responses and found no significant differences between the two groups (age $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 1 . 7 7 ;$ ; income: $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 2 . 4 5 ;$ gender: $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 1 . 4 3 ;$ education: $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 2 . 0 6 ;$ game experience $F _ { ( 1 , 1 2 8 0 ) } = 0 . 9 7 $ ). We thus conclude that non-response bias is an unlikely validity threat.

## 5.2 | Structural model

The model was tested using data from both surveys. Two dependent variables, functional and non-functional item purchases, were drawn from the second survey. The rest of the variables were obtained from the first survey. We measured in-game purchase behaviour in multiple dimensions: purchase frequency, purchase amount, and purchase quantity. In this section, we estimate the model using non-functional and functional item purchase frequency as dependent variables. The data were analysed using AMOS 24.0. Figure 2 depicts the results of our structural model including the estimated path coefficients, the associated t-value, and the overall theoretical explanatory power of the model. Before analysing the data, we first checked for possible multicollinearity risk using the VIF. The maximum VIF value of our independent variables was 3.09, which was below the recommended threshold of 3.3 (Petter et al., 2007). The VIF analysis indicated that there was no multicollinearity risk in our model. The demographics of the respondents, such as age, gender, income, education, and time spent gaming per week, were all controlled.

As shown in Figure 2, game identification was found to exert positive and significant effects on functional item purchase frequency $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 4 , t = 5 . 3 3 )$ and non-functional item purchase frequency $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 2 , t = 6 . 2 6 )$ . Thus, both H1a and H1b were supported. Game Balance $( \beta = 0 . 2 1 8 , t = 2 . 7 5 )$ , Game Fairness $( \beta = 0 . 3 3 5 , t = 4 . 4 6 )$ , Game Novelty $( \beta = 0 . 2 4 2 , t = 3 . 4 0 )$ , and Design Aesthetic $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 9 , t = 3 . 3 4 )$ all showed a positive influence on Game Identification, lending support to H2–H5. Two interaction terms, $P l a y  – t o – W i n \times G a m e$ Balance $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 8 , t = 2 . 6 4 )$ and Play-to-Win Game Fairness $( \beta = 0 . 1 5 2 , t = 2 . 8 0 )$ , were found to significantly predict game identification. Therefore, H6a and H6b were supported. In the same vein, H7a and H7b were supported because both Play-for Fun Game Novelty $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 6 , t = 2 . 9 5 )$ and Play-for-Fun Design Aesthetic $( \beta = 0 . 1 9 3 , t = 2 . 6 5 )$ showed positive and significant effects on game identification. Neither Play-to-Win Game Novelty $( \beta = - 0 . 0 5 2 , t = 1 . 4 6 )$ nor Playto-Win Design Aesthetic $( \beta = - 0 . 0 9 5 , t = 1 . 6 5 )$ was found to significantly predict game identification. The effect of Play-for-Fun Game Balance $( \beta = - 0 . 0 3 2 , t = 0 . 6 9 )$ and Play-for-Fun Game Fairness $\left( \beta = 0 . 0 8 3 , t = 0 . 9 1 \right)$ on game identification were not significant either. These results ruled out the interactions between instrumental game orientation and hedonic game affordances. The direct and moderate constructs jointly explained 53.1% of the vari ance in Game Identification. H6c hypothesized that the play-to-win orientation strengthens the relationship between game identification and functional item purchase. This hypothesis was supported, as the coefficient of the interac tion term Play-to-Win Game Identification was positive and significant (β = 0.115, t = 2.51). Play-for-Fun Game Identification was found to positively predict non-functional item purchase (β = 0.172, t = 2.67), providing support for H7c. Game Identification and its interaction terms explained 20.9% of the variance in functional item purchase and 22.4% of the variance in non-functional item purchases.

## 5.3 | Mediated moderation

Our results suggest that game identification may mediate the relationship between game affordances and in-game purchase frequency. The application of bootstrapping allows for the testing of mediation effects (van Reijmersda et al., 2013). The 5000 resamples in this study generated a 95% confidence interval (percentile) for the mediator. The results are presented in Table 5.

Our results predict that gamer orientation moderates the indirect effects of game affordances on in-game item purchase frequency. Accordingly, we conducted a mediated moderation analysis (Jin, 2012), bootstrapping 5000 samples to compute bias-corrected confidence intervals. The results (reported in Table 6) suggest that the indirect effects of game affordances (balance, fairness, novelty, and design aesthetics) on in-game purchase behaviours vi game identification are significantly moderated by player orientation (play-for-fun and play-to-win). Specifically, th indirect positive effects were stronger for players with high orientations than for those with low orientations. Th results also revealed that gamer orientation moderates the indirect effects of game affordances on purchase fre quency by moderating both the first and second stages of the mediation chains.

## 6 | DISCUSSION

## 6.1 | Main findings

This study reconceptualizes in-game purchasing as a proactive behaviour and theorises it as a result of the fit between game affordances, gamer orientation, and different types of in-game items. Game identification is framed as a key mediator linking game affordances and in-game purchase behaviours. This paper proposes that the influence of game affordances on game identification could be amplified if game affordances fit with a gamer's orientation Moreover, the effects of game identification on in-game purchase behaviours can be strengthened if a gamer's orientation fits with the in-game item offerings. We used a two-wave longitudinal survey to test the proposed model. Th findings can be summarised as follows.

The empirical results revealed that game identification positively influences both functional and non-functiona item purchase behaviours. Game fairness, balance, novelty, and design aesthetic were found to positively predic game identification, with more than half (53.1%) of the variance explained. These results clarified the specific mecha nism through which game affordances influence in-game consumption. Investigating both game affordances and gamer orientation in a single study, we can see that a play-to-win orientation strengthens the effects of game fair ness and balance on game identification, while a play-for-fun orientation strengthens the effects of game novelt and design aesthetic on game identification. Differentiating functional items from non-functional items, we foun that a play-to-win orientation enhances the relationship between game identification and functional item purchases whereas a play-for-fun orientation enhances the relationship between game identification and non-functional item purchases. These results illustrate the importance of gamer orientation, which has often been overlooked in prio studies.

## 6.2 | Theoretical contributions

From a theoretical perspective, the current study makes several contributions. First, this study classifies game affordances, gamer orientations, and in-game purchase behaviours based on the instrumental–hedonic framework Regarding in-game purchase as an overall concept, prior studies have identified ‘lists’ of game affordances without conceptually classifying these affordances (Badrinarayanan et al., 2015; Farivar et al., 2018; Konijn et al., 2007

Park & Lee, 2011). People play mobile games for different purposes. Game companies therefore afford different technological features and different kinds of in-game items to accommodate different gamer orientations. This stud integrates game affordances, gamer orientations, and in-game purchase behaviours into a unified theoretical frame work and classifies them using an instrumental–hedonic dichotomy. This classification establishes a platform for aca demics to elaborate the interrelationships between game affordances, gamer orientations, and in-game purchas behaviours.

Second, this study explores the synergic effects of game affordances and gamer orientation on game identification. We show that the formation of game identification is determined not only by what a game affords but also by whether the affordances fit with a gamer's orientation. Thus, this study extends affordance theory to the context of MOBA games, contributing to the application of affordance theory itself, and further suggests that affordance theory is a novel and solid theoretical framework for guiding future studies on game identification and in-game consumption

Third, this study differentiates functional item purchases from non-functional item purchases and thus clarifie the relationship between game identification and different in-game purchase behaviours from a contingency per spective. According to social identity theory, if a player identifies with a game, they are likelier to be inspired to make a contribution to that game (Cable & Edwards, 2004; Edwards, 1996). The theoretical logic of the positive effect of game identification on in-game consumption is clear. However, previous studies have generated ambiguous result regarding the relationship between the two. While a few studies have found that game identification has a positive influence on in-game purchase behaviours (Badrinarayanan et al., 2015; Park & Lee, 2011; Shukla & Drennan, 2018), others have failed to replicate those findings (Cheng-Hsun & Ting-Yun, 2012). We resolved this puzzle by specifying the relationship between game identification and different types of in-game purchase behaviours and demonstrating how gamer orientation moderates that relationship.

## 6.3 | Practical implications

The results of this study provide operable recommendations to facilitate in-game consumption. The interaction effects between game affordances and gamer orientation offer a strategic window for game operators to establish game identification among players. The critical roles of game balance and fairness have also been supported by these findings; therefore, game developers are advised to balance game challenges with gamer skills. To ensure game fair ness, character growth, resource allocation, and consumer services should be treated equally for both free and paid players. As a game novelty and design aesthetic also show a positive influence on game identification, mobile gam operators are encouraged to continuously introduce new game characters, roles, weapons, and events to engage players with something new and different. Compared to computer games, mobile games are played on relativel small screens and in short sessions, making game interface design a particularly crucial issue. A mobile game interfac should be clear and concise, while also being aesthetically pleasing to the eyes of potential players

A large number of game affordances have been found to benefit game success (Badrinarayanan et al., 2015; Moon et al., 2013; Rong et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2016; Teng, 2018). However, game development often suffers from time and financial constraints that make it nearly impossible for a mobile game to satisfy all the attributes discussed above. As an old saying goes, ‘There are a thousand Hamlets in a thousand people's eyes’. Even if game operators could afford all the attributes discussed above, they still could not please everyone, because gamers' preferences and expectations towards game will vary. Our findings on the fit between game affordances and gamer orientation reveal that game designs should have an emphasis and that this emphasis should match the orientations of potential gamers. Specifically, if gamers pla for fun, game developers should place particular emphasis on game novelty and design aesthetics. If gamers play to win guaranteeing game fairness and balance should be top priorities. The fit between gamer orientation and the types of in game items suggests that the promotion campaign for in-game items should also reflect gamer orientation—game vendors should peddle non-functional items to gamers who play games for fun and recommend functional items to gamers who play to win.

## 6.4 | Limitations and future research

This study is not without limitations. It is acknowledged that focusing on a single aspect of identification (i.e., game identification) may limit the explanatory power of our model. People can develop identification with multiple targets simultaneously, especially when the identities of the targets are interrelated (Badrinarayanan et al., 2015; Vora & Kostova, 2007). In mobile games, players may not only develop identification with the game itself but also with in game characters and the game community. It would therefore be valuable to examine the interrelationships between different identifications and differentiate their roles in predicting in-game purchase behaviours. It would be interesting to know whether game community identification leads to game character identification or vice versa. Under standing the relative importance of multiple identifications is also a prospective avenue for future studies. Gam companies cultivate in-game purchases through different means. If game community identification outweighs game character identification in predicting in-game purchases, game companies are suggested to create virtual communi ties and stimulate interactions between community members. Otherwise, game companies could enhance the appearances and playability of the game characters.

Second, with more and more social functions being incorporated into mobile games, playing mobile games ha increasingly been regarded as an outlet for socialisation (Osmanovic & Pecchioni, 2016). This paper considered two dominating gamer orientations but ignored other orientations, such as socialisation. Hence, future research that com pares the effects of different gamer orientations on in-game purchases will be helpful. This paper situated itself in the context of MOBA games. Studies that validate our model in other subgenres of mobile games, such as socia games, first-person shooting games, and real-time strategy games, while showing similarities and differences, would be helpful.

Third, the data collection process may limit the generalizability of the results. For instance, because ou responses were collected from online game forums, our respondents may be skewed in terms of how active they are in mobile gaming. Data from multiple sources could possibly offer a more representative sample population. For example, future studies are encouraged to recruit potential respondents in collaboration with the game company and observe the actual purchase behaviours of players

## 7 | CONCLUSIONS

This study explores the formation of game identification from the perspectives of game affordances and gamer ori entation and examines its influence on in-game purchase behaviours. Drawing on the instrumental–hedonic framework, we classified gamer orientations, game affordances, and in-game items into the instrumental–hedoni dichotomy. We show that instrumental (hedonic) game affordances interact with instrumental (hedonic) gamer orien tation, leading to enhanced game identification. Moreover, instrumental (hedonic) gamer orientation amplifies th relationship between game identification and instrumental (hedonic) in-game item purchase behaviours. The pro posed model was validated using a two-wave longitudinal survey. This paper paves the way for a better understand ing of how to stimulate identification in free-to-play games as well as in online settings in which social identification can emerge. Game development is a capital- and technology-intensive activity. Game developers cannot realise al the useful affordances simultaneously. Time and resource constraints force game developers to make strategic trade-offs. Prior literature has identified multiple game affordances that facilitate in-game purchase behaviours. Th relative importance of these affordances, however, remains open. Our findings suggest that game affordances ar not equally important. The relative importance of multiple game affordances is determined by whether the affordances fit with a gamer's orientation. Game developers should focus on specific market segmentation and b aware of the orientations of potential players. This paper also establishes a platform for future studies to further explore the strategic fit between game affordances and gamer orientation in determining various market outcomes such as service engagement, brand co-creation, and consumer recruitment
