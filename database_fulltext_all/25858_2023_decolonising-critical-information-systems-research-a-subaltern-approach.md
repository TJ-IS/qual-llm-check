# Decolonising critical information systems research: A subaltern approach

## Silvia Masiero

Department of Informatics, Ole-Johan Dahls Hus, University of Oslo, Oslo, Norway

Correspondence

## Abstract

Theories used in the Information Systems (IS) field come in large majority from authors based in Western countries, a bias that holds for critical theories as well. Such a bias is made more problematic by the mandate of critical theory, which is meant exactly to illuminate the oppressive conditions of the status quo. Against this backdrop, this paper explores the subalternity theory approach – developed by the Subaltern Studies collective from the early 1980s – as an indigenous theory that, proposing a socially and geographically connotated narration of ‘history from below’, can play a major role in the effort to decolonise critical IS research. By positioning subaltern theory in the IS field, the paper offers an alternative to the Western hegemony of critical theories, exploring the potential of such an alternative to voice systematically silenced and marginalised perspectives.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Pluralism is key to enriching Information Systems (IS) research. Since Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991), it has bee remarked that pluralism of theories and paradigms adds breadth and depth to knowledge in the field. Orlikowski an

Baroudi (1991) brought pluralism to the attention of IS scholars, leading to further exploration of the potential of diverse perspectives to increase knowledge.

At the same time, bibliographic examinations of IS research conducted decades later reflect, at best, limite improvement in terms of pluralism. Exercises mapping the theories used in the IS field reveal that such theories are generated, in overwhelming majority, by authors based in high-income countries classifiable as Western nations. In their map on theoretical approaches in the IS field, Davison and Díaz Andrade (2018) offer a clear picture of th phenomenon:

“Of the 104 theories (the vast majority published in English) listed on the IS Theory Wiki (https://is. theorizeit.org/), it is remarkable that when examining the authorship of the original book or article in which a specific theory appeared, 87 involved authors were based in the United States. Non-US authors hail from the United Kingdom (10), Hong Kong (3), The Netherlands (3), Australia (2), Canada, (2), France (2), and Germany (2) and 1 from each of the following: Austria, Belgium, Cyprus, Finland, Japan, New Zealand and Norway. Not a single theory has an author from Africa, South or Centra America, Asia (except the relatively developed economies of Japan and Hong Kong), or the Pacific Islands, ie, the less economically developed countries of the world, sometimes referred to as the Global South.” (Davison & Díaz Andrade, 2018: 759–760)

Crucially, Western bias affects also theories that, such as critical theory, are meant to question exactly th oppression and alienation implicit in the status quo (Myers, 1997). Critical theory is defined by its mandate to combine the theorisation of the status quo with its transformation, drawing an active form of critique in which oppres sive conditions are illuminated and challenged (Myers & Avison, 2002). And yet, representations of critical theory in IS reveal domination of Western perspectives: one of the most established papers on principles for critical research in IS (Myers & Klein, 2011) offers the theoretical strands from Bourdieu, Foucault and Habermas as guiding streams for critical theory, only signalling the presence of non-Western approaches in one paragraph (Myers & Klein, 2011: 21). The result is a picture of critical theory that explores these three streams in detail, but does not focus on per spectives that transcend the grand theorists (Wang et al., 2019).

As Myers and Klein (2011: 18) state, ‘the absence of any criteria or principles makes it very difficult for critica work to be published in our top journals and hinders the acceptance of critical research amongst the wider IS research community’. As such the choice of the three theorists, and the derivation of principles that follows, come from the willingness to enable critical research to enter the top journal domain that is historically precluded to it (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). In this respect, Myers and Klein (2011) perform the impor tant task of aiding critical research to become amenable to top journal standards, establishing principles to assess it rigour and sustain its conformity for publication in the most recognised outlets of the discipline

At the same time, the choice of guiding theorists that the paper makes is candid on the criteria that informed it. Such criteria are illustrated as follows:

“To what extent have they been used in the IS research literature, in particular the leading journals? To what extent are their writings concerned with substantive social issues such as freedom, power, social control and values (as opposed to mostly epistemological or methodological issues)? To what extent have they acquired a reputation as critical theorists in the social science literature? Are they sufficiently different from each other?” (Myers & Klein, 2011: 20)

While aimed to make critical research amenable to publication in top journals, the paper arguably uses the same criteria that inform the Western bias scenario described by Davison and Díaz Andrade (2018). The choice of guiding theorists is predicated on having been used in IS research literature, in particular the top journals , a domain histori cally precluded to non-Western theorists, as Davison and Díaz Andrade (2018) show. While Bourdieu, Foucault an

Habermas are ‘concerned with substantive social issues such as freedom, power, social control and values’, the need for acceptability in top journals seems to preclude voices from non-Western contexts, who largely fail to meet the criterion of ‘having been used in top journals’ before. In this spirit, Myers and Klein (2011: 21) encourage ‘the introduction of new authors into the critical stream of IS research’.

Taking a decolonial approach, meant with Escobar (2018) as an approach that unties the production of knowledge from dominantly Western science and paradigms, this paper responds to the call from Myers and Klein (2011) by proposing an alternative to Western perspectives on critical theory. Drawing on the subaltern theory approac developed by the Subaltern Studies collective, from Guha and Spivak (1988) to more recent theorisations of sub alternity in South Asia (Chatterjee, 2004), the paper explores subaltern theory in the light of its capability to chal lenge the Western hegemony of critical theory in IS. Doing so, it illuminates the epistemic violence perpetrated by substantial part of research that identifies as critical, and positions subaltern theory as a route to a project of libera tion (Abdelnour & Abu Moghli, 2021; Harrison, 2011) in IS research

The paper is structured as follows. We first map the literature on critical research in IS, illuminating its nuance but also the Western bias in it. We then introduce the subaltern theory approach, reviewing its core tenets and potential in voicing critical perspectives beyond the West. Three papers published in the IS field are then used to illustrate the potential of subaltern theory to construct the marginalised as subjects of active resistance. The discus sion positions subaltern theory within IS research, exploring its indigenous approach to knowledge and its potentia to decolonise the field.

## 2 | BACKGROUND: CRITICAL IS RESEARCH AND THE DECOLONIAL CONTEXT

## 2.1 | Critical IS research: A mapping of the literature

Our considerations start from a mapping of critical research in the IS field. Similar efforts, focused specifically on crit ical research, have been previously conducted in IS journals (Falconer, 2008; Richardson & Robinson, 2007). Ou mapping of the literature relies on the AIS Basket of Eight journals, a choice that differs from the set of journals used in Falconer (2008) and Richardson and Robinson (2007). While a different set of journals would have likely led to a greater range of papers, our choice to focus on the AIS Basket of Eight is motivated by the IS perspective that it provides, as a result of its recognition as a set of journals that deal with core themes in the IS field.

To conduct the mapping, we have used inclusive keywords for critical theory and research and applied them to the Basket of Eight journals for all years. As Myers and Klein (2011: 26) note, many researchers use ‘critical theory and ‘critical research’ as synonyms, notwithstanding the character of critical research as an approach whose authors rely on one or more critical theorists. All articles were checked for relevance, resulting in a subset of papers that directly engage critical IS research. We have then supplemented our results with papers included in Special Issues dedicated to critical theory, and with papers that, referred to as critical by Myers and Klein (2011), present the main traits of critical research. The full literature review protocol is reported in Appendix

The result is a corpus of 86 papers that, from 1991 to 2021, engage critical research in top IS journals. This affords commenting on themes and theories within them, but also on the evolution of critical research in the IS field Five core observations emerge from the corpus.

This i reflected in Orlikowski and Baroudi's (1991: 6) note that, differently from positivist and interpretive approaches, crit ical research goes beyond a predictive intent, seeking instead to liberate the subjects of research from diverse form of oppression.

## 2.1.2 | Reliance on common theories

In a corpus spanning 30 years, relatively early papers (Adam, 2002; Brooke, 2002a, 2002b) signal a common ten dency of critical IS papers to rely on Habermas. Brooke (2002a: 49) presents this as a concern, pointing out that critical theory in IS risks to become ‘locked into a Habermasian discourse’. Myers and Klein (2011) recognise the influence of Habermas: they do however note that Bourdieu and Foucault have also informed thought in critical IS, leading to a landscape dominated by approaches from these three grand theorists. In a recent account, Wang et al. (2019) interrogate critical research beyond Bourdieu, Foucault and Habermas , highlighting the importance of diver sified perspectives in the field.

Approaches in our corpus include feminist theory (Adam, 2002; Adam et al., 2006; Howcroft & Trauth, 2008; Reid et al., 2010), critical labour process theory (Saravanamuthu, 2002a, 2002b), boundary critiqu (Cordoba, 2009), postcolonial theories (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019; Kendall et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2015; Ravishanka et al., 2013), emancipatory pedagogy (Kane et al., 2021) and Beck's critical theory of the risk society (Ngwenyam et al., 2021). While such perspectives offer important alternatives, our corpus is in line with Myers and Klein' (2011) point that theories by Bourdieu, Foucault and Habermas prevail in critical IS research. Even works on issues from particular social contexts tend to rely on grand theorists (cf. Vaidya & Myers, 2021), while indigenou approaches, specific to particular contexts or cultures (Davison & Díaz Andrade, 2018), find limited presence in critical IS work.

## 2.1.3 | Expansion of the range of themes

According to McGrath (2005: 86), there are often causes – ‘for example, feminism, environmentalism, less developed economies – that inspire critical research, even though, she argues, the IS field lacks an explicit definition of what ‘being critical’ is. In response Walsham (2005) notes that recurring themes, as well as elements of motivation, research focus and choice of theory, contribute to the definition of being critical, marking the difference between critical and interpretive approaches. Themes characteristic of critical theory pertain largely to groups made vulnerable by economic, social and political conditions, and whose emancipation is sought by the researcher along wit theorisation.

As noted by Myers and Klein (2011), gender, disability and political asymmetries are prominent in critical IS work. Our corpus reveals further thematic expansion: along traditional topics on IS and organisations, critical IS papers from the last 10 years feature themes including crowdsourcing (Schlagwein et al., 2019), digital identity sys tems (Ngwenyama et al., 2021), machine learning (Kane et al., 2021) and agricultural markets (Vaidya & Myers, 2021). This expansion of themes reveals openness of critical research to the most recent objects of the IS field.

## 2.1.4 | Limited evolution in numbers

As compared to the first decade of the 2000s, when 47 papers in our corpus were published, only 33 were publishe from 2010. A declining interest of the IS field in critical research is revealed by more indicators: while four Specia Issues on critical theory were published by IS journals in 2000–2010 (Myers & Klein, 2011), none appeared in th Basket of Eight journals after 2010, a time in which panels, tracks and workshops on critical theory have arguabl found limited featuring in IS conferences. Such a decline is surprising at a time in which the field is openly turning to issues of power and justice, as demonstrated, amongst others, by Special Issue calls on responsible IS researc (Davison et al., 2018), ethical and unintended consequences of digitalisation and platformisation (Rossi et al., 2020) and digital technologies and social justice (Aanestad et al., 2021). While the decline could be ascribed, with Richard son and Robinson (2007), to factors discouraging submission of critical research to IS top journals, the paradox remains, especially at a time when the need for IS to engage issues of power and justice is widely expressed.

## 2.1.5 | Urgency of principles

Finally, the corpus reveals a trend of legitimising the rigour of critical research with principles, especially those b Myers and Klein (2011). The idea of establishing principles for conducting critical research was problematised b Avgerou (2005), who noted that methodological standards risked resulting in approaches that would endanger the emancipatory nature of critical research. Urgency of principles – even if these offer, as in Myers and Klein (2011 21), only one way to conduct critical IS research – can be seen in light of the need of legitimation of critical research enhanced by its limited recognition in IS (Chen & Hirschheim, 2004) and a perceived discouraging attitude from top journals (Richardson & Robinson, 2007). Avgerou's (2005) concern regarding the risk of loss of emancipation in criti cal IS acquires a new value in this time, when seen in the context of increased reliance of authors on principles for conducting critical research.

Such observations point to an increase in the themes and perceived urgency of critical IS research, yet accompa nied by a decline in the number of published papers and, significantly, continued prominence of Western perspec tives. Prevalence of the Western approaches by Bourdieu, Foucault and Habermas poses a concern of lack of pluralism in terms of provenance of theories, which is an especially serious problem in the light of the liberating and emancipatory mandate of critical IS research. With the urge for IS engagement with issues of power and justice, thi time is ideal to take the encouragement from Myers and Klein (2011) to explore perspectives that differ from the dominant Western ones.

## 2.2 | Decolonising IS: The limits of Western-centeredness

To understand the importance of moving beyond Western theories in critical IS research, we need to grasp the issue implicit in a Western-centric approach. Such issues are best illustrated through the experience of other fields, which over time have developed explicit decolonial efforts.

A core notion behind such efforts is that of epistemic violence, defined as a form of violence exerted both through knowledge and against it (Galvan-Alvarez, 2010). Epistemic violence operates through the systematic silencing of marginalised voices, and constitutes, as Mignolo (2013) notes, an integral part of processes of colonial domina tion. By giving authority to the voice of the coloniser, epistemic violence results in the forced devoicing of the colonised, producing systematic injustice against them (Milan & Treré, 2019). This results in the establishment of a narrative that erases the stories of the colonised, cancelling their perspectives from history.

Diverse academic fields have engaged epistemic violence. In anthropology, Harrison (2011) recognises the gene sis of the field as a Western ideological project , embedded in power relations that favour certain classes and penal ise other ones. The favoured classes are associated, Harrison (2011) notes, to groups in the world's white minority from this point of departure, she unveils racist implications in the old anthropological project, implications that risk ending up reinforcing the colonial control of the white minority (Magubane & Faris, 1985, cited in Harrison, 2011). Anthropology, Harrison (2011) concludes, needs to be explicitly tied with a project of liberation, to acknowledge the colonial roots of the field and make a conscious effort to combat them.

Harrison's (2011) liberating project is echoed across forums of contemporary anthropology. In putting forward the notion of an engaged anthropology , which takes responsibility for the anthropologists' actions on the field, Low and Merry (2010: 211) state the importance of decolonising relations between the researcher and research subject Failing to do so, Low and Merry (2010: 211) note, results in risk to harm the research subject: researchers need therefore, to act as ‘anthropological activists’ who are aware of the consequences of their work, as well as the dan gers of irresponsible approaches. The fundamental step of awareness, followed by others in anthropolog (cf. Jordan, 2011), is key to the project of liberation, which faces epistemic violence by problematising th researcher's role.

Similar calls have been made in organisation studies. In their work about research in violent contexts, Abdelnour and Abu Moghli (2021) make a call for political reflexivity, an approach inviting an ‘ethical, intellectua and personal’ (2021: 2) reflection on the positionality of researchers. Openly denouncing the harm that non reflexive researchers may cause through their work, Abdelnour and Abu Moghli (2021) articulate such a risk in terms of objectification of people and cultures, normalisation of violence and silencing of the marginalised. Echoing the liberation project of Harrison (2011), they theorise a decolonial continuum that ranges from complicity, that is research that is complicit with the coloniser, to reparation and liberation, enacted by researchers who, aware of violence that research has inflicted to the colonised (Smith, 2012; Wilson, 2003), engage an explicit project to repai on such violence.

It is in this space of decolonial liberation that works in the relatively recent field of critical data studies, a field centred on the study of data in their social and political contexts (Dalton & Thatcher, 2014), are inscribed. With th proposal of moving ‘beyond data universalism’, Milan & Treré (2019: 1) unveil epistemic injustice in a specific context: the narration of the data revolution from a solely Western perspective, which erases the consequences that data have had in the multiple Souths of the world. In Milan and Treré (2019: 2), the notion of the Souths is opposed to a ‘global South’ (cf. Pansera, 2018) that homogenises many realities, thus erasing their diversity. Telling stories of the ‘data revolution’ from the Souths of the world, Milan and Treré (2019) unveil realities of data-generated inequal ity and injustice, whose narration needs an openly decolonial project.

Taking part in such a project, Couldry and Mejias (2019) coin the notion of data colonialism, defined as a normalised ‘exploitation of human beings through data’ (Couldry & Mejias, 2019: 336). Based on the extraction of data from vulnerable users across the globe, ranging from mobile internet users in sub-Saharan Africa to migrant traversing European borders to escape war zones, Couldry and Mejias (2019) note how data extraction results in power consolidation for global firms, crucially offering the illusion of power to marginalised users. Building on their argument, Ricaurte (2019) notes the gender consequences of the problem: data colonialism, she argues, proceed along gendered lines, giving rise to intersectional dynamics of disempowerment through data extractive measures.

Fields as diverse as anthropology, organisation and critical data studies have shown the importance of decolonia efforts, to acknowledge epistemic violence and combat its insurgence. These efforts, and their pervasiveness in other fields, clarify the urgency of undertaking such work in IS. Many ways exist to engage a decolonial project in the IS field, and the one proposed here, centred on subaltern theory, is introduced as one possible route to decolonisation

## 3 | CORE CONCEPTS FROM SUBALTERN THEORY

Subaltern theory stems from the Subaltern Studies group, a collective of scholars of South Asia founded at the Uni versity of Sussex in the early 1980s (Guha & Spivak, 1988). The theory emerges in opposition to mainstream narrations of India's history, which the collective sees as omissive of the role of peasant insurgents in the transition to colonialism. In response they propose to rewrite ‘history from below’, voicing the peasants by telling history from their perspective (Guha, 1982; Guha & Spivak, 1988).

At the core of subaltern theory is a devoiced entity, the subaltern defined, with Gramsci (1971), by systematic exclusion from the hegemonic structure of society. In early writings. Guha (1988) positions the obiect of interest o Subaltern Studies squarely in the context of South Asian society:

[The word subaltern] will be used in these pages as a name for the general attribute of subordination in South Asian society whether this is expressed in terms of class, caste, age, gender and office or in any other way. (Guha, 1988: 35)

Taking the subaltern as the centre of attention, scholars from the Subaltern Studies group engage a programme of deconstructing historiography , detailed, in the homonymous essay by Spivak (1988), as bringing hegemonic his toriography into crisis by showing its cognitive failures. Such a programme centres on the traditional narrative referred to as elite narrative in subaltern scholarly writings, that views India's shift into colonialism as a transition from semi-feudalism to capitalism. Such a narrative, Spivak (1988) argues, is flawed with cognitive fallacy as it erase the role of peasant insurgents, whose confrontations with the elite have instead marked India's history:

The insertion of India into colonialism is generally defined as a change from semifeudalism into capitalist subjection. Such a definition theorizes the change within the great narrative of the modes of production and, by uneasy implication, within the narrative of the transition from feudalism to capitalism. (…) The Subaltern Studies group seems to me to be revising this general definition and its theorization by proposing at least two things: first, that the moment(s) of change be pluralized and plotted as confrontations rather than transition (they would thus be seen in relation to histories of domination and exploitation rather than in the great modes-of-production narrative) and, second, that such changes are signalled or marked by a functional change in sign-systems. (Spivak, 1988: 2)

Bringing hegemonic historiography into crisis involves identifying its structural shortcomings. In his descriptio of class relations in colonial India, Guha (1982) challenges the dominant view of peasants as blindly rebelling to loca landlords and British colonists, emphasising instead their will and ability to effect political change. In doing so, Guh sets the basis for the transition of the meaning of the term subaltern from the peasant insurgents to all those, in many ways, subordinated in South Asian society. Explicit objective of the subaltern deconstruction of historiograph is that of rectifying the elitist bias (Spivak, 1988: 5) that the dominant narrative displays, thereby making a fundamental step towards repairing the epistemic violence caused by such a narrative.

In narrating history from the viewpoint of the subaltern, scholars from the collective hence develop a vocabular that captures different aspects of subordination, as well as the agency of the subaltern in reversing it. Terms from Gramscian dialectics – hegemony as a process where ruling classes leverage cultural institutions to maintain their power, and the subaltern as those devoiced from hegemonic narratives – are adapted to the context of postcolonia South Asia, and appropriated in the narration of ‘history from below’ (Guha & Spivak, 1988) that constitutes the subaltern project. As noted in Landry and MacLean (1995), this narration is a paradoxical historical project, since ‘the documentary evidence is so one-sided that no positive, or positivist, account of subaltern insurgency is possible (1995: 203). It is in response to this one-sidedness that subaltern historiography is built.

An integral part of such a response is recognition of subaltern agency, in the multiple forms that it has acquired over time and social contexts. In her essay ‘Can the Subaltern Speak?’, Spivak (1985) details subaltern responses to hegemony, drawing on Guha (1982) to articulate peasant rebellion and its role in subverting the hegemonic structur of society. Over time, different forms of agency have emerged: in his analysis of a settlement along the railway track in Calcutta, Chatterjee (2004) notes the settlers' day-today negotiations at the margins of legality, built to carve a space of self-organisation to afford the construction of a political relationship with representatives of the state Faced with hegemony, the subaltern engages an agency that responds to it, emerging as an active subject that iden tifies the sources of oppression and leverages its own voice to challenge them.

## 4 | SUBALTERN THEORY IN IS PAPERS

We now turn to illustrating how subaltern theory can be used to illuminate IS phenomena, realising the theorisin and transformative mandate of critical theory. We do so with three papers that use different elements of subalter theory, choosing different paths to realising the deconstruction of historiography that the theory affords. Our choic of the papers, which focus on diverse thematic and geographic contexts, is due to their explicit use of concepts from subaltern theory, their focus on IS phenomena and their illustration of different parts of the subaltern conceptua apparatus.

Addo (2017) focuses on the case of TRADENET, Ghana's trade clearance system. The system, built to streamlin the functioning of customs, embodies the ideology of ‘trade, not aid’: based on the case, the paper unpacks a West ern rationality of development, based on commerce with other countries (as opposed to inflows of aid) and embod ied in the trade clearance technology. Drawing on core concepts from subaltern theory, the author proceeds to unmask the cognitive fallacy of such an ideology: Critical Discourse Analysis is used to voice narratives of actors grappling with the persistence of administrative issues that TRADENET was designed to address. Their stories silenced by an overarching development narrative, are brought to light by the paper.

The analysis conducted by Addo uses the concepts of subaltern and hegemony as adopted in Guha and Spivak (1988). Such concepts are instrumental to demystify the Western ideology of development as trade: identifying a hegemonic narrative from the government and international funders, he constructs the subaltern as actors whose stories, while having a stake in the system, remain untold. Addo uses Critical Discourse Analysis to operate, wit Spivak (1988), a deconstruction of historiography: he tells the story from the point of view of actors, including loca artisans and street-level bureaucrats (Addo, 2017: 578), whose stories contradict the narratives from the ruling pow ers. By doing so the paper unleashes the liberating intent of subalternity theory: silenced narratives are brought to light, creating the bases to emancipate actors who are purposefully devoiced by the ideology of development.

Addo's paper constitutes an effort towards reparation of epistemic violence. The voices silenced by the main stream narrative are not only those of actors with a stake in TRADENET. They are also, more broadly, the voice overlooked by the ideology of trade, not aid , a neoliberal ideology that equates development with the interest of a selected minority of people. Instead of empowering low-income people in Ghana to state what is best for them this ideology arbitrarily asserts trade as the route to development: Addo's work, illuminating voices beyond such an ideology, is arguably positioned in the repair and liberation end of the decolonial continuum theorised by Abdelnou and Abu Moghli (2021)

Lin et al. (2015) study an ICT4D project in a school in Taiwan, aimed at improving the education and social wel fare of disadvantaged indigenous youth. Presenting its theoretical apparatus as postcolonial, the paper recognises a mainstream discourse from the director of the ICT4D project and the elite around him, presenting a narrative o overwhelming success echoed by the national media. Drawing on notions of devoicing and subalternity, the pape then illuminates the completely different story narrated by the actors involved: the researcher encounters frustrate teachers and parents forced to play by the rules of the media, comply with an image of success and even squeeze teaching between the acts (e.g., a traditional dance performed for the local television) required for promotion of th project. Paternalistic practices by the ICT4D project director, claiming to know what is good for students, parent and teachers, further illustrate the devoicing they experience.

Through their critical ethnography of the project, the authors deconstruct the story of success narrated from th mainstream. It is the authors’ shift to a subaltern perspective that affords deconstructing the narration, allowing teachers and parents to tell the stories of disempowerment that are silenced in the media. Far from being only a too for education, the ICT4D project disciplines its users, forcing them to comply with the operation of projecting a pub lic image of success. The paper strongly illuminates the implications of paternalism: with his claim to knowledge of what is good, the project director reinforces his image of benevolent donor, ultimately strengthening the hegemon catered by the mainstream discourse.

Similarly to Addo (2017), Lin et al.'s critical ethnography aims to do justice to the devoiced. Their analysis has a distinctive intent: not only does it give students, parents and teachers in the study the voice that the principal took away from them, but it also illuminates perspectives that the ideology of technology for development does not con template. The idea of technology as inherently good for development – specifically for education of disadvantaged people, as the principal argues – is in itself problematised, by an ethnography that illuminates the limits and frustration lived by the beneficiaries of that superimposed ‘development’. Not only this means justice for the study respon dents, but it is an unmasking of ‘technology for development’ as a general development ideology.

Masiero (2018) focuses on the National Rural Employment Guarantee Scheme (NREGS), an Indian social protec tion scheme aimed at guaranteeing 100 days a year of employment to rural households who request it. Studying

NREGS in the state of Andhra Pradesh, the paper uncovers a non-dominant narrative: that of the tribal workers, found to be silenced by a programme designed not for a tribal, but for a rural context. The author's encounters with tribal respondents reveal multiple forms of devoicing: public works, which should be agreed upon in village meetings are seen as externally imposed, with outcomes adverse to tribals (e.g., irrigation works being prioritised to jungl clearance). Respondents also reveal that work measurements, on whose basis salaries are determined, do not tak into account the rocky soil of some tribal areas, making it impossible for excavation works to be conducted properly Overall, the narrative of a successful scheme, supported by statistics on the state, is found to amplify voices from the rural areas and omit those of the tribals.

The paper illuminates a hegemonic narrative, that of the success of the programme, questioned by the subalter narrative of tribal workers. The NREGS is a social protection scheme in which the state is encountered, as in Corbridge et al. (2005), in the form of the officials who work for it. In the context of this social protection scheme, the tribal narrative unfolds: with their requests ignored by the state, tribal workers cannot establish a dialogue wit it, remaining marginalised in the devoiced narratives that express their frustration. Illuminating their voices is the deconstruction of historiography made by the author, who uses subaltern theory to illuminate how tribals, silence by a hegemonic narrative, organise into groups and associations to resist the disempowering practices they suffer

Tribal groups in India have suffered decades of epistemic violence (Béteille, 1998). The use of subaltern theor in Masiero (2018) is again an attempt to repair to such a violence, putting her tribal interviewees at the centre of th narrative and building her work on their perspectives. The programme she studies has improved development statis tics across Indian states (Veeraraghavan, 2021): and still, success stories turn out to be omissive of populations for which the programme, with its rural focus, is not designed. Masiero's paper is, as a result, an attempt to use an IS per spective to repair to the systemic injustice that tribal communities in India have suffered.

Table 3 offers an interpretation of the three papers in terms of the core concepts of subaltern theory. Despit their different contexts, the papers have a common project: deconstructing the elite narratives of ideology of development (Addo), the ICT4D project leadership in Taiwan (Lin et al.) and social protection management in India (Masiero). Functional to deconstruction are concepts of the subaltern as systematically devoiced subjects and hege mony as intellectual and moral direction informing the commonly accepted narrative. Together, these concept enable the reconstruction of the subaltern from passive subjects to agents of resistance, enacting the liberating intent of critical theory.

## 5 | DECOLONISING CRITICAL IS RESEARCH THROUGHSUBALTERN THEORY

Subaltern theory is proposed here as a route to decolonisation of critical IS research. Having illustrated its key con cepts through three IS papers, we now position subaltern theory in the IS field at large. Such a positioning involve two steps: the qualifying of subaltern theory as an indigenous approach, specific to a context or culture and designe for it (Davison & Díaz Andrade, 2018), and reflection on how such an approach, challenging Western-centric para digms, can work towards decolonising the IS field.

## 5.1 | Subaltern theory as indigenous theory

In the IS field, indigenous approaches have been recently recognised as a route to overcoming Western bias. In the same paper that notes the Western-centeredness of theories used in the field, Davison and Díaz Andrade (2018: 760) define indigenous theory as ‘a theory of human behaviour or mind that is specific to a context or culture, not imported from other contexts/cultures and purposely designed for the people who live in that context or culture Indigenous approaches build on concepts and value systems that stem in particular contexts, problematising and overcoming the self-affirmed universality of Western theories. In a Special Issue of this journal (Davison, 2021), con tributions centred on Maori value systems (Díaz Andrade et al., 2021), on the Chinese concept of Qinghuai (Xiao et al., 2021) and on the pan-African philosophy of Ubuntu (Abubakre et al., 2021) have enriched IS knowledge beyond its traditional, Western-centric approaches

Subaltern theory qualifies as indigenous theory following the definition of Davison and Díaz Andrade (2018). It is (1) a theory of human behaviour or mind, (2) specific to a context or culture, not imported from other contexts cultures and (3) purposely designed for the people who live in that context or culture, with a contextual focus on postcolonial India. Inspiring the theory is the biased narration of Indian history from colonial sources, and their omissive attitude towards the role of peasant insurgents that works including Guha (1982), Guha and Spivak (1988 and Spivak (1985, 1988) bring to light. The context from which the theory arises is a context of institutionalised oppression, which denies voice to the insurgents that acted towards change and that is countered by the subalter conceptual apparatus.

Subaltern theory contributes, more at large, to an Indigenous Research Paradigm (IRP) as it is theorised in Wilso (2003). Such a paradigm, Wilson argues, indicates a system of thought centred on indigenous beliefs and ways of doing: more than developing a theory, it embeds a set of fundamental assumptions, constituting a scientific paradigm as it is conceived in Lincoln and Guba (2000). In open alternative to Western theoretical assumptions, illuminating the harm done by Western research on Aboriginal populations (Smith, 2012), Wilson articulates the IRP in terms of an Aboriginal system of thought to enable reparation to centuries of epistemic violence on indigenous communities Offering a vocabulary to articulate devoicing, and narrating the stories of the marginalised through their own voices subaltern theory strongly embodies the fundamentals of the IRP.

Such a paradigm is also reflected in contributions that, recognising Western bias in IS research, pursue liberatio of the field through indigenous theory. Contributions from Abubakre et al. (2021), Díaz Andrade et al. (2021) an Xiao et al. (2021) embody indigenous perspectives that question the ability of Western theories to explain contextua phenomena. More works across the fields of IS and ICT4D (cf. Jimenez & Roberts, 2019; Mawere & van Stam, 2019; Pérez-García, 2021; van Stam, 2019) elaborate on the limits of Western-centeredness: a field that neglects context specific theories, along with the richness of the concepts they offer, results in serious limitations to the knowledg produced in it. Other fields that, as illustrated earlier, have embraced decolonial efforts offer important examples of the knowledge that decolonial approaches bring (Escobar, 2018; Harrison, 2011; Mignolo, 2013)

In contributing to the IS landscape, subaltern theory hence enriches the knowledge brought to IS by other indig enous approaches. Delinking from Western knowledge and paradigms, subaltern theory provides the epistemic fun damentals to realise the emancipatory purpose of critical research.

## 5.2 | Decolonising IS: The subaltern alternative

Having illuminated the indigeneity of subaltern theory, the question arises on how a decolonial effort centred on it would function for critical IS research. In answering such a question there is, however, a risk of producing prescrip tive suggestions, with the consequence of constraining, as noted by Avgerou (2005), the emancipatory nature of critical research. Such a risk is even greater in the light of centuries of epistemic violence and harm inflicted on indigenous people by ‘research’ on them (Smith, 2012; Wilson, 2003). Following Smith's (2012) vision of decolonising methodologies, here we do not offer prescriptive principles to decolonise critical IS research, but w provide the incipit of what we hope will become an IS-wide reflection on the topic

IS research has traditionally engaged technology in its organisational and societal context (Avgerou, 2000). Doing so today is, however, different from the early days of the field: the focus on unequal and detrimental effects of ICT sometimes referred to as its dark side , has acquired increasing attention in recent IS research (cf. Rossi et al., 2020) This focus can inspire dialogue between IS and fields that, such as anthropology, organisation and critical data stud ies, have used decolonial approaches to study the inequalities produced by technology (Couldry & Mejias, 2019; Dalton & Thatcher, 2014; Milan & Treré, 2019). But in spite of a common interest, to the present day we still witnes limited engagement of IS research with critical approaches taken in other fields

Such a lack of dialogue is to be read in the light of the aforementioned decline in the number of critica papers published in top IS journals. On the one hand such a trend is reversible, for example through IS calls for papers that engage specifically with social justice, unintended and unethical effects of digitalisation (Aanestad et al., 2021; Rossi et al., 2020). But on the other the problem remains, possibly fuelled, as noted in Richardson and Robinson (2007), by a perceived discouraging attitude of top IS journals towards critical research. Paradig matically, all three papers used here to illustrate a subaltern approach come from ICT4D, an area of IS historicall concerned with power imbalances and where, therefore, a subaltern approach seems more in tune with the his tory of the field.

Our stance is that decolonial IS research should not be limited to ICT4D. Unequal effects of technology are not confined to what Milan and Treré (2019) refer to as the world's Souths': for example business organisations, educational systems, digital identity programmes have raised questions that IS research has dealt with by critica approaches (Kane et al., 2021; Kendall et al., 2020; Ngwenyama et al., 2021). Inequalities produced in these case demonstrate the importance of extending a decolonial vision to the IS field at large, not simply the ICT4D instances where subaltern theory is so far featured. If the IS field is to take seriously the commitment to combat epistemic vio lence, this is a mission to be engaged well beyond the traditional comfort zone of ICT4D

Three final remarks are in order, in terms of how subaltern theory could apply its liberating apparatus to themes of traditional IS. First, seeing the field in subaltern terms can enable an effective shift from the Western perspectives that characterise it so deeply, embracing visions that abandon the Western value systems of the field. An example i the recent work by Jimenez, Delgado, et al. (2022), which reconceives innovation from a Buen Vivir perspective and by doing so, problematises a conception of innovation as centred on growth, individualism and competition, taken for granted in mainstream IS. Similarly, Jimenez, Vannini, and Cox (2022) propose a decolonial approach to librar and information studies, noting how a holistic lens is necessary to deal with multiple sources of colonial oppression It is these conceptual, paradigmatic shifts that a subaltern theory-enabled decolonial approach can afford.

Second, subaltern theory can illuminate themes of sustainability that have become central to recent efforts of I research. A powerful example is the novel literature on Green IS, centred, note vom Brocke et al. (2013), on how innovative technologies can participate in the solution of pressing environmental problems. Subaltern perspective are key to illuminating the power relations underpinning ecological IS projects: in a recent work, Hoefsloot et al. (- forthcoming) study the metropolitan water observatory for Lima-Callao, where data about water resources are shared within the metropolitan city. Such data sharing practices, note Hoefsloot et al. (forthcoming), are at the basis of a fairer resource distribution, ultimately countering the structural inequalities that affected Lima's water infrastructure.

And finally, the vocabulary of subaltern theory can also illuminate stories of voicing and valorisation of mar ginalised voices. Along inequalities, the IS field also studies the reclaiming of indigenous voices, in particular that of communities that use ICTs to preserve cultural heritage (Chughtai et al., 2020; Young, 2018). These are stories that subaltern theory is well positioned to tell: stemming from contexts of devoicing and marginalisation, communities have been able to leverage ICTs for their own survival, combating hegemonic narratives and countering oppression on them. In this perspective, subaltern theory acts even more as a way to voice agency, leading to shifts from histori cally oppressive systems.

## 6 | CONCLUSION

With this paper we propose subalternity theory as an alternative to Western approaches, which can contribute essential concepts in the effort to decolonise critical theory in IS. The turn of the IS field to themes of power and justice presents, in this respect, an important occasion to explore approaches to examine such issues from the viewpoint of the subaltern. With its indigenous theorisation, subaltern theory offers a vocabulary to articulate and deconstruct hegemonic oppression, ultimately challenging a well-established Western hegemony in IS. If pluralism as argued since Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991), is core to building knowledge in IS, a substantial effort is needed for the field to embark on a direly needed decolonial project.

Critical research

Critical paradigm

Bourdieu

Foucault

Habermas

Postcolonial

Subaltern

• Our keyword search generated 86 results. We then checked each paper for relevance to critical research, following definitions by Myers (1997) and Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991). Of all papers, 74 were found to substantially engage critical IS research, which resized the corpus to these papers.

Being included in Special Issues dedicated to critical theory (8),
