# Responses to ambivalence toward social networking sites: A typological perspective

Ofir Turel<sup>1</sup> | Hamed Qahri-Saremi<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Computing and Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, Melbourne, Australia

<sup>2</sup>Computer Information Systems Department, College of Business, Colorado State University, Fort Collins, Colorado, USA

## Abstract

A common assumption in prior research on social networking sites (SNS) has been that users' orientations toward SNS use are positioned somewhere along a bipolar, univalent continuum, stretching from negative to positive orientation. However, considering recent findings unfolding the intricacy and variety of SNS use patterns, such a linear conceptualization of users' orientations is too simplistic with limited ability to explain the intricate patterns of SNS use. To alleviate this deficiency in this paper, we draw on the ambivalence literature and explain that users can simultaneously experience both positive and negative orientations toward SNS use based on the positive and negative aspects of their SNS use experience. Focusing on post- adoptive SNS use context, we theorise archetypes of SNS users' attitudinal responses to ambivalence, and their associated behavioural outcomes in terms of SNS use patterns. We first follow a typological perspective and develop typologies of attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence toward SNS use. Then, we offer six hypotheses that explain the relations between the archetypes of attitudinal responses to ambivalence toward SNS use and users' SNS use patterns. Lastly, we empirically test our hypotheses using latent profile analysis and ANCOVA applied to two-wave data collected from 370 ambivalent SNS users. The findings support the hypotheses and validate our typologies. The findings ultimately point to likely choices from a range of post-adoption SNS use patterns as plausible outcomes of SNS users' attitudinal responses to ambivalence.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

A common explicit or implicit assumption in prior research on social networking sites (SNS) has been that users' ori entations toward SNS use (i.e., beliefs and emotions) are positioned somewhere along a bipolar, univalent continuum, stretching from a negative to a positive orientation (Turel, 2015). As such, studies focusing on the benefits of SNS use often solely capture users' positive orientation, such as perceived enjoyment (Turel & Serenko, 2012), to explai their continued SNS use. In contrast. SNS studies focusing on the challenges and threats of SNS use predominantly emphasise users' negative orientation toward it, such as social overload (Lin et al., 2020), to explain their abstinenc from and abandoning of SNS use. Implicit in this linear conceptualization of users' orientations is the assumption that a positive orientation is the diametrically opposite of a negative orientation, and positive and negative orientations represent two opposite poles of the same dimension (i.e., univalence). As such, a user is assumed and expected to hold a single overall negative, neutral (middle), or positive orientation toward SNS use, which is determined based on a single point along a bipolar, univalent continuum of negative to positive orientations.

However, in light of recent findings unfolding the intricacy and diversity of SNS use patterns and the established fact that Information Systems (IS) use behaviours are functions of users' orientations toward IS use, such a linear conceptualization of SNS users' orientations is deemed too simplistic and unrefined to explain the diversity of th intricate patterns of SNS use. This conceptual deficiency begs the question of why users develop different patterns of SNS use if they indeed maintain relatively similar univalent orientations toward SNS use. For example. what moti vates users toward innovative SNS use (Oahri-Saremi & Turel. 2020) yersus routine SNS use (Pan et al., 2017) if they are both expected to be a function of users' positive orientation toward SNS use? Similarly, what instigates user toward permanent discontinuation of SNS use (Zhao et al., 2018; Vaghefi et al., 2020) if they both are expected to be a function of users' negative orientation toward SNS use? Likewise, why some users choose to remain rather passive with minimum activity on SNS, i.e., lurking (Kokkodis et al., 2020), but do not discontinue their SNS use? In order to properly respond to such questions, we need to (1) enrich our understanding of users' orientations towar SNS use, beyond an over-simplified and inadequate linear view, and (2) understand how they are linked to different patterns of SNS use.

To alleviate this deficiency, we draw on the ambivalence literature, and explain that users can simultaneously experience both positive and negative orientations toward SNS use based on the positive and negative aspects of their SNS use experience. This experience is manifested in a psychological state referred to as “ambivalence”<sup>1</sup> toward SNS use. Ambivalence embodies an uncomfortable experience of simultaneous oppositional orientations toward an object (Ashforth et al., 2014). This is a pivotal concept in the attempt to untangle users' response patter preferences based on mixed, bivariate orientations, like in the case of SNS use, because users' experience of ambiva lence can trigger typologically different attitudinal responses to mitigate its discomfort (Ashforth et al., 2014). These different attitudinal responses to ambivalence (i.e., mix of different levels of positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use) are conceptually aligned and theoretically associated with different patterns of SNS use.

While recent IS studies (e.g., Moody et al., 2017; Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020; Stein et al., 2015; Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2022) have initiated discussions on the triggers and consequences of ambivalence toward IS use, including SNS use (Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020; Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2022), a typological understanding of plausible SNS users' attitudinal responses to ambivalence and how these attitudinal responses can differentially influence users' patterns of SNS use is lacking. As a result, although recent studies have taken the first strides toward a more realistic conceptualization of users' orientations toward SNS use, these conceptual developments have not yet been systematically and theoretically linked to the intricacy and diversity of SNS use patterns. This gap needs to be rectified to enrich our understanding of how users' experience of SNS use can be made more fulfilling for them and more successful for the platform owners.

To that end, unlike recent IS research on ambivalence (e.g., Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020), in this paper, we wil adopt a bidimensional perspective of ambivalent users' attitudes toward SNS use, based on the coexistence of positive and negative attitudes, as two separate dimensions. This bidimensional view enables us to capture archetypes of users' attitudinal responses to ambivalence and identify the plausible SNS use patterns associated with them. To that end, we investigate the following research question: what are SNS users' plausible (i.e., likely) prototypical attitudina and behavioural responses to ambivalence toward SNS use? To investigate this research question, we first offer context-specific typologies of ambivalent users' attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence in the context of volitional SNS use.

We formulate six hypotheses that explain the relations between users' typological attitudinal responses to ambivalence toward SNS use and their typical intended behavioural responses (i.e., their associated patterns of SNS use). We test our typologies and hypotheses using latent profile analysis (LPA) and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA applied to two-wave data collected from 370 ambivalent SNS users. The findings support the hypotheses and vali date our typologies of prototypical attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence toward SNS use. They offe important insights for research and practice, which we will discuss at the end.

## 2 | CONCEPTUAL DEVELOPMENT

## 2.1 | Ambivalence toward SNS use

An individual will experience ambivalence when their thoughts and emotions about the object of ambivalence clash which results in simultaneous positive and negative orientations toward the same object (Ashforth et al., 2014) While the ambivalence notion shares similarities with other concepts that reflect discrepancy in one's thoughts and emotions, such as cognitive dissonance, it is conceptually unique and drives unique theoretical and practical insight (Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020). The distinctiveness of ambivalence notion is primarily rooted in its two attributes

1. The existence of oppositional positive and negative orientations toward the same object, and

2. The simultaneity of their existence. Therefore, the experience of ambivalence occurs when both positive and neg ative orientations about the same object coexist at the same time (Ashforth et al., 2014). In contrast, cognitive dis sonance, for example, occurs “when a person has two beliefs or items of knowledge that are not consistent wit each other (Kantola et al., 1984, p. 417) and both cannot be true at the same time (Mills, 1999). Unlike disso nance, ambivalence goes beyond a simple inconsistency in thoughts to manifest a direct opposition. For example users might have an expectation from their SNS use that is not confirmed (i.e., dissonance). While this reflects an inconsistency in users' thoughts about SNS use, it is different from simultaneously feeling happy (positive) and unhappy (negative) about SNS use (i.e., ambivalence). For a more detailed comparison of ambivalence with other concepts of discrepancy, highlighting the uniqueness of ambivalence notion and the need to study it, see Rothman et al. (2017) and Ashforth et al. (2014).

In this study, we focus on SNS use, because it is a commonly used information system (IS) that can be an object of ambivalence. This is because SNS use can harbour intense paradoxes for the users (Davis, 2012; Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020). These paradoxes can lead to simultaneous positive and negative orientations toward SNS use, manifested in users' salient experience of ambivalence (Mick & Fournier, 1998). Against this backdrop, we defin experience of ambivalence toward SNS use as a psychological state in which a user holds simultaneously oppositiona positive and negative (i.e., mixed) orientations toward SNS use. For example, individuals may perceive SNS use posi tively because of its hedonic and social benefits. However, at the same time, they may view it negatively due to being a drag on their productivity and time and a distraction for their work (Turel, 2015). In such circumstances, th coexistence of positive and negative orientations toward SNS use leads to an experience of ambivalence

Because the concept of ambivalence is relatively new in IS research (Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020), it is important to distinguish it from other apparently similar concepts that describe uncomfortable discrepancies, such as disconfirmation, cost-benefit analysis, dual-factor constructs, and threat/opportunity appraisal. Studying ambivalence toward SNS use is important, because the experience of ambivalence, even small levels of it, can be highly disturbing for SNS users. Failure to resolve ambivalence can make it worse and result in paralysis in decision-making and a sense of disequilibrium (Harrist, 2006). As such, ambivalent SNS users are intrinsically moti vated to resolve or at least reduce the salience of their experience of ambivalence. This manifests in differen responses to the experience of ambivalence toward SNS use, which is the focus of this paper.

Responses to experience of ambivalence are attitudinal and behavioural (Ashforth et al., 2014; Mick & Fournier, 1998) with SNS users' attitudinal responses to ambivalence shaping their behavioural responses (i.e., SN use patterns). Prior studies (e.g., Ashforth et al., 2014; Pratt & Doucet, 2000; Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020) have shed light on the plausible (i.e., likely) responses to ambivalence. Building on these findings, in this paper, we focus on ambivalent SNS users to (1) identify the typological attitudinal responses that these users may develop to mitigate their experience of ambivalence toward SNS use, and (2) elucidate the plausible behavioural outcomes of these typologica attitudinal responses in form of different intended patterns of SNS use. Consequently, this paper contextualises an expands our knowledge regarding users' prototypical attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence towar SNS use. ## 2.2 | Attitudinal responses to ambivalence toward SNS use

Responses to ambivalence are utilised by ambivalent users to avoid, evade, or resolve their experience of ambiva lence (Ashforth et al., 2014). Such responses can be manifested in the extent of focus and emphasis that people put on the two opposing aspects of the ambivalence object — the positive aspects and the negative aspects (Ashfort et al., 2014; Jonas et al., 2000; Pratt & Doucet, 2000). The focus on the positive aspects of ambivalence object determines the extent of positive attitude toward it, while the focus on the negative aspects of the ambivalence object determines the extent of negative attitude toward it (Ashforth et al., 2014; Pratt & Doucet, 2000). For example applied to ambivalent SNS users (i.e., users with at least some levels of positive and negative orientations toward th SNS use), some of them may strive to resolve the experience of ambivalence by suppressing negative reflections and putting more focus on the hedonic and utilitarian benefits of SNS use. Alternatively, other ambivalent SNS users ma respond to their experience of ambivalence by putting moderate focus on both positive and negative aspects of SNS use (i.e., partially suppressing both) and strive to find a middle ground between the two opposite sides as a means to preserve at least some of the positive aspects of SNS use while reducing the effects of major negative aspects of it.

Against this backdrop, Ashforth et al. (2014) have proposed a typology of responses to ambivalence, which provides a useful starting point in our effort to understand the archetypes of attitudinal responses to ambivalence toward SNS use. Rather than building conceptual archetypes of user attitudinal responses to ambivalence toward SNS use ab initio, we draw parallels between Ashforth et al.’s (2014) typology and related concepts and findings in the extant SNS use literature, as well as other pertinent classifications in the ambivalence literature (e.g., Pratt & Doucet, 2000) to offer context-specific description of a typology of five attitudinal responses to ambivalence toward SNS use: Ambivalence Avoidance, Negative Response Amplification, Positive Response Amplification, Concessions, and Holism. Each archetype of attitudinal response in this typology represents a combination of users' extent of concur rent positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use, at a given point in time (see Table 2).

Ambivalence Avoidance, in our typology, is an attitudinal response to ambivalence that is characterised by simul taneously low positive and low negative attitudes toward SNS use. Derived from the literature on psychology of denial and avoidance, Ambivalence Avoidance response aims at minimising the salience of or deny the existence of ambivalence to shield ambivalent users from the discomfort of dealing with it. To that end, the ambivalent SNS use often-consciously maintains a low focus on SNS use as the object of ambivalence. This low focus affords the ambiva lent SNS user to downplay the importance of SNS use, therefore minimises the salience of ambivalence toward it. Given that users' focus on the positive and negative aspects of SNS use determine their extent of positive an negative attitudes toward it, a low focus on SNS use is manifested in maintaining low positive and low negative atti tudes toward SNS use at the same time (Turel, 2015).

In the extant IS literature, Ambivalence Avoidance response is conceptually parallel to “apathy” response in Turel (2015), “apathy” and “neutral” responses in van Offenbeek et al. (2013), and “reluctant” response in Bhattacherjee et al. (2018). For example, Turel (2015) reported that 24% of his sample had simultaneously low continuance and discontinuance intentions toward Facebook use, representing their apathy (lack of focus). Whil users can be apathetic toward a system for various reasons, from the ambivalence perspective, it is a way of minimising the focus on the object of ambivalence, thereby reducing the discomfort of experience of ambivalence even if temporarily (Ashforth et al., 2014). Similarly, van Offenbeek et al. (2013) have identified “neutrality” and “apathy” as plausible users' responses toward an IS use that fall between support and resistance responses. Indeed they have shown the association between neutrality and passive resistance with low extent of IS use (low use) whic suggests users' lack of focus on the system. From the ambivalence perspective, this response can emancipate th ambivalent user from deciding between supporting and rejecting the IS use, which in turn reduces the discomfort of dealing with the ambivalence (Ashforth et al., 2014)

Response Amplification occurs when ambivalent SNS users maintain a high focus on one set of facets of SNS use and a low focus on the facets of opposite orientation (Katz & Glass, 1979). The main motivation underlying response amplification is reducing the salience of ambivalence that a user experiences. By putting more emphasis on one side of SNS use (positive or negative) and suppressing the other, the ambivalent user will be able to align their attitud and behaviours accordingly and reduce the salience of ambivalence, at least temporarily. Response Amplification can appear in two forms: Negative Response Amplification, where ambivalent SNS users choose to put more emphasis on negative facets of SNS use at the cost of its positive facets, leading to a stronger negative attitude and a weaker pos itive attitude toward SNS use; or Positive Response Amplification, where the ambivalent SNS user maintains hig focus on positive facets of the SNS use and low focus on its negative facets, resulting in a stronger positive attitud and a weaker negative attitude toward SNS use

Response Amplification responses often use rationalisation processes, where ambivalent SNS users convinc themselves that the positive or negative side of SNS use (whichever that is more salient to them) is superior to th opposite side, or they trivialise and suppress one side of SNS use (whichever that is less salient to them) until th opposing side can be easily enacted upon (van Harreveld et al., 2009). Either way, the salient side become highlighted, and the less salient side will be trivialised, and this lowers the salience of ambivalence for SNS users, at least temporarily. Moreover, negative response amplification can be a result of “negativity bias” (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2022), where an ambivalent user puts more weight on negative aspects of SNS use as compared to it positive aspects.

For example, some ambivalent SNS users may consciously put more emphasis on beneficial affordances of SN use such as its “relationship formation” and “content sharing” (Karahanna et al., 2018), and discount their concerns about the amount of time and attention that they are investing in SNS use and their psychological dependence on it. This is a context-specific example of Positive Response Amplification that can reduce the salience of ambivalence toward SNS use for these users, at least temporarily, until they cannot trivialise the negative aspects of SNS use any more (e.g., when it becomes more problematic for them). Alternatively, other ambivalent SNS users may consciousl focus on concerns about SNS's drag on their productivity and their psychological dependence on it and trivialise th benefits of its relation formation and content sharing affordances. This would represent a context-specific exam ple of Negative Response Amplification which can similarly reduce the salience of ambivalence toward SNS use fo these users, at least temporarily, until they cannot ignore the positive aspects of SNS use for them (e.g., they fee they need to be active on SNS to build and maintain relation with their social network). Although both of these atti tudinal responses can mitigate the salience of ambivalence for SNS users, they can lead to markedly different behav ioural responses to ambivalence toward SNS use, which we will discuss later in this paper. This highlights the need for the proposed typology and theorising about outcomes of different archetypal attitudinal responses to ambiva lence toward SNS use.

It is noteworthy that response amplification responses do not permanently dissipate all the conflicting orienta tions toward SNS use. Rather, they only enable the ambivalent SNS user to focus on one aspect of SNS use—positiv or negative attitudes—over the other (Harrist, 2006). As a result, although trivial, some extent of the opposing atti tude toward SNS use may always remain in users' memory. In the extant SNS literature, positive and negative response amplifications are conceptually parallel to “growing readiness to continue” and “growing readiness to discontinue” responses in Turel (2015), respectively. Similarly, in the IT use literature, “positive response amplifica tion” is conceptually close to Bhattacherjee et al.’s (2018) “compliant” response.

Concessions reflect moderate foci on positive and negative facets of SNS use, resulting in ambivalent users' simul taneously moderate positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use. This response involves purposeful reciproca compromises between the opposing (positive and negative) facets of SNS use in order to mutually accommodat them (Ashforth et al., 2014). Unlike in Ambivalence Avoidance or Response Amplification responses, in Concessions neither of the opposing facets are completely trivialised; rather, a trade-off between the opposing facets of SNS us is sought. This attitudinal response is aimed at reducing the tension between the opposing facets by partially or alternately satisfying them. In the extant IS literature, Concessions are conceptually parallel to exercise discretion response in Stein et al. (2015) where ambivalent IS users appraise the balance between instrumental constraint and affordances … and respond with a mixture of satisfaction and frustration (p. 382). It ultimately manifests in psychological response that balances between benefits maximising and self- preservation toward the IS use. Concessions can appear in two forms: Partial Concessions and Alternate Concessions.

Partial Concessions involve finding a middle ground between the positive and negative facets of SNS use where the ambivalence can be reduced. This approach can be effective when focusing on neither positive, nor negative facet alone is effective or feasible for addressing the ambivalence toward SNS use (Ashforth et al., 2014). The middle ground is not completely true to either of the opposing facets; instead it represents an area of possible agreement between the two opposing facets where each of them can be partially satisfied (Ashforth et al., 2014).

Reaching this compromise requires concessions on both sides. As an illustration, ambivalent SNS users may respond to their ambivalence between positive facets of SNS use, for example its meta-voicing , collaboration “relationship formation”, or “content sharing” affordances (Karahanna et al., 2018), and its negative facets, for example the feelings of envy (Krasnova et al., 2015) and loss of freedom (Qahri-Saremi et al., 2021) due to SNS use, b slightly abating their focus on both positive and negative facets of SNS use. This may ultimately lead to behavioura responses such as devising limits on how much and for what purpose an SNS is used. This represents a contextspecific example of a middle ground between a complete focus on positive facets or negative facets. Nevertheless this middle ground approach will not be able to fully satisfy ambivalent users' benefits from SNS use, nor can it preserve them from all its negative aspects (they may still feel envious, but to a lesser extent). A case in point is the SN use reduction that is rooted in users' realisation of negative aspects of addictive use of SNS and their “rosy view” of the positive aspects of their over-reliance on SNS use (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020). Osatuyi and Turel (2020) have argued that a balance between these two views drives SNS use reduction. The balance between the two views is a middl ground where the users' realisation of negative aspects of SNS use is strong enough to justify self-regulation of SN use, but it is not sufficiently strong to justify a permanent abandonment of SNS use where the users had to completely give up on their perceived benefits of SNS use. This middle ground between positive and negative facet of SNS use is what we refer to as Partial Concessions response because it is not fully consistent with either the posi tive or negative orientations toward the SNS use, rather it is achieved due to partial compromises on both sides

Alternate Concessions involve temporal alternations between the opposing facets that enable individuals to tem porarily respect the core of each facet of SNS use in a vacillating fashion while keeping an overall moderate-leve focus on both facets over time (Stein et al., 2015). Alternate Concessions response targets the simultaneous presence of positive and negative facets and temporally splits the focus on them in order to reduce their oppositiona pulls (Pratt & Doucet, 2000). It can be effective when the ambivalent user has moderate foci on both positive and negative facets of SNS use, but the core of each of the opposing facets should be preserved (Ashforth et al., 2014) In such cases, neither of the opposing facets can be partially conceded for the benefit of a middle ground, instead, an effective response is in the form of vacillation between choices that put emphasis on opposing facets. For example students who are ambivalent toward their SNS use may address their ambivalence by alternating between emphasising the positive and negative aspects of SNS use, and correspondingly using and not- using SNS, depending on the situation. For instance, they may abstain from SNS use during busy study periods (e.g., final exams), but later restart using SNS routinely to benefit from its favourable affordances. As an example in the broader IS literature

Alternate Concessions response has been demonstrated in findings of Stein et al. (2015), where users of an organisational IS (i.e., a faculty productivity (FP) system) attend to both the positive and negative aspects of the IS instrumentality and “waver between emphasising the positive and negative aspects of the complex stimulu (FP instrumentality)” (p. 382). This response is realised when users temporally split their focus on positive and nega tive aspects of the IS use depending on the situation, which is at the core of “Alternate Concessions” response in our framework.

Lastly, Holism is characterised by maintaining high foci on both positive and negative facets of SNS use, leadin to simultaneously high positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use. It indicates SNS users' complete and simultaneous acceptance of both positive and negative facets of SNS use (Turel, 2015). As such, it is different from Negative / Positive Response Amplification, where one of the opposing facets is sidelined for the benefit of the other facet. It is also different from Ambivalence Avoidance response, where the ambivalence is avoided by marginalising the importance of SNS use. Holism is also distinct from Concessions where both opposing facets are compromised fo the sake of a middle ground or trade-offs. Due to the high intensity of opposing attitudes involved in it, Holism response is difficult to maintain (Ashforth et al., 2014). As a result, its effectiveness is contingent on the ambivalent users' adaptability such that they can wholly accept and accommodate both opposing attitudes at the same time

Therefore, not surprisingly, the evidence of holism responses in IS research is sparce. A rare parallel notion to the holism response in extant IS research is “ambivalence” response described in Turel (2015), shown in 7% of his sample of Facebook users: “This can happen when the use of an IS is habituated and rewarding, and thus, continuance motivation in the short-run exists. At the same time, when the habituated use produces negative consequences and is possibly harming, users may also contemplate discontinuance [motivation]” (p. 433). Despite its difficulty Holism response has been associated with acceptance (Wu, 2019), mindfulness (Ashforth et al., 2014) and creativit (Fong, 2006) and viewed as a reason for positive effects of ambivalence (Rothman et al., 2017)

In sum, these five attitudinal responses to ambivalence present a typology of plausible ambivalent users' foci on positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use as they contemplate how to respond to their experience of ambivalence. Some ambivalent users may decide to avoid dealing with ambivalence experience (i.e., ambivalence avoidance) others may choose to put stronger focus on one side, the positive or negative facet of SNS use (positive/negativ response amplifications) to reduce ambivalence, other users may maintain moderate foci on both positive and nega tive facets of SNS use (i.e., partial, and alternate concessions) which would afford them some flexibilities that can reduce their ambivalence experience, yet a group of users may maintain simultaneously high foci on both positive and negative facets of SNS use (i.e., holism) which would require them to juxtapose their strong positive and nega tive attitudes and convert it into a win–win opportunity, such as an innovative way of SNS use, which could fully sat isfy their positive and negative attitudes.

The influence of users' attitudes on behaviours is an established fact (Wixom & Todd, 2005) that is rooted in classic planned behaviour and reasoned action theories (Ajzen, 1991). On this basis and prior ambivalence researc (e.g., Mick & Fournier, 1998), we argue that SNS users' attitudinal responses to their experience of ambivalence can influence their behavioural responses in terms of their patterns of SNS use (Figure 1). Specifically, ambivalent SNS users are expected to adopt different SNS use patterns consistent with their attitudinal responses to ambivalence To better understand this, we present a typology of plausible SNS use patterns in response to ambivalence, in th next section.

## 2.3 | Behavioural responses to ambivalence toward SNS use

Mick and Fournier (1998) have proposed a typology of behavioural coping strategies in response to ambivalence toward technological products, which provides a useful conceptual foundation for us to develop the different possi ble archetypes of behavioural responses to ambivalence toward SNS use. Mick and Fournier (1998) hav differentiated users' behavioural coping strategies based on the users' technology adoption stage: pre-acquisition and consumption stages

Similar to the extant literature on stages of IS adoption process (Karahanna et al., 1999, Montazemi & Qahri Saremi, 2015), the “pre-acquisition” coping responses in Mick and Fournier (1998) refer to behavioural responses to ambivalence in pre-adoption stage of technology use, and “consumption” coping responses in Mick and Fournier (1998) describe the typological behavioural responses to ambivalence in post- adoption stage of technology use. Our focus in this paper is on ambivalent SNS users and their responses to their experience of ambivalence as a result of their SNS use. As such, we extend and contextualise Mick and Fournier's (1998) typology of behavioural coping strategies in consumption stage of technology use to the SNS use context by drawing parallels with patterns of SN use discussed in the extant literature. To that end, we conducted a systematic review of different SNS use pattern discussed in the AIS Senior Scholars' Basket of Eight journals<sup>2</sup> (see Appendix A in S1). By juxtaposing Mick and Fournier's (1998) typology of behavioural coping strategies in consumption stage and our literature review findings we identified five archetypes of SNS use patterns in response to ambivalence (see Table 3): Neglecting, Abandoning Partnering, Rule-based Distancing, and Adapting.

Neglecting behaviour is driven by a sense of indifference toward SNS use. By neglecting the SNS use, ambivalent users strive to avoid the experience of ambivalence toward it. In such cases, the SNS user can be passive and apa thetic, which can manifest in simultaneously low continuance and discontinuance intentions toward SNS use, wher users are reluctant to engage with SNS and are not motivated to permanently discontinue its use either (Turel, 2015). An example of neglecting behaviour is inactive SNS use, where the users have little to lose b maintaining their SNS account, but also little incentive to engage more extensively with the SNS. This resemble “lurking” pattern of SNS use (Zhao et al., 2018), which happens when users become indifferent toward SNS use and, as a result, reduce their login frequency, usage time, and regularity of use, but stop short of indefinite deactivation of the SNS account.

Abandoning refers to attempts and intentions to permanently terminate SNS use, such as through indefinit deactivation of one's SNS account (Turel, 2015). This is a common behavioural response among some ambivalent users of technological products (Mick & Fournier, 1998), who put more focus on the negative aspects of technolog relative to its positive aspects. In the SNS use literature, abandoning has been operationalised in form of SNS discon tinuance intentions and attempts (also referred to as “exodus”) (Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020; Turel, 2015; Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2022; Zhao et al., 2018) as well as switching from one SNS platform to a rival one (Maier et al., 2015 Xu et al., 2014).

Partnering is another archetype of behavioural responses to ambivalence where, unlike neglecting and abandoning, users try to establish a routine and sustained relationship with the SNS. By building a trusting relationship with the technology, users strive to accept it and embed its use in their life routines and suppress ambivalence toward it (Mick & Fournier, 1998). Mick and Fournier (1998) have mentioned several examples of partnering with different technological products in response to ambivalence. In the SNS use literature, partnering behaviours are operationalised as intentions and attempts aimed at routine, habitual, regular, and heavy use of SNS (Chiu & Huang, 2015; Hu et al., 2015; Kokkodis et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2017). Similarly, Vaast and Pinsonneault (2022 describe a similar behaviour as “sheltering” defined as “[i]ntensifying one's participation in particular social media contexts to protect from difficulties associated with participating in other contexts” (p. 15). Nevertheless, while par tnering behavioural response, such as routine use, are intentional and purposeful, they often include minimal to no innovation with or adaptation of the system (Bhattacherjee et al., 2018).

Rule-based Distancing is an archetype of behavioural responses to ambivalence where users try to keep their interactions with the system within bounds in order to keep their experience of ambivalence under control (Mick & Fournier, 1998). As such, it describes an active, but controlled pattern of SNS use such that while continued us clearly exists, there are rule-based restrictions that control when the SNS will or will not be used and for what pur poses (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020; Zhou et al., 2018). This way, rule-based distancing enables SNS users to reduce th negative aspects of SNS use while maintaining their active user status (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020). In the SNS literature rule-based distancing behaviour has been described in different forms including temporary breaks (abstinence) from SNS use (i.e., intermittent, temporary discontinuance) as well as SNS use reduction and vacillation (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020; Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020; Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2022; Zhou et al., 2018). An example is what Vaast and Pinsonneault (2022) refer to as “retreating from multiple social media” tactic which involves “reducing temporarily the engagement with diverse social media contexts to refocus on key work-related context(s) (p. 15). Rule-based distancing behaviours involve maintaining active and continued SNS use, while devising rules that determine whe and how the SNS is used.

Lastly, Adapting typifies a behavioural response that involves adaptation of SNS use and the users' tasks in a way that increase the fit and synergy between them. This can be realised in form of adapting users' tasks and pro cesses to benefit from SNS use, mastering the SNS features to accommodate users' needs and tasks, or a mix of both. As such, adapting behavioural responses can involve changing tendencies, preferences, routines, and tasks to accommodate perceived requirements and features of a system (referred to as “accommodation” in Mick & Fournier, 1998) and/or mastering system's features and requirements to modify them based on the tasks in hand (referred to as “mastering” in Mick & Fournier, 1998). In the SNS use literature, adapting has been reflected in descriptions of innovative, extended, and varied patterns of SNS use (Pan et al., 2017; Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020) which may be employed by users to navigate their path to avoid negative aspects, while maintaining the positive aspects of SNS use. “Supporting off-line contexts” in dealing with social media contexts described in Vaast and

Pinsonneault (2022) is an additional case in point. Via “supporting off-line contexts”, users engage with SNS “to support small, local contexts in which to share and develop relationships on the basis of shared professional interests and geographical proximity” (Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2022, p. 15). This adaptive use of SNS enabled users to avoid it negative implications from the engagement in other broader contexts, while maintaining its benefits for developin opportunities to learn from and exchange with other professionals on SNS (Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2022).

So far, we have described the typologies of (1) attitudinal and (2) behavioural responses to ambivalence toward SNS use. In the next section, we integrate them by formulating six hypotheses that illuminate how different arche types of attitudinal responses to ambivalence toward SNS use can change the plausibility of behavioural responses to ambivalence toward SNS use (see illustration in Figure 2). This would allow us to explain from a holistic theoretica perspective why ambivalent SNS users may adopt different SNS use patterns.

## 3.2 | Negative response amplification & abandoning

Ambivalent SNS users who engage in Negative Response Amplification toward their ambivalence amplify their focus on negative aspects and suppress their focus on positive aspects of SNS use, which results in having high negative and low positive attitudes toward SNS use. An archetype of SNS use behaviour in our typology that is associate with this attitudinal response is “abandoning”. Abandoning entails ambivalent users' attempts and intentions to dis continue their SNS use indefinitely, which are motivated by a potent negative attitude coupled with a weak positive attitude toward SNS use. As such, ambivalent SNS users who engage in Negative Response Amplification are expected to show stronger abandoning behavioural response than others. In support of this view, it has been show that despite some levels of positive attitude toward Facebook use, users can simultaneously have strong negative attitudes toward it and when this negative attitude dominants, it can drive discontinuance intention (Turel, 2015) Abandoning is a less attractive behaviour for realising other attitudinal responses to ambivalence. For instance, whe the positive attitude level is medium or high, abandoning will not cater to it and can only partially serve users' atti tudes $( \mathfrak { i . e . }$ , only a strong negative attitude). Moreover, when the negative attitude is weak, abandoning is less likel to be a justified choice for a user. On this basis, we hypothesize:

H2. Ambivalent SNS users with Negative Response Amplification attitudinal response toward SNS use are more likely than other ambivalent SNS users to show abandoning behavioural response toward SNS use

## 3.3 | Positive response amplification & partnering

In cases of Positive Response Amplification, we contend that although ambivalent SNS users are aware of the negative aspects of their SNS use, they still consciously or subconsciously choose to put more emphasis on the positive facets of SNS use, as a mechanism for mitigating their ambivalence toward it. This will result in amplified positiv attitude and weakened negative attitude toward SNS use. We contend that the SNS use pattern in our typology that is the most consistent with Positive Response Amplification, i.e., that best caters to the attitudinal state that emerge from this response, is “partnering”.

Partnering behavioural response entails developing a committed relation and a routine use of paradoxical tech nology (Mick & Fournier, 1998). Indeed, it has been shown that despite the negative consequences of SNS use of which users are cognizant, some users still choose to put more focus on the positive aspects of it, such as its relatio formation and content sharing affordances, and continue the SNS use routinely (Cheung et al., 2011; Rainie, 2018)

This is because the weakened negative attitude and the amplified positive attitude in response to ambivalence can lead to “the perfect storm” for stronger partnership with SNS use. For these ambivalent SNS users, partnering wit SNS provides an escape, even if temporarily, from dealing with ambivalence, by downplaying the negative aspects and keep the focus on the positive aspects. Downplaying the negative aspects of SNS use and embracing its positiv aspects also reduces users' motivation toward modifying their SNS use. Therefore, these users are expected to use the SNS routinely, without much adaptation. Partnering is a less plausible behaviour for realising other attitudina responses to ambivalence. For example, when the negative attitude level is medium or high (i.e., Negative Response Amplification, Concessions, and Holism), partnering is a less attractive solution as it will not serve users' negative atti tudes. Moreover, in case of a weak positive attitude (e.g., Ambivalence Avoidance), ambivalent SNS users will lack the motivation for partnering with SNS. On this basis, we posit:

H3. Ambivalent SNS users with Positive Response Amplification attitudinal response toward SNS use are more likely than other ambivalent SNS users to show partnering behavioural response toward SNS use.

## 3.4 | Concessions & rule-based distancing

Recall from our Conceptual Development section that Concessions are characterised by medium-level positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use. From a behavioural perspective, Concessions involve compromises on both sides of SNS use — partnering with SNS on the one side, that is compatible with strong positive and weak negative atti tudes, and abandoning SNS use on the other side, that is compatible with strong negative and weak positive atti tudes. An archetype of behavioural response that is the most compatible with this notion is “rule-based distancing”.

Rule-based distancing falls between partnering, which involves the routine use of SNS, and abandoning, whic involves indefinite discontinuance of SNS use. In rule-based distancing, the ambivalent SNS user will remain an active user, with planned (rule-based) restrictions that control when and how the SNS will or will not be used (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020; Zhou et al., 2018). This behavioural response can only be enacted when users maintai medium levels of positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use. The reason is that strong positive or negative atti tudes toward SNS use could hamper any behavioural response that involves compromises of those aspects of SN use, while weak positive or negative attitudes would reduce motivation to actively plan and execute rules to pre serve those aspects of SNS use. As such, rule-based distancing appears to be the most compatible behaviour with the Concessions attitudinal response. Nevertheless, the specific way in which SNS users operationalise the rule based distancing behaviour seems to depend on the type of Concessions they adopt in response to ambivalenc toward SNS use: Partial Concessions or Alternate Concessions .

Partial Concessions involve finding an area of possible agreement between the two opposing attitudes where both sides are partially satisfied at the same time (Ashforth et al., 2014). On this basis, we contend that a rule-base distancing behaviour that is compatible with Partial Concessions is reduced SNS use. The reason is that, first, reduced SNS use captures users' attempts to control the SNS use such that while the continued use is maintained, the extent and condition of use is restricted to control the negative aspects of SNS use (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020). As opposed to abandoning that focuses on indefinite discontinuance of SNS use, reduced SNS use is less extreme and meets both the continuance and discontinuance behaviours halfway (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020). Therefore, reduced SNS use typ ifies a middle ground that partially caters to both opposing attitudes toward SNS use, making it a reasonable behavioural response associated with Partial Concessions attitudinal response to ambivalence. Second, reduced SNS use i a conscious and planned (rule-based) behaviour focusing on continued SNS use while maintaining a “safe distance’ from it to mitigate its negative aspects (e.g., psychological dependence on SNS use). As such, it is conceptuall aligned with rule-based distancing archetype in our typology of behaviours. Reduced SNS use is a less plausible behaviour for other attitudinal responses where a middle ground is hard to find (e.g., when the foci on positive and negative attitudes are too high), or is not needed (e.g., when the positive or negative attitudes or both are too low) On this basis, we hypothesize:

H4a. Ambivalent SNS users with Concessions attitudinal response toward SNS use are more likely than other ambivalent SNS users to show rule-based distancing behavioural response in form of reduced SNS use

As another form of Concessions response, Alternate Concessions involve temporal alternations between the opposing attitudes that enable ambivalent SNS users to respect the opposing facets of the SNS use wholly, but alter nately (i.e., not concurrently). On this basis, we contend that a rule-based distancing behaviour that is compatibl with Alternate Concessions is taking temporary breaks from SNS use when the negative attitudes dominate, and the going back to SNS use afterwards when the positive attitudes dominate, until the negative attitudes become domi nant again (Zhou et al., 2018). Our reasons are twofold.

First, taking temporary breaks from SNS use involves planned, and calculated alternations between using and not using the SNS depending on the situation. This way, the ambivalent SNS user can maintain their active user status, while maintaining a rule-based distance from SNS use when one needs to control its negative aspects. Second, taking temporary breaks from SNS use requires an alternate attention to the opposing facets of the SNS use, which is at the core of Alternate Concessions attitudinal response. While both reduced SNS use and taking temporary breaks from SNS use are forms of rule-based distancing behaviour, they have one main difference. Reduced SNS use is a stable, non-interrupted use pattern, whereas taking temporary breaks from SNS use is a dynamic (vacillating) pat tern of use based on the situation (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020). Depending on the strength of negative or positive atti tudes toward SNS use in a particular situation (e.g., during the exam week for students), an ambivalent SNS user may choose to temporarily continue or stop SNS use (Osatuyi & Turel, 2020). Taking temporary breaks from SNS use i less effective when there are strong positive or negative attitudes (i.e., Response Amplifications and Holism) that hamper the alternations. It is also inconsistent with users' needs when the positive and negative attitudes are both low (i.e., Ambivalence Avoidance). Thus, we hypothesize:

H4b. Ambivalent SNS users with Concessions attitudinal response toward SNS use are more likely than other ambivalent SNS users to show rule-based distancing behavioural response in form of temporary breaks from SNS use.

## 3.5 | Holism & adapting

Holism attitudinal response is characterised by maintaining simultaneously high positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use. Its effectiveness in addressing the experience of ambivalence typically revolves around the adapta tion of SNS use and the tasks in hand to create a synergy between them. As such. Holism attitudinal response is con: ceptually related to taking rational and innovative actions to adapt oneself to the ambivalence circumstances (McCrae, 1984). On this basis, we contend that a behavioural response in our typology that entails the adaptation of task and SNS use and has the potential to cater to high positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use (Holism) i adapting. Adapting involves improvising users' tasks and routines to accommodate perceived requirements and fea tures of the SNS platform and/or mastering the SNS platform features and affordances to accommodate the tasks in hand. Workarounds are an example of adaptation of one's computer mediated tasks and routines (Azad & King, 2008).

To illustrate in the context of SNS use, consider students who are ambivalent toward SNS use: seeing it helpfu for forming and maintaining social relationships (i.e., positive attitude), but also harmful for being a distraction and a drag on their academic performance (i.e., negative attitude). These ambivalent students may respond by seeking a synergy between SNS use and their academic needs as a way to accommodate both attitudes. This can be realised by relying on specific affordances of SNS use beyond its original hedonic purposes, such as “collaboration” (Karahanna et al., 2018), for sharing homework ideas and solutions with others, asking questions about the exams, consulting with senior students about which courses to take in each semester, obtaining advice from externa sources, and building a professional network on SNS. This use pattern is innovative and adaptive because it deviate from the common hedonic use of SNS. As such, we expect the adapting behaviour to be more likely when the foc on opposing facets of the SNS use are simultaneously high. This scenario is less optimal for Positive / Negativ Response Amplification, Ambivalence Avoidance, and Concessions responses, because all of these responses do not address the existing high negative and positive attitudes. On this basis, we hypothesize:

H5. Ambivalent SNS users with Holism attitudinal response toward SNS use are more likely than other ambivalent SNS users to show adapting behavioural response toward SNS use.

## 4 | METHODOLOGY

## 4.1 | Procedure and sample

A two-wave online survey was administered to 476 SNS users who were undergraduate students at a large North American university, out of which 392 (82%) provided complete responses. Participants were given two bonus points in exchange for their time. Criteria for being included in our sample consisted of being at least 18 years old, being a SNS user for at least 1 year, and experiencing ambivalence toward the SNS use. Based on these criteria, we excluded 22 respondents who reported zero level of ambivalence toward their SNS use<sup>4</sup>. This reduced our sample size to 370 for our analyses, including 52% women and 48% men, with average age of 23.4 (SD 3.78), and more tha 5 years of SNS use experience (SD 2.46), on average. Participants were active on 4.3 (SD 1.93) SNSs, on aver age, with Facebook being the most popular SNS among them (96.8% of respondents were Facebook users). In the survey, the respondents were asked to only focus on their most frequently used SNS. In the analyses, we controlled for respondents' choice of most frequent SNS and their extent of SNS use experience.

We made this sampling choice for two reasons. First, SNS use is widespread among university students (Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016, 2018). Second, university students are prone to developing problematic use of SNS due to thei flexible schedules and free time, little external control over their Internet use (e.g., no parental or organisational con trol), and their habituated use of SNS for developing their identities and social networks (Wegmann et al., 2020, Far ivar et al., 2018, Turel & Bechara, 2017). Therefore, they are likely to have a mix of positive and negative attitude toward SNS use (Turel, 2015).

We collected the data in two waves (t1 and t2) for two reasons. First, to reduce common method bias in our data. Second, to reduce the possibility of carry-over effects between the measurement of ambivalence, positiv and negative attitudes, and the SNS use patterns. A one- week time separation between t1 and t2 was implemente because it is not too short to allow carry-over effects between measures of t1 and t2, nor is it not too long such tha the respondents forget or lose interest in the study (Turel, 2015; Turel & Qahri-Saremi, 2016). This is particularl important in our study because ambivalence is a psychological state which might change over longer periods of time

## 4.2 | Operationalization and measures

We operationalised the attitudinal responses to ambivalence in terms of combination of ambivalent SNS users extent of positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use (see Table 2): (1) simultaneously low positive and negativ attitudes for Ambivalence Avoidance, (2) high negative and low positive attitudes for Negative Respons

Amplification, (3) high positive and low negative attitudes for Positive Response Amplification, (4) simultaneousl medium positive and negative attitudes for Concessions, and (5) simultaneously high positive and negative attitudes for Holism.

Moreover, drawing on our SNS use literature review (Appendix A in S1) and the ambivalence literature (Mick & Fournier, 1998), we used different intended patterns of SNS use for operationalising the behavioural responses to ambivalence. Two points are noteworthy in this regard. First, in our operationalizations, we focus on the users intended SNS use patterns. The reason for this choice is that SNS users might face more challenges and potentia impediments in implementing some behaviours, such as adapting and abandoning, as compared to others, such a partnering. These potential impediments and their intensity depend on contextual and individual factors specific to user (e.g., how easily users can adapt SNS use can partly depend on their innovativeness, and how SNS use can b adapted to the context of their work (Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2022). To reduce the effects of such potential impedi ments on our tests, we focus on users' intentions, which are strong predictors of behaviours as well as proximal con sequences of attitudes. Second, sometimes different SNS use factors discussed in the extant literature (e.g., routine use, habitual use) are conceptually consistent with one behavioural response to ambivalence (e.g., partnering) (Pan et al., 2017). In such situations, we draw on the most common factor in the extant literature for operationalising that behavioural response. The most common factor for a behavioural response was determined based on the number of SNS use studies that have used that factor (as per our literature review) as well as how established that factor is i the general IS use literature.

On this basis, we employ discontinuance intention for operationalising abandoning, routine use intention for operationalising partnering, reduced SNS use intention and intention to take temporary breaks for operationalising rule based distancing respectively in response to Partial Concessions (see H4a) and Alternate Concessions (see H4b) atti tudinal responses, and innovative use intention for operationalising adapting. In addition, we used the interaction of reversed measures for routine use and discontinuance intentions — i.e., routine use intention (reversed) discontinu ance intention (reversed) — for operationalising neglecting. This approach is adopted because, based on its definition neglecting occurs when users have both low intention toward using SNS routinely and low intention toward dis continuing SNS use indefinitely (Turel, 2015). As a result, a multiplicative term is used to represent this low an low” condition. The multiplicative approach has been used in prior research, including IS studies, for measuring constructs that depend on simultaneously high or low levels of multiple conditions or variables. An example is “fit a interaction measure (Venkatraman, 1989) used by Nelson and Cooprider (1996) for measuring the fit between th levels of appreciation, understanding, and commitment that a business department within a firm has toward the I department and vice versa. The fit was high only when both sides (i.e., IS and business departments) show high level of appreciation, understanding, and commitment toward each other (Nelson & Cooprider, 1996)

Measurement items for all factors were adapted from established and reliable instruments (see Appendix B in S1). Experience of ambivalence was measured at t1 to identify and remove non- ambivalent SNS users by adapting the scale for measuring felt (a.k.a., “subjective”) ambivalence from Newby-Clark et al. (2002) to the context of SNS use. Positive and negative attitudes were separately measured at t1 using the attitudinal component technique b Kaplan (1972). Specifically, we adapted eight items on unipolar scales from prior pertinent studies (Priester & Petty, 1996, 2001) to separately (independent of each other) assess individuals' positive and negative attitudes toward using the SNS (four items for assessing positive attitude and four items for assessing negative attitude; see Appendix B in S1). After refining and pilot-testing these items (see Appendix C in S1), each participant was asked to focus on one positive or negative attitude item at a time (ignoring the oppositional attitude item) and rate it based on a unipolar 7-point scale (scored from 0 to 6).

Routine use intention was measured at t2 using a scale adapted from Liu et al. (2019) and Zhang et al. (2020) to the SNS use context. Discontinuance intention, reduced use intention, and intention to take a temporary break from SNS use were measured at t2 using scales adapted from Osatuyi and Turel (2020). Innovative use intention was measured at t2 using scales adapted from Li et al. (2013) and Ahuja and Thatcher (2005) to the SNS use context (se Appendix B in S1). Lastly, demographics (age and gender), a list of other SNS used by participants, their most frequently used SNS, and the number of years of using their most-frequent SNS were collected at t1 for control and descriptive purposes.

## 5 | ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

Prior to conducting the main study, two pilot studies using separate samples of SNS users (n1 = 15; n2 = 84) were conducted to ensure the acceptable validity and reliability of the measurement items. Similarly, preliminary analyses ensured the quality of data collected for our main analyses (see Appendices C and D in S1).

## 5.1 | Stage 1 of main analyses: identifying profiles of ambivalent SNS users using LPA

To test our hypotheses, we first needed to identify the different profiles of ambivalent SNS users based on their positive and negative attitudes toward the SNS use. To do so, we employed LPA with the continuous measures of users positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use as inputs

LPA was deemed appropriate for this study over other clustering methods, such as k-means, due to its severa unique advantages. First, unlike other clustering methods, LPA belongs to the family of Finite Mixture Model (FMM), which offer a “model-based clustering” approach. As such, LPA derives clusters using an estimated probabilistic model based on the distribution of data. One important implication of this approach is that LPA clusters are latent , meaning that they represent latent subpopulations in the sample with different distributions and possibl relations between the variables. Therefore, instead of heuristically finding clusters with some arbitrary distance measures (e.g., Euclidean distance measure in k-means), LPA estimates a probabilistic model to assess the probabilities of users' membership in different latent clusters (also referred to as latent profiles and latent classes ). This attribut renders LPA (and generally FMM) a top-down approach, which starts with identifying the distribution of data for estimating a probabilistic model that generates probabilities of membership in different latent clusters. This is different from bottom-up clustering algorithms that start with calculating the distance between cases for forming cluster without considering the distribution of data underlying different clusters (Wang & Hanges, 2011). In other words methods such as k-means generate simple clusters of cases based on their distances from each other in the sample while LPA estimates the latent structures (subpopulations) represented in the sample.

Second, because LPA uses a probabilistic model for assessing class membership of different users, it can generate statistical (likelihood-based) goodness-of-fit measures for different solutions (e.g., AIC, BIC, and sBIC) based on their fit to the data (Melas et al., 2014; Nylund et al., 2007). This is different from other clustering techniques, such as k-means, where no goodness-of-fit measures are produced (because they are not model-based). Indeed, thi aspect of LPA renders it an appropriate method for confirmatory research (e.g., hypothesis testing), while more con ventional clustering techniques, such as k-means, are heuristic, exploratory approaches

Third, unlike conventional clustering techniques, such as k-means, LPA can include covariates (e.g., control vari ables) in estimating its probabilistic model which influence its final results (i.e., number and formation of clusters) This is particularly important for confirmatory research where the effects of other important variables need to be controlled, such as demographics and SNS use experience in our study (see Appendix E in S1 for more details).

Using Mplus 8.5 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017), we conducted LPA iteratively with different number of possi ble latent profiles—from one to six profiles (the maximum number of profiles that LPA converged). The optimal number of profiles in the data was assessed based on parsimony and interpretability of the profiles (Merz & Roesch, 2011) and the goodness-of-fit measures, namely AIC, BIC, and Sample-Size Adjusted BIC (Mela et al., 2014). These criteria suggested six profiles in the data. Nonetheless, upon a closer examination of the six rec ommended profiles, we noted that two of the profiles (n 72 and n 13) demonstrated markedly similar character istics in terms of users' positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use, hence were theoretically identical (they both represented Negative Response Amplification profile). As a result, we collapsed (recoded) these two profiles into one larger profile $( n = 8 5 )$ . This resulted in five theoretically distinct profiles (see Appendix E in S1). The five LPA profiles were not different in survey completion times $( p = 0 . 8 5 0 )$ , suggesting that equal attention to the survey was paid by the members of all profiles.

Each of the five profiles represents a distinct archetype of attitudinal response to ambivalence as expected (1) Ambivalence Avoidance: low positive and low negative attitudes $( n = 1 2 ) ;$ (2) Negative Response Amplification: low positive and high positive attitudes (n 85); (3) Positive Response Amplification: high positive and low negative atti tudes $( n = 1 1 2 ) ;$ (4) Concessions medium positive and moderate negative attitudes $( n = 8 7 ) ;$ ; and (5) Holism: high pos itive and high negative attitudes $( n = 7 4 )$ . The determination of high/medium/low positive and negative attitudes for each profile was based on their statistically significant difference from zero. We conducted t-tests between the mea of each attitudinal item in a profile versus the mean of that item in the whole sample, which was zero because al attitudinal items in LPA were sample-mean-centered for ease of interpretation. Attitudinal items in a profile that were significantly larger than zero (the sample-mean) were considered high, those that were significantly smaller than zero were considered low, and the ones that were not significantly different from zero were considered medium<sup>5</sup> in our results (see Table 4 and Figure 3)

## 5.2 | Stage 2 of main analyses: hypotheses testing using ANCOVA

After empirically identifying the expected five profiles of attitudinal responses to ambivalence, ANCOVA was conducted to test the hypotheses. It assessed whether ambivalent users' affiliation with different profiles can explain the differences in their SNS use patterns. Covariates included age, gender, choice of most-frequently used SNS, and the extent of SNS use experience.

Results (see Table 5 and Figure 4) show that the interaction term of reversed routine use and discontinuanc intentions (i.e., neglecting) for Ambivalence Avoidance profile was significantly larger than that of other profiles, full supporting H1. Moreover, ambivalent SNS users in the Negative Response Amplification profile showed significantly higher discontinuous intention (i.e., abandoning) than other profiles, fully supporting H2. Furthermore, ambivalent SNS users in the Positive Response Amplification profile had significantly higher routine use intention (i.e., partnering) than other profiles with the exception of Holism profile, where the difference was in the expected direction, but not statistically significant. Therefore, H3 is largely supported

In addition, ambivalent SNS users in the Concessions profile had significantly higher levels of reduced use inten tion (i.e., rule-based distancing for partial concessions) than other profiles, except for the Negative Response Amplifi cation profile, where the difference was in the expected direction but was not statistically significant. Hence, H4a i largely supported. We also found partial support for H4b, as users in the Concessions profile held significantly higher intention to take a temporary break from SNS use (i.e., rule-based distancing for partial concessions) than Ambiva lence Avoidance and Positive Response Amplification profiles, but the mean differences were not statistically signifi cant for the Negative Response Amplification and the Holism profiles. Lastly, as expected, ambivalent SNS users in the Holism profile held the highest level of innovative use intention (i.e., adapting) among all profiles, full supporting H5.

## 6 | DISCUSSION

## 6.1 | Contributions to theory

Colquitt and Zapata-Phelan (2007) explain that theoretical contribution contains not only grounding in existing theories (i.e., theory testing), but also extending existing theories significantly (i.e., theory building). This paper makes important contributions to theory along both of these lines. First, it contributes to the SNS use literature by identifying profiles of ambivalent SNS users' attitudinal responses to ambivalence and their associated SNS use patterns. Our findings offer novel perspective to understand why ambivalent SNS users react differently and choose different behavioural paths. W argue and show that variations in SNS use patterns can be associated with variations in profiles of attitudinal responses to ambivalence, which reflect the ways users cope with their ambivalence toward SNS use. Second, this study contributes to the existing ambivalence literature by extending it to a volitional technology use context (SNS use) and offering novel, context-specific conceptualization of attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence

Along the first line of contributions, this paper sheds light on five profiles of attitudinal responses to ambivalence in the SNS use context. These can explain why some ambivalent SNS users have stronger intentions than other ambivalent users to engage with SNS use (partnering), despite their ambivalence, while others prefer rule-based distancing from SNS use, abandoning SNS use, or adapting their SNS use. Prior SNS use research has typically focused on explaining one pattern of SNS use at a time. While this approach is an important starting point, it limits the sys tematic understanding of when and why other possible SNS use patterns emerge. This paper bridges this gap b explaining how the attitudinal responses to ambivalence toward SNS use can influence the likelihood and prevalenc of different SNS use patterns.

Along the same line of contributions, this paper illuminates the importance of the bi-dimensional view for understanding SNS users' attitudes and behaviours. Unlike the unidimensional view of SNS users' attitude that portrays a merely linear perspective of users' intentions (continuance or discontinuance), the bi-dimensional view offered in this paper provides a more nuanced and comprehensive account of users' attitudes toward SNS use This perspective better describes the diversity and intricacy of SNS use patterns based on users' attitudina responses to ambivalence. For example, while the unidimensional perspective advocates that continuance discontinuance behaviours respectively stem from users' high/low position along a unidimensional, bipolar attitu dinal scale, the bi-dimensional perspective demonstrates that variations in SNS use patterns are a function of users' simultaneous positions on the opposing, unipolar attitudinal scales (i.e., separate positive and negative atti tude dimensions). Grounded in prior research on behavioural responses to ambivalence (Mick & Fournier, 1998) this paper distinguishes among plausible SNS use patterns, several of which are understudied in the extant SNS literature. such as neglecting, rule-based distancing, and adapting. This provides new opportunities for future research on SNS use.

Moreover, our findings offer a more nuanced perspective on the relations between the attitudinal and behav ioural responses to ambivalence. In particular, while behavioural responses are not necessarily limited to particula archetypes of attitudinal responses to ambivalence, they are more likely under certain archetypes of attitudina response than others (see post-hoc analysis 2). These findings point to the importance of understanding the attitudi nal basis of new patterns of SNS use, and in general IS use.

In the same vein, the bidimensional perspective in this paper extends the recent research on ambivalence towar SNS use (e.g., Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020). These recent efforts have utilised a “subjective” and self-report measurement of ambivalence and the attitudinal responses to it. However, this approach paints only a part of the full picture because ambivalence and responses to it might not always manifest consciously (Ashforth et al., 2014; van Harreveld et al., 2015). As an extension of these findings, in this paper, we offer a more encompassing conceptualization of atti tudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence by capturing the coexistence and intensity of positive and negative attitudes toward SNS use, as two separate dimensions (i.e., a bidimensional view). This bidimensional view of users' positive and negative attitudes is consistent with the concept of “objective ambivalence” (van Harreveld et al., 2015). It enabled us to offer a typological perspective of users attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence. Indeed, this extension is in line with Qahri-Saremi and Turel's (2020) call for future studies to focus on th objective and bidimensional conceptualization of ambivalence toward IS use and the users' responses to it. In doing so, we drew on established typological frameworks (Ashforth et al., 2014; Mick & Fournier, 1998) that offered a sys tematic and theoretical basis for the archetypes of attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence. These find ings provide a sound foundation for future studies to build on.

Along the second line of contributions, this paper extends the ambivalence literature to the context of SNS use. The advancement and pervasiveness of social media in all aspects of people's personal, professional, and social lives have led to a number of paradoxes for the users. For example, while SNS use can serve users in rela tionship formation and content sharing, it can also enslave them by hooking them onto an unrealistic, often toxi SNS environment. These paradoxes of SNS use can trigger an experience of ambivalence for its users. As such ambivalence is inherently embedded in SNS use. Therefore, it is important for the findings of ambivalence litera ture to be extended and contextualised to this domain, which this paper strived to achieve. While SNS use is a prime example of an IT use context that can trigger ambivalence for its users (Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020), it is not unique in this regard. Indeed, the increasing advancement and pervasive presence of digitalization have spawned new, ubiquitous generations of digital technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI) based systems, and virtual reality applications (e.g., Metaverse). that cross life boundaries and can lead to paradoxes for their users (Liang et al., 2021). Similarly, the access and exposure to others' diverse opinions and subjective evaluations on online reviews and social media platforms can induce ambivalent attitudes within users (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019, 2022). As such, it is important for ambivalence scholars to extend their knowledge to thes emerging areas to better understand users' ambivalent experiences and responses to them

## 6.2 | Implications for practice

Our findings provide important insights for SNS service providers, developers, and users. For SNS service provider and developers, we provide a model to better understand users' behaviours. As opposed to many prior models that focused on positive, negative, or combinations of positive and negative attitudes as markers for understanding on behaviour at a time (e.g., Cho, 2015; Osatuyi & Turel, 2020; York & Turcotte, 2015), our simultaneous consideration of positive and negative attitudes using an ambivalence lens of analysis allows service providers to better predict which user is likely to neglect, distance, partner, abandon, or adapt their SNS use

Furthermore, while our study was not conducted in an organisational setting, it can provide a glimpse into what managers should probably do to better understand and change IS user behaviours. In organisations, many IS implementation projects adopt a unidimensional view for understanding users' orientations toward an IS by focusing on one attitude (e.g., positive or negative attitude). Consequently, the suggested interventions are often unidimensional. For example, increas ing user involvement for augmenting their positive attitude toward IS use (Barki & Hartwick, 1994), or increasing organisational support for lowering users' negative attitudes toward IS use (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). Nonetheless, our findings point to the possible advantage of adopting a bi-dimensional view in IS implementations projects, which can be basis for identifying ambivalent users and understanding their tenable attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambiva lence. This would allow more tailored and focused interventions or implementation strategies. Extensions from our voli tional SNS use context to mandatory IS use in organisational settings requires further research.

## 6.3 | Limitations and future research directions

Notwithstanding the contributions of this paper, several limitations are noteworthy. First, our study focused on the spe cific context of SNS use for the benefit of offering more contextualised arguments. Consequently, in operationalising different SNS use patterns, we focused on specific patterns of use that were already validated in the extant SNS literature (e.g., innovative use for adapting and taking temporary breaks from SNS use for rule-based distancing). Nonetheless ambivalence experience is not limited to SNS use. There are other IS contexts such as intelligent algorithms (Cram et al., 2022) and online reviews (Qahri-Saremi & Montazemi, 2019) that can harbour intense paradoxes and trigger experience of ambivalence for its users. As such, extensions of our findings to other IT use contexts are warranted

Second, the operationalizations that we used in our study can be extended to other post-adoptive patterns of IS use such as adaptive use (Sun, 2012) and enhanced use (Carter & Grover, 2015) for operationalising the adapting behavioura response. Furthermore, while in this study, we focused on the post-adoption stage of SNS use, ambivalence can also b relevant to the pre-adoption stage of IS use. Unlike the post-adoption stage where users' prior experience with IS use i an important trigger of ambivalence, in the pre-adoption stage, external information sources, such as online reviews and electronic word of mouth (e.g., positive, negative, and mixed reviews), can play important roles in sowing the seeds of ambivalence toward an IS in potential users. Identifying theses triggers and understanding their roles will provide important insights for new IS products and start-up firms. Moreover, the behavioural responses to ambivalence can be differen in the pre-adoption stage, as compared to the post-adoption stage of IS use. Behavioural responses such as ignoring th information about the characteristics or availability of a new IS and refusing or delaying the opportunity to own or engage with an IS can be formed in response to potential users' ambivalence toward the IS use in the pre-adoption stage (Mick & Fournier, 1998). To that end, shedding more light on the triggers of and responses to ambivalence in the pre-adoption stage of IS use is a ripe area for future research.

Lastly, the triggers of ambivalence, particularly the paradoxes embedded in SNS use, as well as the long-term consequences of SNS use patterns (e.g., long term outcomes of neglecting, rule-based enhancing, and adapting) were left unexplored in this paper. Future research may extend our findings by examining these areas. In this vein, an ave nue for future research is the longitudinal examination of the changes in attitudinal and behavioural responses to ambivalence over time. For example, future studies can investigate whether in the longer run, the ambivalent neglecters will switch to a different attitudinal and behavioural response and if this represents a systematic pattern.
