# A method for resolving organisation-enterprise system misfits: An action research study in a pluralistic organisation

David Morquin<sup>1</sup> | Roxana Ologeanu-Taddei<sup>2</sup> | Guy Paré<sup>3</sup> | Gerit Wagner<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Montpellier University Hospital, Montpellier, France

Correspondence Roxana Ologeanu-Taddei, TBS Education, Toulouse, France. Email: r.ologeanu-taddei@tbs-education.f

## Abstract

Although off-the-shelf enterprise systems (ES) have been widely adopted in organisations, the extant literature repeatedly documents ES failures caused by misfits between organisational processes and the ES. Although some misfits can be identified early in the ES lifecycle, others emerge in the onward and upward phase (i.e., after the implementation) and, hence, must be resolved reactively. Prior research on misfits and resolution strategies has primarily focused on the implementation phase, often assuming that close-to-perfect information on the misfit's nature and characteristics is available. However, no study has examined how to effectively complete a shared diagnosis and resolution of misfits when diverging individual user perceptions are taken as the starting point. Such situations may be particularly pronounced in pluralistic organisations, where a variety of interdependent processes and potentially competing perceptions of processes are prevalent. The main objective of this study is to address this gap. To this end, we propose a pragmatic method for the diagnosis and resolution of misfits between organisational processes and enterprise systems, which builds on an actionable conceptualization of misfits. This method builds on theoretical concepts of affordances, affordance actualization, user participation, and change agentry. To demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed method, we conducted an action research study in a university hospital. Our analysis focused on a specific misfit involving the hospital's ES-supported clinical processes. The findings suggest that the method effectively diagnoses and resolves misfits and optimises the resources required for their resolution through efficient management of user participation. We conclude with a discussion of the theoretical and practical contributions of our work.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The deployment of enterprise systems (ES) in organisations has led to remarkable success stories, continuing chal lenges, and failures. Off-the-shelf ES, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP), customer relationship management (CRM), and electronic health record (EHR) systems, can offer several advantages, such as quick delivery, incorporation of best practices, professionally produced technical solutions, and the potential to integrate value chain activitie across suppliers, customers, and business partners (Bajwa et al., 2004; Holsapple et al., 2014). Notwithstanding such benefits and despite numerous studies on critical success factors for these systems (e.g., Haines, 2009; Hong & Kim, 2002; Seddon et al., 2010; Umble et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2006), the extant literature repeatedly documents underutilization and failures of ES in both small and large organisations (e.g., Chadhar & Daneshgar, 2018; Liu et al., 2011; Salmeron & Lopez, 2010; Staehr et al., 2012; Wagner & Newell, 2007)

The concept of misfit between organisational processes and the ES (hereafter called Org-ES misfit) is among the causes often associated with such failures (e.g., Sia & Soh, 2007; Strong & Volkoff, 2010; van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016; Wang et al., 2006; Wei et al., 2005). Such misfits are either imposed externally or they are cau sed by discretionary changes in organisational structures or business processes (Strong & Volkoff, 2010) in the onward and upward phase of the ES lifecycle (Markus & Tanis. 2000). Prior research has made significant progress in distinguishing types of misfits, with exemplary classifications covering functionality misfits, data misfits, usability mis fits, role misfits, control misfits, and organisational culture misfits (e.g., Sia & Soh, 2007; Soh et al., 2000; Strong & Volkoff, 2010; van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016; Wu et al., 2007). When such misfits persist, individua users, groups, or even whole departments may initially respond by developing workarounds, shadow IT, or feral IS (Ejnefjäll & Ågerfalk, 2019; Malaurent & Avison, 2016; Spierings et al., 2017). The restricted scope and the unofficial nature of these adjustments can trigger more official initiatives to collectively analyse and resolve misfits. During such resolution efforts, misfits can be addressed through combinations of resolution strategies, ranging from system adaptations (Brehm et al., 2001; Soh et al., 2000; Swan et al., 1999) to business process modifications (Chiplunkar et al., 2003; Gattiker & Goodhue, 2002; McAdam & Galloway, 2005; Paré & Jutras, 2004; van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016). This plethora of misfit types and resolution approaches highlights the diversity of research on misfits and indicates the difficulties faced in anticipating, diagnosing, and resolving misfits

Although prior work has made considerable progress in our understanding of Org-ES misfits, it fails to provide a formal method for diagnosing and solving misfits in practical contexts where multiple users have different percep tions of the misfit. Extant research on this topic is primarily observational and focuses on structural distinctions including the surface, latent, and deep structure of the ES (e.g., Sia & Soh, 2007; Soh et al., 2000; Strong & Volkoff. 2010: van Bejisterveld & van Groenendaal. 2016: Wu et al., 2007). Misfits are often assessed by individua experts and consultants, with cursory attention being paid to the skills and knowledge of users. Indeed, in th context of misfit diagnosis and resolution, prior research has considered users to be in need of education (Strong & Volkoff, 2010), or their perceptions of misfits are suspected to lead to unnecessary system modifications, and are therefore considered illegitimate (van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016). These assumptions can be questioned, particularly in organisational settings where users are the primary and authoritative sources of information on th Org-ES misfit. Moreover, to diagnose and resolve a misfit in such settings, it is essential to recognise that even trained experts have difficulty agreeing on how to classify a particular misfit (van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016). This implies that more significant disagreements can be expected when seeking to pin down and resolve misfits at a more granular level. Misfits, by definition, involve complexity and multi-level interaction between interdependent entities (Strong & Volkoff, 2010). This complexity can be further aggravated in situation involving many intertwined and ES-supported business processes fulfilling different goals, which are often referred to as pluralistic organisations (see Berente & Yoo, 2012; Denis et al., 2001; Kohli & Tan, 2016). Such organisations are characterised by divergent objectives (individual patient care, population health, cost control) and multipl actors (professionals, managers, community groups, policy makers) linked together in fluid and ambiguous power relationships” (Denis et al., 2001, p. 803). Considering that multiple groups of actors in such organisations use an ES to pursue different tasks and goals, they have authoritative but possibly incomplete views of a business process Therefore, in such settings any interventional method used to diagnose and solve misfits should be based on users understanding of the process, instead of considering it as a perception that is “out of the loop”. Such a method is stil lacking in prior literature.

The present study addresses this shortcoming by proposing a method focused on the collective diagnosis of Org-ES misfits, taking, as the main starting point, the understanding of the misfits expressed by various expert users Our method's conceptual and theoretical foundations are aligned with the philosophy of social constructivism (Searle, 1995). From this perspective, we propose addressing misfits through the conceptual prism of affordances which makes the method accessible and actionable. Moreover, we build on participatory approaches (Markus & Mao, 2004; McKeen & Guimaraes, 1997), which focus on constructing a shared understanding among the various groups of actors in pluralistic organisations and a change agent required to lead the intervention. In line with these philosophical and theoretical foundations, our method starts with an examination of the perceptions of expert user (who execute or perform the process) and other stakeholders (who support the process or are affected by it). Such perceptions reflect their specific responsibilities and therefore constitute a partial view of the process and may not coincide with the perceptions of other actors. Consequently, it is assumed that users, individually, cannot obtain perfect and unanimous understanding of the misfit in the initial steps of the method.

In short, we present the first theory-based method for diagnosing Org-ES misfits that takes the individual and collective perceptions of expert users as its starting point. This method is an artefactual contribution, that is, a contri bution that results from intervention-oriented research and produces an artefact suitable for solving problems in practice (Ågerfalk & Karlsson, 2020; Sein et al., 2011). It results from theory-based canonical action research (Davison et al., 2012; Davison et al., 2021), and is corroborated by an empirical account of the method's applicabilit in practice. More broadly, the method aligns with the evidence-based information systems perspective (Wainwright et al., 2018), which draws inspiration from other applied disciplines, such as the health sciences, and management disciplines. The evidence-based practice paradigm emphasises the benefits of systematically incorporating the best evidence available to ensure the adoption and effective use of technological solutions in organisations (Austin & Ciaassen, 2008; Gerrish & Clayton, 2004). In line with these philosophical roots, our action research approach is taken in response to numerous calls by researchers for the development of relevant knowledge for practice (e.g., Davison et al., 2004; Malaurent & Avison, 2016; Sein et al., 2011) and to a call for the development of action able knowledge for decision-makers (Jabagi et al., 2016).

The following sections present a literature review and a critical summary of prior research on misfit resolution methods; develop the theoretical background and the proposed misfit resolution methodology; explain the methodologi cal approach that allowed us to validate the usefulness of the new method; and present the main findings of the stud and our reflections on the conditions for the success of the proposed method. We conclude by presenting the theoreti cal and practical contributions of our study and discuss its limitations as well as some avenues for future research

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 | Fit and misfit in the ES lifecycle

Fit and misfits have been studied extensively throughout the ES lifecycle, which is commonly divided into fou phases: chartering, project, shakedown, and onward and upward (Markus & Tanis, 2000). When the chartering phase leads to the decision to deploy an ES, one of the primary concerns of the project phase is to improve the Org-ES fit by customising the ES and adapting the operational business processes (Hustad et al., 2016; Strong & Volkoff, 2010) Customization efforts and the degree of fit achieved in the project phase are key predictors of ES success (Hong & Kim, 2002; Shiang-Yen et al., 2013). In the shakedown and onward and upward phase, customization, and adaptation measures ideally pay off in terms of stable systems operation, as well as lower efforts required for maintenance and upgrade work (Law et al., 2010; Wei et al., 2005).

In the onward and upward phase, insufficient adaptation and changes in the IT and the broader organisational environ ment can lead to misfits and trigger ad-hoc responses or resolution attempts that are not officially mandated (e.g., Alter, 2014). Prior research has analysed how individuals and groups cope with Org-ES misfits by implementing workarounds (Debono et al., 2013; Ejnefjäll & Ågerfalk, 2019), relying on personal devices and shadow IT (Haag & Eckhardt, 2017; Spierings et al., 2017), or single-handedly modifying the ES, thereby contributing to the feral IS phenome non (Alter, 2014; Spierings et al., 2017). What these practices have in common is that they are unofficial, that is, occur with out involving management or IT. While prior information systems literature has proposed positive, neutral, and negative views of such practices (Alter, 2014), research in the healthcare context has leaned towards emphasising adverse effects including compromising patient safety and problem hiding, which prevent organisational learning (e.g., Debono et al., 2013).

Research on officially mandated misfit resolution initiatives is considerably scarcer, with few definitive studies. Most notably, Sia and Soh (2007) analyse three settings of Org-ES misfits, distinguishing external impositions from voluntary misalignments and misfits that are rooted in the surface of the ES, that is, its user interface, from others that are rooted in the deep structure, that is, data architecture and information system functionality (Strong & Volkoff, 2010). In a further study, van Beijsterveld and van Groenendaal (2016) propose a framework for distinguishing perceived misfits from structural misfits (referred to as actual misfits). For those authors, these types of misfits vary in severity as well as in the typical resolution approaches used. According to the authors, misfits connected to users' wishes, ignorance, and resistance to change (perceived misfits) contribute to unnecessary system modifications that should be avoided through misfit diagnosis.

In sum, prior work lacks interventional research (such as action research and design-oriented work) in the latte phase of the ES lifecycle. There is non-interventional and design research in the chartering and project phases focused on achieving alignment or anticipating and reducing misfit through system modification (e.g., Brehm et al., 2001). There is non-interventional research on misfits and resolution measures that are not officially mandated and there is considerably less research on officially mandated misfit resolution initiatives in the onward and upward phase (e.g., Sia & Soh, 2007; Strong & Volkoff, 2010), but, to the best of our knowledge, there is no action an design-oriented work.

## 2.2 | Misfit diagnosis and resolution

Prior research has focused on the implementation of resolution measures, covering a range of options, which are broadly related to system modification or business process adaptation (Davison et al., 2004; Gattiker &

Goodhue, 2005; Soh et al., 2000; Soh & Sia, 2004), and include specific examples such as bolt-ons, workflow pro gramming, and package code modifications (Brehm et al., 2001). A common type of recommendation for practitioners is not to overstep efforts at system customization (Law et al., 2010; Sia & Soh, 2007). Additional implication are presented in the form of resolution strategies or associations between misfit types and resolution measure observed in practice (Sia & Soh, 2007; van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016). These observations, frequently considered as having direct implications for practitioners, help us better understand the actual practices of misfit res olution used in different organisations and industries. At the same time, it is essential to remember that these suggestions are not derived from an overarching methodological process explaining how to identify, select, and justif resolution measures. Such due process is particularly important in high-stake contexts, in which purely observationa knowledge (e.g., Sia & Soh, 2007; Strong & Volkoff, 2010; van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016) may not have the reliability and legitimacy to justify resolution decisions. Practitioners may be reluctant to follow such advic because patterns and observed correlations between measures and types of misfits do not necessarily mean that th measures will effectively solve the misfit. Furthermore, misfits may be characterised by contextual factors, for example, requiring practitioners to consider interactions between the features of different ES, mutual dependencies between processes, and organisational contingencies (Gresov, 1989; Morton & Hu, 2008). Although prior research on misfits and misfit resolution measures provides a foundation, there is a lack of insights on how a resolution pro cess should be designed to effectively diagnose and resolve misfits

While prior work sheds light on the different types of misfits, there are significant shortcomings related to thei diagnosis in practical settings. The academic and non-interventional evaluations of misfits (e.g., Strong & Volkoff, 2010; van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016) are primarily aimed at describing, classifying, and under standing misfits. Extensive studies have focused on descriptive goals instead of pragmatic ones (i.e., solving a misfi in practice), producing valuable insights, but not necessarily indicating how a more efficient diagnosis can be accom plished in practice. Similarly, the dominant structural perspective of misfits—for example, dissociating deep and latent IS structure (Strong & Volkoff, 2010; Wu et al., 2007)—provides instructive knowledge that resonates with IT personnel and informs system modifications. However, it assumes that potential informants have structural and techni cal knowledge and may therefore exclude key stakeholders with essential knowledge about business processes an perceptions of IT systems. Finally, participants' evaluations of misfits cannot be expected to agree perfectly (see van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016), underlining the need for a diagnostic process that elicits and reconciles ke stakeholders' perceptions.

## 2.3 | Critical summary of extant research

While our review explains how extant research can inform the resolution of misfits, it also suggests that key limita tions render prior knowledge insufficient for effectively guiding the diagnosis of Org-ES misfits. Despite the signifi cant strides that have been made, previous work has, to the best of our knowledge, not established a formal metho for misfit diagnosis, that is, a systematic process that would detail how practitioners can be assisted in diagnosing misfits as well as in identifying, selecting, and justifying resolution measures. There is scarce knowledge on the steps, activities, participants, and conditions of success of such measures. The need for such a method is particularly evi dent in the contexts of pluralistic organisations, in which leadership is shared among various actors (Denis et al., 2001), systems and practices are loosely coupled (Berente & Yoo, 2012), and actors have to share their individ ual knowledge on the misfit, processes, and affordances provided by the ES. This context also requires resolution methods with specific properties. First, misfits may not be accessible to individual experts studying the system structure and need to be elicited from users with the expertise in their business processes. Second, the convergenc towards a shared understanding has to be supported, especially for cross-functional processes where leadership is not hierarchical and authority is more fluid and ambiguous (Denis et al., 2001). ## 3 | THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS

Theories play a critical role in guiding each step of the action research process, ensuring that CAR-based method successfully navigate each difficulty “to develop a precise and holistic understanding of the organization's situation and its processes” (Davison et al., 2012, p. 765). As mentioned above, our work is rooted in theoretical concepts of (actualization of) affordances, user participation, and change agentry.

The theoretical foundations align with Searle's philosophical work on the construction of social reality. More specifically, we conceive misfits as being a socially constructed reality (Searle, 1995), meaning that they are not inde pendent of human beings and institutions but collectively constructed by them; nevertheless, they are objective in the sense that they exist independently of the intention of an individual or a concrete social interaction. On the epistemological level, misfits are accessible through perceptions, implying that actors hold individual perceptions and may disagree on whether a particular problem constitutes a misfit. However, they can attain a collective, objectivized understanding of a misfit, depending on their competencies and awareness. On the ontological level, actual misfit are represented by a subjective (i.e., socially constructed) yet objectivized collective understanding (because they are external to individuals and their partial perceptions of the ES and the business processes). This suggests that methods aimed at resolving Org-ES misfits cannot assume that a perfect and unanimous understanding of the misfit is given initially, but rather that such methods should start by drawing on the individual perceptions of stakeholders Theories of IS affordances provide a preeminent lens for understanding such perceptions of an ES and it functionalities.

To guide our method, we selected the perspective of affordances because the analysis of affordances and affordance adjustments is considered particularly actionable for the implementation of interventional approaches (Strong et al., 2014). We expect the prism of affordances and, more specifically, of affordance actualization, to facilitate a reliable and precise diagnosis and, consequently, the implementation of resolution methods that are wel suited to the context. The concept of affordance, which dates back to the work of Gibson (1977) in ecological psy chology, has garnered considerable interest in the field of information systems (e.g., Majchrzak & Markus, 2012; Markus & Silver, 2008; Silver & Markus, 2013) as have the organisational transformations resulting from its use (Leonardi, 2013; Seidel et al., 2013; Volkoff & Strong, 2013; Yoo et al., 2012; Zammuto et al., 2007). For Gibson (1977), an affordance represents “what is offered or provided for someone by an object,” that is, the opportunity for a person to use something in his or her environment to achieve a particular goal. Depending on the information that people have about an object, they may or may not perceive its affordances (Gibson, 1977). Therefore, the existence of an affordance and its perception are necessary (but insufficient) conditions to trigger action (Greeno, 1994 Norman, 1999). Subsequently, several studies were conducted on the perception and actualization of affordances, as well as their (unanticipated) impacts (Faraj & Azad, 2012; Leonardi, 2011; Pozzi et al., 2014).

The actualization of affordances has been defined as “the actions taken by actors as they take advantage of one or more affordances through their use of the technology to achieve immediate concrete outcomes in support of organizational goals (Strong et al., 2014, p. 70). Following an examination of the conditions for these actualizations Strong et al. (2014) suggest that by evaluating the circumstances of these actualizations, it is possible to comprehen the interdependencies between processes, between actors or groups of actors engaged in the same processes, and ambiguous situations. This can uncover dependencies between individual affordances and collective or share affordances in which the collaboration of several actors is required to actualize an affordance and achieve a collective outcome (Oborn et al., 2011). The concept of affordance actualization guides the diagnosis of misfits, which w conceive as the identification of affordance actualizations, affordance non-actualizations, and partial affordance actualizations. The prism of affordances and their actualizations thereby allows us to propose an operational concep tualization of the misfit, because the concept of affordance makes it possible to identify and circumscribe misfits at granular level, improving diagnostic precision and the relevance of the proposed solutions.

To guide the diagnosis and resolution steps, we propose a conceptualization of misfits that is actionable in th collaborative process, that is, one that cannot only contribute to our understanding of the phenomenon itself but also “generate useful knowledge with the goal of building a better future” (Markus & Mentzer, 2014, p. 365). Specifi cally, we build on the concepts of affordance and affordance actualization presented above to conceptualise an Org ES misfit as a combination of the actualizations, partial actualizations, and non-actualizations of affordances of the E with a given business process that is inappropriate, in the sense that it restricts the ES's ability to attain the process' underlying objectives. This conceptualization indicates that the misfit may result from any combination of affordance fail ures (i.e., the ES does not do what it is expected to do) and affordance actualization failures (i.e., a user is unaware o the presence of an affordance or cannot actualize an affordance if another user has not carried out another task).

Due to organisational interdependencies, misfit resolution requires an optimal participation strategy, as facilitate by a change agent. User participation research has traditionally focused on the initial phases of an ES project, covering aspects such as the ES configuration or change management (Kappelman, 1995; Markus & Mao, 2004; McKeen & Guimaraes, 1997; Yetton et al., 2000). For Markus and Mao (2004), the effects of participation are due to the emerging properties of the relationships between stakeholders (users, IT specialists, process owners), which in turn depend on the participants' interests or motivation, how they were selected, the purpose of their participation, how participation is implemented, and whether or not there are facilitating conditions. Ultimately, there are three theoretical explana tions for the effect of user participation on system quality. They include higher buy-in by participants (psychologica involvement), a better understanding of system requirements by developers, and emergent interactions between sys tem developers and users (Markus & Mao, 2004). Although prior work has mainly studied user participation in the initia system design phases, these explanations may be equally or even more critical in the later phases

Several studies have highlighted the importance of user participation for the sustained use of such a system and the realisation of benefits (Chadhar & Daneshgar, 2018; Liu et al., 2011; Salmeron & Lopez, 2010; Staehr et al., 2012; Wagner & Newell, 2007), especially in the onward and upward phase of an ES project. At this advance phase of the project lifecycle, users are more likely to propose relevant adjustments or changes related to the ES or the business processes (Wagner & Newell, 2007) because they have used several functionalities of the system and have developed skills related to its use (Chadhar & Daneshgar, 2018; Gallagher et al., 2012). As in the initial phases, the effects of participation depend, at least in part, on the characteristics of the relationship between the users and the IT specialists or ES vendors for the necessary adjustments to be made. Ideally, their interactions are facilitated b a change agent.

In addition to user participation, prior research points to the pivotal role of change agents for IT projects (Markus & Benjamin, 1996; Markus & Mao, 2004; Paré & Jutras, 2004). For Markus and Mao (2004), change agents are people who play important roles in designing and executing participation opportunities for stakeholders (p. 529). Their primary responsibilities are to establish the group of stakeholders (the “who”), the approaches, or techniques used to engage them (the “how”), and the timing of their involvement (the “when”). In addition, chang agents organise, animate, and facilitate exchanges and discussions during meetings (Markus & Mao, 2004).

In pursuit of their responsibilities, change agents can adopt different roles, including facilitator, advocate of change (Markus & Benjamin, 1996), or orchestrator (Diriker et al., 2022). Under the facilitator model, change agents believe that clients create change; therefore, they intervene to facilitate group and organisational processes that wil increase clients' capacity to make a change. For their part, change advocates work to influence people's behavior in particular directions that the change agents view as desirable, whether or not the change targets themselves hol similar views” (Markus & Benjamin, 1996, p. 397). In the present study, we are particularly interested in chang agents in their role as facilitators because they lead, guide, and assist stakeholders during the misfit diagnosis and resolution steps.

## 4 | DESIGN OF THE MISFIT DIAGNOSIS AND RESOLUTION METHOD

Based on the abovementioned literature, we formulate the initial guiding principles of our method: (1) the process starts with a diagnosis step dedicated to achieving a shared understanding of the misfit, as conceived through the lens of affordances. Framing the misfit in terms of affordances is deemed helpful because affordances simultaneously relate to the business process's purpose and to the possibilities that the IS offers to users. Therefore, the diagnosi should refer to the combination of the ES's actualizations, partial actualizations, and non-actualizations of affordances with a given business process that is inappropriate, in the sense that it prevents the ES from attaining the process's underlying objectives. Diagnosis and selection require a shared, comprehensive view of a process b the stakeholders involved, beyond their specific tasks and roles in only a part of the process. In this way, they ca understand the interdependencies between their tasks and the affordances (actualized or not) as well. This diagnosi therefore requires discussions and information sharing among the stakeholders. Similarly, (2) the diagnosis as wel as the identification and selection of resolution measures should be achieved through a participatory process involv ing the relevant stakeholders. This is essential, because stakeholders need to share their understanding of the whol business process, keeping in mind that they are the only experts in their specific work tasks and therefore have th knowledge needed to evaluate the proposed resolution measures. Together, they can agree on a resolution plan, which may involve changes to their tasks and practices, the availability of new affordances, or the actualization of existing affordances. Involving stakeholders and having their buy-in is particularly critical in pluralistic organisations which often lack an authoritative entity that can decide how to redesign a business process (Denis et al., 2001) Otherwise, the planned resolution measures may be derailed through power struggles or inefficient implementation (3) Change agentry is the third principle, which can help in making participation effective and directed at the overal goal of solving the misfit. The change agent needs to have legitimacy and related knowledge of both the business process and the ES to play the roles of facilitator, advocate, and orchestrator of change, meaning that the change agent manages the efficient participation of the groups of actors.

Once the method is triggered, the step of misfit diagnosis is initiated by the change agent, who presents this problem to the other process stakeholders and assumes the role of advocate of change. Then, he/she facilitate stakeholders' participation in dialogical interaction (Klein, 2004) to reach a shared understanding of the underlying combination of affordances and why they create the misfit. The goal is to develop a detailed and precise diagnosis of the misfit, its focal and intertwined business processes, the ES, and the actors involved. This is accomplished by itera tively probing into the sources of the misfit (Bygstad et al., 2016) and exploring the combination of actualizations non-actualizations, and partial actualizations. We propose that this diagnosis be recorded in the form of a combination of actualizations, non-actualizations, and partial actualizations of multiple ES affordances. This initial step is intended to establish a comprehensive understanding of the misfit, including the relevant processes, ES, and stakeholders. More specifically, it can reveal the following situations: (1) affordances that were implemented by the E vendor and have been actualized; (2) affordances that have not been actualized, in particular because the users are not aware of them; and (3) affordances that have been partially actualized, that is, they did not have the expected effect or were actualized for only some of the actors involved, because of the context, the lack of awareness or the existence of competing affordances (Strong et al., 2014). Beyond the initial description of the misfit, eliciting the per ceptions of each actor or group of actors makes it possible to reveal further information about the misfit. By examin ing what the actors (or groups of actors) do and by highlighting the various misfits, it is possible to identify th combination of actualizations, non-actualizations, and partial actualizations of multiple affordances

The next step is to develop a solution, that is, to identify and select the measures aimed at solving the misfit. The intended solution corresponds to a recombination of affordances, which may involve adjusting the ES, modifying affordances of the IS (e.g., to facilitate their actualization), or adjusting the business process by introducing new roles or tasks or modifying existing ones. This step concerns all stakeholders, including users, the process owner, th actors most likely to make the required adjustments (in both technical and organisational terms), and the decision makers likely to allocate the resources needed to make these changes. It is used to determine a combination of adjustments acceptable to all the actors based on their knowledge of the effects of these adjustments on each group of actors. In this step, we propose a mindful approach (Haines, 2009; Trudel et al., 2012) that is not aimed at deter ministically prescribing resolution strategies (e.g., van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016; Wu et al., 2007), but rather at taking into account the organisational context and producing a diagnosis of the misfit that is shared by th various actors involved. This gives credit to the interdependent situations encountered in practice, with individua adjustments affecting several misfits, and individual misfits often requiring a series of adjustments. Here, the chang agent should assume the role of orchestrator and facilitator. After the solution has been implemented and evaluated the method may be terminated if the misfit has been resolved, or it may trigger another iteration of the process.

## 5 | RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

To assess the effectiveness of the proposed method and to refine its principles, we conducted a canonical actio research study (Baskerville & Myers, 2004). In line with its pragmatic roots, action research emphasises that collabo rative interventions of researchers and practitioners can be used to improve our understanding of social realities solve organisational problems, and generate scientific knowledge (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996; Daviso et al., 2004; Iversen et al., 2004; Malaurent & Avison, 2016). Like related studies (e.g., Malaurent & Avison, 2016; Sein et al., 2011; Street & Meister, 2004), this action research study suits our purposes because we intend to diag nose and resolve a specific organisational problem in situ and with the active participation of key stakeholders

To guide our fieldwork, we relied on the canonical action research (CAR) framework of Davison et al. (2004) and established guidelines (Checkland & Holwell, 1998; Davison et al., 2012; Susman & Evered, 1978). CAR is a specific form of action research focused on iterations, rigour, and close collaboration with the organisation. A series of iterations through planned and executed cycles of activities allow researchers to develop an increasingly detailed understanding of the problem and, at the same time, move closer to finding a solution (Davison et al., 2004). We followed the CAR process to implement the proposed misfit resolution method and to assess its feasibility. More specifically, we followed the five CAR principles suggested by Davison et al. (2004). The principle of researcher-client agreement (I), which seeks to develop a mutual understanding and a shared commitment to the project, was applied throughout the entire process. More precisely, the main actors (stakeholders) in the process were involved, and a researcher (first author of this paper), who is also a practitioner, led the CAR process based on the information provided by the actors. The principle of a cyclical process model (II) includes the steps of diagnosis, action planning, action taking, eval uation, and reflection (Susman & Evered, 1978). These steps may occur in iterative cycles. The principle of theoryguided action (III), which calls for a theoretical framework for reflection, was applied in all steps. More precisely, thi principle was put in practice through the design and testing of the misfit resolution method anchored in the solid theoretical foundations presented above. The principle of change through action (IV) was applied throughout th action-taking step. The principle of learning through reflection (V) was implemented during all the steps, especially in the action taking, evaluation and reflection steps, with a focus on testing the method and the subsequent learning and communication of lessons learned.

## 6 | REPORTING THE ACTION RESEARCH PROJECT

The CAR process was initiated in 2014 in response to a request from the clinical process owner, who had noted a major problem with unfortunate consequences for the hospital's performance, its health professionals, and the patient concerned. The method was applied to a clinical process used to screen newborns for hearing impairments. Since 2005, French law has required that each region establishes a specific structure to ensure that all newborns ar screened for hearing disorders at birth and, if necessary, are treated by an otorhinolaryngology (ORL) specialist. In th region served by the Alpha hospital, the corresponding regulatory oversight is organised in a regional network that use data from this hospital and other health facilities. The heads of the ORL, paediatrics, gynaecology, and obstetrics departments have received written orders to facilitate this public health mission. Despite these formal orders, the pro cess used to screen newborns at Alpha hospital is informal. At the time of this study, there was no written procedur describing the tasks to be carried out by the paediatric nurses, and the responsibilities of each of the actors had not been set out in writing. In 2014, the physician who was the process owner at the regional level had complet information on only 68% of the newborns, falling short of the national objective of 96% completeness in carrying out the test in maternity and/or neonatal care. Moreover, because 46% of hearing impairments detected at birth were identified by chance and outside the systematic screening process, several unscreened newborns may have had undetected, and therefore untreated, hearing problems. Concerning this problem, the regional network's 2014 activit report stated: “A total of 1,086 children who should have benefited from a diagnostic consultation in pediatric audiol ogy did not, in fact, see an ORL. This represents 56.9% of the total number of children referred for an ORL consulta tion. This rate is too low compared to rates reported in the scientific literature […] Given these findings, it is necessary to consider taking concrete measures to improve these results.” According to the process owner, the EHR system use at Alpha plays a role in the incomplete data, the redundant data, and the transmission errors. As she said in a meeting “Theoretically, the EHR should facilitate the screening of newborns, but this is not what is happening.”

## 6.3 | Evaluation step

The change agent performed the evaluation through two interviews with the regional network coordinator and th process owner, held 6 months and 2 years after the changes were implemented. The interviews confirmed that data on ORL screening of newborns no longer needed to be retrieved from the biomedical devices once the network coordinator had confirmed that the data were being transmitted correctly. According to the network coordinator, data entry practices now relied on a single structured data collection form and were standardised across all the medi cal departments involved in the process.

In March 2018, 2 years after the last meeting, data retrieved from the EHR showed that the paediatric nurses wer using the appointment scheduling module directly, outside the business hours of the secretariat. The midwife responsible for data entry outside the maternity ward reported that she was satisfied with the new approach to entering data in the EHR (the work list, which allowed her to identify newborns). Although this midwife was not always replaced when she was on leave, she could use the work list to complete the data after the fact. The physician process owner felt that the approach taken had resolved the problem. The regional network coordinator reported that, since the selecte adjustments had been finalised, she had over 90% of the useful data in 2016 (compared to 68% 2 years earlier, in 2014). Furthermore, the EHR data indicated that the percentage of newborns who should have had a consultation but did not had fallen from 46% in 2014 to 22% in 2016, and that the rate of newborns who had an appointment befor hospital discharge had increased from 42% to 77%. Last, the rate of newborns with a positive screening who had follow-up ORL consultation rose from 54% to 78%. At the last interview, the ORL physician who initiated the proces said of the misfit resolution method used, “I don't see how we could have done it any other way.”

## 6.4 | Reflection step

In reflecting on the conditions that facilitated a successful resolution of the Org-ES misfits, the research team engaged in a process of analytical reflexivity (Srivastava & Hopwood, 2009) to revisit links with the theoretical concepts and to refine the underlying principles (as summarised in the Discussion section). In particular, we recognise that certain conditions must be met for the proposed method to be effective. First, the process must begin with problem or misfit perceived by someone who has the authority to make the necessary changes. Second, as illustrate above, it requires the participation of stakeholders in a way that is sensitive to participation richness and avoid wasting their time (Markus & Mao, 2004). Third, the process needs to be orchestrated by a change agent who has both organisational legitimacy and the required expertise. He or she must have a good understanding of the ES' actualized and non-actualized affordances, the ES structure, as well as the business processes involved. In the present case study, the change agent was a physician who had worked at Alpha for over 10 years, who was able to easily grasp the characteristics of the process, and who also had advanced knowledge of the ES's affordances and how they could be altered. Finally, as with any similar change and improvement initiative, the actions of this method can not be considered definitive for the outcomes. Depending on the changes in the process (of users, their roles, knowl edge of affordances, and changes in EHR affordances), the proposed method may require further iterations and corresponding resources.

## 7 | DISCUSSION

The main purpose of this study was to develop an actionable method for diagnosing and resolving Org-ES misfits Our specific intervention revealed the following: the change agent, the IT specialists at Alpha and, to a lesser extent the EHR vendor revealed affordances that were not recognised by the users involved in the process, and the users in turn, revealed deficient affordances of the EHR. All the stakeholders, including the process owner, discovered th partial nature of the actualizations, non-actualizations, and situations of competition and dependency. By narrowin the analysis to a single process, the change agent and the stakeholders were able to obtain a 360-degree overview and identify the misfits and their interdependencies. The recombination step made it possible to determine, by con sensus, a series of technical and organisational adjustments (e.g., changes to the roles played by midwives, and modi fications of data retrieval formats). Taken together, these changes made it possible to resolve the misfits initiall identified.

In the sections that follow, we explicate and refine the method's initial trigger, steps, outcomes, guiding princi ples, and boundary conditions, as summarised in Figure 2 and Table 6. A more formal specification of the method wil enable follow-up research to replicate it and to assess its generalizability. It also provides practitioners with ke insights on misfit resolution in practice. Although the misfits, in our case, were resolved successfully in a single 13-month cycle, other misfits may require additional iterations. As displayed in Figure 2, we align the steps of our method with the archetype of cyclical teleological process models (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995), and thereby account for the possibility that multiple iterations may be required to fully resolve a specific misfit

## 7.1 | Signalling trigger

The process is triggered when the process owner signals a need to resolve one or several misfits. When a user signals a misfit, as the tip of a potential iceberg, its nature and criticality are yet to be assessed. In this trigger, evolving tech nological and institutional environments (Davidson & Chismar, 2007) as well as the individual and collective sensemaking of decision-makers (Griffith, 1999) can play roles. In line with our case and prior literature, there ar distinct characteristics that are expected to facilitate the method's effectiveness. Indeed, the misfit resolutio method was backed by the process owner as well as key stakeholders accountable for its success. Especially in plu ralistic organisations, achieving collective leadership can be vital to promote change (Denis et al., 2001) and as a means to prevent political struggles and user resistance (Meissonier & Houzé, 2010; Rivard & Lapointe, 2012). Fur thermore, the need for regulatory compliance in our case meant that the criticality of the misfit was undisputed More generally, establishing the misfit's criticality based on solid qualitative or quantitative evidence can be highl valuable to avoid unnecessary efforts and to ensure that key stakeholders start the process with a shared awarenes of the problem.

## 7.2 | Steps and outcomes

The trigger initiates the first step of the method (illustrated in Figure 2 and Table 6), the misfit diagnosis, which is aimed at achieving a shared understanding of the misfit by all the groups of actors concerned. This step is achieved when the groups of actors reach a shared understanding of the whole process model, the misfit(s) and their causes mostly related to affordances and work roles. A map of the process as-is is a useful visualisation tool to facilitat the construction of this shared understanding. The next step focuses on the shared elaboration of a solution to th misfit. Conceptualization of a revised process, including work roles, the data process (i.e., data recording and sharing) and a recombination of affordances, are at core of this solution. A “to-be” process map therefore plays a useful rol in this step, to facilitate reaching a shared understanding of the possible solutions and a consensual choice among them. The implementation of the solution, including planning, resolution actions by each actor, and evaluation by the process owner, are the final steps of this process. Nevertheless, the end of this process is not locked in; the changes to the information system, the work roles or the business process can generate a new unsatisfactory perception of its effectiveness and lead to an additional iteration of the process.

## 7.3 | Guiding principles

In this section, we reflect on our study as a way to refine the guiding principles and discuss the generalizability of the method. This approach is similar to prior works (e.g., Lindgren et al., 2004) and in line with the latter stages of the action research process (Sein et al., 2011).

The first guiding principle suggests establishing a shared understanding of the misfit through the lens of affordances. Our findings corroborate that the affordance lens facilitates a shared understanding between stake holders and the change agent, which is particularly important in the misfit diagnosis and solution conception (recombination) steps. Prior work in software engineering (Glinz & Fricker, 2015) sensitises us to the possibility that such a shared understanding can be partly explicit and partly implicit, adequate or reflect misunderstandings, relevant or irrelevant to the misfit, and complete or incomplete (when relevant information goes unnoticed). Although diagnosis and recombination require stakeholders to externalise and recombine knowledge, Glinz and Fricker's principles suggest that it is also imperative to achieve an optimal balance between the efforts made and the benefits receive (e.g., reflected by clarified misunderstandings) from explicating the stakeholders' understanding. This underlines th potential benefits of practices that are simultaneously in line with a targeted approach to explicating a share understanding and with the lens of affordance actualizations. In fact, post hoc reflection with the change agent suggests that such practices have already been employed in the field. For example, the domain problem and solution modelling, as well as the search for and negotiation of potential solutions, served as practices that enabled and built shared understanding (see Glinz & Fricker, 2015) of the misfit and potential solutions, that is, the (re)combination o the actualizations, partial actualizations, and non-actualizations of the affordances of the ES with a given business process. Beyond the practices employed in our study, it would be reasonable for future investigations to examine the role played by trust, as a precondition for developing a shared understanding, as well as assessment practices which are aimed at identifying potential misunderstandings and false assumptions (Glinz & Fricker, 2015).

Concerning the second guiding principle, we propose the refinement of selective and consensus-based participa tion. Overall, our study shows that the stakeholders made valuable contributions to diagnosing the misfit as well as to identifying and selecting resolution measures. The participatory processes were supported by materials, such as the business process maps, designed for the specific meeting purposes. In particular, the stakeholders leveraged their individual professional expertise to go beyond common resolution strategies (ES customization and business process adaptation) and considered practical alternatives, such as how affordances are combined, to solve a specific misfit Notes taken by the change agent also indicate that key stakeholders had many responsibilities and were not able to attend every meeting. The targeted approach to participation (Markus & Benjamin, 1996; Markus & Mao, 2004) which was orchestrated by the change agent, brought key stakeholders into the process at specific times when their presence was considered essential. This aligns with the notion of selective engagement of users, as suggested by Wagner and Newell (2007), and adds further nuances to prior research that examined the effect of user involvement on ES quality (see Wang et al., 2006). Last, prior research on participative systems design distinguishes different levels of participation, including consultative and consensus-based levels (Mumford, 1983). The consensus-based approach that was adopted in our study can be considered particularly effective because it requires stakeholders in an organisational setting that involves divergent objectives and fluid power relationships (Denis et al., 2001) to reach an agreement on the resolution measures. In such settings, other forms of participation, such as consultative partici pation, could easily trigger resistance from key stakeholders (Rivard & Lapointe, 2012; Wagner & Newell, 2007)

Concerning the third guiding principle (change agentry), insights from our case lead us to formulate the revised principle of adaptive change agentry modes. The need for adaptive practices from the change agent has severa facets. For instance, follow-up discussions revealed that the role of the change agent evolved throughout the process, switching among roles of facilitator, advocate of change, and orchestrator of actors' participation, meaning that he/she leverages different forms of openness and closure (Diriker et al., 2022) and the efficient participation of th groups of actors. In addition, the change agent's approach should also be adapted to the various stakeholders and their roles and availabilities in the organisation. In our case, the change agent worked with a series of actors in smal groups and kept longer meetings to a strict minimum (e.g., when the overarching map needed to be developed) Thereby, the change agent adopted an orchestrator role, made the most of each stakeholder's time, and avoided hav ing people participate without adding value. These small-group meetings also enabled greater richness in participation activities and more tangible contributions to solution outcomes (Markus & Mao, 2004). Last, post hoc reflection with the change agent suggests that the level of trust between the stakeholders and the change agent plays a critica role. In our study, the change agent was a doctor who was trusted by the stakeholders and who was also responsible for the acceptance and use of information systems in the hospital. Prior research and theory suggest that such trusting relationship is an important pre-condition to developing a shared understanding (Glinz & Fricker, 2015) and to organising solution measures that are supported and sustained beyond the resolution effort (Wagner & Newell, 2007).

## 7.4 | Boundary conditions

To clarify the generalizability of the proposed method, we state a number of boundary conditions related to its appli cability (see Bacharach, 1989). First, the method applies to Org-ES misfits in the onward and upward phase of the ES lifecycle, that is, after initial implementation and customization (Markus & Tanis, 2000). Second, it applies to pluralistic organisations in which decisions require the collaboration of multiple stakeholders and are not prescribed through hierarchies (Denis et al., 2001). Third, the method is situated at the level of business process misfits (see van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016), which require in-depth knowledge of how stakeholders carry out their work The resolution of other types of misfits, such as strategic misfits (see Davenport, 1998), might require differen inputs, steps, and guiding principles.

## 7.5 | Contributions and implications

The primary contribution of this study is artefactual (Ågerfalk & Karlsson, 2020) with several implications for research and practice. We propose the first fully specified method for diagnosing and resolving Org-ES misfits. Thi method comprises a trigger, a series of steps (including actions and outcomes) along with guiding principles, and explicit boundary conditions. At its core, the guiding principles, which build on pertinent IS theories (participation and change agentry) and an actionable conceptualization of Org-ES misfits from a perspective of affordances advance what was previously known about the resolution of Org-ES misfits. Table 7 summarises the contributions of our study. Last but not least, the empirical action research study was used to refine the underlying principles and to crystallise the method for practitioners.

A key research implication of our work is the need for more extensive study of misfit diagnosis and the need to seize the opportunity of conceiving and theorising it differently. Prior works have primarily pursued expert-driven approaches that quickly proceed to a structural assessment of misfits at different levels, such as deep IS structure (e.g., Strong & Volkoff, 2010; van Beijsterveld & van Groenendaal, 2016). Our method, instead, starts by emphasisin the challenges in developing a shared understanding of the misfit and the need for a collective diagnosis involving various actors. Therefore, the foundations of our method are rooted in social constructivism and theories of affordance actualization, participation, and change agentry. They were particularly helpful in a pluralistic setting, like that of our study, and can provide inspiration and paradigmatic diversity for future studies on Org-ES misfits

The method proposed herein also has important practical implications. We have demonstrated that it can effec tively resolve Org-ES misfits, and we expect that it can be applied in similar organisations deploying other types of ES (e.g., ERPs, CRMs). We have carefully demonstrated the method's feasibility, an approach that we believe is both understandable to and manageable by practitioners. To successfully implement the method in their organisationa settings, we provide practitioners with the following recommendations:

• The method must be led by a change agent who has the authority, legitimacy, and expertise to make necessary changes;

• To avoid unnecessary efforts, the misfit’ criticality must be established early based on solid and indisputable evidence;

• Conceptualising Org-ES misfits as affordances and affordance actualizations enable expert users, change agents, and other stakeholders to develop a shared understanding of misfits and solutions

• The method relies on selective and consensus-based participation orchestrated by the change agent and supported by materials such as detailed business process maps; and

• Change agents must be able to switch roles between advocate, facilitator, and orchestrator as the situatio entails.

Importantly, many opportunities can be found to fit our method into an overall strategy that will make mor effective use of an ES in the onward and upward phase. For example, in an organisation facing constantly changing external constraints, a project team using this method could, within the framework of process-based management try to continually recombine various elements when a misfit emerges. Moreover, in pluralistic organisations misfit seem to be particularly common because of the wide range of processes, their capacity for upgrades, and the limite potential for standardisation. Acquiring this capacity for the collective misfit diagnosis and resolution through recom bination of affordances could constitute a significant advantage in these organisations.

## 7.6 | Limitations and suggestions for future research

Recognising that action research studies have their own experiential quality, validity, and generalizabilit (Checkland & Holwell, 1998), we can see a few limitations that suggest fruitful starting points for future research First, this study was conducted within a particular organisational context, that is, a pluralistic organisation. The misfit resolution method was applied to an EHR, that is, a specific type of ES. In addition, the effectiveness of the metho may vary with the characteristics of the change agent, the process's complexity (at the macro or micro level, it cross-functional nature, the number of groups of actors involved, and the potential divergence of their objectives) and how the misfit resolution process is initiated (by the actors in the process or at the request of management). Sec ond, although our focal business process was intertwined with several other functional processes, it remains to b analysed how the method performs in resolving Org-ES misfits when processes have even more interdependencies. Third, the misfit resolution method does not address how business processes are prioritised when there are multipl misfits to which the method could be applied. Instead, this study focuses on the appropriateness and feasibility of using the proposed method for a given process. Central to the requirements of the misfit resolution approach is th issue of how to prioritise the processes that could justify the related investment. The requirements include the tim to be invested by the actors involved, including the change agent. In the present case, 13 months were required to effectively resolve the misfit.

## 7.7 | Concluding remarks

In conclusion, this study sought to propose a pragmatic and actionable method for the diagnosis and resolution of Org-ES misfits. It offers an approach that facilitates dialogue between stakeholders about a misfit and convergence towards a shared diagnosis and resolution, which may involve technical and organisational adjustments. The method was applied in a pluralistic organisation where it effectively resolved the misfit and efficiently allocated the resources for their resolution due to targeted management of the stakeholders' participation. In a context where misfits identi fied after ES deployment are recognised as one of the leading causes of ES failure, we believe that the present study offers promising opportunities for future research. We hope that information systems researchers will build on the conceptualization of misfits proposed herein, and enrich the misfit resolution method, in a spirit of committed research and allowing for the production of knowledge that can guide practice in the field

## AUTHOR BIOGRAPHIES

David Morquin is a physician in internal medicine and infectious and tropical diseases at the Teaching Hospita of Montpellier (France). He received a Ph.D. in information technology (IT) management in 2019 from the University of Montpellier. Since 2012, he is in charge of the Department for clinical use of digital technology. In addition, he is currently in charge of a research and development unit related to the design and use of digita technology in healthcare.

Roxana Ologeanu-Taddei is an associate professor of IT management and digital transformation at TBS Business School (France). Her research area is focused mainly on the digital transformation of healthcare practice. She has led several research projects with healthcare professionals. Her papers have been published in leading international journals, including Journal of the Association for Information Systems and European Journal of Information Systems.

Guy Paré is a professor of IT and the endowed chair in digital health at HEC Montreal. He is a fellow of the Roya Society of Canada. He received his Ph.D. from Florida International University College of Business. He is currently senior editor of the Literature Review section of the Journal of the Association for Information Systems. His work has been published in premier journals such as Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, and European Journal of Information Systems.

Gerit Wagner is an assistant professor of information systems at University of Bamberg, Germany. His research focuses on digital work and digital health, as well as literature review methods and tools. His research has been published in international journals, including the Journal of Strategic Information Systems, the Journal of Information Technology, the Journal of Medical Internet Research, Information & Management, and Decision Support Systems. He is a member of the Association for Information Systems and regularly serves as a reviewer for Information Systems journals and conferences.
