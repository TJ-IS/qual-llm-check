# Theorising moderation in the configurational approach: A guide for identifying and interpreting moderating influences in QCA

## Abstract

Given the multifaceted nature of digital phenomena, theorising has shifted from the correlational view toward the configurational approach, which embraces equifinality and seeks to identify conjunctural causal conditions culminating in a given outcome. Despite growing scholarly interest in the configurational approach, little is known about how moderation functions in conjunctural causation. Although there is a growing urgency for elucidating the boundary conditions of conjunctural causation to bolster the precision of theoretical explanations, a systematic approach to theorising, identifying, and interpreting moderation in configurational theories is missing. To bridge this knowledge gap, we first establish the theoretical grounding for moderation in the configurational approach. We then articulate the theoretical mechanism underlying how the interdependence of causal factors in a causal recipe is altered by the presence of a moderator. We also offer guidance on how to validate and interpret moderation in the configurational approach based on Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA). We conduct an illustrative study in the context of technostress to showcase the utility of our proposed guidelines and their value in aiding theory development. Our study hence contributes to extant literature by attesting to the significance of moderation in the configurational approach and offering recommendations for theorising such moderations. Insights from this study can be harnessed to guide future theory development by identifying and validating moderated configurational relationships, which in turn can further enhance our understanding of nuances in multifaceted digital phenomena.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The growing ubiquity of digital technologies and their embeddedness in a myriad of organisational and environmental settings have culminated in the growing complexity of recent digital phenomena (Park et al., 2020). Particularly complex digital phenomena—characterised by increased environmental turbulence requiring organisations to culti vate dynamic capabilities and invest in sophisticated, interdependent systems—have compelled researchers to shift from investigating each element independently to a more holistic approach that can mirror the multiple logics behind diverse performance outcomes (El Sawy et al., 2010). Given the multifaceted nature of digital phenomena, the widel applied variance approach is becoming inadequate to capture, comprehend, and explain the inherent complexity of digitalization trends (Furnari et al., 2021; Pappas & Woodside, 2021). A key reason for this inadequacy is that the variance approach is primarily concerned with deciphering individual relationships between causes and outcomes based on correlational logic; it does not articulate the combination of factors that could lead to a given outcome (Rasoolimanesh et al., 2021; Wilden et al., 2016). In response, researchers have begun to advocate for a configurational approach to deciphering complex digital phenomena (de Guinea & Raymond, 2020; Misangyi et al., 2017) The configurational approach, as an emerging paradigm stemming from the systems view, focuses on comprehending complex wholes and places greater emphasis on interactions among system elements as well as between a system and its environment (Burton-Jones et al., 2015). In contrast to its variance counterpart, the configurational approach embraces (1) equifinality whereby the same outcome can be achieved through different conditions and by different paths, as well as (2) causal asymmetry, whereby causes leading to the presence and the absence of an outcome can be different (Fiss, 2011). It shifts attention from net effects to conjunctural causation whereby multiple causes interact in an asymmetrical, equifinal fashion to bring about a given outcome (Park et al., 2020). The configurationa approach has been extensively applied in the fields of Information Systems (IS) and management to advance our understanding of how a phenomenon can be explained as an outcome of multiple conditions configured in specific ways (Dwivedi et al., 2018; Park et al., 2020).

Despite growing scholarly interest in the configurational approach, its promise remains unfulfilled (Soto Setzke et al., 2020). The configurational approach often embraces multiple theoretical lenses in explaining the configurational complexity of multifaceted phenomena, but little is known about the boundaries of applicability for these theoretical lenses (Hong et al., 2014; Kumar et al., 2022). Because theories can be rendered more or less relevant under distinct boundary conditions (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007), identifying the boundaries governing relationships between causes and outcomes is central to theory development and constitutes a form of theorization known as moderation (Andersson et al., 2020). A moderator is traditionally conceived as an extraneous factor that alters the relationship between an independent variable and a dependent variabl (Baron & Kenny, 1986). It generates insights into the boundary conditions of a theory by specifying when the theoretical mechanisms linking the independent variable to the dependent variable are modified (Andersson et al., 2020; Edwards, 2010). In so doing, moderation bolsters the precision of theoretical explanations and predictions.

But at the same time, contemporary approaches to the identification and theorization of moderators are mostly grounded in correlational logic (Carte & Russell, 2003). From the correlational view, moderation of a causal relationship is intended to offer an elucidate how the presence of a moderator alters the relationship between a cause and an effect. The same principle of moderation, however, may not be applicable to its configurational counterpart where multiple causes combine to bring about the same outcome (Park et al., 2020). Consequently, an elusive challenge in the configurational approach pertains to how the theoretical mechanisms of multiple conjunctural causations of an outcome can be modified under certain boundary conditions (i.e., moderators). Although a handful of studies hav begun to explore moderation in the configurational approach, they tend to treat moderation mechanisms in an athe oretical and inconsistent manner (Apetrei et al., 2016; Hernández-Perlines, 2016). For this reason, a systematic guid for theorising, validating, and interpreting moderation in the configurational approach is lacking, which in turn hin ders theoretical advancement.

Fiss (2011) alleged that the comprehension of causal mechanisms in the configurational approach should move away from a pure holistic view because not all causal factors are equally important: the configuration of causal fac tors is much more relevant as opposed to their net effects. Unlike the correlational view, which captures the causa relationship between a cause and an effect based on isolated net effect thinking, the configurational approach derives causal recipes that reflect the interdependence of multiple causal factors leading to the outcome (Fiss et al., 2013). Indeed, there is empirical evidence demonstrating that the impact of organisation-level causal factors on a given outcome of interest (e.g., firm performance) can be influenced by the external environment, thereby indi cating changes in the interdependence of causal factors in response to an external factor (Bell et al., 2014). In other words, there is evidence that hints at the possibility of moderation effects in the configurational approach (Apetre et al., 2016). Arguably, a moderator in the configurational approach could be construed as an extraneous factor that is theoretically independent of the causal factors leading to the outcome, but at the same time, it can alter th interdependence of causal factors in the causal recipe (Greckhamer et al., 2018). Responding to scholarly calls for diversity of theoretical approaches to capture complex phenomena (Park et al., 2020), we, in this study, aspire to advance and theorise how moderation can manifest in the configurational approach.

The mounting attention paid to the configurational approach has led to the rise of Qualitative Comparative Anal ysis (QCA), a configurational analysis technique. QCA is a set-theoretic method grounded in Boolean algebra (Mattke et al., 2022). Advanced by Charles Ragin with the purpose of analysing complex patterns in the social science (Ragin, 2014), QCA has now been widely employed by IS scholars to ascertain unique configurations of factors that give rise to a desirable outcome (Mattke et al., 2022; Park et al., 2020). Yet, despite the tendency among IS scholars to employ QCA for understanding multifaceted digital phenomena, there is no systematic guide on how moderatio can be validated and interpreted via the QCA method.

Taken together, the primary aim of this study is to theorise moderation in the configurational approach and pre scribe a set of guidelines for testing and interpreting moderation in the configurational approach based on QCA Moreover, to demonstrate the utility of our prescribed guidelines, we conduct an illustrative study in which w extend extant literature on technostress by not only disentangling the interdependencies among technological condi tions that conjuncturally cause technostress but also revealing how these interdependencies could be moderated b social support norms.

In summary, this study marks an initial attempt to address contemporary challenges of the configurationa approach by provoking discourse on how moderated configurational relationships could be theorised. Additionally, employing QCA as a complementary methodological technique, we further devise recommendations for validating and interpreting moderation in empirical studies that subscribe to a configurational standpoint. Through blendin theoretical grounding with methodological execution, this study takes a concrete step toward deepening our under standing of the crucial role played by moderation in conjunctural causation. In turn, insights from our study can enable researchers to develop configurational theories with well-defined boundary conditions

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In the next section, we discuss the theorization of moderation in the configurational approach. Then, we prescribe a set of guidelines for identifying, validating, and interpreting moderation in the configurational approach based on QCA. Next, we provide an empirical illustrative study in the context of technostress. Finally, we conclude with theoretical and practical implications of our work from which future research could spawn.

## 2 | DECRYPTING MODERATION IN THE CONFIGURATIONAL APPROACH

## 2.1 | Nature of the moderator

The moderator can be treated as the ‘third’ variable that modifies the primary mechanism between the causal factors and outcome variable (Baron & Kenny, 1986; Koeske, 1992; Tsang, 2015). It infers how the primary mechanism of the theory would perform across different theoretical environments (i.e., boundaries of theory) (Roberts, 1991). For example, Venkatesh and Morris (2000) study on individual acceptance of new technologies in organisations find that the effect of subjective norms on technology acceptance is contingent on the gender of the prospective user, that is, females are more strongly influenced by subjective norms than males. Thus, the gender of the prospectiv adopter can be a boundary condition for the theory of subjective norms in explaining an individual's technology adoption behaviour. In this case, gender works as a moderator for the theoretical mechanism of subjective norms i influencing technology adoption (Allen, 2017; Baron & Kenny, 1986; Koeske, 1992; Tsang, 2015)

By validating the extent to which the primary mechanism holds, a moderator is identified, introduced, and teste to unravel the boundary condition for the relationship between the independent variable and the dependent vari able. By elucidating how relationships in the primary mechanism can be altered by variated theoretical environment reflected by moderators, researchers can increase the precision of theoretical explanations and develop a more com prehensive understanding of the applicability of the theory. Given the theoretical importance of critical boundar factors to reflect theoretical environments, moderators have been extensively explored in the areas of sociology psychology, and business research to advance theory development (Aguinis et al., 2017; Baron & Kenny, 1986).

## 2.2 | Moderator in the configurational approach

Moderators are mostly discussed based on causal logic from the correlational view (Park et al., 2020). However, the theorising of the moderator should not be limited to theoretical approaches. We believe that theory advancemen can also be achieved through identifying moderation based on the configurational approach, a nascent yet rising theorising perspective for capturing causal complexity in management and IS research. In the next section, we wil discuss how the moderator can be theorised in the configurational approach.

## 2.2.1 | Configurational approach to causation

Academic attention has increasingly been paid recently to capturing the causal complexity of managerial and organisational phenomena based on the configurational approach (Liu et al., 2017; Meyer et al., 1993). Manageria and organisational phenomena are usually more complicated than what a linear relationship can explain (Fis et al., 2013). For instance, from the correlational view, the stock market performance of a company is associated with the company's executive incentives (Bell et al., 2014). However, it may only capture part of the phenomenon by rely ing on a simple “the more, the better” linear relationship (Park et al., 2020). There is evidence showing that a comp any's stock market performance can be the result of the coexistence of a set of interconnected causal factors including executive incentive, industry type, firm age, and underwriter prestige (Bell et al., 2014). Although the linear relationship offers a theoretical understanding of how a single factor influences the outcome based on isolated net effect thinking, situations where multiple causes combine to bring about outcomes are dismissed (Park et al., 2020) Correlational analysis can thus rarely explain a complex phenomenon where multiple causes coexist and contribute to the given outcome in equifinal ways (Furnari et al., 2021). Researchers have increasingly applied the configurational approach to investigate multifaceted digital phenomena and advance our understanding of how a phenome non can be explained as the outcome of configurations of multiple causal factors (Fiss, 2011; Fiss et al., 2013)

According to the configurational approach, a constellation of interdependent causal factors can combine and achieve the desired outcome (Meyer et al., 1993). The theorization proposed by Furnari et al. (2021) suggests that there can be more than one set of causal recipes leading to a desired outcome, and each causal recipe has its own orchestration theme that represents the theoretical mechanism of the interdependent causal factors. To be specific the orchestration theme of a causal recipe is the primary mechanism that explains how the causal factors of a causa recipe are interacting with each other in bringing about the outcome (Fiss et al., 2013). For example, Bell et al. (2014 research shows that in one causal recipe leading to a high value in the stock market, the alignment between execu tive incentive and shareholders' interest can be absent when the company is old. The orchestration theme of thi causal recipe is rooted in the mechanism where the reliability of business indicated by a long-term well-established company (i.e., the presence of large firm age) may substitute for the presence of aligned executive incentives. In another equifinal causal recipe, when the company is relatively new (i.e., absence of large firm age), the alignment of executive incentive with shareholders' interest should be presented. The orchestration theme of the corresponding causal recipe is based on the mechanism where the alignment of executive incentive with shareholders' interest is needed for a young company as an indicator of business reliability to achieve a high value in the stock market.

## 2.2.2 | Characterising moderator in the configurational approach

In the configurational approach, the primary mechanism of the causal recipe may perform differently in explaining the phenomenon of interest under different theoretical environments (Greckhamer et al., 2018). Bell et al. (2014 show that the interdependence between executive incentives and prestigious underwriters in causal recipe change under different legal protection conditions. Although Bell et al. (2014) did not attempt to examine the moderatio effect, their results indicate that the orchestration theme of a causal recipe may vary in response to an external fac tor. Hence, it is possible that, in the configurational approach, there also exist boundary conditions for conjunctura causation that can shape the interdependence among multiple causal factors of a causal recipe in bringing about th outcome.

A handful of studies have begun to consider moderation in the configurational approach. They first used the traditional correlational method for identifying the moderator such as Structural Equation Modelling (SEM), and then incorporated the moderator into configurational analysis (Hernández-Perlines, 2016). Some other studies examined moderation in the configurational approach through splitting data analysis (Apetrei et al., 2016). Although these stud ies attempted to incorporate the moderator into complex configurations, their efforts were piecemeal. They exam ined moderation effects in an inconsistent manner and did not explicitly connect the theorising of moderation and the configurational approach. The dominant approach to theorising and identifying moderation is still based on th correlational view; whereas there is little consensus on the moderation mechanism in the configurational approac (Carte & Russell, 2003). As a result, there remain challenges in regard to systemically theorising, testing, and inter preting moderation in the configurational approach.

To advance our understanding of moderation in the configurational approach, we start by discussing how a mod erator can be characterised. A moderator by nature can be understood as an extraneous factor that interacts with the causal unit and modifies the primary mechanism (Koeske, 1992). In the correlational view, the primary mechanism refers to the causal relationship between the independent variable and the dependent variable; and the causa unit in this view is the independent variable (Baron & Kenny, 1986). In contrast, in the configurational approach, th primary mechanism refers to the orchestration themes that capture how multiple interdependent causal factors in a causal recipe can lead to the presence of an outcome; and the causal unit in this approach is the causal recip (Furnari et al., 2021). Hence, we theorise that the moderator in the configurational approach is the extraneous factor that can modify the orchestration theme of a causal recipe in bringing about the outcome. Table 1 summarises the key characteristics of a moderator. First, by definition, a moderator as an extraneous factor should be independent of the causal unit (Baron & Kenny, 1986). In the correlational view, a moderator affect the causal relationship with the independent variable remaining unchanged. Meanwhile, in the configurationa approach, a moderator as the extraneous factor is independent of the causal recipe and affects the orchestration theme without changing the causal factors incorporated in the causal recipe. Second, moderation effects can b reflected in the variation in the primary mechanism between the causal unit and the outcome. In the correlationa view, the variation in the primary mechanism can be reflected in the changes in the strength and/or direction of the causal relationship caused by the inclusion of the moderator (Baron & Kenny, 1986). Meanwhile, in the configurational approach, the variation in the primary mechanism can be reflected in the changing interdependence among the causal factors in the causal recipe after the introduction of the moderator (Greckhamer et al., 2018). Hence, th moderation effects in the configurational approach can be captured by the variation in the interdependence of th causal factors leading to the given outcome with the inclusion of a moderator (Fiss et al., 2013). The theorization of moderation in the configurational approach can help researchers examine how the theoretical environment is interacting with the orchestration theme. This can improve our understanding of the boundaries of the applicability of theories by incorporating boundary conditions in configurational theorising.

## 3 | IDENTIFYING MODERATION USING QCA

## 3.1 | QCA as a set-theoretic method

With the growing importance of the configurational approach in extant social science research, set-theoretic methods have emerged to capture complex configurations in data analysis (Goertz & Mahoney, 2012; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). They provide a new perspective for concept formation (i.e., components of a concept) and typology creation (i.e., cases classified on multiple dimensions) (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Among the current set-theoretic methods, QCA has been found to be a powerful tool to handle causal complexity (Park et al., 2020) Compared with other set-theoretic methods, QCA has advantages in extracting knowledge from data (Fiss, 2007) First, beyond simply creating typologies or forming concepts in basic set-theoretic methods, the aim of QCA is to provide causal interpretations for observed phenomena, which allows for exploring and validating theories in practice (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Specifically, the process of QCA involves a reference to an outcome, which acts as the base for the formation of causal interpretations. Second, QCA uses the truth table as a function to visualise an analyse the features of causal complexity, accommodating multiple paths to a given outcome (i.e., equifinality (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Third, based on the data presented in the truth table, QCA makes use of the principles of logical minimization to generate common knowledge. This approach provides results in a more parsimoniou yet logically equivalent manner, by searching for commonalities and differences among cases that lead to the sam outcome (Berg-Schlosser et al., 2009). In contrast, other set-theoretic methods do not rely on logical minimization approaches to data analysis like QCA. For example, Coincidence Analysis (CNA) is another set-theoretic tool for identifying causal inferences that analyzes data using bottom-up logic (Whitaker et al., 2020). In contrast, the minimization process through standard inference rules from Boolean algebra in QCA is conducted from a top-dow approach, allowing for a more focused analysis of a particular outcome as well as associated effects around the main causal inferences (Whitaker et al., 2020). Given its advantages, a growing amount of research is applying QCA as a focal approach in theory development and testing (Park et al., 2020). Hence, to identify and test moderation in the configurational approach, we focus on QCA as the focal method in this study.

Ragin (1987) first proposed the idea of using QCA as a method based on set theory to interpret and analyse social science data. Interest in this method has been growing dramatically, which raised large-scale debates on empir ical methodologies. As neither a purely qualitative nor quantitative method, QCA is usually presented as a third option between quantitative statistical techniques and case-based methodology (Goertz & Mahoney, 2012). As a hybrid method that involves the characteristics of both quantitative and qualitative methodologies, QCA combines the advantages of both. The results obtained through QCA are based on the qualitative understanding of cases, which relies heavily on the perspective the researcher adopts in determining membership scores and considered con ditions of cases. Moreover, QCA provides an alternative way to quantitatively structure the findings for a specific outcome in the manner of combined conditions. Through a systematic and formalised process, QCA illustrates th procedure of generating results and provides higher visibility and consistency than traditional case-based studie (Greckhamer et al., 2018).

With the proliferation of QCA-based studies in recent years, the theoretical foundation of QCA rooted in equifinality and multiplicity has been systematically discussed and summarised under the frame of set theor (Misangyi et al., 2017). Specifically, using QCA from a set-theoretic perspective not only helps explain analytic features in a succinct manner but also makes use of mathematics, principles, and practices in social science simulta neously (Roig-Tierno et al., 2016). A recent review of QCA-based studies in the IS area by Soto Setzke et al. (2020 shows that QCA is mostly applied to analyse questionnaire-based survey data and is widely used as the main approach in extant studies. Moreover, despite the increasing popularity of QCA in various areas, it is still an emerging method in the area of IS. Its benefits have not yet been fully unlocked despite its potential to transcend the qualitative-quantitative divide in the IS area, which also leads to the calling for research in the IS domain (Iannacci et al., 2021). Thus, to better use the advantages of QCA to improve our understanding of complex digital phenomen and generate new insights, it becomes imperative to deploy QCA for examining moderation that is dominantly rooted in correlation-based methods

## 3.2 | An overview of extant literature on QCA-based moderation

Given the differences between variance-based methods and QCA in terms of analytic approaches, types of relation ships, and model-building foundations, the original statistical logic may not be applicable to QCA (Levallet et al., 2020). Recently, a handful of studies have started to discuss the applicability of QCA in investigating moderation (Apetrei et al., 2016; Reyes-Mercado, 2018; Yong et al., 2020). It is argued that the conjunctural causation achieved through QCA could vary under certain boundary conditions (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). These studie have proposed different approaches to identify the moderating mechanism in the conjunctural causation for a specific outcome (Apetrei et al., 2016; Franklin & Marshall, 2019; Yong et al., 2020). Although they sought to provid insights into how QCA can be used for moderation testing, these studies applied QCA in an inconsistent manner to identify the moderator and interpret the moderation mechanism.

## 3.2.1 | Moderator testing using QCA in existing approaches

To test a moderator, one popular stream in QCA-based studies is to combine variance-based methods (e.g., SEM and multi-variate regression) and QCA: a moderator will first be proposed and examined in the variance-based method and then QCA will serve as the second method to further explore changes in the configurations for confirmatory or complementary purposes (Ho et al., 2016). In this approach, the moderator identified from the theory is validated mostly relying on the variance-based method. By treating the moderator in the same way as other causal factors in QCA, the differences between the moderator and the causal factors in the configurational approach are disregarded (Yong et al., 2020). Because the moderator can rarely be distinguished from other causal factors in the QCA method QCA in this approach only serves as a complementary analysis for variance-based methods, limiting its explanatory power. Additionally, because the moderator is not distinguished in QCA, it is difficult to observe how the presence or absence of the moderator changes the results. This makes it challenging to interpret the moderation mechanism in a systematic manner.

Another stream for testing a moderator in QCA-based studies relies on the separation of data by creating two datasets based on the proposed moderator as the separator (Apetrei et al., 2016). This approach no longer relies on the variance-based method as the foundation, and the moderator in QCA can be tested by performing QCA twic for the two datasets, respectively. Like the multi-group regression in variance-based methods, this approach depend on the comparison of the QCA results in different datasets (Ho et al., 2016). Based on the two rounds of analysis the variations in the configurations serve as evidence for the validation of a moderator. A critical limitation of this approach is that it does not explicitly specify what kind of changes could be treated as evidence for a valid moderator. Criticism has been raised against this approach because, given the complexity of conjunctural causation in QCA it is unconvincing to fully attribute the changes in configurations to the introduction of the moderator. Further, th separation of datasets restricts the value of the moderator to 0/1 (i.e., for two separated datasets) for the analysis of crisp-set QCA (cs/QCA), which can hardly be applied to calibration strategies in multi-value QCA (mv/QCA) or fuzzy-set QCA (fs/QCA) for complex situations.

## 3.2.2 | Moderation interpretation using QCA in existing approaches

Although prior studies have proposed different ways of explicating how a moderator affects the conjunctural causa tion of a given outcome using QCA, most of them tend to agree that the moderation mechanism should be illustrated through changes in causal recipes leading to the outcome (Apetrei et al., 2016; Reyes-Mercado, 2018; Yong et al., 2020).

One way to interpret moderation is by comparing the number of causal factors in different configurations. It sep arates the results into two groups: one with the moderator in presence condition and the other with the moderato in absence or “do not care” condition. This method proposes that if the moderator can hinder the emergence of an outcome, the number of causal factors in the configuration in the former group will be more than that in the latter group. For example, Yong et al. (2020) found that a country with high-level cultural tightness (i.e., the moderator needs more culture value dimensions to promote creativity than a country with low-level cultural tightness. How ever, directly comparing the number of causal factors in different configurations neglects the interdependence of th causal factors in the configurations, given the equifinality feature of QCA. Additionally, the moderator is treated as a causal factor in data analysis without distinguishing it from other causal factors. Thus, separating results into two groups based on the moderator's condition in the configuration and comparing the results may not be sufficient to interpret moderation.

Aside from the approach involving conducting QCA once in data analysis, some studies adopt a two-ste approach to interpret moderation. Specifically, they divide the data into two groups, one before the inclusion of the moderator and the other after the inclusion of the moderator, and then they run QCA twice for the two dataset respectively (Apetrei et al., 2016). The two-step approach also relies on the comparison of results. Different from th one-step approach, which focuses on the differences in the number of causal factors in different configurations, the two-step approach concerns differences in causal factors incorporated in causal recipes. For example, using the two step approach, Apetrei et al. (2016) investigated the moderation effect of a financial crisis on the configuration of factors that could lead to entrepreneurship. Their results reveal that causal recipes for entrepreneurship involve dif ferent causal factors before and after the crisis. For example, strong demand is no longer important for entrepreneurship after a crisis. One limitation of the two-step approach is that it may induce selection bias by separating the data hindering the results' reliability (Apetrei et al., 2016). Another critical issue with the two-step approach is that it inter prets moderation as changes in the causal unit (i.e., causal factors in the causal recipe) of an outcome, whereas a moderator as an extraneous factor should be independent of the causal unit without affecting the causal factors incorporated in the causal recipe. Although this approach attempts to offer some insights into how moderatio works, it is not grounded in the proper theorization of moderation, limiting its theoretical validity

In sum, the interpretation of moderation using QCA in current studies is relatively atheoretical. A systematic consideration of the well matching between theorization of moderation and empirical investigation using QCA is lacking, which limits contributions to both theory-building and practical implications (Schneider & Wagemann, 2010)

## 3.3 | Guidelines for testing and interpreting moderation using QCA

Given the limitations of existing approaches, researchers are confronted with the challenge of testing and inter preting moderation using QCA systematically and consistently with theorization. Going beyond prior approaches the current study overcomes the mismatch between theorization and methods and offers a set of guidelines on how to apply QCA to examine moderated configurational relationships

## 3.3.1 | A two-stage approach

Given that moderation is used to frame the boundary conditions of causal relationships (Carte & Russell, 2003), w argue that the examination of its influence should be separated into two progressive stages. In the first stage, we use QCA to achieve primary conjunctural causation without the consideration of the proposed moderator; and in th second stage, we include the proposed moderator and run QCA again to examine its influence on primary conju nctural causation by checking how the moderator affects the interdependence among causal factors in causa recipes.

The benefits of the two-stage approach for examining moderation using QCA are summarised as follows. First, the two-stage approach can clearly produce the primary conjunctural causation in the first stage, which serves as a foundation for discussing moderating influences in the second stage. Compared to the one-step approach, thi approach provides a solid base for comparing the results before and after the inclusion of the moderator. Through a comparison of the interdependence of causal factors in causal recipes between the two stages of analysis, th changes brought by the moderator can be checked more explicitly.

Second, the prior literature suggests that the gap created by the division of data may statistically cut off the relationships among datasets (Preacher et al., 2016). In that case, the results obtained can merely reflect two indepen dent realities under two different situations. Different from the two-step approach, the data in this proposed approach are not divided into two groups. Instead, the datasets used for QCA in the two stages stay identical except for the inclusion of the moderator in the second stage. The unified dataset provides a shared context for the exploration of the boundary conditions of the conjunctural causation. It not only alleviates the issue of selection bias but also leads to more comparable results for the further illustration of moderation

Third, given that a moderator should be theoretically independent of causal factors, prior QCA approaches mostly fail to distinguish the proposed moderator from causal factors of the outcome in the analysis. In contrast, in the two-stage approach, we use QCA to identify how causal factors combine to bring about the outcome in the first stage and achieve the primary conjunctural causation. We bring in the moderator in the second stage to observe how the moderator interacts with the primary conjunctural causation. By doing so, we treat the moderator differ ently from causal factors, consistent with the theorization of moderation

Fourth, previous discussions on moderation using QCA have not reached a consensus on validating moderation in data analysis. One critical challenge is to decompose changes in results into direct causal influence and moderating influence. Based on the theorization of moderation, a moderator should alter the interdependence of the causal factors in the causal recipe leading to the outcome while causal factors of the causal recipe remain unaffected. In the two-stage approach, we consider each primary identified causal recipe as the causal unit and treat it as a whole when interacting with the moderator. By doing so, we not only reveal the direct effects of the combined causal factors in the causal recipe but also capture the changes in the interdependence of the causal factors within the causal recipe This allows the moderating effects to become apparent. Based on this, we argue that a valid moderator should moderate a causal recipe in the form of a sufficient condition in the configuration without changing the causal factors within the configuration in the second stage of the analysis. If the moderator does not appear as a salient conditio in the configuration, the statistical support can be insufficient for attributing changes in results to the moderator. This is in line with previous studies' suggestion that the moderator is vital for the existence of moderation effect and the interpretation of changes in results (Apetrei et al., 2016; Yong et al., 2020).

In sum, compared with pre-existing QCA approaches, the two-stage approach proposed in this study provides a better explanation for how a moderator can influence conjunctural causation by focusing on changes in the interdependence among causal factors in the causal recipe. The approach involves developing a systematic mechanism to identify and capture changes between the two stages through the inclusion of the moderator. Statisti cally, without separating the dataset, this approach makes it possible for the analysis to be applied to mv/QCA and fs/QCA. Theoretically, whereas existing approaches like the two-step approach focus more on how to reduce theo retical ambiguity by investigating changes in causal factors of an outcome, the proposed two-stage approac attempts to capture theoretical complexity by identifying a moderator where causal influences and moderating influ ences can be explicitly distinguished and decomposed.

## 3.3.2 | Requirements for QCA-based moderation

Although past studies have made some initial efforts to validate moderation using QCA, the requirements for a valid moderation still remain vague and inconsistent (Denk & Lehtinen, 2014). Rooted in the theorization of moderation the current study has developed requirements for QCA-based moderation validation using the proposed two-stage approach.

Theoretically, a moderator should alter the interdependence of causal factors in the causal recipe. According to Fiss (2011), the interdependence among causal factors in a QCA configuration can be considered from two aspects (1) the combination condition (i.e., presence/absence/do not care) of each causal factor in the causal recipe and (2) the evidence strength of the corresponding condition for each causal factor in the causal recipe. To reflect th evidence strength of the corresponding condition, Fiss (2011) proposed the concept of coreness to classify the condition into core and peripheral ones. The concept refers to the reliability and consistency of a condition in generating the expected outcome. It is identified from the comparison between parsimonious and intermediate solutions in QCA (Fiss, 2011). Specifically, the core conditions are the ones appearing in both parsimonious and intermediat solutions, whereas the peripheral conditions are the ones appearing only in intermediate solutions. Because the intermediate solutions introduce only easy counterfactuals in simplifying assumptions, they could signal the “core” (vs. “peripheral”) conditions that persist under more general situations (i.e., parsimonious solutions). The core condi tions are believed to have stronger evidence in generating the expected outcome. Consistent with this logic, Fiss (2011) still deemed parsimonious solution to be important, opting to present analytical results in the format of intermediate solution because it contains the missing information (i.e., “peripheral” conditions) in parsimonious solutions (Misangyi et al., 2017). Since the theoretical logic put forth by Fiss (2011) has been embraced in recent IS studies (Pappas & Woodside, 2021; Park et al., 2020; Park & Mithas, 2020), we feel that it acts an appropriate theoretica anchor on which to guide the conceptualization of moderation effect in QCA

Based on the above, we first argue that for a moderation effect to be identified in the second stage, changes in the interdependence of causal factors in the causal recipe should not involve any changes in the emergence or disappearance of causal factors in the causal recipe derived in the first stage. Specifically, this means the conditions that casual recipes are composed of should be consistent in two ways. First, the status of the presence/absence condition should not become do not care , and no new condition should emerge in the recipe except for the moderator condi tion. Second, the status of presence and absence conditions should remain the same, and no transitions of status from presence to absence or vice versa should be observed. If the two rules are not met, there is a change of a causa recipe rather than a change of the orchestration theme. According to the theorization of moderation in configura tional approach, a moderator ought to influence the orchestration theme rather than the causal recipe. The chang of a causal recipe is beyond the scope of moderating effect. Therefore, it is proposed that every single causal recipe identified in the first stage should be treated as the causal unit for analysis, and any changes in the structure of th causal recipe should reject the validation of the proposed moderator in the second stage. The first requirement for moderation is that there should be no changes in the causal factors within the causal recipe from the first to the second stage.

Second, for specific changes in the interdependence among causal factors, two additional requirements should be considered. The second requirement for moderation is that there should be at least one moderated configuration that has change(s) of core/peripheral conditions (i.e., from core to peripheral or from peripheral to core) between the two stages The third requirement for moderation is that in at least one of the moderated configurations, the moderator should be a core presence condition. For the second requirement, if there is no change in the interdependence among causal fac tors in any of the causal recipes before and after the inclusion of a moderator, the moderating effect may not exist Thus, change(s) of core/peripheral conditions should be observed as a basic requirement for the existence of poten tial moderating influences. For the third requirement, the rationale behind it is that it can guarantee the effect th moderator imposes to be relatively independent. The coreness of the presence of the moderator can provide strong evidence that it consistently influences the interdependence of causal factors in the causal recipe. If the moderator appears as a peripheral condition in the moderated configuration, there can be concerns that it may be a norma causal condition that interacts with other causal factors in the second stage.

## 4.1 | Research motivation

The use of collaborative technologies at work is pervasive, especially in the post-pandemic workplace. Although the use of collaborative technologies facilitates employees' communications and teamwork, it may also generate technostress. Technostress is the stress phenomenon caused by the use of information technology (IT) (Ayyagar et al., 2011; Tarafdar et al., 2007). In our context, we refer to technostress as the stress generated from the use of collaborative technologies for work-related tasks. Technostress is associated with various negative consequences such as a decrease in employees' performance, satisfaction, and commitment. It thus represents an important issu that organisations should address (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008). To prevent or alleviat technostress, it is vital to understand what causes it.

The existing literature suggests that technostress is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon, the salient aspects of which include different IT features and social contexts (Salo et al., 2022; Tarafdar et al., 2007). However, knowl edge of how multiple theoretical perspectives compete or complement each other in understanding technostress a a multifaceted phenomenon remains limited (Ayyagari et al., 2011, Tarafdar et al., 2007). To fill in the gap, this stud intends to capture the important causal complexity of technostress based on the configurational approach and dis cuss how complex causal explanations of technostress vary across different conditions with QCA (Park et al., 2020)

Prior studies on technostress found that technostress can be attributed to IT characteristics (Ayyagari et al., 2011, Tarafdar et al., 2007). Although extant research has provided rich insights into the effects of IT charac teristics on technostress, most studies have adopted a variance-based approach and examine

d the net effects of individual IT characteristics. The findings on the role of IT characteristics are complex, and little attention has been paid to the combined and complementary effects of IT characteristics on technostress. Fur ther, IT characteristics may influence technostress from different perspectives, such as differentiating users' engagement with information via information features or shaping users' interactions with the system through system features. The interplay between information features and system features is likely to affect technostress. Therefore there is a need to develop a deeper understanding of how IT characteristics can combine in configurations to give rise to technostress. Moreover, previous research has acknowledged the importance of an organisation's social envi ronment as an external factor that can moderate the causal effects of IT characteristics on employee well-being in general. However, studies on technostress have predominantly focused on the direct net effects of organisation fac tors (Wang et al., 2008). Hence, we believe it will be interesting to examine how an organisation's social environment can play a moderating role in the configurational interplay of IT characteristics leading to technostress. This woul shed light on how the interdependence of causal IT characteristics leading to the outcome of technostress varie across diverse social environments. Considering the above, this illustrative study aims to adopt the configurationa approach to investigate how an organisation's social environment moderates the causal recipe of IT characteristics for technostress.

In particular, this study focuses on four IT characteristics that have received much attention in the technostress related literature: information relevance, information equivocality, pace of change, and invasion of privacy. The first two characteristics represent the information features of collaborative technologies, and the latter two reflect the system features (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2008). Specifically, information relevance is associated with information-induced work overload, information equivocality involves information-induced communication overload pace of change is related to dynamic-driven system overload, and invasion of privacy reflects privacy-driven system insecurity (D'Arcy et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2016; Srivastava et al., 2015). We expect different types of overloads and system insecurity to be interdependent causal conditions for technostress. IT characteristics may have conjunctura causation effects because different overloads may complement or substitute each other while complementing system insecurity. To illustrate, an employee may be overwhelmed by a large number of work-related ambiguou messages received from a collaborative technology that monitors all work-related activities and invades employees privacy (i.e., high information relevance, high information equivocality, high privacy invasion), causing high level of technostress. Another employee may use a frequently updated collaborative technology for work-related information exchange and worry that the technology monitors all work-related activities and invades employees' privacy (i.e., high information relevance, high pace of change, high invasion of privacy), also contributing to high level of technostress.

In terms of an organisation's social environment, the subjective social support norm appears to be an interesting factor to explore (Maier et al., 2015; Polites et al., 2018). It refers to the perceived extent to which one's social envi ronment expects support from people (Maier et al., 2015; Yan et al., 2013). In this study, we define subjective socia support norm as an employee's perceived extent to which the organisational social environment expects instrumen tal, informational, and emotional assistance among employees. Prior research has investigated social support as a moderator when studying workplace well-being and organisational behaviour. However, it has reported inconsistent results in terms of its moderating effects (Yan et al., 2013). When bringing in subjective social support norm as a condition, we expect that their absence and presence may change the interdependence of IT characteristics in the causa recipe. For example, high subjective social support norm may facilitate the communication process while urging peo ple to help others. Thus, it may affect the interplay of information equivocality and information relevance suppressing the effect of information equivocality in the causal configuration

In sum, given that the four IT characteristics may have combined effects in promoting technostress and that sub jective social support norm may moderate such combined effects, the current study explores how the interplay of information relevance, information equivocality, the pace of change, and invasion of privacy leading to technostres varies across different levels of subjective social support norm. In doing so, we extend the prior literature on technostress by exploring the conjunctural causation of IT characteristics and by identifying the moderating influ ence of the organisation's social environment on the focal conjunctural causation.

In cs/QCA, a case is either a member of a set or not a member, with membership values of 1 and 0 (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Because of this restriction, cs/QCA is not widely adopted in IS and other management studies, given the fact that cases in reality always manifest adherence to criteria in varying degrees (Pappas & Woodside, 2021; Rihoux & Ragin, 2008). Although mv/QCA allows for multiple categories, it operates on multi-value sets instead of fuzzy sets. Membership values are often manually set, largely depending on analysts' experience an expertise (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). In contrast, fs/QCA addresses the limitations of cs/QCA and mv/QCA by allowing differences in the degree of set membership to be captured with differentiations between 0 and 1 (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Thus, fs/QCA can analyse data in a superior manner given its flexibility (Rihoux & Ragin, 2008). We believe that fs/QCA is more appropriate for our analysis

We first transformed the latent variable scores of focal constructs into membership scores through calibration (Berg-Schlosser et al., 2009). Consistent with previous empirical work deploying subjective measurement (Dus¸a, 2018; Ragin, 2014), we designated the 95%, 50%, and 5% interval for each variable as full membership (i.e., fully in), crossover point of maximum membership ambiguity (i.e., crossover), and full non-membership (i.e., full out) respectively. After specifying the three breakpoints for each variable, raw scores were calculated into member ship scores through the calibration function built-in analysis software (i.e., fs/QCA 3.0).

According to Soto Setzke et al. (2020), one critical issue in extant QCA studies is that they have largely neglected necessity analyses, which may affect the results of sufficiency analyses. Thus, before conducting moderation analysis in QCA, we followed the guideline Mattke et al. (2022) proposed to first examine whether each condition is necessary for the outcome to occur. Because outcomes are represented in sets, necessary conditions are tested for both high-level and low-level outcomes separately (Ragin, 2014). To account for noise in the data, randomness, measurement inaccuracies of data, the high or low level of a condition needs to exceed a consistency threshold of 0.90 to be considered a valid necessary condition (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Moreover, for the coverage score of the tested conditions, the widely accepted rule of thumb for the coverage threshold is 0.60. This is to ensure that the identified necessary condition is not trivial. Trivial necessary condition is a much bigger superset than the outcome (Mattke et al., 2021). Moreover, a trivial necessary condition may be caused if the condition lacks an obvious varia tion in the entire dataset. This means that the value of the condition is almost the same and does not change among cases. To control for this type of trivial necessary condition, it is recommended to calculate the relevance of neces sity (RoN) score (Ragin, 2014). The rule of thumb is that a necessary condition should exceed an RoN score of 0.60 (Mattke et al., 2021).

Following the convention of configu ration results, we identified whether a causal element is present (solid circle ●) or absent (crossed-out circle ⨂) and is core (large circle) or peripheral (small circle). ## 4.4 | Interpreting the results

Using fs/QCA, we identified three equifinal causal recipes that produce technostress (S1–S3). Our results reveale that subjective social support norm can moderate part of the interplay of IT characteristics in causal recipes for causing technostress. Specifically, for S1, including subjective social support norm does not affect the interdependence of IT characteristics in the configuration. For S2 and S3. after including subiective social support norm. the interdependence of IT characteristics in the two configurations is affected. That is, subjective social support norm moderates S2 and S3 but does not moderate S1.

Results show that, for S1, high pace of change, high privacy invasion, and high information relevance are the core conditions that can combine to promote high level of technostress, and incorporating subjective social support norm does not affect the interdependence of these causal factors in the configuration. Frequent changes to IT systems increase the complexity of using collaborative technologies and are associated with system overload (D'Arcy et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2016; Srivastava et al., 2015). High invasion of privacy creates a sense of intrusiveness where people feel they are being monitored and lose their sense of privacy (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Receiving work-related information stimulates work overload (D'Arcy et al., 2014). The combination of these three causal factors has a strong causal relationship with high-level technostress, regardless of whether subjective social support norm is considered or not.

When the information is not directly relevant to work, frequent changes of systems, ambiguous information, and low level of privacy invasion together produce technostress. In contrast, under low level of subjective social support norm, the importance of low information relevance is weakened in its interplay with other causal factors in the causa recipe. Results show that, with low-level subjective social support norm, low information relevance shifts from a core condition to a peripheral condition in the causal recipe. To interpret such moderating effects, one can associate the low-level subjective social support norm with impediments to seeking and offering help that could result in a lack of reciprocity and resistance to social interactions (Huang & Zhou, 2021; Maier et al., 2015). Under such conditions, employees may not feel comfortable interacting with each other. They may feel stressed even if conversations on collaborative technologies are not so irrelevant to work. Therefore, when there is a low level of subjective social support norm, low information relevance tends to be less important when combined with other causal factors for pro ducing technostress. Low level of subjective social support norm also means that employees have to handle th challenges of system updates by themselves, which suggests that high pace-of-change remains to have a strong causal relationship with high level of technostress when in combination with other causal factors. Additionally, wit low subjective social support norm, employees are less inclined to figure out who they are engaging with when using collaborative technologies. And when the system has low invasion of privacy, employees are unable to identify eac other, which causes stress during interactions. Thus, under low subjective social support norm, invasion of privacy remains to be a core condition in the causal recipe (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Furthermore, under such an environment employees may not ask for clarification, and thus the ambiguity of information will remain significant in the causa recipe for promoting technostress (Maier et al., 2015). Thus, low-level subjective social support norm weakens the importance of low information relevance, whereas high pace of change, low privacy invasion, and high information equivocality remain to be important in the independence of causal factors in the causal recipe for high technostress. Therefore, based on these results, this illustrative study effectively expounds on the variations in the interdependence of causal factors when the moderator is introduced.

## 4.5 | Theoretical and practical contributions

The illustrative study has demonstrated the effectiveness of our proposed guidelines for identifying, testing, and interpreting moderation in the configurational approach. We extend the prior literature on technostress not only b exploring its configurational multiplicity but also by investigating the moderating role of social environment in such configurational interplays. Specifically, we contribute to technostress research in the following aspects

First, we extend the current understanding of technostress by capturing its configurational multiplicity and theoretical complexity. By identifying the interplay of IT characteristics in configurations, our results show that these IT characteristics can combine and cause technostress in several equifinal ways. We find that information features com pete with or complement each other, as do system features, and these two types of features are also interdependent in causing technostress. Moreover, technostress may be influenced by various IT characteristics from different perspectives (Tarafdar et al., 2007). This study shows, with multiple orchestration themes that, technostress may be cau sed via different mechanisms. By revealing the interdependence of information features and system features, w highlight various potential theoretical perspectives for interpreting IT characteristics' influence on technostress Thus, our study provides additional insights into technostress that supplement the widely used correlational view of studying technostress and offers a new lens for theorising antecedents of technostress in future research.

Second, we identified and interpreted the moderating effects of subjective social support norm in the configura tional approach, and through that, we open a new direction for studying the configurational complexity of technostress. We find that the importance of information features and system features in causal recipes varies depending on the level of subjective social support norm. While the causal factors in the causal recipes of technostress remain unchanged, their interdependence may alter in terms of the causal factor's relative importance when there are different levels of subjective social support norm. This finding advances the knowledge of technostress by revealing the variation of IT characteristics' multiplicities across different social environments enriching theoretical insights into how social environment moderates the antecedents of technostress in the configu rational approach.

Practically, our findings can guide practitioners to alleviate the negative impacts of technostress via proper design of collaborative technologies that are suitable for their organisation's social environment. Instead of merely focusing on individual influences of single IT characteristics, practitioners should pay more attention to the combinations of these factors that lead to technostress. Generally, if the manager is uncertain of the organisation's level of subjective social support norm, or if they want a generic solution, they should seek to ensure that the collaborativ technology does not invade users' privacy, the technology is used to share a wide range of information, and there is no frequent change to the technology. If an organisation has a high level of subjective social support norm, it shoul be cautious when designing collaborative technologies and ensure that such technology respects users' privacy, has low tolerance for ambiguous and equivocating messages, and can be used for various purposes. However, if an organisation has low level of subjective social support norm, it should avoid frequent changes to the technology, avoid equivocating information, and allow for sufficient inclusion of personal information in the technology. Ideally, the pri mary use of the technology should be for work.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

The configurational approach provides an opportunity to advance our understanding of multifaceted phenomena by developing multiple conjunctural causations of an outcome. However, there remain challenges in identifying moder ating mechanisms of conjunctural causation in the configurational approach. The current study thus aims to theorise the moderation relationship in the configurational approach and offer a guide for examining such moderation using QCA. We then show how these guidelines may be applied through an illustrative study in the context of technostress.

## 5.1 | Implications for configurational theorising

First, going beyond the focus on the correlation analytical view in moderation investigations, this study considers moderating influences using the configurational approach. Despite existing efforts to identify boundary conditions of conjunctural causation (Bell et al., 2014), there is still a need for a more systematic approach to theorise and empiri cally explain moderation in the configurational approach. This study bridges the gap in the literature by discussing the significance of moderation in the configurational approach and by providing guidelines for theorising such mod erations. It provides the foundation for theoretically explaining how the moderating condition can alter the interpla of the causal factors in the causal recipe. Based on the results, researchers can theoretically explain moderated con figurational relationships precisely. Supported by illustrative studies in the context of technostress, we empirically demonstrate how the organisation's social environment (i.e., subjective social support norm), an extraneous factor theoretically independent of IT characteristics, affects the interdependence of specific IT characteristic (i.e., information relevance, information equivocality, pace of change, and invasion of privacy), leading to employees technostress. New insights into the current area of technostress are generated by identifying the boundary condi tions of technology-level causes leading to technostress. Our study provides empirical support for the notion that investigating boundary conditions of conjunctural causation makes us more aware of potential situational boundar conditions of our theories. We believe this study will elicit deeper discussions regarding building a more concrete foundation for understanding the boundary conditions of the multiple conjunctural causations of an outcome

Second, we expound on the testing and interpretation of moderation in the configurational approach using QCA through a set of guidelines. By applying the two-stage approach to the context of technostress, we identify the mod erating role of subjective social support norm and empirically demonstrate how the interdependence of IT characteristics causing technostress is changed when subjective social support norm is taken into consideration. By clearl showing the influence of the moderator through a companion of the main effects in the first stage and th moderation effects in the second stage, our illustrative study provides empirical support for the effectiveness of the two-stage method for identifying moderating influences in the configurational approach. The guidelines developed in this study are broadly applicable to IS research. They serve as tools that can be adapted and modi fied according to specific research questions in IS domains. They can also be used to generate new insights into theory development by identifying and assessing moderated configurational relationships. These insights will in turn add to our understanding of multifaceted digital phenomena and help increase the practical relevance of I research.

## 5.2 | Implications for practice

Given the complex ways in which digital technologies and organisations are fused with environments in digital phe nomena, a new approach is needed to understand the dynamic interactions among these elements for making better managerial decisions. Apart from contributing to configurational theorising, the proposed approach for identifying moderated configurations in this study also yields pragmatic implications for practitioners. Specifically, our approach alerts managers to recognise the important role of environments under which their resources and capabilities ar combined to bring out performance outcomes. Although organisations can take advantage of a combination of specific resources and capabilities to achieve good performance based on the current configurational approach they have limited knowledge of how these resources and capabilities can be more efficiently allocated under different conditions. The proposed approach can provide strategic insights into how the relative importance of resources and capabilities in combinations leading to a given outcome will change under a certain environment It also enables organisations to better place priorities and weights when allocating certain resources and capabilities. This in turn facilitates more realistic and efficient investment for minimising risks and achieving strate gic advantages.

## 5.3 | Limitations and future research

We have some suggestions for avenues for future research. First, researchers can build on this research for identify ing moderation in the configurational approach for more complex situations. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed guidelines through an illustrative study in the single context of technostress. Future studies can appl the guidelines to other complex empirical contexts to identify potential moderators of configurational relationships For example, researchers can apply this approach to capture the fusion of IT systems with the dynamics of organisa tions and the unrest of the environment through decomposing their effects that incorporate both conjunctural cau sation and moderation. It may reveal a more dynamic way in which organisations function. Moreover, this study onl considers the moderation effects under the situation of one moderator. Future research can extend this research b taking into consideration multiple moderators in the configurational approach. For example, as digital systems get more embedded into the work processes, closer attention could be paid to how the IT characteristics and the multiple social environment factors become entangled in the working context (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Apart from subjec tive social support norm, other social environment factors such as structure centralization, organisational integration co-worker competitiveness may also play important moderating roles in this context. By exploring multiple modera tors in the configurational approach, we can have a deeper understanding of why certain technologies can promote stressful situations. Furthermore, the present study employed percentile-based calibration in place of adopting th calibration thresholds of $\epsilon ,$ 4, and 2 for seven-point Likert scales as recommended by Mattke et al. (2022). The deci sion for employing percentile-based calibration is a deliberate one to account for skewness in data distribution Because the collected data exhibited skewed distributions for certain constructs, relying on uniform calibration values across constructs may fail to accurately capture discrepancies in their underlying characteristics. In contrast percentile-based calibration takes into consideration variations in distribution across causal conditions and “for most variables, the percentiles give the same values as if we had chosen directly the values of $\epsilon ,$ 4, and $_ { 2 " }$ (Pappas $\&$ Woodside, 2021, p. 7). But at the same time, we do acknowledge that percentile-based calibration may not accu rately represent the sample whenever focal constructs of interest are negatively skewed and we would encourage future research, which subscribes to our prescribed approach for identifying and interpreting moderation, to calibrat the data based on characteristics of the focal constructs of interest.

Furthermore, the approach proposed in this study can be extended by taking a more comprehensive consider ation of the method of OCA. First, the set of guidelines does not consider the features of different types of OCA (e.g., fs/QCA, cs/QCA) in data analysis. Future research can build on this research by identifying the key characteristics of operationalization for different types of QCA analytic approaches. It will improve the applicability of the approach for explaining complex digital phenomena and further contribute to the development of configurationa theories. In addition, there is an ongoing debate on the usage of intermediate solution which has culminated in two separate streams in interpreting the analytical results of QCA, relying on either intermediate $\tt o r$ parsimonious solution. Although this study uses intermediate solution and by extension, the concepts of core and peripheral conditions in our approach, we deem parsimonious solution to be important. Thus, future research can explore the usage of par simonious solutions in identifying moderation. Additionally, given that QCA is a rapidly evolving method, related studies are constantly introducing new updates to its applications. This dynamic nature provides massive possibilities to existing ways of data analysis in QCA, including the moderation analysis proposed in this study. Specifically although this study adopts parsimonious solution and core/peripheral concept to discuss moderation effects in QCA, they could be replaced with other alternative ways of capturing the interdependence among conditions. However, other principles, including using the same dataset for analysis, the comparison-based two-stage approach, and th constraints on condition changes, are more essential for the discussion of moderation effects in this study, and they are not limited to any specific analysis method. Therefore, the proposed moderation effect in this study is ready to evolve with the development of QCA and to provide insights for future studies.
