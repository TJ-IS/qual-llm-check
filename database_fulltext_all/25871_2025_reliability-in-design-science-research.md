## Abstract

Reliability, which is concerned with whether something performs as it should, is well-accepted as an important part of any scientific research, with recognised methods for assessing it in the natural sciences. Reliability is a prerequisite for validity, which assesses knowledge production. Design science research is unique in that it produces an artefact and makes contributions to a design knowledge base and is tasked with the duality of design and science. Therefore, reliability should be reconsidered to address the assumptions underlying this type of information systems research.

This paper proposes a Design Science Reliability Framework. The framework identifies the part of design science research that should be evaluated (artefact, methods, measures and design knowledge). It also considers when reliability should be assessed as a project progresses by differentiating synchronic versus diachronic evaluation. The framework is used to derive a set of techniques for establishing reliability that can be applied during the evaluation process to both a project's knowledge production and its artefacts. Application of the framework should assist design science researchers when considering how to assess their results.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Design science research focuses on developing new ways to approach complex challenges in information systems b creating and evaluating artefacts that can solve real-world problems or improve existing solutions. It invents a new purposeful artefact to address a generalized type of problem’ and evaluates it for solving problems of the same type (Venable &, Baskerville, 2012 p. 142) This kind of research has a science component and a design componen: (Baskerville et al., 2015). Science is focused on analysis; design on synthesis (Simon, 1996). The science component requires a scientific approach to research. The design component recognises the important role of a designer who might be required to demonstrate creativity in solving a design problem, based on the production of an artefact. The two basic activities of design science research are build and evaluate. Building involves constructing an artefact for a specific purpose. Evaluation assesses how well an artefact performs (March & Smith, 1995, p.254) Evaluation is crucial and required to demonstrate the utility and quality of the artefacts and knowledge outcomes, as well as the rigour of the research process (Venable et al., 2016).

Design science research is pluralistic, contextual and dynamic, producing various types of knowledge as a study pro gresses (Akoka et al., 2022; Baskerville et al., 2015). A design is an abstraction of a problem that is in a designer's mind (Hooker, 2004), which may be difficult to extract and repeat. The objective of scientific research is to produce knowledge so any academic study should be validated in order to make a contribution to science. For scientific knowledge, its qualit is generally expressed using reliability and, subsequently, validity (Kirk & Miller, 1986). Different types of research requir different types of validity criteria (Baskerville et al., 2023), yet the assessment of reliability and validity in design science i less common than the evaluation of efficacy or usefulness (Larsen et al., 2020; Prat et al., 2015)

Reliability is required to establish validity to ensure that research findings are accurate, consistent and trustwor thy. Although reliability in design science research is assumed to be similar to reliability in other forms of science, it may not be as easy to establish as in the natural science, since the outcomes of design science research can take on different forms: a concrete, material artefact or generalised design knowledge. The outcomes can also depend upon the context. However, unless we can assess the reliability of design science research, it will continue to be difficult to convince researchers in other fields of the scientific validity of the results

The question posed in this research is: ‘What does reliability mean for design science research and how can it be achieved?’ Our objective is to investigate how to establish such reliability. To do so, we propose a Design Science Reli ability Framework for identifying and assessing different forms of reliability in this type of research. The framework has three dimensions and provides a way to investigate different aspects of the design science research process. Th first dimension is the what of design science research (artefact, measures, method and design knowledge) and the second, the ‘when’ in the research process the research reliability is being assessed (synchronic versus dia chronic). These two dimensions are used to derive the third, how to establish reliability, presented as a set of eight possible techniques that could be helpful to researchers in formulating assessments of their studies. This, in turn should help to justify the validity of the research. In this manner, this paper is intended to help focus design scienc researchers to avoid a reliability gap that occurs when validity measures lack reliability. To carry out the research, w follow an approach to the development of a conceptual framework (Jaakkola, 2020)

This paper proceeds as follows. Related research on design science and concepts related to reliability are reviewed in Section 2. Section 3 provides an overview of our methodology. Section 4 presents the Design Science Reliability Framework, with proposed techniques for establishing reliability detailed in Section 5. Section 6 discusse how this research extends prior work and Section 7 concludes the paper.

## 2.1 | Design science research

Design science differs from other types of research because of the development of an artefact that must lead to additions to the (design) knowledge base. It is ‘fundamentally different from both the theory-building and theorytesting approaches, which model themselves after the natural sciences and seek explanation based on observation (Holmström et al., 2009, p. 67). Its characteristics have been described by Iivari (2015) and adapted by Brendel et al. (2021) as follows. (1) Design science research produces innovative and novel artefacts and design theories as its research outcomes. (2) The construction and evaluation of artefacts constitute the main research activities. (3) From an epistemological perspective, design science seeks utility over truth, rendering it pragmatic in nature. Table 2 summarises how these characteristics relate to design science reliability as presented in this paper

As its name suggests, design science research is an amalgamation of design and science. It delivers symbiotic results in which design benefits from scientific rigour and science benefits from practical design grounding. Both the validity of new theoretical knowledge and the novelty of the design must be justified (Baskerville et al., 2017) Knowledge is created by analysing a given design problem, synthesising the possible solutions based on the analysis and evaluating that solution.

To produce scientific knowledge, common expressions of the quality of such knowledge include validity and reli ability (Carmines & Zeller, 1979; Kirk & Miller, 1986). Validity is concerned with whether an output from a scientifi study is correct. (Gummesson, 2000). Reliability is concerned with the ‘stability, dependability, stability, predictabilit and accuracy of the results’ (Kerlinger, 1973, p. 42). According to Boudreau et al. (2001, p. 2): ‘reliability is a statement about measurement accuracy, i.e., the extent to which an instrument produces consistent or error-free result Since there is ‘no validity without reliability’, it is important to demonstrate reliability as a prerequisite for esta blishing validity (Lincoln & Guba, 1985, p. 42). Although research in information systems has studied validity in a vari ety of ways (Boudreau et al., 2001; Lukyanenko et al., 2015; Pillet et al., 2023; Schmitz & Storey, 2020), there ha been much less effort given to studying the validity of design science research processes and outcomes (Larsen et al., 2020).

To achieve high scientific quality, generally, any measure used should be valid, reliable and replicable (Bateson & Martin, 2021). These three are distinct concepts (Garner, 2005), even though reliability has been described as to ‘be about repeatability and the extent to which measurements are reproduced when a study is repeated under the same conditions’ (Bateson & Martin, 2021, p. 159). Reliability focuses on measurement accuracy (Boudreau et al., 2001; Rogers, 1995).

It is possible to replicate something, but it might not be reliable; for example, by using a scale that is calibrated 3 kg too high or too low.<sup>1</sup> Repeated applications of the scale will consistently provide relative, but not accurate, results. Another example is a tuning artefact used to tune an orchestra that is not at standard A440 pitch. This would be extremely problematic for musicians with perfect pitch or geographically dispersed orchestra members playing simultaneously, as in a virtual performance. These artefacts are replicable but not reliable. In contrast, an explosiv device artefact could be applied to implode a building. It is reliable for one instance, but not replicable. In design sci ence research, something might be both replicable and reliable. Brendel and Muntermann (2022), for example, focus on the replication of design theories (one type of design knowledge). Rosenkranz et al. (2017) report on repeate applications (multiple replications) of their artefact. In general, replicability is expensive for design science research so one way to view our work is, essentially, as a way to reduce such an expense.

Design science research fundamentally creates an artefact using a method. The selection and execution of th method impact the reliability of both the artefact and resulting design knowledge (e.g., principles and theories). Thus we start by considering the reliability of the method and how it increases the reliability of both the artefact an design knowledge. Since reliability is required for validity, the reliability of the artefact and design knowledge, as required outputs, impact the validity of the study. Evaluation of the artefact is a further requirement, so the mea sures used in the evaluation must also be reliable.

Thus, to validate a design science research study, the reliability of the artefact and design knowledge are required, as well as the method used to create the artefact and the measures used in the critical evaluation. Th validity of a design science research project increases confidence in the design science study being scientific and having the desired generalizability. Only then, can the results can be projected to other projects, consistent wit Simon (1969)'s argument that a design is useful only when it can solve related problems. In this way, reliability affect the generalisation, projectability and scientific quality.

## 2.2 | Role of reliability

Reliability in design science research is different from behavioural research and can also be established by other means (e.g., mathematical proof or logical deduction, or, perhaps, non-tangible means such as aesthetics (Baskerville et al., 2018)). The design knowledge (abstract solutions) created by this research plays a more important role in engi neering practise than theories in social science practise and tends to be more complex. Engineering involves formu lating a problem in such a way that it can be solved through design. Science focuses on formulating a question tha can be solved through investigation. The design process of engineering is somewhat similar to that of the scientifi method (Dieter & Schmidt, 2009). Both begin with existing knowledge, and gradually become more specific as they search for knowledge (in pure or basic science) or for a solution (in applied science). The main difference is that th engineering process focuses on design, creativity and innovation, whereas the scientific process emphasises discovery.<sup>2</sup>

There is a difference between the reliability of design science research and reliability in empirical research, since design science can be anchored in the artefact; specifically, in the instance solution (Lee et al., 2011). The artefact itself can be reliable. Reliability and validity are features of a study, implying an empirical context and empirical data For example, a mathematical proof (potentially based on the law of physics) may be at least as good at establishing reliable relationships between different constructs. Therefore, establishing the technical reliability of an artefac (e.g., that software is safe for landing an aircraft) could apply a mixture of (empirical) tests and mathematical proofs.

The engineering and computer science disciplines have established practises to evaluate reliability, which includ methods for artefact creation and measures for evaluation. An automobile producer, for example, primarily estab lishes the reliability of the parts delivered by its suppliers by auditing adherence to quality norms (e.g., ISO 9000) tha check whether a structured process exists and is adequately documented. Automobile producers also have test strat egies for checking reliability. They have different sampling strategies in that they typically focus on edge cases, rather than repeatedly evaluating standard cases. This is not so in standard empirical studies or design science evaluations.<sup>3</sup> There are also empirical evaluations of the artefact's utility. The strive for utility is implemented by defining a mean ingful scope and then measuring it

In other engineering efforts, reliability is a feature of an artefact that advances reliance; that is, it advances an observable human behaviour. Validity and replicability are features of a study such as that used in the evaluation Research in information systems, generally, focuses on more than just technical artefacts; it focuses on socio technical systems (which is beyond the scope of this research). Analogously, design science focuses on more tha the material artefact. It also has requirements for the production of design knowledge and an evaluation component.

There is a great deal of interest on assessing reliance on automation and artificial intelligence (AI), including gen erative AI (GenAI). There are also efforts to understand human trust in AI (Lukyanenko et al., 2022; Oyekunl et al., 2024). Reliance is an observable behaviour, in contrast to trust, which is an attitude that cannot be directl observed. It may be argued that reliability is a feature of an artefact that advances reliance; validity, reliability an replicability are features of a study.

An outcome of design science research is a contribution to the design knowledge base. Lee et al. (2011) stress the importance of theory in design science research, emphasising that design theories (as part of the knowledge base have specific components (meta-requirements, meta-design, design method, testable product hypotheses, testabl process hypotheses, etc.). Design theories ‘encompass a design process for applying a meta-design for the purpose of instantiating a designed artifact’ (Lee et al., 2011, p. 2). Lee et al. (2011) argue that the study of the instance prob lem is frequently similar to a classical (qualitative) empirical study (e.g., ethnography or experiments), even thoug the outcome variables are utility-based.

The abstract problem is grounded in some theory, and thus, reliability could follow the standards of behavioura research. The abstract solution (most importantly, the design principles) is typically described as an architecture or an algorithm (similar to engineering). This is part of the design knowledge produced. According to Walls et al. (1992) thi could be technology rules, or other forms. Much research has focused on the abstraction of design principles o design features or testable propositions.

## 2.3 | Problem solving research

Design science research is a problem-solving paradigm that focuses on providing prescriptive solutions that serve human purposes through the production of artefacts (March & Smith, 1995), and often conducted in an iterativ manner (Baskerville et al., 2023). The difference between the reliability of design research and reliability in empirica research can be anchored in the artefact, specifically, in the instance solution (Lee et al., 2011). The artefact can itsel be reliable. Validity and replicability are features of a study that have an empirical context and empirical data.

Contributions come from both the artefact produced and the abstract, design knowledge, found in (design knowledge base of principles, theories, and so on (Baskerville et al., 2015; Woo et al., 2014). As explained by Sonnenberg and Vom Brocke (2012, p. 384), the evaluation of the artefacts should be approached differentially compared to the study and evaluation of phenomena in the natural or human sciences’. This is because the scope and interest of design science research is not to explain how the world works through observations, theories, and so on, but rather ‘to shape the world by means of artefacts’. They further note that evaluations need to account for the emergent nature of the research and decisions made by the designers, which has implications for the ‘validity of knowledge created while the artifact emerges’.

## 2.4 | Artefact reliability

Design science research produces both an artefact and design knowledge. The reliability of an artefact requires it to function in a predictable way; for example, a machine learning model should behave in the same way given different data. Artefact reliability can also be dependent upon its goal or situation, which might be dynamic. This is because design science study is pluralist, experiencing different knowledge moments as it progresses (Baskerville & Myers, 2015). While law-like, design knowledge falls short of natural laws. Imperatives, rather than causals, charac terise design knowledge: Do a and b happen (not simply a causes b)? In practise, both a and b can be complex events In a future context, some variation of a may be sufficient for b to happen. It is the present and future usefulness of design knowledge imperatives that establishes reliability (and validity). Consequently, the goals of design knowledg are generative and inventive, even though they must adhere to variances in requirements and constraint (Baskerville et al., 2015).

In information systems research, there is a well-recognised need for both rigour and utility. Validity, broadly deals with the extent to which the credibility of findings and conclusions are established through evaluative proce dures (Trochim, 2006). In design science, validity refers to how well an artefact performs (March & Smith, 1995) and is a measure of whether the results from the research are correct. Validity can be crippled by low reliability becaus results that are unreliable cannot subsequently justify validity. Reliability must be established for both realised and realisable generalisations, a quality known as projectability, which relates to how the new research context and goal align with those of the projects from the knowledge base (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2014; Baskerville & Pries Heje, 2019).

Evaluation is important. Design science research results are typically anchored to the evaluation of an artefact.<sup>4</sup> If an artefact is unreliable, we cannot estimate the reliability of any evaluation measures, and, subsequently, the validity of any knowledge produced, including design principles or theories. Similarly, the method that produces the artefact must be reliable (Peffers et al., 2007). Reliable methods ensure that the design science research is indeed scientific.

## 2.5 | Additional perspectives and assumptions

Traditional conceptualizations of reliability often rest upon assumptions of repeatability, replicability and consistenc (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). However, in design science, we must consider a dynamic view of the world, where situation change and the outcome can be subjective, depending upon the purpose or types of design knowledge and artefacts.

The literature in science and engineering provides several forms of reliability estimates that can be applied to design science artefacts. These include measures, methods and design knowledge. Since design science researc depends on both the creation of an artefact and the subsequent contributions to design knowledge, it is important to consider each of these when assessing the reliability of a design science research artefact. The reliability of design knowledge should depend on the accuracy and completeness of the conveyed knowledge. Accuracy means that design knowledge correctly transfers the design principles and/or theory. Completeness means that the design knowledge extends to cover the context(s) in which the design is known to operate. Completeness is necessary because a designer seeking to apply this knowledge in a different context needs to know enough about the origina context(s) to adopt or adapt the knowledge as appropriate for a new or different context.

Design theory is divided into two types of knowledge: product (meta design) and process (design process) (Wall et al., 1992). The former is the design principles/theory and the latter, the adaptation process. Design knowledg accuracy without completeness (or vice versa) cannot be reliably reused. Research produces knowledge. Reliabilit characterises knowledge, and reliability characterises the artefacts produced by design science research.

Especially in qualitative methods, reliability is challenging and entails a repeated investigation that is expected to obtain the same result (Gummesson, 1991). In research approaches such as case studies, ethnography, and grounded theory, both the subjects and the situation or context within which the subjects appear naturally change over time Establishing reliability is even more difficult with interventionist methods, such as action and design science research because these methods purposefully introduce social changes or new artefacts into the research settings. Then, th concept of dependability provides an alternative to reliability (Guba, 1981) because it values the trustworthiness of knowledge instead of its truth value. Most social or design interventions do not have clear quantitative evidenc found in other kinds of research. The outcome measures are often loose notions of artefact properties; for example whether the task was completed with a valuable impact (Venable et al., 2016) or the dependability of an artefact in its capacity to perform failure-free in different use conditions (Piccoli et al., 2020)

## 2.8 | Synchronic versus diachronic

An important consideration related to reliability assessment is the time at which the assessment occurs, which w classify as synchronic or diachronic.

## 2.8.1 | Synchronic reliability

The assessment of synchronic reliability, or internal reliability is associated with single cases. Synchronic artefact reli ability is important because many design settings are idiographic: concrete, individual or unique (Baskervill et al., 2015). Design settings may also have social, cultural and behavioural aspects. Then, idiographic accounts can be used: narratives and understanding instead of observations and objectivity as the empirical means to assess arte fact reliability. It is an attempt to gain an understanding of a situation by ‘obtaining first-hand knowledge of the sub ject under investigation’ (Burrell & Morgan, 1979, p. 6).

Synchronic reliability helps researchers reconcile differences in multiple, simultaneous observations (Kirk & Miller, 1986). Synchronic reliability tests provide an opportunity to evaluate the reliability of related aspects at the same time, such as the performance of an artefact in two different, but simultaneous, settings. It can also be applied to different issues of a study: individualised characteristics; causal relationships of variables within a single case; idio graphic predictions based on patterns of data about a single case; specific subjective analysis of a certain case; and identification of the central themes in a certain case (Runyan, 1983).

Techniques for determining the synchronic reliability include comparing data that is acquired through different forms such as split-half testing or inter-rater correlations (Kirk & Miller, 1986). For example, two or more observers could observe the same phenomenon during the same time frame. Alternately, a researcher could use a number of different chains of evidence from the same case study. Synchronic reliability is similar to triangulation in the sens that it uses simultaneous measures of the same event.

## 2.8.2 | Diachronic reliability

Diachronic reliability is the extent to which the same observation made at different times yields the same information’ (Kirk & Miller, 1986, p. 79) and is also known as external reliability. Diachronic reliability assessments are generally associated with multiple cases. This association is not absolute. Diachronic reliability assessments may be associated with either the stability, or evolution, of a single case

In assessing diachronic reliability, a specific type of observation is expected to yield the same measurement when exposed to a particular phenomenon at different points in time. It focuses on the stability of an observation over time, which is the similarity of measurements or findings taken at different times (Baskerville, 1996, p. 18) Techniques for evaluating diachronic reliability include conducting repeated experiments or examining the changes over a period of time. Barley (1990), for example, illustrated diachronic analysis by contrasting the use of a singl technology in a hospital setting during an earlier and a later time period.

## 4 | DESIGN SCIENCE RELIABILITY FRAMEWORK

The Design Science Reliability Framework is presented in Figure 3. The framework development involved: the litera ture on reliability; known, unique characteristics of design science research; and prior work. The framework captures the knowledge dimensions (what is reliable) and the knowledge moments (when reliability is assessed). The ‘what (artefact, method, measures and design knowledge) and the ‘when’ (synchronic versus diachronic), lead to the identi fication of 8 ways (the ‘how’) in which design science studies can demonstrate reliability that are important for justi fying validity.

The framework focuses specifically on the assumption that design science research attempts to address a generalised type of problem. However, this is only one strategy for conducting this type of research. Others focus on addressing specific problems and then starting to generalise retrospectively after several cases have been docu mented (Iivari, 2015; Rohde et al., 2017)

Reliability in design science is more multifaceted than in other research paradigms. It involves what is being reg arded as reliable, when it is regarded as reliable and how it is regarded as reliable (Butler & Gray, 2006). What is reli able: the artefact itself, often considered in terms of its function and utility, but here, also, the method to develop it measures to evaluate it, and contribution to the knowledge base produced from it. When these are reliable: at wha knowledge moment in the research does this artefact reliability assessment take place? How it is reliable: the tech niques used in its assessment in the evaluation process

Reliability can be conceptualised as a criterion closely connected to viewing design knowledge as nomothetic but imperative, knowledge. Nomothetic knowledge refers to general theories or concepts that cover the entire set of classes of a given case, in contrast to idiographic knowledge obtained from situated artefacts (Baskervill et al., 2015).<sup>8</sup> Design knowledge, as nomothetic knowledge, is reliable if it can be used in the same context again.

However, contexts are frequently dynamic, so the possibility to apply it to other contexts, and to be useful at another time, becomes critical to establish design knowledge as reliable. It is this conceptualization of design science research that led to the derivation of our framework

Furthermore, because design science research attempts to address wicked, real-world problems, there can b some subjectivity in any design science outcome. Design science research has an iterative process, performed b researchers who have their own individual style and capabilities. The outcome of a project depends on the context purpose and types of design knowledge, and the artefacts produced. Therefore, making the situation, or context and design choices as explicit as possible, should make it feasible to achieve a shared understanding among researchers regarding their perceptions of the outcomes when idiographic knowledge is produced. This, in essence, can align th mental models of researchers and make the outcomes comparable on an abstract level, while acknowledging bot subjectivity and interpretations and still striving to find general outcomes.

The distinction between nomothetic and idiographic reflects the need for an artefact, as an important outcome of a design science research study, to behave in a predictable way (nomothetic knowledge) versus for a given context (idiographic knowledge). The latter is due to the acknowledged pluralism of design science research that impacts the evaluation process so that different criteria are needed to evaluate different parts of a study.<sup>9</sup>

## 4.1 | ‘What’ dimension

Researchers must, generally, be concerned with the reliability of the output: the artefact created and the desig knowledge extracted. Also important are the reliability of the method used to create the artefact and the measures used to evaluate it. The what dimension thus identifies the four assessable scientific aspects of design scienc research: (1) the reliability of the artefact created; (2) the reliability of the method used to create the artefact; (3) th reliability of the measures applied to evaluate the artefacts; and (4) the reliability of the design knowledge embedde within and extracted from, the artefact.

## 4.1.1 | Artefact reliability

Artefact reliability is more than the dependability of an artefact's functionality. It is also dependent upon the design process, the manner in which observations are made, and the application of the design scientist's knowl edge and creative thinking. Many claims in design science research findings assume reliability of the materia artefact in delivering its function and utility. Does the artefact do what it is supposed to do within the built envi ronment in a dependable way? Is it robust in working as intended? The reliability of an artefact ideally aligns with the dependability or robustness in the way the artefact behaves in the ‘build-and-evaluate’ cycle. It is not just ‘how well the artefact performs’ (March & Smith, 1995, p. 254). It is whether it performs reliably in idea circumstances, or even in less-ideal circumstances. The concept of reliability in design science artefacts, then, is similar to the concept of reliability in engineering. For engineers, the end-goal is functional performance. This view of artefact reliability is ideal for material artefacts. This view also inhabits conceptualization of artefact reliability for other kinds of design science artefacts: constructs, models, methods and instantiations, design patterns, design propositions and so forth.

## 4.1.2 | Method reliability

Method reliability is concerned with the need to understand and evaluate a method (Thoring et al., 2023). It ensures that the method for conducting the research leads to valid and consistent results. There are widely-used design science research methods, such as that proposed by Peffers et al. (2008)

## 4.1.3 | Measures reliability

The reliability of the evaluation method provides assurance that the artefact evaluation is conducted in ways the sci entific community will recognise and accept. Reliability of measures refers to any measurement scale or measures of observations. For data collection, design science evaluation is similar to other forms of science, where empirical mea sures and observations are subject to assessments of reliability. The centrality of measure or observation reliability is perhaps most characteristic of a quantitative context, where instrument reliability is critical to accurately reflect th phenomena under observation (Carmines & Zeller, 1979)

## 4.1.4 | Design knowledge reliability

Design knowledge reliability is crucial in design science research because design knowledge focuses on knowledge that is intrinsic to the design and development of an artefact. Design knowledge outcomes can come from both th creation of new kinds of artefacts and the process of designing them.

The researcher's knowledge provides the foundation for an artefact, as well as theoretical knowledge necessarily embedded within it. Therefore, the reliability of the researcher's knowledge is critical to the functioning and utility of the artefact. The researcher brings his/her knowledge, experience and creative thinking into the design process Design knowledge usually includes specialised knowledge of a design domain (management, finance, manufacturing government, etc.). Regardless, there is an underlying axiom that acknowledges forms of knowledge special to th awareness and ability of a designer (Cross, 1982, p. 224). In engineering design, knowledge concepts are generated from inventions, theoretical engineering research and experimentation, whereas practical considerations arise from design practise, production and trial (Vincenti, 1990).

## 4.2 | ‘When’ dimension

A design science research project has knowledge moments, through which it might pass on its way to completion, making the research process a dynamic one (Baskerville et al., 2015). Akoka et al. (2022) propose knowledge paths, to represent the knowledge contribution of a single project as a succession of knowledge moments, characterised by project's goal and scope. The when dimension (knowledge moment) is shaped by the iterative nature of design sci ence research, reflecting its pluralistic nature. Reasons for iterations include: the complexity of the problem (Peffer et al., 2007); the aspect of search that yields the interfacing artefact between the inner and outer environments (Simon, 1996; Thomas et al., 2022), and the process of evaluation and circumscription (Kuechler & Vaishnavi, 2012).

Justification for when to evaluate artefact reliability can involve investigations that occur at a specific point in time (synchronic) or over a period of time (diachronic) (Swinburne, 2001). The concept of time in design science research can be more flexible, referring to the extent of the similarity of observations within the same general tim period.

## 4.3 | ‘How’ reliability established

The two dimensions of what is being assessed, and when the assessment is being conducted, help to understand how a reliability assessment can be achieved. The what is concerned with what is being characterised as reliable: artefact method, measures or design knowledge. The when is the temporal relationship between reliability and time. The how dimension varies with the what and when and used to explore and evaluate artefact reliability on both dimensions of the framework.

## 5.3 | Method: Synchronic reliability

Synchronic reliability of a method means that a method's application at a specific time, and within a specific context yields significant results. It is a form of a one-time reliability, established through significant scientific outcomes from a sin gular study in which the intensiveness of the study propels results so forcefully that reliability concerns about errors o distortions from biases are virtually eliminated. Yin (1994) justifies the belief in idiographic results based on multipl sources of evidence, such as a case study database with a structured survey, open-ended interviews, protocol-driven observations and organisational documents. Such preponderance of evidence justifies the reliability of the results through the intensiveness and forcefulness of a study's evidence. Parsons and Wand (2008), for example, consulted a panel of experts in a protocol-driven analysis to evaluate their proposed set of classification rules, which were derived from cogni tive notions of inference and economy. Gottschalk et al. (2022) propose a crowd-validation process when designing a software artefact, although they used three design cycles to obtain the design knowledge and implementation

## 5.4 | Method: Diachronic reliability

Diachronic reliability exercises the method used by different researchers in a different place at a different time Alternately, it could be the use of a specific method over a long period of time through a prolonged engagement as in action design research (Sein et al., 2011). It assumes that a method will remain stable under different research con texts. Since the context may differ, it is likely that repeating the research at a different time, or within a different context, may yield differing results. Therefore, in assessing the reliability of a method across different contexts, the results from using the method might differ somewhat. It then becomes important to assess whether the metho worked as intended, did not work in a particular situation, or requires modification.

This kind of reliability is established by demonstrating significant scientific outcomes, when the same method i used in multiple studies, different research cases or settings, different researchers and at different times. A metho can be considered reliable, even when it must be modified to fit another setting. Such reliability depends on showing a history of significant usage, although it can take considerable time to establish this form of reliability

The design science research methods of Peffers et al. (2007) and Vaishnavi and Kuechler (2015) form the basi of many published studies. A prior study (Baskerville et al., 2017) reviewed articles that specifically referenced th method proposed by Peffers et al. (2007). As a scientific method, it is reliable because it guides design scienc research that delivers scientific contributions that are novel and substantial. A variety of applications of the metho suggest that the method has a certain robustness and flexibility that permit its adaptation by significantly different researchers in different settings and times. Publication of these efforts in leading information systems journals fur ther demonstrates the reliability of the method as robust and adaptable

Lacity et al. (2021) formalise action principles for new technologies, while complementing design science and action research. Their work is based on methods related to sourcing, business processes, automation, blockchain and possibly others. In their development of action principles, they considered 22 cases across multiple contexts.

Anderson et al. (2023) take a design science research approach to address the problem of patients' self management of consent of online data sharing. The researchers evaluated the artefact, DApp, through simulations using validated measures of latency and throughput, demonstrating practical or pragmatic utility and reliability, as well as engineering or material artefact reliability. If the researchers had developed a prototype without articulatin their efforts to establish the reliability of the outcomes, then the research might have demonstrated a novel idea, but might not have been able to effectively establish rigour and relevance. The researchers describe their method a using two iterations based on Peffers et al. (2007) and distinguish how the artefact was different from that produce in an idiographic project. From their method, the researchers established design principles and design features. Thi abstraction approach resulted in a design that could be projected beyond the original problem of patient consent to any kind of personal data management. Using a well-established design science method provided rigour and enabled projectability of the artefact. The project progressed from a specific problem to general design principles; and from a specific artefact to a generalizable artefact design adaptable to other problems in the same class.

## 5.5 | Measures: Synchronic reliability

Synchronic reliability of an artefact means evaluating an artefact at a certain point in time instead of evaluating th performance of the artefact over time. It can be established by different ways of measuring or observing at the sam time or in the same setting. The timing helps to establish artefact reliability. Using first-hand measures (e.g., observing what people do, rather than asking them what they did) diminishes the effects of self-reflection on reliability measures, while facilitating interpretation by the researchers. Similarly, using multiple observers can produce reliability where objectivity is desired, instead of a more subjective interpretation. Scholta et al. (2020) create model for decision support in personal project management (PPM) to address flexibility and context awareness. They engaged subjects in two types of evaluation consisting of experiments with two scenarios at the same time as multi ple sources of evidence. They also included a questionnaire with open-ended questions to assess usability.

Reliability can also be shown by consistency in the results of multi-level observations; for example, observations of the performance of an artefact at both the organisational and individual levels. An example that demonstrates synchronic reliability of an artefact is the evaluation of the Cybergate prototype instantiation using an Enron email corpus (Abbasi & Chen, 2008).

Synchronic reliability can help establish that an artefact works dependably; its operation is robust. For example evaluating a single instance of a prototype in an ideal setting could confirm validity, but demonstrate only weak reli ability. If, instead, a single instance evaluation takes place in a setting at the extreme edge of the operational condi tions (contra-ideal or worst expected condition) for the prototype's design, it can confirm both the validity and th reliability of the prototype. Such an evaluation is equivalent to a single test of an engineering artefact in proximity to its most likely failure mode.

Studies often evaluate the functionality of an instantiated prototype in a single, difficult case study. For example through a single case study based on a financial services website, Albert et al. (2004) provided a real-world exampl of the implementation of their general model for gathering, inferring, segmenting and tracking online and offline visits to websites that could be used for customer relationship management. This demonstrates the model in a natu ralistic setting, providing the most robust test of its utility.

## 5.6 | Measures: Diachronic reliability

Diachronic reliability refers to the stability of observations over time, or at different times (Kirk & Miller, 1986). Th observations may be concerned with the performance of the designed artefact. In engineering, reliability is based on an artefact performing its purpose over time and under the conditions encountered (Barlow, 1998). It aligns with a diachronic view of artefact reliability and can be accomplished through measures such as persistent observation, tri angulation or transferability using different approaches such as focus groups, interviews and questionnaires.

This kind of reliability can be established by repeating the measures of the artefact's performance and utility in different settings, with different users, on different problems and at different times. In this way, the utility of th artefact is shown to be independent of the unique context for which it was originally designed. Miah et al. (2014 provide a diachronic assessment of reliability of a collective decision support system artefact in an agricultural set ting. Adomavicius et al. (2008) report on two case studies of their proposed artefacts for technology landscape analy sis. The temporality aspect of these cases demonstrates the diachronic reliability of the artefact.

Diachronic reliability of measures indicates that any measurement of the artefact is reliable when used in different settings or at different times. The reliability under different scenarios demonstrates the applicability of the results beyond the current study, yielding confidence in its generalizability. Paralleling the concept of reliability, dependability, refers to the stability of the findings over time

To establish this kind of artefact reliability, a study can demonstrate the execution of multiple instances of mea surements or observations at different times and/or in different settings with consistency in the results obtained The measures and observations can themselves require a degree of abstraction, generality or interpretation needed to surmount the uniqueness of settings and reveal essential consistency. Techniques that can be used for such arte fact reliability claims include model-based observations in comparative or longitudinal case studies. For example

McLaren et al. (2011) developed and evaluated the reliability (and utility) of their artefact (a multi-level strategic fit measurement model), using a multiple case study.

Dincelli and Chengalur-Smith (2020) created an artefact for a gamified approach to security education longitudi nal experiment. They tested it with multiple measures (e.g., attitude, intention) and reported reliability for the mea surement constructs which were adapted from a prior scale. Akello et al. (2023) propose a blockchain-based mode for gun control and tracing based on a chain-of-custody. They show multiple strategies to demonstrate the value o the artefact and demonstrate utility through different types of focus groups. They also used different measures to demonstrate the quality and efficiency of the design. In response for a need for governments, generally to be abl to standardise forms, Scholta et al. (2020) design a ‘meta-model for a reference form modelling language in governments (p. 536) by applying the model of Peffers et al. (2007). Their artefact (meta-model) is intended to be usefu for defining reference form models. They instantiated their model and used 3 cycles of evaluation for assessment o efficacy (with focus groups), completeness (with case studies) and effectiveness.

Piccoli et al. (2020) develop a sociotechnical artefact for providing feedback for digital skill accomplishment at scale. The researchers specifically state that they strive for diachronic reliability by analysing the performance of th artefact with different users on different problems, while being aware of false positives and false negatives (p. 116) The results led the researchers to conclude that their artefact was better than humans. This shows that this type of designation of reliability influenced the way in which reliability was assessed because it was identified at the begin ning as appropriate.

## 5.7 | Design knowledge: Synchronic reliability

Synchronic reliability considers the degree to which design knowledge is dependable for a specific context. Syn chronic reliability is often inferred from the idiographic methods used to produce it. Since idiographic design knowl edge focuses on providing insights, the reliability of such knowledge considers how the knowledge was distilled from the phenomenon (Baskerville et al., 2015). Lincoln and Guba (1985) recommend that such knowledge be evaluated not just for the knowledge itself, but also for the methods of its production. The latter may include contextualization dialogical reasoning and sensitivity to multiple interpretations (Klein & Myers, 1999). To an extent, synchronic reli ability of design knowledge implies that the knowledge is ready-to-use elsewhere; it is generalizable

Synchronic reliability is established when the design knowledge leads to the discovery of novel and significant scientific findings and/or novel and important artefacts. Because synchronic studies focus on a singular instance of an artefact, reliability is shown in the design process through iteration, such as in prototyping. With each iteration the reliability of the design knowledge is justified by the consistency of the results (in terms of additional, incremen tal, and valuable discoveries) in comparison with the results of prior iterations. Kohler et al. (2011), for example, present a virtual co-creation system, called Ideation Quest, which was created, deployed, evaluated, and improved in two action research cycles. The cycles took place over 2 years. The evaluation involved open coding and design prin ciples related to co-creation adapted or added.

## 5.8 | Design knowledge: Diachronic reliability

The diachronic view of reliability of design knowledge focuses on whether the designer's knowledge, as embedded in the artefact, is useful over time (e.g., the principles of a particular design theory prove appropriate for making valu able artefacts in different projects). The diachronic view of design knowledge is concerned with the degree to whic design theories or other forms of design knowledge (design principles, design models or design methods), can b applied to develop artefacts for other settings.

The reliability of such knowledge, thus, lies in the successful application of the design knowledge in producing new artefacts for different settings, different design problems and different times. Establishing reliability typically involve using the design knowledge iteratively across different settings and/or problems. In other words, reliable design knowl edge evolves by iteration in artefact use. The reliability is evident by the presence among the outcomes of each of th various iterations of important discoveries in design and/or science. An example of diachronically reliable design knowl edge is the concept of a theory nexus originating in Carroll and Kellogg's (1989, p. 8) design theorising for a ‘training wheels interface . This design concept was adapted later as the basis for a design theory nexus in multiple cases, includin organisational change design and user engagement design (Pries-Heje & Baskerville, 2008).

## 6 | DISCUSSION

Design is a complex and creative activity that arises in different disciplines (Baskerville et al., 2011). It is also funda mental to research in information systems (March & Storey, 2008). However, design science research is different from other types of research because of the existence of an artefact. The artefact must be developed (requiring reli ability of the development method) and evaluated (requiring reliability of the evaluation measures). The development of the artefact leads to additions to the design knowledge base (requiring reliability of the design knowledge).

This research has proposed a Design Science Reliability Framework, intended to help design science researchers systematically consider the reliability of their work outcomes (artefacts and other knowledge contributions). Th framework has three dimensions: what (artefact, method, measures, design knowledge), when (moment), and how (techniques). The framework has been illustrated by representative design science research projects. The intent of our framework, as is the purpose of all frameworks, is to provide a general, and useful, way to understand a phenom enon (Leonard et al., 2016); here, reliability in design science research.

It is always important to identify the qualities of an artefact that would make an important contribution to desig science research. Evaluation is crucial because it demonstrates the utility and quality of the artefacts and knowledge outcomes, as well as the rigour of the research process used to obtain them. Although the importance of evaluating different types of design science research outcomes (artefacts, design knowledge) is well-recognised, reliability is seldom discussed to confirm the rigour of the studies or their assessment. This led to the need to distinguish th evaluation of the rigour of the research process (method and measures) from the evaluation of the quality of the out comes (artefact and design knowledge).

## 6.1 | Extension of prior research on evaluation

The Design Science Reliability Framework extends prior work on evaluation in design science research, including that o Peffers et al. (2012) and Venable et al. (2016). It is intended to improve reliance on face validity generalities; for example any appropriate empirical evidence or logical proof (Peffers et al., 2007, p. 56). Instead, the framework requires the eval uation activities to consider whether such evidence can be considered reliable, which helps support the assumptions tha design science research results have scientific standing. In design science research, this evidence is often embodied in th functionality and utility of the artefact as an outcome. Showing that this evidence (or near-proof ) of functionality and utility has the character of being scientifically reliable, leading to claims about validity, can bolster the acceptability of it design principles or theory, into the body of received knowledge in design science research.

The direct outcomes of the framework should be of practical use. The identification of the importance of what and when, as well as the eight techniques (how), should help design scientists classify their evidence of reliability. The examples provided illustrate successful techniques in each category, which design scientists could use analogously to gain insights into how to conduct and present their own efforts to establish reliability. A design scientist must first recognise the kind of reliability (artefact, measures, method or design knowledge) needed. It should then be feasibl to map the characteristics of a specific outcome to the appropriate kind of reliability assessment.

## 6.3 | Future research

It would be useful to examine fundamental scientific concepts such as ontologies, epistemologies, methodology and axiology to gain additional supporting justification for the choice of the assessment approaches. There are know aspects of design science research where challenges of reliability may be extremely difficult to address, for example aesthetics, especially with respect to the user interface (Baskerville et al., 2018). The creativity of a design scientist is difficult to replicate, even though there has been much research carried out to understand creativity in various field of research.

Much current design science research tends to be idiographic, focusing on a single instance of an artefact. Esta blishing scientific reliability in idiographic studies is quite different than establishing it in more nomothetic studies Future research is needed to consider how to attend rigour throughout the life cycle of idiographic forms of design science.

Future research is also needed to show reliability in nonmaterial artefacts. Nonmaterial artefacts include methods, models and constructs. The methods in this sense are not usually scientific methods, but rather practica methods intended for utilitarian purposes outside of scientific research (March & Smith, 1995). Other kinds of theo retical formulations might also serve as possible artefacts. It is likely that techniques, similar to those proposed her for reliability in scientific methods, will help establish reliability in nonmaterial artefacts, although further investiga tion is needed.

Our research derived a Design Science Reliability Framework based on a conceptual argument grounded in logic and previously published work. Had this paper used a design science research approach, the framework might be considered an artefact, and future researchers might use a design science research approach to reflexively apply the framework for its own reliability assessment. In this way, the reliability of the design knowledge in the framewor could be established by using multiple evaluation methods (synchronically) during a relevant practical project, or b developing evidence for the useful application of the knowledge in other design science projects (diachronically). A less resource-intensive approach would be to apply the design knowledge, retrospectively, to previous design sci ence research projects (diachronically).<sup>10</sup>

The Design Science Reliability Framework is intended to be a useful addition to the toolbox of design science researchers who are encouraged to use it selectively, according to their projects and interests. Application of the framework to many research projects reported in additional publication outlets (other journals and related confer ences) could be helpful in creating a repository of examples for future researchers. The paper focuses on a top-dow approach to conducting design science research (inventing an artefact to address a generalised type of problem) and not inverse approaches that generalise after several cases have been documented for example, strategies of Iivar (2015); (Rohde et al., 2017). Further, this research does not consider other, potential bigger effects; for example, researchers are not addressing problems that are relevant or the proposed artefacts are not actually effective irrespective of reliability.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

Reliability is important for the evaluation of any research endeavour, yet has different characteristics in design sci ence research and appears to be under represented and reported. This paper has proposed a Design Science Reliabilit Framework, in which reliability is addressed in terms of what is reliable (artefacts, method, measures and desig knowledge) and when reliability is assessed (synchronic versus diachronic evaluation). From the framework, eight techniques emerge for how to assess and demonstrate reliability for the artefact and knowledge products. Outsid of the information systems discipline, the framework might be useful to computing and engineering design disci plines that focus specifically on artefacts.
