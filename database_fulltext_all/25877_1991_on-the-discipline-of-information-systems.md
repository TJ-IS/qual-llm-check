# On the discipline of information systems

J Backhouse, J Liebenau and F Land\*

London School of Economics and \*London Business School, London, UK

Abstract. Although information systems is growing rapidly, it has little theoretical clarity. An article in the Times Higher Education Supplement of March 1989 by Liebenau and Backhouse sparked a debate on the character of information systems as a discipline. This paper reviews that debate, bringing out the main points of many of the discussants, and presents an analysis which is intended to carry the discussion further in order to help clarify and to galvanize opinion. Keywords: information systems, semiotics, theory of information systems.

## INTRODUCTION

The study of information systems is a rapidly growing field which has yet to attain a recognizable shape. Coming as it does out of computer science, management studies, and a variety of social and technical fields, it is hardly surprising that it does not have any theoretical clarity. As it grows, the study of information systems attracts academics who bring ideas and approaches from many different intellectual traditions. That very growth is forcing us to make a choice. Either we can accept this patchwork as it is developing, or we must work to find coherence. If there is a possibility of finding common ground for those working in information systems, then surely it is worth our effort to look for it. At least then, when we are asked by our fellow academics what it is that we study, we can feel confident that the answer we give bears some resemblance to the answer given by our colleagues in information systems elsewhere.

## THE ISSUES

In an article in the Times Higher Education Supplement on the need for a clear understanding of what constitutes the academic study of information systems Liebenau and Backhouse (1989) proposed a set of concerns to help provide the foundation for a better established discipline. That article provoked a good deal of controversy and led to Land conducting a follow-up exercise to gauge responses and to bring the debate to a higher level. In this paper we present an analysis of those responses to the article, some suggestions of how to characterize the types of responses and identify trends of thought, and some modifications of the original thesis. The overall purpose of this is to continue our effort to identify sources of intellectual congruity and help to define the academic discipline of information systems as would be appropriate for a university setting.

Before we present the analysis, however, let us reiterate the issues raised in the original paper (given in full in the Appendix). Liebenau and Backhouse (1989) begin with the supposition that the study of information systems has come about in the first instance to meet practical needs in industry, commerce and administration. The original impetus came from the need to solve mainly technical problems. The current attempt by the profession in the UK to attain the status of chartered engineers reflects the continued perception by many professionals of technology as the driving concept. Now, as practitioners have realized that some of the problems of using the technology effectively stem from political, organizational and social problems, other disciplines coming from the social sciences throw light on the problems faced by those designing and implementing information systems in a variety of organizational contexts. The definition of information studies thus sprang from the haphazard compilation of the field from its various component parts.

The authors go on to identify two firm foundations on which to build: sociology and semiology. Other traditional disciplines are also helpful in part, especially economics and anthropology, and a deliberate effort was made to include, but to subordinate, the role of computer science in this analysis. The inter-relatedness of these elements appears necessary in order to see how research agendas are constructed and research projects connected. The discipline needs a believable 'map' to identify lesser known territories, and to see clearly how we might get from one position to another. The authors finish with a plea for the study of information systems to be more than the sum of its academic parts.

Sociology and semiology provide the foundation upon which to rest a theory of information: sociology, because it addresses social behaviour and provides a set of tools and a means to understand the organizations in which information is used; and semiology, because it addresses sign processes, and provides the means to analyse the properties of signs in their context of use. Taken together, they can provide us with a reasonably secure platform upon which to develop a theory using elements from fields such as computer science, operational research, linguistics and anthropology.

In the original paper, the authors set out six different levels of analysis to apply to information systems problems. At the top levels they are concerned with the business world and the specific context in which the signs are used. Then they move to the levels of meaning and syntax, and finally to the levels at which signals and the physical elements prevail. Other disciplines can be brought to bear on this framework to create a coherent theoretical structure.

The test of a good theory is that it provides convincing explanations of existing phenomena, and it enables new phenomena to be predicted. Its prime use is to act as a guide to practice. Besides the general value of good theory, we have a special interest in understanding the topography of the study of information systems. The work of an organization such as the International Federation of Information Processing (IFIP) is supported by divisions where people work on topics as diverse as man-machine interfaces, artificial intelligence, analysis and design methodologies, simulation, databases, and social and economic aspects. How these divisions inter-relate, how they define research projects, what their pedagogical connections are, and how they might conflict with one another, are all issues which a theoretical structure can help resolve. These are precisely the kinds of problems which we in information systems are still struggling to come to terms with.

We believe we now have the means to create a harmonious picture from the present messy patchwork which has developed in such a haphazard way. We carried out this exercise to test the idea that there is an ‘academic community’ of shared interests. Using the term academic community should suggest a common adherence to shared assumptions, beliefs and expectations, or else we must abandon the notion that we form a community at all. Whereas for disciplines such as chemistry we can see the relationship between the fields of organic and inorganic chemistry, the significance of laboratory and theoretical work, the utility of tools such as electron microscopy and X-ray crystallography, and research programmes such as genetic structure analysis and effluent toxicology, we cannot see anything as coherent as this in our own field. Even in academic philosophy, we can see the relationship between ethics and formal logic, the significance of empirical and theoretical work, the utility of tools such as predicate logic and moral rationalism, and research programmes such as medical ethics and theory construction in the philosophy of science. We may not be able to find the analogous elements for information systems through this process, but at least we can see the kind of agenda that we might be able to work to.

Shortly after the article appeared, Professor Frank Land suggested to its authors that it should be circulated. It would then serve as an appropriate follow-up to a meeting which had recently been held at the London Business School to discuss future directions for the study of information systems. That meeting had sparked a great deal of lively debate, but no proposals emerged. This article was used as a first cut at a substantial proposal which could lead to further debate.

Around 60 copies of the paper were distributed to leading scholars and commentators in the field, along with a covering letter requesting considered responses. Almost half of the people contacted prepared formal replies, and over a dozen informal responses were solicited in conversation.

## THE RESPONSES

The responses varied greatly. For example, Hans-Erik Nissen calls into question the need for any definition of information. He is concerned that we will be misguided by any definition because he thinks 'information' cannot be pinned down. In a somewhat different vein, Tom Stonier believes that 'information is a physical property of the universe and not a construct of the human mind', and so cannot be defined in terms of social relations.

Most respondents can be divided into three categories: those who felt that the whole process was entirely unnecessary, those who agreed with the process but thought that the article began at the wrong starting point, and those who felt that the article needed to be adjusted. There were also some people who were generally in agreement but failed to offer constructive comments. The majority of the people who offered thoughtful replies proposed reasonable modifications. It is from these writings that we can draw a number of valuable conclusions.

Kalle Lyytinen proposed a categorization of criticisms in his response which is a useful starting point for our general analysis. He discerns five different themes which are touched on in the article and which he sees as important issues. Some of these are best expressed as dichotomies. These are the foundational/pragmaticist orientation, the theory/practice dichotomy, technology versus social orientation, the role of adjacent disciplines, and descriptive versus normative aspects.

In the first theme, Lyytinen points out that the pragmatist approach claims that we need move no further than to accept that a discipline can be simply defined by the activity of those involved in it. What practitioners do is the discipline. At the other end of this continuum, the foundationalists have a clearly defined view of what constitutes their research area: the concepts, the theories, the methods and the approaches. Furthermore, they require that members of the community must adhere strictly to this definition if they are to continue membership.

The second theme which Lyytinen regards as an underlying feature of the article is the dichotomy between theory and practice. Whereas for other social sciences theoreticians theorize and practice is secondary, in information systems, practice is pre-eminent. Some respondents go so far as to claim that the original article is entirely devoted to a peripheral consideration, avoiding the 'real' issue of design, and other issues of importance such as data storage.

Next, the distinction between information technology and the social orientation of systems is identified as a source of conflict. Practitioners see themselves working back from practical problems and their need for theory is given low priority. The computer science emphasis comes from a concern with the apparent need for new tools. The technology is seen to resolve perceived problems. This is so much a priority that theory is almost irrelevant to them. This brings into question the role of computers in information systems, which Lyytinen believes must be fundamental. Many respondents felt that the orientation away from technology was too extreme. Some of those, such as Roger Clark, who were concerned with the balance between the social and the technical, felt that the subject should have more of a technical basis than was indicated by the article. Clive Holtham adds that 'decision-makers need to understand the business implications of key technical decisions and those studying information in organizations need to find ways of maintaining a sound understanding of technology'. This brings to mind the recent spate of discussions on the need for the 'hybrid' manager.

Fourthly, the role and influence of disciplines adjacent to information systems are things which Lyytinen believes must be assessed. For example, David Avison is concerned that by categorizing the various contributing disciplines we might limit the choices available to us and lose a necessary eclecticism. Furthermore, he points out that there is no requirement for a discipline to agree upon one methodology. Clive Holtham similarly believes that 'there may be as important a role to play in motivating or persuading other disciplines to understand and use the subject, as in ourselves trying to do all the building work'. Indeed the question needs to be asked, who is best fitted to teach and study; the subject area specialist—in this case operations management—or the information systems specialist? If it is the former, and we believe that this proposition may be valid, what is the role of the information systems specialist?

Another approach to this problem is taken by Pentti Kerola, who argues that although we may agree on the most appropriate adjacent disciplines for our study, the way in which these are brought together may well bring us to different points.

Finally, Lyytinen stresses the importance of the tension between descriptive and normative aspects of our work in information systems, which he believes is at the heart of our academic studies.

In a most thoughtful analysis, Barbara Farbey proposes the following definition. 'An information system is a system for transmitting and receiving messages. It defines, formally and informally, and often by default, when and where messages are sent, who receives them and what makes them comprehensible'. The technical components of information systems are important, she believes, and she cautions that too great an emphasis on the social dimension will lead to inappropriate expectations about what systems analysts are capable of delivering: '(it) implies a much greater emphasis on, and skill in, social analysis than we are able to provide. We raise expectations that we are not trained to fulfil.' Although she appears to agree substantially with the Liebenau and Backhouse article, she shifts the emphasis onto a concept of meaning which combines elements of pragmatics, semantics, and syntactics, with a number of other concepts. These include concepts of the geography of a message constrained by technology, of the activity of sending and receiving messages, of the economy of an information system, and of the distribution of power in social structures.

The debate is continuously being enriched by new contributions from a variety of sources. For example, Wand & Weber (1990) attempt to bring concepts derived from Chomsky (1965) and Bunge (1977, 1979) into their definition of the deep structures and surface structures of information systems. We need different disciplines and different kind of theories to understand and explain the two types of structure. Surface structures are concerned with the relationship between an information system and its user. These are best understood through the social sciences. Deep structures—their main concern—can be thought of as independent of the social domain and are to be understood via the discipline of logic. Other interesting new contributions come from the speech act theorists who see an information system not in terms of messages with a transmitter and receiver, but as conversations where each act of conversation follows certain norms which define the shape of the conversation. The reason for mentioning these contributions is not to attempt to evaluate them but to note that in attempting to define a discipline we have to be aware of the extent to which we must retain an openness to absorb the new ideas.

The role of the debate as a whole is usefully seen in the context of the large number of departments which each have a foot in information technology. The issues are important when we need to deal with the organizational structure of academic departments, the recruitment and career structure of academic staff, the funding of research, and the development of courses.

## ANALYSIS

The community in general seems to be clustered around those whose main concerns are with the technology and those who wish to introduce business-school type thinking. Both groups, however, tend to focus on what they see as practical issues. Theory is often regarded as a luxury indulged in by pipe smoking academics. Indeed, too often even in academic institutions such as business schools, theoretical concerns are not seen to inform and evaluate practice but to be inimical to effective practice. In addition, there is a minority whose interest is in the more philosophical issue of what information is about. It also seems apparent that these groups are not well acquainted with each other's views and have not resolved their conflicts.

We have been most influenced by the constructive criticisms of Lyttinen and Farbey. Not only do they both seek to readjust the balance back towards the technology, as do many other contributors to the debate, but they also analyse the meaning and implications of their alternative structures. They offer an intellectual framework for the study of information which is different from the one in the original article, although they are not synthetic in the sense of being prescriptive.

We cannot expect the study of information to be the same as chemistry and philosophy. The first reason is that there is not yet a clear agreement about the boundaries of the fields in our subject. In the study of information, the applications of information technology to organizations tend to prevail over theory forming. As the field originated recently from practice, there is still this bias. Although there are plenty of tools in information technology, there is no clear relationship between them and their basis in theory. Furthermore, it is difficult to see the connections between research programmes in our field in the way that one can discern them in other fields such as chemistry and philosophy.

The lack of coherence in our understanding of what the domain comprises, and the relationships between the many topics which come under discussion and are the subject of research, also leads to argument on what are appropriate research methods: what constitutes legitimate research, and what is, in the words of critics, 'mere journalism'. Many academics seek acceptance from their peers by choosing to adopt the research methods of the natural sciences and engineering. Others have adopted research approaches derived from the empirical research style of social scientists such as psychologists. A few have risked academic credibility by choosing a more interpretive research approach associated with the followers of critical theory. There is now a lively debate about appropriate research methodologies, although no indication of a convergence of views. This lack of agreement about research styles is yet another symptom of the problems faced by a discipline which has failed so far to develop an identity.

Our discipline tends to overstate the extent to which the theoretical arena is constrained by the current views on what is practically possible. So Farbey's objection, that we cannot be concerned primarily with social issues because we cannot offer the necessary skills of social analysis, cannot stand as a general objection to our ambition to create a better balance between the structure of ideas and the practice of the discipline.

This discussion does seem to have raised the level of debate. Although many of the respondents took the opportunity to air their own pet theories, the need to debate them has led to some important clarifications. It has also helped us to modify our views, at least in tone, but not much in substance: too many people believed that the semiotic approach was a dogma rather than an organizing principle.

## CONCLUSION

We see the issue as one for continuing debate. Nevertheless, for the reasons stated above there is some urgency in providing a template for the discipline if it is to develop in any kind of coherent form, and if it is to gain credibility which is necessary for it to advance. The threat to the study of information systems as a valid academic domain stems from its current fragmentation and lack of common frameworks. If we believe in the importance of the domain then it is necessary for the community to be seen to get its act together. We hope that the debate which led to this paper is seen as a step in that direction.

## A need for discipline

Jonathan Liebenau and Jim Backhouse on information systems

There is now, as never before, a crying need for a clear understanding of what constitutes the academic study of information systems. The need has come about partly as a result of the almost haphazard compilation of this field from its various component parts. Indeed, the lack of any underlying precepts, to say nothing of unifying principles, has now brought those of us in the field to the point where we are reduced to 'ad hocery' when justifying what we do. Another contributing reason is that one main stream of this field has long been associated with the teaching of the management of information systems within business schools. The move to the university environment has given us more opportunities and set us new challenges. We wish to open this debate to broad involvement from people all over the area, and to propose some guiding principles.

First it is a positive statement of what we see as the study of information systems. Two well-established areas of study provide us with a firm foundation on which to build. These are sociology on the one hand and semiology on the other. The former already constitutes the main thrust of research into social organization, institutional dynamics, group interaction, working conditions and social policy.

The latter brings together the range of studies associated with contexts of language and communication, meanings, grammars, signs and codes. Other traditional disciplines contribute considerably. Economics and anthropology have a great deal to contribute; policy and administrative studies are also integral, as is computer science. The whole range of social studies must be taken into consideration when we begin with the presumption that information systems are social systems; but that is not enough. We must have some sense of how to organize these studies so as to know not only how they interrelate, but also to help us to plan our trajectory for research and curriculum development.

We require a deep understanding of how the subject areas we concentrate on are related. We need to know how apparently disparate research projects contribute to the field as a whole. We need to have the confidence to be able to plan broad research programmes, which incorporate a range of approaches but which also have integrity. We need this, on the practical level, to direct better our research and that of our research students into efforts which unambiguously contribute to the improvement of the field. We also need to establish standards of research which will distinguish well meaning schemes, suggestions and observations from more solid scholarly work. We need to have some boundaries such that the work of business consultancies can be seen to be different from that of scholars.

How shall we begin? Let us start with something of a negative approach, a first cut for a definition by contrast. Researchers into information systems should not be primarily concerned with the technical components of computer-based networks. That rightly belongs to the field of computer science. Those who regard computers to be fundamental to information systems often work from technical capabilities to social problems. This should not be the case; although most modern systems involve computers, systems analysts should not work as though they are fundamentally computer based. Where research problems focus on the computer or computer software, what distinguishes the study of information systems is the contribution it makes to understanding the social context, the organizational utility and the primacy of people.

Sociologists concern themselves with analysing what might be seen as systems of social behaviour. All information systems are fundamentally social systems, regulated by the same processes which govern other norm-based activity. Norms can provide the basis for describing and modelling social organizations at the informal level. In prescribing the formal part of any informal system, we can employ rules to analyse and then specify the organization. While norms are descriptive, rules prescribe behaviour. Information systems as a discipline should address itself to the relationships among formal and informal, rule-based and normative aspects of organizations, institutions, groups, and even of working conditions. When we are able to do that, we will be able to avail ourselves of the powerful interpretive methods of sociology and to apply them with confidence to the whole range of social systems, even to the point of addressing policy issues.

Fundamental problems are also found with the conceptualization of what information is, and with how the transmission of information constitutes communication. For this we look to semiology for guidance. The study of signs is itself a marginal academic field but it is at least firmly composed of analyses of four classes of activities. The contextual and pragmatic aspects of communication bring the study into most close proximity with mainstream aspects of sociology and are supported by high quality work especially within the sociology of knowledge, sociolinguistics, and anthropological studies of institutions. Second, semantic issues are the key to understanding the content of information systems. Not only do problems of meaning plague the design of systems which are intended to perform specific functions; they also open up to us a range of questions about relevance, comprehension, timing, intention, and so on. Semantics has been studied by people in many disciplines but linguists and philosophers have most to offer us.

Syntactical issues form a third level of analysis in this recasting of semiology. Again, philosophers of logic and linguists have much to contribute, but the tools of syntactics currently at our disposal leave much to be desired. As software engineers strive to develop even more sophisticated means of handling programming problems, they need to concern themselves with syntactics. Finally, we come to the set of issues which used to be known as information theory. Despite the apparent disconnectedness of the mathematics of information, high-speed transmission and codes, these areas can contribute to our new approach to information systems. These are matters of concern to us partly because they set limits of feasibility and partly because they provide mediation between information systems and computer science.

One reason we are working towards a conceptualization of the field is to allow us to see the connectedness of the variety of research projects which are currently under way. How does current work in the technicalities of computer graphics relate to work on expert systems for tutoring? How can we bring together simulation modelling with studies of decision analysis? What is the relationship between the investigation of the information needs and capabilities of commercial organizations and policy-setting for long-term, large-scale commitments to information technology?

Another reason is to allow us to see how elements of the field interrelate to identify a coherent dynamic of change in the area. We should be able to see more clearly where, within a large research programme, individual projects fit. Until we see that, it is difficult to know where new fundamental research might be needed. Is it important for more work to be done now in systems software or can we rely on what the software engineers can provide and where they promise to lead? Do we need more studies of corporate organizations' needs, or do the case analyses which business schools offer provide us with the raw material we need? Is information technology conceptually different from other technologies (in the sense of Daniel Bell's notion of the 'information society'), or can we generalize from and apply the growing scholarship in the social studies of science and technology to our areas of interest?

We have much to do to build a foundation for our field. We need to study contexts of information, problems of meaning, appropriate syntax, and information handling and digesting capacities. We need to be able to define and show ways of working through 'action research' problems. We need to be able to understand how strategic and policy aspects of information technology and of information systems impinge on our work, and how we might contribute to strategy and policy formation.

It is not just that we crave academic respectability, or that we need to find some way of showing that we are something other than a form of cheap business consultancy. We need to be more than the sum of the academic parts which influence us, and we need to have the confidence to know that we are able to make use of those sources of influence which will provide us with rigour. Casual borrowings, cavalier attitudes and amateurish eclecticism, resting uneasily on the solid but inappropriate foundation of computer science, may be enough to make management information systems an aspect of business school curriculum, but it will not provide us with the necessary base on which to build.
