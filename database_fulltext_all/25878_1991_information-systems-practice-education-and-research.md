# Information systems practice, education and research

DE Avison and G Fitzgerald\*

Department of Computer Science, Aston University, Birmingham, and \*The Oxford Institute of Information Management, Templeton College, Kennington, Oxford, UK

Abstract. The launch of a new journal in information systems prompts thought and debate concerning the state of the subject area and some contemplation on its past and future. This introductory paper reflects the views of the editors, views which will not be shared by all. We find a wide spectrum of interpretations and experiences concerning the nature of information systems and this wide range of views is both a strength and a weakness. It is a weakness in the sense that it is very difficult to put boundaries around the area and establish common definitions and accepted norms. The latter are usually associated with more stable and established disciplines that have accepted underlying theories. It is a strength in the sense that the wide variety of views, experiences and background subjects adds richness to the discipline. From the point of view of the Journal of Information Systems this should mean a wide variety of interesting topics from which we can draw. Our paper begins with an introduction to the debate about the nature of information systems, a debate which is continued in the following paper. We also give an overview of practice, education and research in information systems. In order to keep the paper to a reasonable length, this overview will necessarily be selective and will probably be Anglocentric.

Keywords: information systems education, information systems practice, information systems research.

## AN INTRODUCTION TO THE DEBATE

Perhaps we ought first to ask why such a debate might be useful. Is information systems under threat in academic circles and in business? Do we want to expand information systems in universities and polytechnics? Is it to try to obtain a bigger slice of the research cake? Are we attempting to increase its status? Is it a move to make information systems a profession? Is it to put information systems on a firmer academic footing? Is it to make information systems more respected in business? Is it merely an intellectual exercise? Although we do not attempt to answer the question in this paper, the question is valid because we may wish any response to be tuned to the objective.

Correspondence David Avison, Department of Computer Science, Aston University, Aston Triangle, Birmingham, B4 7ET, UK.

Secondly, we must ask whether we want to have such a debate. There are debates of this type within a number of disciplines which have been destructive (for example, that in English departments in universities, the argument relating to 'English literature' or 'cultural studies'—see Small (1989)—and the debate in anthropology which started with a controversy over the nature of field work and has led to the questioning of the disciplinary status of anthropology itself—see Leach (1961)). Graff & Gibbons (1985), when discussing the debate about structuralism, formalism and deconstruction in many humanities disciplines, argue that the debate has been destructive: the 'debate' normally being more like 'bitter disagreement' caused by a clash of dogmas. Might our debate then lead to the increasing specialization of different cliques within the information systems community (effectively splitting that community) and lead to a reduction in the status of the discipline, rather than any resolution of conflicts? We note and concur with Backhouse et al.'s comment in this edition of the Journal that semiology is an organizing principle and not a dogma. We view it as a useful methodological tool: a way of seeing and understanding, and appropriate to a whole variety of disciplines. But it is not seen as such by everyone, indeed Backhouse et al. (1991) state that 'too many people believed that the semiotic approach was a dogma'.

Nevertheless, it is our view that it is important to have a debate about the basis of our discipline, and it is our belief that there are a number of foundation disciplines. Liebenau and Backhouse (1989) stress sociology and semiology, but there are others, for example, systems thinking, computer science (or information technology) including software engineering and formal methods, and politics, ethics, applied psychology, ergonomics, linguistics, economics, and mathematics, and other disciplines are also relevant. Information systems has a multidisciplinary nature. Placing too rigid boundaries on information systems may reduce its richness. We believe that the new journal offers an opportunity to open up horizons, and though information systems should not be viewed as a set of tools and techniques (Avison et al., 1988) nor just the applications of computers, it should also not be seen as some sort of hybrid of two or three other disciplines.

Information systems has a rich multi-disciplinary nature, where 'human

## factors' are at least as important as 'technological factors'

In our view there is nothing wrong intellectually or institutionally in an academic discipline relying on a number of other disciplines as foundations. Medicine, French, geography, business studies, and many others, are all subjects in academic institutions which have no single, simple disciplinary status. Furthermore, these can all be described as a collection of social practices and appropriate theories, like information systems.

However, there are some constraints attached to this stance, but none, in our view, disabling. First, although different areas of study might be used as a foundation for information systems, and these may have different epistemologies, they should not conflict. Liebenau and Backhouse (1989) do not discuss whether the epistemologies of sociology and semiology conflict. There is a vexed debate within these disciplines. There are competing models of grammar within semiology, for instance. A single discipline can tolerate competing methodologies. The discipline of history, where competing methodologies coexist, has not experienced a similar sense of crisis, even though, like English, Sociology and Anthropology, it is not a discipline to which the current politic is particularly sympathetic.

An important lesson from the above discussion might be that questions about methodology (and to some extent about epistemology) are secondary in the sense that they can only be settled once there is an agreement about an object of study. It seems to be the case that for a subject to be considered a legitimate discipline of knowledge there must be general agreement that its object of study is unique and possesses social utility (though cosmology is, perhaps, a subject which has an object of study but does not possess social utility). Clearly information systems possesses social utility, though there is no firm agreement on what is the object of study.

Perhaps it is worth looking through the other end of the telescope, i.e. rather than asking questions about epistemology and methodology, what are the sufficient and necessary conditions for information systems to be considered as a discipline of knowledge, we should ask ourselves:

Is there anything that we do in common which we could call the study of information systems?

Only when we have explicitly identified this set of practices can we set about establishing theories for them. As a starting point to this exercise, we present an overview of information systems practice, education and research.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS PRACTICE

We begin with computerization in organizations. This is in stark contrast to others who believe that this only reflects the latest tool associated with information systems and that the subject really began as far back as 600 BC. We start our review of information systems with the introduction of the computer because we believe that this is really the effective start of the subject. The computer is not a simple, single-purpose tool like any other, but an extremely powerful, flexible and multi-faceted one with infinite potential. It is this tool that changed the nature of information systems from that of the Greeks to that which we see today. This technological starting point is not to imply that the technology itself is the main focus of information systems. As we indicated earlier, we believe the subject includes study of information irrespective of its technological delivery system and that it includes both informal information as well as formal. Indeed, we agree with Stamper (1973) that 'the explosive growth of information technology has not been accompanied by a commensurate improvement in the understanding of information', and although this was written in 1973 it would still appear to be true today.

Typically, the first activities in organizations that were computerized were accounting-related, followed by stock control and invoicing. The approach was simple and did not involve changing the nature of the business. What changed was the tool used. Over time, organizations computerized more and more of their operational activities. Each functionally related operational system was developed in isolation, often using different hardware or programming languages. This depended on the state of technology and the technology market at the time the system was developed. The result has been described as 'islands of automation', with little or no integration between the various systems (McFarlan & McKenney, 1982, 1983).

Subsequently, a consolidation stage was entered. This involved an attempt to integrate the isolated pockets of automation into a whole that would support not only the individual operations or applications but the totality of the operational needs of the organization. This stage is often associated with the use of databases to provide more flexible access to information and to help integrate systems across functional boundaries. A centralized approach to managing the computing resources of the organization was usually adopted.

Some organizations began to establish an additional role for the technology. This was to support the activities of management as well as the low level operational activities of the organization. This has been described as 'improving the effectiveness of management', and contrasts with the role of computing as simply improving the efficiency of operations. This role of computing is closely associated with the concepts of management information systems (MIS) (Davis & Olsen, 1984) and decision support systems (DSS) (Sprague, 1980). This role is continuing to develop and evolve, and the use of expert systems in decision making is likely to be a further part of this process.

As the role of computing in organizations developed from an objective of cost-reducing efficiency to an objective of more effective management, the problems of managing what was originally known as the data-processing (DP) function also grew. Computers and software were not the only technology, there were others such as communications, networks, satellites, CAD/CAM, robotics, graphics, video, lasers, and so on. These related and converging technologies together became known as information technology (IT). The widening of the technological basis dramatically increased the complexity that managers had to address. This, combined with the ever increasing demand for systems and IT solutions, overloaded the traditional centralized DP departments. The resources required to service the demand was enormous, and the effectiveness of the systems produced was beginning to be questioned. The systems were viewed as being ineffective primarily due to their inflexible nature. It was proving very difficult for systems to be modified rapidly and cheaply to cope with the changing needs of the business (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988a). Maintenance of existing systems was an ever increasing burden (Leintz & Swanson, 1980). The result was that a declining share of the resources of the DP department was available to service new demands. Three- to five-year backlogs of systems waiting to be developed were common. DP departments were no longer adequately able to cope with the task the organization required of them (Alloway & Quillard, 1983).

One response to these problems was a move towards decentralized systems and end-user development. This, together with the improved power/cost ratios of the hardware and packaged software, is seen as a way of satisfying some of the demand for applications, leaving the DP department more time to address the systems which cannot be approached in this way (Leitheiser & Wetherbe, 1986). A more user-oriented service from the DP department was required, which led to the development of Information Centres (Hammond, 1982). Whilst this move towards decentralized end-user computing has to some extent been successful, there remain considerable problems, particularly in the support of end users as their needs and expectations become ever more sophisticated and as these end-user systems need to be integrated with other systems and enhanced and maintained as the business changes.

For systems which are still developed in the traditional way by IT/IS departments, methodologies and new and improved techniques and tools which increase the productivity of system developers have been sought. This is basically an attempt to overcome some of the problems associated with the traditional life cycle of systems development. Indeed some authors have gone further and suggested that the use of the traditional life cycle can actually prove harmful (McCracken & Jackson, 1982).

One particular criticism of the life-cycle approach is that it is a one-off development. This is reflected in its starting point which is a particular problem area, i.e. single problems are addressed and solved. This leads to a wide variety of different information systems in organizations, and because of their separate development they may well (and often do) turn out to be incompatible. This ad-hoc development neglects the fact that most systems need to overlap and integrate with each other. The life cycle, it has been argued, has no place for an Information Systems Strategy or Plan which would co-ordinate developments in the organization and ensure that the information systems address the overall business needs.

The linear nature of the traditional life cycle has also been criticized. The notion that you must finish the whole analysis before starting the design means that valuable resources, for example, programmers, may not be fully utilized, but perhaps more importantly, it precludes the breaking down of complex activities into manageable chunks, some of which could be developed in parallel. The linear nature of the traditional life cycle contrasts with the concept of top-down (or structured) development (Gane & Sarson, 1979).

The documentation inherent in the traditional approach has also been heavily criticized. First, because it is often very descriptive, particularly in regard to the specification of user requirements. This type of narrative description is not a good way of specifying a process in an unambiguous way. The descriptions are generally long, unstructured and repetitious so that it is very difficult to be clear and precise. The specification is thus not a good communication tool and is also very difficult to update. The idea that a user or manager can 'sign off' the specification as an accurate statement of exactly what they require is very difficult if the specification is ambiguous. A second problem is the idea of the 'sign off' itself. It implies that the requirements are frozen and will not change during the development process.

A further criticism of the traditional life cycle is that the users only really see the system that has been developed for them at implementation time. At this point they appreciate it fully for the first time and find numerous things wrong with it or improvements that could be made. The notion of producing a prototype and the use of 4GL (Fourth Generation Languages) are attempts to overcome this particular criticism.

As a result of these problems, some organizations and consultancy houses began experimenting with 'new' approaches. Some of these proved quite successful and evolved as methodologies for systems development. Other methodologies were developed in the academic environment from, perhaps, a more theoretical standpoint. Often the originators then set out to sell their methodologies as a panacea for the problems of systems development and methodologies began to be marketed as products themselves. There are hundreds of different methodologies available, although it is probable that many of these are very similar and only differentiated for marketing purposes.

Methodologies have often been adopted in organizations in order to control the development process more tightly rather than to improve the quality of the final developed system. In this sense the methodology is used as a project control mechanism rather than as a way of radically changing the way systems are developed. In fact many methodologies exhibit much of the same structure as the original life-cycle approach. There are, however, a number of more radically different methodologies such as Ethics, Soft Systems Method, and those supporting prototype production. Avison & Fitzgerald (1988b) provide an overview of methodologies, techniques and tools. In the future we may see more use of these approaches and perhaps a move towards a combination of development approaches (Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990). In addition, there has been a significant move towards supporting methodologies with a range of software tools such as analyst and designer workbenches (Pergamon, 1987).

In addition to the role of IT in support of the operational activities and of management decision making, a third role of computing and information technology in business is emerging. This is the use of IT as a direct tool for obtaining competitive advantage. IT can be used to improve the business in the market place and the external environment. This is over and above the use of IT to support the organization's internal functions and processes (Porter & Millar, 1985). Information technology has been used in this way, according to Earl (1989), to help:

(a) redefine the boundaries of particular industries,

(b) develop new products or services,

(c) change the relationships between suppliers and customers, and

(d) establish barriers to deter new entrants to marketplaces.

The use of information technology in this role has been shown to have the possibility of generating very significant rewards for the organization. Returns of 1000% on investments have been quoted in areas where 10% return is the norm on IT investments (Norton, 1985). Information technology used as a competitive weapon is regarded as an income-generating venture rather than one simply to achieve cost savings, which is the more traditional way of justifying an IT investment.

The implication of these developments, and the changing role of IT in organizations, is that the management of IT can no longer be treated as it has been in the past—as a low level, isolated function. The task is too complex and the penalties of failure too great. As IT becomes more and more influential in determining the success or otherwise of an organization, then the responsibility for its direction, planning and control must be taken by the most senior management. One critical aspect of this is the necessity of planning the use of IT/IS in organizations and the development of relevant strategies. Although many organizations have begun to recognize the need for an IT strategy, the correct philosophy and approach to developing such a strategy, is by no means clear or agreed. This is one of the major challenges in information systems for the new decade.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS EDUCATION

In the UK, information systems education has been very patchy. Traditionally the establishment has been more concerned with computer science courses which reflect a technology-driven view of the world and such courses have been running since the mid-1960s. The teaching of applications and implications of the technology developed somewhat later, although it was recognized as early as 1968 that education was required not just for computer scientists but also for 'those concerned with the analysis and design of computer-using systems' (Buckingham et al., 1987). The IFIP Technical Committee for Education (TC3) initiated a Working Group to prepare a curriculum in 1968 and this group reported in 1974. The aim was primarily to address systems analysis and design professionals but it also had the foresight to recognize that there would also be a need for those whose primary interests were in business and administration and to whom 'computer technology would be one management tool among others' (Davis, 1987). The 1974 curriculum formed the basis of a number of new courses, in particular (in the UK) at the London School of Economics, Hatfield Polytechnic and North Staffordshire Polytechnic.

In the UK at least, the polytechnics have tended to embrace the new and emerging discipline of information systems. The universities were much more reluctant and less influenced by the growing demands of industry and commerce. This polarization of responsibilities was unfortunate because the universities tended to control the research, and information systems as a discipline was not well supported in terms of funded research. Indeed it was sometimes viewed by the universities as not a proper subject for academic institutions to embrace. It was difficult to define, or bound, there was no theoretical basis, and it was over-influenced by practitioners in business and organizations (this is a partial response to the question asked in the second paragraph of this paper, why have such a debate?).

We still see the influence of these academic origins today. The original Alvey Programme had no place for information systems. This was somewhat remedied at a later stage and the subsequent European Esprit programmes (the work of Loucopoulos et al., 1991, was part funded by this programme) do not have the same solely technology-enabling view of the world.

The original information systems courses were quickly augmented. The emphasis of most of these courses were primarily on systems analysis and design and they aimed at developing information systems practitioners for the large number of organizations struggling with systems development. Indeed many of these courses still exist and although they have developed dramatically in terms of content, their focus is still often on the same market, i.e. training systems development practitioners. In 1987, IFIP and the British Computer Society (BCS) published a major revision of the 1974 curriculum. It reflected the much broader scope of information systems as influenced by the fundamental changes in technology and the recognition that many of the issues concerning practitioners were no longer technical. As Buckingham et al. (1987) point out: 'the information system designer, using this title now in its broad sense and at the highest level, is concerned with the application of information technology in organizations and society and with the design, development and use of information systems which add effectively to their welfare and successful activity'.

However, despite this widening, it is still focused on the information system designer and not, as envisaged back in 1974, on the manager who seeks to utilize information and technology as part of the armoury of skills to enhance the business of organizational effectiveness. This person is beginning to be catered for, less in information systems or computing science departments, but more in business schools. We now see information systems or information management groups within the traditional business schools in both the universities and polytechnics. Some cater for a business information systems specialist but most advocate a more seamless integration of information systems/information management into the mainstream business courses, for example, into business strategy. This seamless integration is often more theory than practice but nevertheless it characterizes the notion that management is now about information systems as much as it is about finance or marketing or other specialities.

In the United States, according to Davis (1987), the situation is somewhat different. The distinction is clearly made between Computer Science and Information Systems. Information Systems, commonly called 'Management Information Systems' or 'Computer-based Information Systems' is usually (80%, according to Davis, 1987) to be found as part of the Business Schools (or Schools of Management or Commerce).

Another recent development, particular in UK computer science departments, has been the growth of postgraduate conversion courses. To some extent this reflects user development and, in general, greater use of computers. These conversion courses consist of students who have graduated in other disciplines as their intake. They are wide in scope and enable people of other disciplines to use the information technology as appropriate to their field, and also to convert people to the speciality of information technology.

Another aspect of information systems education concerns how the subject is taught, as against what is taught. The conventional way of teaching information systems is to give a series of lectures backed by a text book, along with selected readings taken from journals. This is supplemented by tutorial sessions which give students the opportunity to tackle problems. Sometimes this is backed up by case studies which are more substantial pieces of work, very often stemming from real-life examples, ideally gained from the tutor's own experience but very often taken from a text.

As Buckingham et al. (1987) and Nunamaker et al. (1982) make clear, these conventional methods of teaching do not provide a good enough grounding for information systems people to enter the commercial world. The traditional way of providing 'real' experience, the case study, is by no means ideal. Although they usually stem from real life, case studies are simplified, and very often simplified in a way that greatly reduces their usefulness, because what is omitted are often those difficulties contained in real-world situations. Such simplifications often lead to students having the view that there is one 'solution' to a case. Indeed, some books of case studies provide lecturers' aids which suggest 'model solutions'. Of course there can be many good design alternatives (as well as thousands of bad ones). Much will depend on people factors, not technological ones, but these people factors are rarely explored fully in case studies.

The case study still has a place, the traditional ways of teaching information systems will be appropriate to the large classes of students who are interested in the subject in the early years of a degree course. But information systems specialists need real experience before they graduate. Action learning allows students who have already completed courses in information systems to practice using some of the methodologies, techniques and tools learnt in a formal course in a real-world situation. Details of one action learning scheme can be found in Avison (1989).

Students of information systems need to gain experience in analysing, designing and implementing information systems of comparable size and complexity to information systems in the 'real world' and to tackle them in project teams. They would also gain from using the information systems and seeing how others use them, integrating them in the organization, using them as an agent of change, identifying appropriate strategies . . ., the list of appropriate experiences is very long. Action learning schemes attempt to address this requirement. One of the major problems of such schemes is the resources required to run them. In an area popular with students, providing resources for good education in information systems is a major problem. In the authors' view, the problem is less with hardware or software resource provision (though this is most frequently cited), and more to do with human resources.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS RESEARCH

Keen (1987), in an overview of research in management information systems, critically examines particular areas of research and also researchers. He argues that the mission of information systems research is to study 'the effective design, delivery, use and impact of information technologies in organizations and society' and he goes on to spell out some of the implications of this. For example, he uses the term 'effective' to imply that practice should be improved through research. He regards systems 'design', described as a craft, as the core area of information systems. It concerns the possibilities and constraints of applying technology. The interest is not with the information technology itself, he argues, but with its application.

Keen (1987) gives a potted history of information systems research in the following form.

1 The early 1970s—managing systems development, design methodologies, economics and computers.

2 The mid 1970s—decision support, managing organizational change, implementation.

3 The early 1980s--productivity tools, database management, personal computing, organizational impacts of technology, office technology.

4 The mid 1980s—telecommunications, competitive implications of IT, expert systems, impact of IT on the nature of work.

There is a high proportion here of hard information systems research (i.e. towards the technology, for example, design methodologies, computers, implementation, productivity tools, office technology, telecommunications), and also research looking for particular gains for businesses in an economically competitive environment (for example, economics and competitive implications), and also research looking for 'solutions' to perennial problems (for example, productivity tools, database management, personal computing and expert systems, which have all had claims, some argue wild claims, made in support of them). The early 1990s may include technological research, but we believe that the emphasis will be more towards social and human aspects. The paper by Lyytinen et al. (1991) reflects this movement.

The set of papers contained in Mumford et al. (1985) sprang from a Colloquium in 1984 called 'Information systems research—a doubtful science'. This was a provocative title because much research in information systems is not concerned with scientific method (although some is—particularly aspects in formal methods and software engineering, structured methods and data analysis) but is concerned with human activities, and may therefore be more in the realm of social science methods.

One tenet of scientific method is that an experiment can be duplicated. An hypothesis is put forward and a series of practical experiments justifies or refutes the hypothesis. The environment of information systems is one where a situation can rarely be duplicated. The environment is one where people, not inanimate objects, are important. Even so, some methods which are used in information systems research—such as a survey/questionnaire—are often used in the sciences. An analysis of case histories illustrating the success or failure of information systems may also be handled in a scientific manner. But survey/questionnaires might require interpretation by the researcher, and interview techniques certainly will. Here, it is more difficult to argue that the methods are objective and therefore 'scientific'.

Other approaches rely on political, ethical and philosophical argument, rather than scientific research, but are relevant to information systems. These do not represent attempts to be objective and rigorous in the sense that research in the natural sciences attempts to be objective.

Some information systems researchers regard attempts to be scientific or reductionist as leading to a loss in the richness of the problem domain. Only a small part of the area of interest may be suitable to scientific method. How can research in information systems, they argue, be narrow enough to avoid questions about objectivity, rigour, repeatability, and yet be broad enough to be useful? It might be argued that information systems research is not amenable to scientific research methods because it concerns human, social and organizational issues or even that it is an area which is unresearchable.

One specific area of concern is that research funds world-wide are directed more to the 'scientific' end of this scientific to people-oriented spectrum of research in information systems. Furthermore, non-scientific papers and research work may not gain the respect of researchers in other areas within academic institutions. This distorts information systems research and thereby limits the future usefulness of that research. Researchers are also handicapped by the lack of a dominant model or framework which has gained general acceptance and against which research efforts in the area can be evaluated.

The categorization of research methods is itself a rich domain. Churchman (1971) examines five authors, Leibniz, Locke, Kant, Hegel and Singer and characterized their 'ideal types' as formal/deductive (based on formal operations and primitive truths), consensus/inductive (generalizations based on experimental data collection), synthetic/representative (a synthesis of these two ideal types), dialectic/conflicting (a method of enquiry based on an assessment of conflicting models) and pragmatic/interdisciplinary (a multi-disciplinary approach having a logical and ethical dimension) respectively.

Other authors have developed this, notably Van Horn (1973), Douglas (1976), Dickson et al. (1977) Ein-Dor & Segev (1981), Galliers (1985), Benbasat et al. (1987) and Wood-Harper (1989). Research methods which may be appropriate to information systems are:

(a) Conceptual study,

(b) Mathematical modelling,

(c) Laboratory experiment,

(d) Field experiment,

(e) Surveys,

(f) Case studies,

(g) Phenomenological research/hermeneutics, and

(h) Action research.

Some of these are formal and mathematical, others social and humanistic, others organizational, others pragmatic and flexible, and, in practice, research may involve the bringing together of a number of these.

It is not appropriate here to describe each of these in detail, partly because there is not uniform acceptance of what each of these is, nor uniform boundaries (the subject requires further conceptual study!) and the topic needs full discussion (see, for example, Wood-Harper & Avison, 1991). The choice of research method should depend on the area of concern. But it also depends on factors alluded to above, such as what type of research is acceptable to funding bodies, to university departments, and to assessors of various kinds. Action research (see Mansell, 1991) is particularly vulnerable. In action research it is recognized that the researcher is not impartial. In scientific method there is an assumption that the researcher is impartial, but when the domain of concern is information systems, which includes human activities, viewpoints and policies, then the notion of impartiality is less achievable. Action research is sometimes seen as consultancy and is sometimes regarded as unacceptable in university departments. There are exceptions, for example at Lancaster University (Checkland, 1981).

An interesting review of information systems research taken from a survey of information systems researchers is found in Nissen (1986). The survey asked the question (amongst others): 'What are the boundaries of information systems research?'. Many respondents did not wish to put any such boundaries on information systems research. Furthermore, when asked whether information systems only referred to formal, fully predictable systems, about 90% responded that they disagreed whereas only about 5% agreed fully. The information systems community may be 'liberal', but their paymasters are not!

## Biographies

David Avison has worked for a number of companies as chief programmer, systems analyst and project leader and as independent consultant. He is senior lecturer at Aston University's Department of Computer Science and has had 17 years lecturing experience. He has had two visiting fellowships in Australia and has also lectured widely in the United States. He leads the information engineering research group at Aston, and has published several research papers as well as 10 books on information systems. As well as being joint editor of the Journal of Information Systems, he is joint editor (also with Guy Fitzgerald) of Blackwell Scientific Publications' series of texts on Information Systems.

Guy Fitzgerald worked with British Telecom and followed this with several years' experience as a consultant with, for example, CACI International and Euroco Computer Systems. He was senior lecturer at Thames Polytechnic and was a lecturer in the Department of Computer Science at Aston University, then at the School of Industrial and Business Studies at the University of Warwick, before taking up his present position as Fellow in Information Management at Templeton College, University of Oxford. He has published widely in the area of information systems.
