# Action research in information systems development

G Mansell

School of Computing and Mathematics, The Polytechnic, Queensgate, Huddersfield HD1 3DH, UK.

Abstract. Action research is located within a spectrum of problem-solving activity that ranges from pure basic scientific research, the purpose of which is to add to knowledge, to human action designed to achieve a purpose without thought of contributing to knowledge. Action research both adds to knowledge and applies it in practice. Action research is particularly appropriate in information systems development. A number of alternative methodologies for action research exist that make differing philosophical assumptions. A body of theory exists that enables a choice to be made among methodologies. This theory is useful in analysing information system problems.

Keywords: action research, information systems, methodology choice, systems methodology.

## INTRODUCTION

This paper considers the relevance of action research in the subject area of information systems. The philosophy of action research is explained, as is its suitability for research into information systems development. A number of alternative methodologies exist for use when conducting action research, and these will be reviewed. The problem of choosing among these methodologies will be discussed. Finally, the implications for information systems development will be discussed.

## ACTION RESEARCH

Problem-solving activity can be ranked on a spectrum ranging from pure basic research in subjects such as mathematics and physics, through to the activity involved in managing complex organizations. Basic scientific research is characterized by objectivity and empiricism. Scientific problems are defined by academics and their research is published in specialist academic journals. The process of managing complex organizations is not thought of as research at all. Management is characterized by subjectivity and pragmatism, and descriptions of the process and the way in which managers solve problems are rarely published. One end of the problem-solving spectrum is conventionally dignified by the title of 'research', but the other end is apparently pure 'action', with no explicit attempt to generate new knowledge.

Action research falls midway in the spectrum between pure basic scientific research and pure action, designed to achieve a purpose without adding to knowledge. Action research has the following characteristics.

1 The researcher seeks to add to knowledge, but is also concerned to apply knowledge and become involved in the implementation of plans.

2 The problem to be solved is not defined by the researcher but by participants in human activity (e.g. managers in organizations).

Heller (1986), in analysing the application of knowledge in the social sciences, distinguishes between research action and action research. In a research action project a 'real-world' problem is investigated, but there is greater stress on fact-finding than implementation of recommendations. In action research there is less of an emphasis on fact-finding and more on implementation of a plan designed to solve the problem. Heller usefully highlights the tension between analysis and intervention when making this distinction, but both types of project will be regarded as action research in this paper. Some academics become involved in projects where there is no prolonged analysis of the problem situation, and expertise and judgement is relied on to make and execute recommendations. This type of project is on the borderline of action research and consultancy.

In summary, action research must involve analysis of a problem situation not controlled by the researcher, the making of plans for intervention in the situation, and the attempted execution of these plans. Projects may vary in the proportion of these three elements they exhibit. Action research is not objective empirical research, but neither is it straightforward consultancy. A good action research project will add to knowledge, either about the area in which it is applied or about the process of applying it.

The acceptance of problems to be solved from a world not under the control of the researcher, ('real world' rather than 'laboratory problems') raises important issues of ontology and epistemology. Problem owners may make use of concepts that are alien to the researcher or are value-laden. Problem owners do not typically provide objective facts to a supposedly neutral researcher. Problem owners have their own theories about the cause of their problems and the solutions required. The would-be problem solver similarly makes use of value-laden concepts, and is perhaps faced with the challenge of justifying 'academic' rather than 'local' concepts. For example, the researcher is likely to have to introduce a method for problem structuring to the problem owners, and facilitate its collaborative use. The researcher may have to negotiate pluralist or coercive situations where there is no common agreement on the objectives to be sought.

The need for the researcher to gain acceptance from the problem owners, in particular if there is to be substantial involvement in implementation, means that there is a risk of the distinction between ‘researcher’ and ‘researched’ becoming blurred. Checkland (1981) accepts this risk and argues that the participation of the researcher in the project is part of the subject to be researched. Action research is, therefore, said to be reflexive. The researcher acts and simultaneously observes himself acting. Action involves reliance on theory and provides a means of substantiating that theory, while at the same time providing research material for enhanced theories of action.

There is no agreed philosophical basis for action research, and this explains the great variety of methodologies recommended for use when applying it. Four of these methodologies will be described later, but some underlying issues are as follows.

1 Do human activity systems exist as entities which have a life relatively independent of the human beings that perform the activity?

2 Is the appearance of human activity, whether systemic or not, to be explained by unseen structure-generating mechanisms?

3 Is it possible to obtain objective facts about human activity, or is there necessarily an element of subjective interpretation involved in 'fact finding'?

4 Is 'problem solving' correctly to be regarded as external intervention in a malfunctioning system, or is the problem solver himself part of a wider system and, from some points of view, part of the problem.

The varying answers that can be given to these questions explain the differences between positivist, structuralist and interpretive approaches to research (Burrell & Morgan, 1979; Morgan, 1983), and between ‘conventional’ and ‘critical’ management science.

## ACTION RESEARCH IN INFORMATION SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT

An information system (IS) consists of a set of stored data and a set of processes that operate on the data. The stored data are often visualized as representing the values of the attributes of a set of entities which are related in a number of ways (Chen, 1976; Tsichritzis & Lochovsky, 1982; Howe, 1983). Information system processes respond to events in the environment of the IS and alter the attributes of entities and/or the relationships between them accordingly. IS processes also retrieve and transform data as required by information users. Typically IS are developed and used by formal organizations concerned with facilitating communication between organizational workers, and coordinating and controlling organizational activities (Davis & Olson, 1985). Organizational IS typically make use of information technology supplied by specialist vendors of hardware and software.

The major problems that arise in the development of organizational IS are as follows:

(a) secure determination of requirements for IS,

(b) matching appropriate technology to IS requirements, and

(c) bringing about successful implementation of IS projects.

Failure in any of these activities can bring about user dissatisfaction and waste of investment. IS development provides rich opportunities for the practice of action research. The treatment of IS development as a form of engineering which ignores the social dimension, is liable to fail (Klein & Lyytinen, 1985; Nissen, 1985; Lyytinen, 1987; Willcocks & Mason, 1987). Many aspects of the development of IS need more than routine consultancy to ensure success; particularly requirements determination and the non-technical aspects of implementation.

## METHODOLOGIES FOR ACTION RESEARCH

Four methodologies for use in action research will now be described. These are Operational Research (OR), Soft Systems Methodology, Sociotechnical Systems Design, and Viable Systems Diagnosis. It is not claimed that these are the only methodologies suitable for action research, but they form a coherent set because of their origin in systems thinking. Their use may not give rise to information systems development (unlike methodologies such as Structured Systems Analysis and Design Method (SSADM) described by Downs et al. (1988)), although arguably an OR project generates required information. Nevertheless, their use may bring valuable clarification of problems, and allow design to proceed more securely if that is what seems desirable.

## Operational research

An OR study consists of the following activities:

(1) problem definition,

(2) data collection and analysis,

(3) model building and testing,

(4) solution (option) formulation and verification, and

(5) implementation.

In a ‘classic’ OR study, stage 3 involves the construction and testing of a mathematical model. The underlying philosophy of OR is realist, in that OR models are assumed to be of systems that exist in the world. The OR paradigm is also positivist, in that stage 2 involves establishing ‘facts’ about the real world system, and stages 3 and 4 involve ‘experiments’ that test theories about the operation of the system. In essence, the OR paradigm consists of discovering and representing the nature of a system whose operation is to be controlled or influenced. By manipulating a model of the problematic system and drawing conclusions from this, recommendations for action can be made.

The following example, provided by Wells (1985), is of OR used to help management take a decision. This is a very common application of OR techniques. The decision to be taken was whether to retain an in-house distribution system using company vehicles, or to use third-party hauliers. If the in-house system were retained, a number of logistical changes might be desirable. If third-party hauliers were used, a choice had to be made among competing hauliers. The need for the decision arose because forthcoming changes to EC regulations altered the economics of the existing operation. The technique of decision analysis was used to present the options open to the decision-makers.

Although this study might appear to be of a mechanistic system raising only technical issues, the organizational context generated issues peculiar to human activity. For example, the case study makes it clear that decision analysis had been ‘pioneered’ in the company over a period of years prior to its use in this case. Successful use of the technique in this case arguably depended in part on the readiness of the management to co-operate with the OR analysts, and management’s expectation of a successful outcome. OR techniques are not neutral with respect to organizational context. Another aspect of the case is the fact that the problem context gave rise to industrial relations issues. The outcome of the project was that a decision was taken to use third-party hauliers. This meant that the company drivers were no longer required and they lost their jobs. As there had been a history of conflict between the management and the company drivers, use of OR in this case was clearly not politically neutral. The nature of coercive problem contexts is referred to later in this paper. OR has been criticized for the 'managerial' orientation it frequently exhibits (see for example Rosenhead, 1987).

## Soft Systems Methodology

Soft Systems Methodology (SSM), as defined by Checkland (1989a), consists of:

(a) finding out about a problem situation,

(b) naming relevant human activity systems in 'root definitions',

(c) building conceptual models from the root definitions, and

(d) taking action in the situation to bring about improvement.

Finding out about a problem situation can be established by applying the methodology itself (i.e. the methodology can be used iteratively). Alternatively 'finding out' can be performed via three related analyses.

1 Identifying occupiers of the roles of ‘clients’ ‘would-be problem solvers’ and ‘problem owners’.

2 Defining the actors in the problem situation as a set of role holders obeying norms of behaviour, whose performance is evaluated according to certain standards.

3 Analysing the politics of the problem situation.

Root definitions and conceptual models are of ideal-type human activity systems. They are not intended to be models of any existing real world system. Each makes sense as an expression of a particular Weltanschauung.

Taking action to bring about improvement necessitates the specification of changes which are both desirable and feasible in the problem situation. The need for, and the nature of, these changes may be established by comparing conceptual models with real world actions and drawing conclusions.

The underlying philosophy of SSM is idealist, in that conceptual models are not of systems that exist in the real world. Different systems may be perceived as relevant by different stakeholders, each with a different Weltanschauung. SSM is not positivist because it assumes that there are no objective 'facts' to be had, only interpretations valid from a particular point of view. SSM is a practical expression of the interpretive paradigm in social science.

An example of the use of SSM is provided by Checkland (1989b). The methodology was applied to the information and library function of a UK company in a science-based industry. The client for the project was the manager of the function. The would-be problem solvers were three managers within the function, together with two academics. Possible candidates for the role of problem owner were potential and actual users of the information service, the professionals providing the service, the company itself, the study team, and the manager of the function. In the spirit of SSM no clear-cut 'problem' was defined which the team had to 'solve'.

Among the large number of problem themes that emerged, however, were the need to respond to company requirements for reductions in staffing, and the need to improve liaison with the users.

Four relevant systems were defined, one of which seemed to be central—a system to manage information as a resource. Two alternative root definitions were drafted, one from the point of view of company senior management, and the other from that of the information users. During the process of conceptual modelling and comparison with real world action, the perspective of the team changed from viewing the service as a reactive function responding to user requests, to a proactive function that provided professionals with the information they needed. A user-orientated model was elaborated and used to define required roles and manpower requirements.

Among the recommendations for change were that information provision should play a more central role in the company, and that users become more 'active' in contributing to the provision. The end result of the project was a re-definition of the role of information management in the company, and a revised means of performing the function.

## Socio-technical Systems Design

The socio-technical systems approach, as defined by Susman (1983), makes the following assumptions.

1 A problem situation can be visualized as two correlated technical and social systems (i.e. a socio-technical system).

2 A socio-technical system produces less than optimal performance when there is a mismatch between the social and technical systems, or between the socio-technical system and its environment.

3 Certain properties of each system need to stay within specifiable limits to maintain a consistent pattern of relationships.

The technical system is analysed as follows. Identify:

(a) the system boundary,

(b) the system's inputs and outputs,

(c) the activities within the system by which inputs are converted to outputs, and

(d) the spatio-temporal flow of activities.

The social system is analysed as follows. Identify:

(a) the roles of social system members,

(b) the values of social system members,

(c) the nature of the rewards received by the members, and

(d) the expectations of the members about their participation in the system.

The socio-technical analysis is used to diagnose potential problems, e.g.

(a) inappropriate boundary location,

(b) no consensus on values—no means of resolving conflict,

(c) unsatisfying jobs, and

(d) an inefficient and/or ineffective technical system not under the control of social system members.

The socio-technical paradigm is inherently dualist, in that it is assumed that empirical knowledge can be gained about objectively existing technical systems, but that these systems are created and operated by social system members striving to achieve objectives that can be understood only by an act of interpretation.

Socio-technical systems ideas have influenced the work of Mumford (1983), who has developed a methodology known as ETHICS. This consists of the following stages:

(a) defining the problem or opportunity,

(b) describing the existing technical system,

(c) identifying the mission of the social system members,

(d) listing efficiency reducers in the technical system,

(e) listing job satisfaction reducers in the social system,

(f) identifying contributory factors to job satisfaction in the social system,

(g) identifying contributors to effectiveness in the technical system,

(h) forecasting changes to the environment of the socio-technical system, and

(i) specifying required change to the socio-technical system.

An application of ETHICS is described in Mumford (1985). A Management Services department was studied, responsible for information systems development in an organization. Problems encountered by this group were associated with control of projects and internal communications. Both technical efficiency and job satisfaction were reduced by these problems. Changes required to overcome these problems were specified as the development of a database to assist in monitoring and control of projects, and the development of an electronic mail network to improve company communications.

## Viable Systems Diagnosis

Viable Systems Diagnosis is a process of reviewing organizational activity in the light of the Viable Systems Model (VSM; Beer, 1985), and making appropriate recommendations for change. It is also possible to use the VSM to assist in organizational design from scratch. These processes are described by Espejo (1989a). The managerial significance of the VSM is assessed by Jackson (1989), who summarizes Beer's account of the structure of the VSM as follows. All viable systems need to possess five functions (known as Systems 1–5). The System 1 of an organization consists of the parts of it that perform operations. Each part should be autonomous so that it can absorb environmental variety. This means that the components of System 1 must themselves be viable systems, and must exhibit the five functions—the model is 'recursive'. The management of the organization (Systems 2–5) emerges from the need to facilitate the operations of System 1. System 2 co-ordinates the activity of System 1. System 3 performs a cybernetic control function. System 4 is an intelligence function that scans the environment of the organization and captures internal data. System 5 is responsible for policy. Great importance is placed on the nature of the information channels linking Systems 1–5, and the organization and its environment.

Viable Systems Diagnosis has been used to detect a number of organizational malfunctions. Examples of these are:

(a) mistakes in articulating levels of recursion, so that a sub-system at some level is not viable,

(b) performance of irrelevant or inappropriate tasks,

(c) vital organizational functions missing or ineffective, and

(d) weakness in information flow and processing.

The underlying philosophy of the VSM is realist, in that organizations are assumed to exist in the world. Viable Systems Diagnosis is structuralist rather than positivist, however, according to Jackson (1987), because surface phenomena are assumed to be explicable by an underlying structural mechanism. The VSM is argued to apply not only to organizations but to all forms of systemic functioning.

An example of the use of the VSM is provided by Espejo (1989b). A manufacturing company in the electrical engineering sector was studied. Its main business was the assembly of engine-driven electrical generating sets. It also provided spare parts to its customers and offered a servicing capability. A viable system diagnosis revealed the non-viability of its spare parts and servicing business. Also, the intelligence function of the whole company was inadequate and the company had a weak identity.

## THE PROBLEM OF METHODOLOGY SELECTION

The problem of selecting among alternative methodologies for action research has been addressed by Jackson & Keys (1984). They categorize the contexts in which problems may be perceived in terms of variations in the complexity of the systems in which the problem is located, and variations in the relationships between the participants in the situation. Whether a system is viewed as simple or complex depends on the interest and purpose of the observer of the system. Simple systems will be perceived as consisting of a small number of elements with few or regular interactions. A system may be experienced as complex for a variety of reasons, including the large number of its components and their inter-relationships, whether it is difficult to observe the operation of the whole system, whether it is difficult to construct a quantitative model of the system and whether the system changes its structure dynamically. Systems complexity is also analysed by Flood & Carson (1988). Jackson & Keys (1984) distinguish between mechanical problem contexts, which contain simple systems manifesting relatively easy problems, and systemic problem contexts, which contain complex systems manifesting difficult problems.

The system in which the problem exists is one factor in determining the character of the problem context, and the other is the nature of the relationships among the decision makers in the system. If the decision makers agree on a common set of goals for the system and make decisions in accordance with those goals, then the problem context is described as unitary. If the decision makers cannot agree on a common set of goals and make decisions which are in accordance with differing objectives, then the problem context is described as pluralist.

As a result of their analysis Jackson & Keys (1984) classify problem contexts into four different types:

(a) mechanical-unitary,

(b) systemic-unitary,

(c) mechanical-pluralist, and

(d) systemic-pluralist.

They then suggest suitable methodologies for tackling problems in each of the four contexts. It is suggested that problems within a mechanical-unitary problem context may be adequately dealt with using the techniques of classical OR. In systemic-unitary contexts the systems of concern have many elements in close inter-relationship and exhibit behaviour which is difficult to predict. There is, however, full agreement about the goals of the system(s). In these circumstances Beer's VSM (Beer, 1985) is thought to be relevant. It seems likely that socio-technical systems design is also compatible with systemic-unitary contexts. Jackson & Keys (1984) define a mechanical-pluralist problem context as one in which the pluralism concerns differences amongst decision makers (of whom the problem solver may be one) outside the system, because the component parts of mechanical systems are passive, not purposeful. If the disagreements among decision makers can be resolved, then the problems remaining can be solved using OR. In a systemic-pluralist context, however, the systems components are purposeful, e.g. conscious actors or organized bodies, and are not in agreement as to the goals to be pursued. In this context Jackson & Keys (1984) recommend a soft-systems approach such as SSM.

Jackson (1987) and Keys (1988) have separately elaborated the contingency theory for methodology selection, imparting a different orientation in each case. Keys (1988) develops a 'methodology for methodology choice'. Jackson (1987) extends the contingency framework by redefining pluralist contexts as those in which the participants have to some extent differing objectives, but a genuine compromise can be reached upon which all agree (because their fundamental interests are not irreconcilable). He then identifies a new type of relationship that may exist between participants in a problem context—a coercive relationship.

If, however, any consensus that exists is only achieved through the exercise of power and by domination (overt or more or less concealed) of one or more groups of participants over others, then the problem-context will be called coercive. . . If management scientists are simply content to serve the powerful in organizations they can use one of the approaches already mentioned (traditional, cybernetic, soft) in these contexts, content that the existence of coercion will help guarantee success, at least in the short term. If, however, they wish to bring about 'whole' system improvement they must in some way equalize the balance of power in the situation and get rid of the structures of inequality which allow some groups to dominate others.

According to the logic of the Jackson and Keys framework coercive situations may be either mechanical or systemic. Jackson (1987) argues that Ulrich's Critical Systems Heuristics (Ulrich, 1983) is based on assumptions consistent with the mechanical-coercive category and suggests 'an approach based upon radical-structuralism is more apt in systemic-coercive contexts'.

More research into problem solving in coercive situations is clearly required because of lack of guidance on how to proceed. Many situations that are treated as unitary or pluralist may in fact be coercive.

## IMPLICATIONS FOR INFORMATION SYSTEMS RESEARCH

In the case of mechanical-unitary contexts where an information system is required, a distinction must be drawn between situations where no research is required at all and those where there is some degree of uncertainty. In the former case a methodology such as SSADM may be used to implement well understood requirements. Where there is some degree of uncertainty, use of OR techniques may be beneficial. Examples of OR techniques relevant to IS development include: decision analysis to select among alternative hardware and software packages; simulation to size hardware accurately; queueing theory to design multi-terminal systems; and network analysis to control projects. OR techniques can also be used to implement information requirements directly, particularly when these match the capability of a standard software package. Examples are the use of spreadsheet packages in financial modelling; the use of visual interactive modelling in production applications, and the use of expert system shells in decision support systems.

In the case of systemic-unitary contexts a socio-technical approach may prove to be of value in IS development. Information systems are examples of technical systems, and when implemented using advanced information technology their technical nature is made entirely explicit, for example in the logic of computer programs or in rule bases. Nevertheless, the purpose of an IS needs to be agreed as a result of social interaction (Mansell, 1990). Moreover, as technical systems, IS merely produce data. For these data to be useful information they have to be presented to a user in a form and at a time that facilitates the taking of a decision or the performance of a task. Information systems are therefore 'embedded' in social systems. Use of the VSM can be made in diagnosing the viability of technical systems. Socio-technical systems design and viable systems diagnosis are not incompatible, and their use together may be beneficial.

The socio-technical approach and viable systems diagnosis contain no explicit method of confronting and managing conflict about system objectives. SSM may be used in pluralist situations to clarify the views of stakeholders about desirable ends. SSM can be used both to clarify issues and define primary tasks. Wilson (1984) has shown how an SSM primary task conceptual model may be used to derive information systems requirements.

Coercive situations are not uncommon in IS development and may give rise to severe implementation problems. Examples include the introduction of information technology in the printing and publishing industry, the introduction of MINOS into the coal mines, and the automation of the recording, assessment, calculation and payment of UK social security and welfare benefits. All these examples are described by Willcocks & Mason (1987). Information technology is often introduced as part of a deliberate policy of job degradation (Child, 1987). In coercive situations the action researcher is faced with a moral dilemma. Action on behalf of management may be inimical to the interests of other stakeholders. Whether SSM is likely to be helpful in these circumstances remains a subject for debate in the systems movement (Checkland, 1982; Jackson, 1982; Wilmott, 1989).

## Biography

Gilbert Mansell is a lecturer in the School of Computing and Mathematics of Huddersfield Polytechnic, specializing in systems methodology and information systems development. He has practical experience in the computer industry as a systems analyst, and has worked for a major computer manufacturer and a number of computer users. He has a Bachelor's degree from the Open University including credits in systems and computing, and a Master's degree from Aston University in computer science. He is a chartered engineer, and a member of the British Computer Society and the Operational Research Society. His master's dissertation was on data analysis for database design. His recent research has been into software usability, concentrating on problem identification and structuring.
