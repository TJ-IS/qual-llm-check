# Information systems and uncertainty: a conspiracy of misplaced confidence

B Straub and IO Angell

Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics, London, UK

Abstract. Information technology is becoming an increasingly important factor in contemporary management. Information systems are being set up in business explicitly to accommodate the new opportunities of this technology, and these are having a lasting effect on managerial practice. The full implications of this technology-driven development have not been appreciated by organizations, which in absorbing technological systems, are tolerating a great drain on their resources.

The efficacy of computers is predicated on the acceptance of some very specific perspectives. A growing discontent and disappointment with their limited achievements is our reason for advocating a different point of view. We will discuss how a shift in perspective, particularly in respect to the perception of uncertainty, will affect thinking and practice in the field of management support systems. We will render our view of the application of computerized decision support systems (DSS), and especially, we will focus on the beliefs and assumptions that have shaped this technology thus far. To this end we will discuss the concept of uncertainty, by juxtaposing current thinking and practice with our understanding of the context of its application. Finally, we will place our understanding of uncertainty in a managerial context and explain the implications this would have for strategists.

Keywords: decision support systems, information systems, uncertainty.

## CURRENT DSS THINKING AND PRACTICE

Basically, the software of a computerized information system can be thought of as a structured set of numerical codes. Depending on particular combinations of binary logic, set theory and combinatorics, software is differentiated into species, like word-processors, expert systems, or DSS. This logico-mathematical nature of software requires the pre-eminence of numerical theories in software production. One apparent manifestation of this is that probability, rather than uncertainty, is the guiding principle for DSS. Probability has even been called the 'language of uncertainty' (Schmitt, 1969). It is important to note here that probability and uncertainty are used interchangeably in every-day language. However, in the case of DSS, probability is fundamental to classifying decision-making into the different realms of certainty, risk and uncertainty $^{1}$ . Neither certainty nor risk nor uncertainty play an important role here, they are just labels to indicate which application of probabilities is to be used. Consequently, everything that springs from this theory is subjugated to the acid test of probabilistic rigour. This predominance of probability is reverberating deep in people's working lives.

We see a trend in favour of the unquestioned investment in new technology, especially hi-tech, in preference to its rational and sensible assessment. In fact, there is even a tendency to search for new ways of satisfying technical conditions just to apply the technology. A consequent numerical bias leads to dubious attempts at mapping social phenomena into numbers and functions. On the one hand, the new technology fosters a perceived 'need to apply probability—our measure of uncertainty—to whatever is of concern to us' (Schmitt, 1969) in order to satisfy the technological imperative. On the other hand, the human condition does not lend itself to simplistic 'sociometrics'. Caught in this conflict of interest, current DSS thinking and practice tilts in favour of the numerical option. The belief that 'the act of balancing (several goals against each other) can be said to reflect a decision maker's desire to maximize a utility function that is not explicitly known' (Hemming, 1978) or that '... if point estimates² are given for all states (of the problem) the optimal action can easily be identified ...' (Hederstierna, 1981), shows clearly that in this conflict common sense has been abandoned in favour of mathematical sophistication. Likening a manager's behaviour to a 'utility function' and promising an 'optimal action' are indicative of this misguided vision of the human predicament. Such simplistic, although mathematically elaborate, concepts are a threat to managerial responsibility, which has come about not least through enthusiastic self-promotion of the IS community. 'Complete information' (Lindley, 1985) is only possible within simplistic concepts of reality. That we are charmed and fascinated by it, should prompt questions about its feasibility, justification, likelihood and desirability.

Whether a manager goes about their decision making by formulating a problem and consulting solely numerical information, or whether they really do make decisions, is rarely on the agenda $^{3}$ . Yet the manager is effectively prevented from responding creatively, because they are limited from the very start, not only to a certain conceptualization of a problem, but also to a certain conceptualization of what consistitutes a problem $^{4}$ . Such implications must be recognized. Such argument is not ‘too philosophical to be of practical value’. The reluctance to discuss these issues, and the preference to jump on the bandwagon of current DSS thinking and practice, will have severe repercussions.

However, due to the unfortunate fact that the more imprecision being accepted in the probability estimate, the less likely that the optimal action can be identified, the analyst would be tempted to “force” the assessor to be more precise than knowledge admits’ (Hederstierna, 1981). What is more, the analyst may inadvertently conceal this application of force because it is not even apparent to him. An ideological bias towards numbers and measurement, which led him to use computerized information systems in the first place, leads him to presume that forced probabilities are valid representations of reality. In fact, what happens is that a system of numerical reasoning is applied to a situation which might have completely different systemic properties, or perhaps none at all. As a consequence, tensions arise that are created by the very use of the computerized system.

It is reasonable to argue that there are enough problems where numerical data is of paramount relevance for a decision maker. We accept this, but maintain that no problems exist where numerical data in itself suggests a particular course of action. No number of 'what if' simulations on spreadsheets can tell a manager what he will be facing in the future. He cannot deduce appropriate actions solely from numerical output.

Unfortunately, computerized information systems lend themselves to a practice which obscures the need for interpretation. Because an information system cannot assess its data critically, the manager is called upon to make the connection between numerical data and its relevance to a decision $^{5}$ . He is left with the task of using his critical powers to evaluate the apparently unsystematic emergence of frictions, which constitute more uncertainty. In addition to dealing with the problem, the manager also has to worry about the efficacy of the information system. This is a serious burden on the manager, who now has to face an increased professional load placed upon him by the restricted validity of the numerical system, aggravated by the technical task of managing the machinery.

The lust for numerical solutions is spreading. It may be only a matter of time until your job is under 'numerical attack'. To support this argument, it shows how the above problem of increased professional workload has already found a numerical solution. Simon (1960) suggested the classification of problems of uncertainty along a linear scale, from 'programmable' to 'non-programmable'. That way, he claimed, a manager was relieved from worrying about the appropriateness of the DSS. Yet, this only appears to be a solution because the uncertainty about the correct usage of this classification is at least as acute as the uncertainty about the efficacy of DSS. The application of such simple concepts, such as a linear scale, turn out to be simplistic, and cause additional hiccups for the management of the information system. In any case, the reader is welcome to imagine the organizational turmoil springing from the necessity of judging the uncertainties within a large organization along a linear scale of 'programmability' $^{6}$ .

The pitfall of the computerized information system, not to be able to assess reality other than by a numerical concept, is mirrored in the IS approach to strategy. In fact, the forecasting techniques of IS are merely an assignment of numbers to the future. That these numbers can at best be as adequate or as inadequate as the input, points to the poverty of this approach. Using such forecasts as a strategic tool can, therefore, only be the result of the belief that numbers are meaningful in relation to the future. The strategy becomes more a matter of controlling the future by labelling it, rather than a matter of continually (re-evaluating the situation. This approach is, akin to numerology, searching for the right number to represent the future $^{7}$ ). If one considers the dumbness of the information system and contrasts it with the manager's intimate relationship to his environment, it is evident that it is the managers apprehension that directs this search rather than 'impartial science'.

In summary, the perspectives and assumptions at the heart of current DSS thinking and practice are based on a belief in a rule-based reality. They suggest that rule-based information systems can give meaningful output without the interpretation of the manager. Linked to this is an assumption that numbers are autonomously and uniquely meaningful. Numerical measurements are assumed to be capable of representing human and social phenomena; a case in point is a manager's 'utility function' (see earlier). A manager's reality is supposed to consist of a succession of problem situations that can adequately be expressed numerically, and that the numerically derived result is a good approximation to a solution, if not the solution. Reality is assumed to be equivalent to, and treated as identical with, the information that represents it, so that optimal actions can be taken using the power of an information system.

## ANOTHER PERSPECTIVE OF UNCERTAINTY IN ITS RELATION TO A MANAGER'S TASK

The contrast between our position and this belief in measurement and numbers could not be more pronounced. To us, uncertainty is a foreboding, where the surprise of imminent changes is outside a person's control. Insecurity, stress, fear, panic, immobility, but also trust, confidence and courage are human responses in such circumstances. Personal characteristics, needs and aspirations, social integration, quality, motivation and loyalty are relevant for the evaluation and significance of uncertainty. It has to be understood in a context of 'communicative actions' $^{8}$ (Habermas, 1981), rather than as an instrumental concept for numerical models. Decision-making under uncertainty is a social phenomenon that becomes meaningful only in a manager's relation to their environment and is, thus, part of their self-determined personal reality. As such, it eludes any generalizing characterizations, and even more complex attempts at grasping the concept of uncertainty and its significance to managerial reality appear pseudo-scientific rather than real. Sensing that an attempt to come to terms with an idealized notion of uncertainty is fruitless, it nevertheless seems worthwhile to consider uncertainty to be perpetual. Perpetual uncertainty is experienced as an integral part of the total managerial reality. As such it is subjective and individual, and any frameworks that lead the manager to deal with uncertainty in a specialized (numerical?) way are bound to lead to dilemmas. With such an understanding of uncertainty, it is apparent that a numerical response to uncertainty by a computerized information systems is nonsensical.

Rejecting an instrumental treatment of uncertainty leaves many questions unanswered. Answers will have to be expressed that lack the convenient readiness of pull-down menus. The manager's reality is essentially without explanation. Conceptualizing this reality as a sequence of problem situations and dealing with it in a generalized way, reduces reality to comforting impersonal concepts. Linking in computers and asking for the appropriate information makes sense only in such an 'aseptic' representation of a manager's reality. No manager could ever relate to the meaning of 'appropriate' in this context, nor could he see why a particular machine should solve his problems.

Nevertheless, many managers prefer to deal with machines and methods, because these are seemingly more controllable than people, with all their idiosyncrasies. It seems to be a cultural preference that the stability of concepts, even if not particularly applicable, is to be trusted in preference to the 'awkwardness' of people. It is mirrored in the scientific treatment of uncertainty; Luce & Raiffa (1957) prefer to look for a reasonable method of optimality, rather than to 'flounder in semantic debate'. Yet, rather than trying to safeguard against human nature, managers should use their insight to sense the problems of dependency on machines and methods, and avoid faltering in 'semantic silence'.

The conceptual clarity and the apparent lack of ambiguity make computational methods so appealing. But a manager's job is also characterized by such vague notions like hunches or feelings as well as peer pressure or opportunism. If faced with uncertainty, a manager cannot know what might crop up and from where. This means that they have to assess the past, the present and the future, free from any limitations that impede their competitiveness. Thus, uncertainty is not just characterized by the absence of a meaningful assignment of probabilities (just as peace is not characterized by the absence of war) but by an existentially critical interpretation of a manager's position.

No amount of probabilistic or speculative input is sufficient to indicate emergent effects $^{9}$ . What is more, the probabilistic treatment of a critical situation would even lull the manager into a false sense of security and control, because it focuses by definition on the measurements and assumptions of the past (always assuming that what is to be measured has a past), substituting the unknown future with an (elaborate) extrapolation of past trends. Yet, the manager not only has to face uncertainty about the known variables of their environment, but also the unknown variables in the changes around him. A manager's job is characterized by profound uncertainty, the unknown and unknowable. This is where we see a fundamental difference between 'probabilistic uncertainty' which underpins DSS, and 'emergent' or 'transcendental uncertainty' which is symptomatic of so much management decision making.

## TACTICS AND STRATEGIES

Facing uncertainty is, therefore, a matter of accepting that the unimaginable can and will happen and being prepared to deal with it on a level of personal choice. Responses based on mechanistic feedback and other systemic constructs will have little meaning in a context which is not systemic, or where the existence of system is not even perceived. These plans are 'formulations of antecedent conditions and consequences of action that account for action in a plausible way' (Suchman, 1987). Emergent phenomena are only apparent in hindsight, and give no prior indication of emergence. This leads us to advocate a treatment of dealing with uncertainty that can be likened to the cybernetic concept of steering with appropriate tactical responses. All actions are situated, and only in a manager's self-determined reality do they acquire their meaning; in any other context they will acquire a different interpretation. Thus, managers have to steer in the flow of events as they appear meaningful to themselves. This must entail a disposition to act, which is free of the organizational guidelines and of the preconceptions of problem solving and planning.

Without coherent ways of thinking about the place of information systems in respect of the perpetual changes around them, companies can no longer place their faith in technological solutions. A balance must be found between the present emphasis on simple functional issues and short-term advantages of information technology, against the application of information technology both to simplify and to enhance a company's long-term competitive position in light of this profound uncertainty. Organizations must be flexible in the face of the unknown, while at the same time optimizing their response to the opportunities and risks at hand. It depends on a manager's quality to draw a boundary around what he considers the competitive position of his enterprise. Only within such temporary boundaries is it conceivable to approach 'complete information', so that decisions about 'point estimates' can be made. But a boundary is chosen according to the observer's particular purposes and priorities. For most physical phenomena, it is not a problem to achieve a consensus on the choice of boundary, however, the choice of boundaries for social systems is very likely to be a source of doubtful classification, and hence ambiguity. Doubts about a consensus demarcation of a boundary imply that social systems are intrinsically ambiguous! Recognizing the inevitability of such ambiguity means that even the very identification of the coherent whole we call 'system' involves purpose-driven choice. So there can be no absolute analysis, design and implementation of systems; we are left only with perpetually changing questions of appropriate or inappropriate choice.

That information technology will automatically come to the rescue is a vain hope, on the contrary, information technology is now seen as another source of uncertainty. A proficient information system is one that collects and communicates sufficient information concerning changes in the environment to enable a timely and effective response. The raison d'être of computerized information systems is the belief that computers can enhance the effective communication of information, which in turn puts managers in control of their actions so they can make sensible decisions, and hence run the organization successfully. Much of the confusion and application of information systems is caused by the attempt to over use computers for the poor control function to the detriment of communication.

Environments are continuously changing, and to be effective any organization must be capable of a variety of responses to match those changes in the environment (Ashby's 'Law of requisite variety' (Ashby, 1956)). Within this understanding, we differentiate between deterministic/probabilistic mechanisms (with parameters based on sufficient information) which can be the basis of planning and tactics, and unknown processes in the environment, apparent only in hindsight, which can only be dealt with strategically. The dynamic nature of an organization, the accrual of components and sub-systems, feedback, the reality of emergence and synergy, the development of new systems, and the breakdown of old systems from interactions within the environment, imply that there can be no permanent control over an organization which is continuously evolving and emerging. An effective information system helps steer an organization through this environmental turbulence, taking tactical advantage of the environment and avoiding risks, but only within the constraints of strategies that will enable it to deal with the unknown.

To prosper, an organization has to evolve a disposition that will satisfy the Law of Requisite Variety; it must have the facility, by sufficient internal variety, to respond to perpetual changes in its environment, and hence to survive and flourish. A disposition to act is the implicit property of every organization, perhaps even the very essence of being an organization. However, such a disposition is in no way fixed and inflexible. Organizations have complex predispositions before the strategists even start, and these dispositions will evolve and emerge from human actions, with or without, or even contrary to, the considered attentions of these strategists. System inversion is probably the most extreme situation, where the emergent disposition of the system may diametrically oppose the original intentions of the strategists, designers and planners. Poorly applied and inappropriate strategies will result in tension, disruption and constant and perpetual reorganization within an organization, with highly detrimental effects.

We now introduce the adjective ‘strategic’ to describe any influence that has a lasting effect on the disposition of an organization. This influence will have emerged from human actions, but not necessarily human designs! It will be perceived as a benefit if it increases the organization’s ability to generate variety, and a loss otherwise. The optimization of short-term, or even long-term results, is not strategic; the ability to optimize within the organization under varied conditions is. A strategy is taken to be a purposeful human decision and action that is intended to be strategic. But, not all strategies are strategic and not all strategies achieve their intended strategic aims. Just as importantly not all strategic effects are the result of strategies, they can be the result of unintended actions. Time constraints cannot be imposed on a strategy, the time factor only refers to the duration of the effect on the disposition. So strategies are not identified with long-term plans! Neither are they identified with beneficial changes, but only with intended change, and that may not be achieved. Strategies may increase or decrease internal variety, ultimately they may or may not be for the success of the organization. A strategy is not the panacea many textbooks make it out to be. Strategy is not the sole domain of management, it is not just the resolution expressed by the powerful; the actions of everyone in the organization will influence its disposition to a certain extent.

By its disposition, a commercial organization must attempt to profit from its environment. To do this, it must interpret and react to events occurring in the environment. Data on these events are transmitted by an information system to the organization's control, where the management treat it as information, and formulate predictions of trends, model the data, derive plans and transmit tactics (via the information system) for taking advantage of circumstances. Variety is the potential to innovate; innovation affords a greater store of tactics. So from this interpretation, competitive advantage is seen as tactical and not primarily strategic. The tactics used to achieve competitive advantage may turn out to be of lasting effect on the organization's disposition, but whether this strategic influence turns out to be beneficial is by no means certain. It is perfectly conceivable for a tactically advantageous approach of concentrating on previously successful procedures to reduce internal variety and so restrict future capacity to innovate.

Information systems, therefore, can be used both for tactical and strategic applications, which could be mutually contradictory. Their appropriateness cannot be foreseen. Given the uncertainty and continual change in the environment, any tactical gain will be transitory; consequently, changing trends will require changing tactics. The ability necessary to change perception and tactics will require much internal variety. This demonstrates the paramount importance of strategic management over short-term tactical manoeuvring; without continuous innovation and generation of the required internal variety, an organization will be in a permanent state of crisis management.

All tactics lead to crisis if they are not abandoned in time. Exactly because they are meant to exploit confidence in the circumstances of an enterprise to prescribe specific and potentially successful moves, they falter as soon as doubt springs from change of circumstances. If tactics are continued despite change, they effectively prevent the manager from being strategic, and introduce an organizational inertia that ignores new environmental conditions. If information systems, which are supposed to administer tactical goals, change from a means to an end, then they change from a competitive resource to a competitive burden.

In addition to an inertial influence on decision making, tactics may allow new and unintended problems to arise, of a complexity that is obscured by the scale of the organization. These emergent problems can, even over a short period of time, acquire such prominent importance, that a manager is compelled to concentrate their energy on the system that was designed to support them in their initial enterprise (Parkinson, 1986). For example, many of the bureaucratic structures, that stifle so much of modern business, have emerged in the self-defence of bureaucracy.

All this points to problems that arise from the inadequate and inappropriate application of a 'computational strategy' (Montagna, 1980) in a context that, on the evidence of the emerging problems, warrants only a 'compromise strategy' or perhaps an 'inspirational' one (Montagna, 1980). Yet it is not just a question of which type of strategy should be applied. Our criticism aims deeper. How can the application of methodical solutions to barely understood phenomena be justified? In fact, our suggestion of interpreting strategy, not as a plan or method, but as an intention to cause a lasting effect on an organization's disposition, should be taken as a matter of common sense. Plans and methods have their place in what could, in analogy to Kuhn's 'normal science' (Kuhn, 1970), be called 'normal management' (day-to-day administration). Yet, acting under uncertainty, as we described it earlier, is essentially action where the tried and tested does not inspire enough confidence for the application of tactics. A strategy is called for, that allows for the development of 'anomic' or 'anarchic' tactics (Lerner, 1980). When the ambiguity of the situation cannot be resolved into some, possibly systemic, pattern, then the jumping to the application of methods is merely a measure of impulsive stress-relief.

Just as tactics cannot fulfil a strategic task, strategy does not replace tactics. A tactic responds to its environment with a certain 'syntax' of answers, or with certain 'moves'. For these moves, Ashby's 'Law of Requisite Variety' (Ashby, 1956) states a relationship between the variety of actions of two players. If one side has a set pattern of moves, then the other side has complete control over the course of the game. The more variety the opponent introduces in his pattern, the less control the other side has about the course of the game, or as Ashby wrote 'only variety can destroy variety'. For our purpose a manager could be considered to be one player, whereas the manager's entire environment is the second player. In this context the environment has an unlimited variety of moves in store. The manager's tactics though, are limited by their syntax, and consequently are unable to 'destroy' the environment's variety and to have a sufficient effect on disposition, for the survival and prosperity of the enterprise. Of course, in reality a situation cannot be boiled down to a game of two players. But this example refutes simplistic solutions. A manager's reality, alas, asks for 'variety' in a real sense, and a manager's disposition to act can lead them to change tactics, or choose no tactic at all. This action is anomic, and therefore arguably beyond scientific prescription.

The numbers-driven perspective and the one given in this paper, both valid in their own way, account for the fundamental differences in the assessment of DSS, and the ambivalence of management towards it. The difference between the two is that the 'controlling' perspective projects its limited certainty about the future into its strategy, coming up with precise solutions of limited validity, whereas 'steering' projects its unlimited uncertainty into its strategy, suggesting vague statements based on personal justification. The holistic reality of a manager cannot be supported adequately by a machine that is essentially numerical, nor can computerized information systems be trusted to create as much internal variety as people do. In fact, we think that internal variety is reduced by stressing the importance of control rather than of communication in the application of computerized information systems.

## CONCLUSION

It should be clear that uncertainty is as much a hazard as it is an opportunity. The fact that uncertainty is perpetual, supports the point of view that decision making under uncertainty is not a matter of finding the right spot to link in computer power, or of finding the right information. It is not a professional problem, but a matter of a manager's perpetual (self-) analysis; it is a personal affair. A strategy which supports this understanding of uncertainty has to advocate flexibility and variability, as well as the manager's responsibility to excel individually.

The penetration of the managerial environment with information systems leads more and more managers to try some 'what if' scenarios with the computer. Each time these systems are used, the manager should ask themself whether the implicit concepts of the machine procedure are adequate for the situation. Unfortunately, especially in opaque situations, the temptation to get an 'independent' output is great. That this independence is highly dependent on the assumptions and beliefs described here, is possibly not apparent. Besides, using the machine is so convenient and trendy. But it is in exactly these situations that the manager's qualities to steer the enterprise are required. Management is not just a matter of 'machine minding'. It is the manager's decision, and they should choose with care, whether to apply computer power or man power.
