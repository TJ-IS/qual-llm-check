# The effectiveness of office information systems: a social action perspective

K Lyytinen, H Klein\* and R Hirschheim†

Department of Computer Science, University of Jyvaskyla, Finland, \*School of Management, SUNY at Binghamton, and †College of Business, University of Houston, U.S.A.

Abstract. The purpose of this paper is to show that a number of basic issues have not been adequately addressed in existing office information systems research. Prominent among these are the nature and role of offices, the goals of office information systems development, and the nature of its organizational and managerial consequences. It is proposed that office information systems should be analysed as social action systems the behaviour of which is strongly affected by socially determined forces and constraints such as the behaviour-channelling influences of authority, norms, customs, habits and precedence. Four types of social action are discussed: instrumental, strategic, communicative and discursive. Three contexts for perceiving and analysing the effects of social action in offices are introduced: technology, language and organization. Office information systems changes affect elements and relationships in these three contexts in different ways. By cross-relating social action types and contexts, nine classes of object systems are identified. Each object system class implies a different category of effectiveness concerns which in turn implies different office information system design requirements. The paper notes that the existing research literature has primarily been concerned with only three of the nine object systems. For more effective office information systems development, however, the other systems also need to be considered. The paper concludes by exploring how this may be done.

Keywords: effectiveness; human activity systems; office information systems; social action; social systems.

## INTRODUCTION

For many years, academics and practitioners alike have written about the potential benefits of applying information technology in the office. The broad area of office information systems (OIS) grew out of the need to understand better how information systems could and should be applied in offices. In the attempt to design and implement successful OIS, it became apparent that OIS differ from other forms of IS in a number of important ways. First, they focus much more Correspondence Dr Kalle Lyytinen, Department of Computer Science, Seminoarink 15, University of Jyväskylä, SF-40 100 Jyväskylä, Finland.

on the social nature of organizational activities, concentrating on the interaction of social actors in a social setting, i.e. they focus on work (Hirschheim, 1985). Secondly, OIS can be distinguished by their informal and diffused nature (Bracchi & Pernici, 1984). Verrijn-Stuart (1985) described OIS as being: 'characterized by, among other things, variety, 'informality and irregularity, but often interacting strongly with the more orderly, formal and predictable, computer-based information systems used in that organization'. This irregularity manifests itself through the large number of exceptions in carrying out office activities (Sheil, 1983). Thirdly, OIS embrace a richer variety of organizational activities than traditional IS. These activities involve discontinuity in the performance of office tasks, use of heuristics and local (domain-specific) knowledge, and the application of incomplete (even conflicting) data (Borgida, 1986; Auramaki & Leppanen, 1988).

More specifically, in order for OIS to be successful, they must handle the informal nature of office activities, which include conversations (Wynn, 1979; Suchman, 1983), friendly and adversarial social relationships, spontaneous interaction, and 'organizational culture' which embraces myths and rituals (cf. Jellinek et al., 1983). These informal aspects cannot be ignored in establishing system requirements, because they are part and parcel of office work—they allow work to be done. There is nothing new about this contention. Wynn (1979), for example, showed that office conversation carries key information which is crucial for interpreting ambiguous events; Emery & Trist (1969) noted that a friendly social relationship network can be vital for coping with stress; Churchman (1972) and Klein (1980) proposed that adversarial relationships nurture motivation and provide for checks and balances; and Kolb (1984) claimed spontaneous interaction contributes to creativity and learning.

While it should be clear that OIS need to support office activities which are inherently informal, unstructured, rich in variety, and social in nature, much of the literature in the area portrays the office in a rather simplistic light. The current orthodoxy tends to view the office in terms of 'activities' (Engel et al., 1979; Uhlig et al., 1979; Christie, 1981; Cook, 1981; Poppel, 1982), 'functions' (Hammer & Zisman, 1979; Hammer & Sirbu, 1980; Aklilu, 1981; Sirbu et al., 1982), or 'tasks' (Conrath et al., 1981). It is postulated that the current orthodoxy provides too narrow a conception of the office; one which is unable to permit a true appreciation and understanding of the nature of what OIS must support. This we contend leads to system failures, misused systems and/or undesired effects in the larger office environment (cf. Lucas, 195, 1981; Klein & Hirschheim, 1983; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987). It is for this reason that a new framework for understanding the office and OIS is proposed, one based on Habermas' (1984) social action theory. We call this framework a 'social action perspective' and feel it is an improvement on what currently exists in the field because it is based on coherent theoretical foundation of social action. While Habermas' work may be known in current social theory, its application to OIS is novel. The paper argues that by adopting a social action perspective, a much better understanding of both the office and office information systems development is possible.

To show the usefulness of the proposed framework, we develop a taxonomy of effectiveness measures to evaluate OIS designs. These measures provide a theoretically sound basis to evaluate and analyse OIS designs in a variety of settings. By using these measures, OIS developers should be able to assess proposed designs in a broader context than is currently available, and to understand better the nature and composition of the complex we call 'the office'. Applying the framework to OIS research reveals many areas which have yet to receive sufficient attention in the literature. In this way, the framework acts as a guide to future research.

The paper is organized in four sections: initially we introduce the social action perspective then propose a taxonomy of effectiveness measures for OIS design, we then discuss some weaknesses in the taxonomy and propose some ways to use it, and finally we conclude by suggesting how the next generation of OIS design methodologies could be developed based on the social action perspective.

## THE SOCIAL ACTION PERSPECTIVE OF THE OFFICE

The social action perspective fundamentally views all activities of OIS development and use as manifestations of social action. We say 'social' rather than 'human' action in order to emphasize that all human behaviour is influenced by socially determined constraints. Some of these are conscious, such as documented office policies, procedures or public laws; others are subconscious, such as customs, habits, learned precedents, beliefs, managerial ideology, charismatic authority, and so forth. The social action perspective implies that the important aspects in the development and use of OIS are in one way or another socially determined and conditioned. In contrast to the more conventional view of the nature of OIS, the social action perspective claims that human and social concerns cannot be treated as secondary problems which arise at the boundary of the technical system and are solvable by improving the man-machine interface design. Rather, office information systems are social action systems, which to a greater and greater extent, rely on technology for their operation.

## The office

An office may be considered part of human activity system and part of social community. The former stresses the conscious design of a set of interlinked, purposeful human activities. The latter reflects the more informal, random, non-purposeful, and evolutionary character of organically developed social collectivities which are tied together by tradition and emotion. Both aspects are always present in social systems which exhibit this important duality (cf. Checkland, 1981; Vickers, 1983). An office possesses characteristics of purposeful systems in that individuals perform tasks to achieve certain goals (e.g. meeting budget constraints) and non-purposeful systems such as the evolutionary pattern of friendships. It is thus an example of a social system. Conventional approaches to OIS development treat offices as purposeful systems omitting their non-purposeful element, which results in a too narrowly focused and defined OIS. The emphasis is on 'instrumental action' and neglects social relationships. In the following, the basis of a more evenhanded approach is proposed by adopting a framework which is more sensitive to the way OIS development affects the office as a social community. The need for such a view has also been noted by Wynn (1979). In her research on office workers, she found that a background of office practices existed (e.g. practical understanding, coping with day to day living, shared beliefs, assumptions, experiences, etc.), which could not be made explicit because it was so pervasive. Yet it is the totality of these practices which constitutes the inter-subjectively shared world of the office worker; which makes the office a social community; and which enables the office workers to interact meaningfully. ## The social action perspective

The totality of office practices shape a complex of formal and informal office roles. Office actors in playing out their roles often perform activities whose purposes are not at all apparent from a purely rational perspective: hidden agendas which serve the deflection of conflict; the blockage or distortion of communication to maintain psychological consistency; filtering to enhance group interests; etc. It is therefore of key importance to understand the purposes of their actions in trying to analyse the significance of what people do in offices. Office actors can pursue radically different types of purposes in very rapid succession. For instance, in one moment two office workers may try to make sense of a difficult document and then in the next moment one of them gives a specific order to the other and expects it to be followed unquestioningly. The purpose of communication in sense-making is to achieve a common understanding of an ambiguous text. The requirements that must be met for achieving this purpose are very different from the requirement of communication in giving orders. Yet other purposes of communication imply bluffing or misrepresentation, for example when two offices negotiate a new project or contract (see, for example, Ciborra's (1983, 1985) notion of 'opportunism'). In all these cases, the role of OIS must be different. A system defined for recording and tracking instructions and deadlines must meet social acceptance criteria which are quite different from those supporting bargaining and negotiation. In order to take such differences systematically into account, a typology of social action in offices is offered in Table 2.

Table 2 shows four types of social action which are related to two dimensions: the domain of action and the actor's orientation. The two row headings distinguish the two domains of action to which an actor can relate. Actors can see themselves as intervening in either a social or non-social (natural) domain of action. In the former case they consider the co-operative, communicative and emotional arrangements between people in office work and see people as partners. Actors see themselves as being part of the system. This includes the case where they think of the others as opponents or enemies. For example, a system developer can either seek agreement and co-operation with users or treat them as opponents which will resist their rational design. In either case they relate to the office as social domain. In the case when the actor abstracts from the human nature of people they are viewed as objects to be manipulated in a quasi nature-like fashion. For example, if actors merely consider the measurable characteristics of task performance such as letters typed or orders processed, then they treat people as the equivalent of machines. The office is perceived as if emotions, human meanings, and the like can be ignored.

The column headings identify three types of actor orientations which can be used to classify human actions: the achievement of success, shared agreement, or justification of propositions and claims. Each is related to differing values and attitudes. The first is the drive to be successful in the sense of achieving a desired state, outcome or goal. The success orientation is associated with instrumental and strategic action, examples of which are provided under (1) and (2) below. This orientation is sometimes referred to as a 'teleological orientation'. The other two orientations arise only in the social domain, i.e. through human interaction. The category of achieving shared agreement reflects the view that people are partners in communication and singles out human actions aimed at reaching understanding and agreement. This type of action is called communicative action. If understanding or agreement is not achieved, for whatever reason, then discursive action can take over. It is aimed at the justification of propositions and claims which are met with disagreement.

By mapping the two dimensions onto each other, four social action types, as shown in Table 2, are possible. In understanding these social action types, it should be noted that office work is performed by actors or agents which can be either individuals or collectivities (e.g. departments, offices, and committees). Although actors can engage in different types of social action, usually one type of action is dominant, this can be established by probing the basic attitudes with which an actor engages in a specific activity.

Actions that are directed towards achieving success are called purposive-rational or teleological. In this action an actor attempts to achieve measurable objectives. They measure the success of their actions by how nearly they achieve their objective. Purposive-rational action applies technical rules which have passed an empirical means test. There are two types of teleological action: instrumental and strategic.

1 Instrumental action is directed toward objects as though they were inanimate constraints, which can be manipulated in ways that will serve the actors' self interest. Here, human qualities are ignored.

2 Strategic action is directed against rational opponents (actors) who engage in intelligent counter-action. Accordingly, an actor's strategic behaviour must be measured by taking into account the effects of their actions on situations: what serves the benefit of one actor may be harmful to the other. Thus, an actor must cope with both co-operative and conflicting interest situations and find the best strategy to pursue their self-interest.

3 Communicative action replaces the success/teleological orientation with a desire to achieve an agreement or understanding between communicating parties. Communicative action is oriented towards 'consensual norms' which define mutual expectations on how the actors in a given situation should behave in terms of communication. Communicative action takes place through language or sign systems in general. Through language use, a mutual understanding about the world is achieved. This, of course, presupposes that a shared or common pool of background assumptions and beliefs exists.

4 Discursive action occurs when a group of actors' agreement about a shared background is no longer taken for granted. Here, various assumptions concerning communication background are carefully examined, clarified and their validity tested. Discursive action is oriented towards the co-operative search for truth, clarification of unclear message content, analysis of their intended use, and so forth. It is entered into if doubts arise as to whether a message is produced sincerely, whether it is understandable, true, correct, and appropriate for the situation. Thus, discursive action tries to find and weight actors' arguments which are articulated for or against a message in terms of its clarity, truthfulness, correctness and appropriateness. These last four criteria define the validity claims inherently attached to any communication. Discursive action is aimed at justifying (redeeming) any or all of these claims should one become the subject of doubt. This requires that all actors respect certain ground rules (Habermas, 1973; McCarthy, 1978), which can be summarized as allowing everyone the chance to express their opinions, and that only the 'force of the better and more rational argument' is honored.

It should be also noted that an actor's choice of an action type is not in any sense predetermined $^{1}$ . Instead, the choice depends on their cultural background, subjective assessment of the situation, and the domain of action. Additionally, (in the social domain) an actor has the ability to switch from one type to another (e.g. from communicative to strategic). Moreover, the choice between action types may also change quickly, and depends on the interpretation of the actor, i.e. how they read the situation. As an example, consider the process of writing this paper. It may be viewed in instrumental terms (efficiency, cost); in communicative terms (sharing a theory of office action); in strategic terms (improving chances to get tenure and promotion, and/or increasing the visibility of a particular school); or in discursive terms (justification of claims about office designs). Each of these 'readings' may become prevalent in a particular situation.

Our conjecture is that the suggested social action typology is a fruitful vehicle for classifying the rich and varied forms of office activity. It also helps us to obtain a richer understanding of what goes on in the office by providing us with a conceptual scheme for highlighting essential components in office work. For example, consider the physical environment of the office. It is perceived by many as a natural target for instrumental action, particularly when office workers are seen as being replaceable by machines because only their physical or behavioural (deterministic) capabilities are considered. In strategic action, office actors pursue their self-interests and view others as enemies or partners in achieving their ends. This is manifested in offices in many ways: political arrangements, bargaining, truces, bribery, horse-trading, and the like. In communicative action, people try to reach an agreement on what goes on or what should be done in an office based on a presupposed common background (called the 'life-world' (Habermas, 1984)). This happens, for example, when people establish a shared understanding of terms and happenings, define new activities, etc. Discursive action is simply aimed at reaching a rational agreement about what has been said or done before, or what should be done. It is therefore concerned with what is called 'articulation work' (Hewitt, 1986): the resolution of inconsistent beliefs and evidence by some sort of 'due process'.

The social action perspective has several important implications for OIS research. It can be used to analyse the organizational consequences of OIS if they are designed for only one particular action type (cf. Klein 1986), or to assess the effectiveness of OIS design methodologies in dealing with various design problems (cf. Lyytinen, 1986). The typology to study the notion of OIS design effectiveness, is discussed in a later section.

## Contexts

One might refer to that which is changed through design as the domain of design. For example, one can think of certain parts of nature as being the domain of design in engineering projects (e.g. rivers for bridges, the earth's surface for buildings, etc.). In OIS design, there appear to be three domains, technology, language and organization structures (Etzioni, 1968; Lyytinen, 1986). These three domains are very complex and need to be perceived in a variety of ways in OIS design. That which facilitates the perception and documentation of a single domain in OIS design we term a context. A context acts as a generic cognitive filter or frame of reference through which all designs related to a single domain can be grouped together $^{2}$ . The general content of each context is described as follows.

$^{2}$ Office information systems design is not concerned with changing a part of nature (such as civil engineering is when building bridges, highways, dams, etc.), but culture. The nature–culture distinction is therefore a suitable starting point to differentiate OIS design from engineering systems design: the latter impinges on nature, the former on culture. See Klein (1986) for further details.

1 Technology context: the physical means and the technical know how by which office tasks are carried out, i.e. office technologies, storage, processing and transfer of codes (Bakopoulos, 1985). In the technology context, the design of office systems can be looked upon as the introduction of better storage, processing and data transfer means.

2 Language context: the nature, form, content and use of messages recorded and exchanged in the office, i.e. its professional languages and other sign systems in which the messages are represented. Language elements are any symbolic forms of representation including conversations and conventional data. In the language context, OIS designs considers possible changes in form, structure, content and use of linguistic media. Typically this includes the replacement of free human conversation by technically more efficient means.

3 Organization context: the nature and content of organizational roles through which office activities are carried out, i.e. the office organization. In this context, office systems design changes social behaviour and work arrangements. An important focus is on the allocation of tasks to formal office roles and the allocation of roles to positions. Another concern is the allocation of legitimate power (authority) and how an OIS change affects social influences among office actors.

It is apparent that all three of these contexts are necessary to achieve any meaningful office activity. Without technology, offices could not fulfil their functions of storing and exchanging messages. Without sign systems, it would not be possible to create messages which form the central locus of all office activity (Klein, 1986). Without organizational roles, offices would lose their organized nature in supporting the various organizational objectives/functions.

But how are the contexts and action types related? It is our belief that these concepts are two interrelated dimensions of office activity. Neither the action types nor the contexts can be treated in isolation. Most of the four action types cannot be fixed beforehand to any specific OIS design domain. For example, language is not harnessed just to carry out communicative or discursive actions. Language can serve as a domain in instrumental, strategic, communicative and discursive action, as will be shown later (cf. Lyytinen, 1986). Therefore, in the study of any office activity we must distinguish in more detail the essential media and resources employed in that activity, how they are changed in OIS design, and how they interfere with various action types carried out in an office. Taken together this will provide a framework for office design elements, where each element has its own rationality, change strategy and effectiveness measure (see Taxonomy of effectiveness measures and object system classes, later).

Office information systems design always affects one or more of the three contexts. The contexts covered in the OIS design form the scope of the OIS design. This scope is mostly determined by the inherent 'theories-in-use' (Argyris & Schon, 1978) that guide developer's perception and analysis of the OIS design problem. The purpose and rationality of the OIS design, on the other hand, is dictated by what action types are assumed to exist in a given office activity. The assumed action types form another essential part of the 'theories-in-use' about office activity (such as why people behave in a specific way). Therefore, it is not surprising that the 'preselected' (by theories-in-use) rationalities of OIS design radically affect how the contexts are changed within the scope of OIS design.

In the following, we explore the relationship between OIS design strategy, contexts and action types. More specifically, we note that each context and action type combination defines a set of criteria for OIS design strategy selection, and that for each of these a specific effectiveness measure exists.

## TAXONOMY OF EFFECTIVENESS MEASURES AND OBJECT SYSTEM CLASSES

Effectiveness is concerned with the evaluation of the effects of an action. Actions are effective if they produce effects that realize human purposes or interests. A theory is effective if it helps to meet human interests and so forth. The social action typology suggests that the interests and purpose of a particular human action depends on the social action type. Therefore, a single effectiveness measure for OIS design cannot suffice. Whereas the idea of multiple effectiveness measures is by no means new, the social action approach leads one to consider a new set of effectiveness measures. The strength of this set is that it not only includes all prior measures (primarily under the instrument action type), but extends them significantly (Table 3). All effectiveness measures are related to the purposes of specific action types. The purpose of instrumental and strategic action is to achieve measurable objectives; the purpose of communicative action is to achieve mutual understanding; and the purpose of discursive action is to test various validity claims; to clarify the intended meaning of a message, to check its sincerity, to test its truthfulness, to check its correctness, etc.

## Taxonomy of object system classes

We propose that the effectiveness of OIS is a function of both the social action type to be supported and the context of design. Taken together, they form an object system, which is defined as the particular role complex to which the development of an OIS is directed. The object system is that part of the office which is selected as the target of study. The selection of an object system is an abstraction process which embodies both a context (or frame of reference) and a social action type. It is therefore possible to map contexts with the social action typology to yield what we term a taxonomy of object system classes (Table 3). Each class has particular effectiveness measures associated with it. Table 4 applies the taxonomy to a cross-section of the existing OIS literature.

It can be noted that conventional approaches to OIS development are clearly visible in the taxonomy—they are object systems (1–3) in Table 3. As technology by definition excludes human components, the only social action type that can be directed at it is instrumental action (the other three action types only apply to the social domain). Therefore, instrumental effectiveness is the same as the widely applied technical effectiveness measures. Applying the instrumental action type to the language and organization contexts results in formal studies of data and data transmission (technical communication models) in the case of the former, and a machine model of organizational behaviour in the case of the latter. Research in this area contributes to the analysis of offices as 'quasi-technical systems'.

## Effectiveness measures

The following section concentrates on the definition of effectiveness measures which are much more comprehensive than those of current approaches to requirements specification. This by itself is not sufficient to deal with all concerns of OIS requirements analysis, but is nevertheless a necessary first step to understanding system specification. Some of the effectiveness measures are observable in existing OIS methodologies, while others point to the shortcomings of current approaches. The proposed nine effectiveness measures for the taxonomy of object system classes are as follows.

1 The effectiveness of instrumental action in the technology context has received much attention in the literature. From this perspective, OIS development is effective if new technology changes such attributes as speed, accuracy, and reliability, or if the functionality and power of systems can be improved. For example, graphics systems are more effective in this sense, because they can handle both alpha-numeric text and iconic signs; relational database machines are more effective than regular computers because they can execute relational data manipulation commands much faster, and so forth. A third concern is flexibility and compatibility. A system which allows easy switching between ETHERNET-type local area networks to token-ring LANs is more effective than one that does not.

2 In order to improve the effectiveness of instrumental action in the language context, language has to be considered merely as a means to measurable ends in a world that abstracts from human and social qualities of language use. There are several concerns associated with the instrumental effectiveness of language design. The first is format. The effectiveness of different forms of expression (tables, figures, text, colour and highlighting, fonts, and types) are evaluated from the perspective of how they improve measurable task performance. This is similar to (1) except that now design is abstracted from the physical level to the logical-conceptual level. The second concern relates to message throughput and efficient coding structures. They are considered through mathematical methods such as queuing theory and entropy models. A third concern centres around the effectiveness of prediction and control, necessitating an abstraction from human qualities as free will. In order to understand this concern the denotational and behaviouristic theory of meaning has to be considered. Here, signs stand for objects in the world without any recognition of their possible uses (Chen, 1977). This type of 'meaning theory' ignores or even suppresses ambiguous connotations of 'unwanted' meanings which reflect differing interests in order to achieve desired behaviours (cf. Lyytinen, 1985). A good example of instrumental language design is 'NEWSPEAK' in Orwell's

Nineteen-Eightyfour or data dictionary controls in a centralized database if it is used to eliminate all ambiguities and multiple interpretations.

3 Considering how OIS can improve the effectiveness of instrumental action in the organization context leads us to a subset of 'organizational effectiveness' measures, namely those that relate to the rationalization of formal office roles and tasks. The primary focus is on the prespecifiable, formal relationships in organizations; span of control, different forms of organization, work flow, work unit configuration, variance analysis in socio-technical systems-oriented design methodologies, decomposition of tasks to minimize interdependencies, elimination of functions, and so forth. Again it should be noted that the instrumental action view abstracts from the truly human qualities of life in organizations to one where organizations are viewed as machine-like structures (Hirschheim, 1986).

4 Effectiveness of strategic action in the language context focuses on the effects which OIS development produces for changes in control and power. Networks can wipe out the centrality of a human role or increase the power of one group at the expense of another by giving access to data that were formerly 'owned' exclusively. Therefore the language and communication pattern changes which follow in the wake of OIS development activities can become strategic weapons in the struggle among different groups within the organization. The language that best induces desired behaviours and states of minds is the most effective. Information systems become means of persuasion, such as in advertising, propaganda, fund raising or political letter campaigns. The classical reference discipline is rhetorics: how to phrase convincing arguments effectively which can make the weaker cause win. Work reported in this category is primarily speculative. For example, Mathiassen & Bogh-Andersen (1985) use the ploy of a hypothetical story to focus on the possibility of using information systems 'as a lie' in order to further one's interests.

5 From a strategic action perspective, OIS are effective in the organization context if they enhance the realization of interests of a particular group. Descriptions focus on the politics of the 'moves' of organizational actors and how the structural changes associated with different system design options change the power base and resources. Important case studies have been documented in Markus (1983), Kling (1980), Hirschheim (1986), and Newman & Rosenberg (1985).

6 Effectiveness of communicative action in the language context is concerned with the question of how OIS can facilitate agreement and better mutual understanding by changing the 'symbolic universe' of the organization. An example is the introduction of new concepts, distinctions, and meanings. From the many reported complaints of users that information systems outputs are ambiguous, incomplete, erroneous and generally poor on intelligibility, it is obvious that changes in the symbolic universe due to OIS do not necessarily result in improvements in this area. Unfortunately, little work has been done to identify how the design of OIS can facilitate shared understanding (however, see Boland, 1979, 1985).

7 Effectiveness of communicative action in the organization context is concerned with changing structures and processes of interaction to facilitate consensus formation about unintelligible or ambiguous meanings. The object system is seen as consisting of amorphous and overlapping roles with interactions continuously emerging and evolving. The purpose of OIS design is to facilitate consensus formation by creating formal and informal opportunities for the exchange of opinions and observations (cf. Klein & Hirschheim, 1985).

8 Effectiveness of discursive action in the language context is concerned with improving the logical-conceptual means by which an inquiring system (cf. Churchman, 1972) can check its own warrant or 'guarantor' and the validity of its results. The warrant consists of the justifications for the claim that the system produces results which can be trusted. The discussion of these warrants or 'guarantors' transposed communication on a meta-level as prior communication becomes the object of critical discourse. Ideally one would have the designer of an inquiring system offer a guarantee that the results produced are valid as claimed at least with a certain high probability. As a guarantor of this type does not exist, discursive action deals with challenges to validity as they arise in interaction. Inquiring systems in this sense can include people and even animals as information processors and knowledge stores (cf. Churchman, 1972). Validity claims not only concern the correspondence between what is said (or 'output') and the actual state of affairs in the sense of the correspondence theory of truth, but also other validity claims. These are sincerity (what is said is claimed to correspond to what is internally meant by the actor), intelligibility (correspondence between what the sender meant to communicate with what was understood by the receiver), correctness (effectiveness of proposed means to achieve agreed-upon ends), appropriateness (social acceptability of engaging in a particular type of communication), and legitimacy (informed social acceptance and consistency of norms and values to which a speaker appeals). OIS design in this area can contribute to improved effectiveness by clarifying background assumptions (cf. Flores & Ludlow, 1981), the reconstruction of formal message systems which are able to capture the intents and meanings of professional languages better as they evolve (cf. Goldkuhl & Lyytinen, 1982, 1984), or providing support for rational debate (cf. Stodolsky, 1985). This area has largely been left unexplored.

9 Effectiveness of discursive action in the organization context is concerned with making structural changes to improve the validity of knowledge. From this perspective, object systems are seen as courts of rational debate which elicit understanding and truth among office actors in a similar fashion as a court of justice supposedly reveals the truth to the jury. Various structural means to achieve this are: to eliminate the distorting effects of hierarchy, to duplicate channels for cross-checking data, to introduce checks and balances against subconscious bias and self-deception, to reduce defensiveness and other psychological barriers to free inquiry, to provide for the widest possible debate of all important issues, etc. (cf. Wilensky, 1967; Etzioni, 1968). Additionally, ethical and moral issues associated with bureaucracy and policy formulation are also considered (cf. Kling & Iacono, 1984; Hirschheim, 1986).

In summary, this section has suggested that nine different categories of effectiveness should be considered when establishing requirements for OIS. Yet, most of the OIS literature is concerned with the effectiveness requirements of instrumental action only. The effectiveness requirements of strategic, communicative and discursive action are hardly considered. Whereas Table 4 gives some representative research on the nine types of object systems, the amount of literature cited is by no means proportional to the amount of work done in each category. This point becomes more obvious if the reference disciplines which can best contribute to improving our understanding of the nine object system classes, and their effectiveness measures, are considered. ## DISCUSSION

The nine effectiveness measures provide a vehicle for understanding the ultimate human purposes of what office workers do and what is changed by introducing office information systems. They make it apparent that OIS change not only the office as a human activity system, but also the office as a social community. More specifically, measures 1–5 of Table 3 relate to human activity system aspects, whereas measures 6 and 7 relate to social community aspects. This implicitly links instrumental and strategic action with the human activity system part of the office, and communicative action with the social community view. Discursive action is related to both in that it serves to redeem the validity claims arising in the other action types. For example, discourse may be needed to substantiate the beliefs on which either strategic or instrumental action is based.

Discursive action may also be needed in communicative action in order to maintain a common background of assumptions and values without which communication is impossible. For example, whenever office workers interact, communication can break down; discursive action is needed to mend the deficiencies such as unintelligibility, challenges to factual correctness, sincerity, and social appropriateness. The coping with such deficiencies, semantic ambiguities, differing interpretations, and the like, is an inescapable part of office conversation and has been clearly documented by Wynn (1979). Hence the linkage of discursive action to communicative action.

Another important issue is how the various action types can be mapped to a real life sequence of human interactions. There are two sub-issues involved here: (1) the identification of action types in 'real' human interaction; and (2) the identification of how the various action types relate to one another.

The identification of action types requires a finer level of resolution than is provided by the concept of the four action types themselves. It is necessary to introduce the notion of a speech act which links speech (communication) to an action type. Speech acts relate to speech sequences which are 'linguistic performances' (Searle, 1979). A speech act is defined as a meaningful utterance which is 'performed' to achieve some human purpose or intent, taking into account the reactions of people under a set of given social practices and circumstances. The analysis of speech acts differs from the analysis of sentences in that it includes: (1) the study of different communicative intentions; (2) the study of the various psychological and behavioural reactions characteristically evoked in a receiver during communication; and (3) the study of social consequences of acts of communication (Allwood, 1977). The studying of the office in terms of speech acts was undertaken by Flores & Ludlow (1981) who suggested that the analysis of speech acts would reveal the complexity of interactions in offices. Additional research in the application of speech acts to information systems has been performed by Goldkuhl & Lyytinen (1984) who provided some insight into how the rules underlying social communication practices could be reconstructed, and Lehtinen & Lyytinen (1986) who provide a method of modelling and documenting communicative action sequences which take place in formalized OIS.

The identification of how the action types relate to one another in a complex sequence of interactions is difficult because the order in which they occur in time is not necessarily indicative of how they have affected each other (cf. Ngwenyama & Klein, 1986). In real life, speech acts which are not consecutive in time may nevertheless contribute to the performance of the same social action. For example, several speech acts will typically constitute one instrumental action which in turn is pursued in order to change the opportunities for, say, strategic action. It is therefore apparent that the performance of one action type can affect the performance of others which may occur later in time. An example of this is provided by Markus (1983) in her FIS (financial information system) case. FIS was developed to summarize financial data automatically into reports for corporate accountants and for particular divisions. This is an example of instrumental action which changed the opportunities for strategic actions between divisional accountants and the corporate accounting group. FIS reduced the opportunity for divisional accountants to present financial data in a way which specifically served the interests of their own division. A similar argument can apply to the introduction of networks and centralized file servers in the office domain. The social action perspective helps one to anticipate the potentially dysfunctional side-effects of one action type on the others, as in Markus' case which shows the side-effects of instrumental action (the development of FIS for speedier report creation) on the conditions of strategic action (for the particular division accountants to maintain their independence from the corporate accounting group). This example shows that instrumental action can effect a change which restructures the whole organizational 'ecology' in which work and communication takes place. Thus, the investigation of how different action types interrelate in a specific case can shed more light on how OIS affect the interaction between the human activity and social community aspects of offices.

Ultimately, the goal of using the social action framework is to provide a vehicle for better understanding the nature of human actions in the office—their web of interconnections through which the mission of the office is achieved. This entails the need to account completely for all types of action which can be observed in the real life office; that we can somehow identify an action in a complex sequence of interlinked human behaviours and then classify it to one predominant type. One prerequisite, however, is a classification which is mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive in terms of all human action types. The taxonomy presented here makes the claim that its categories are mutually exclusive, in the sense that it allows the allocation of each action to one dominant type. It is, however, not collectively exhaustive because it is a simplification of the Habermas (1982, 1984) categorization which in turn has evolved over time. As in any categorization, it is very difficult to find a guarantor of its exhaustiveness.

## CONCLUSION

The taxonomy as presented here is sufficient to point to the possible benefits which can be derived from grounding the development of OIS on an explicit categorization of all social action. First, as hopefully has been shown, it leads to a richer and better understanding of the dual nature of offices. Secondly, it can be applied to OIS development for the identification of the action types observable in the system development process itself. Most of the existing OIS methodologies assume that system development is a sequence of instrumental actions to achieve predefined ends. Yet the ends are not given, which points to the need to include communicative and strategic action in the construction of methodologies. If ends are problematic, conflict is inevitable which must be resolved by either communicative action or 'fought out' by strategic action. Current OIS methodologies assume 'an organizational consensus model' which most organizational theorists now believe to be too superficial (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). Furthermore, system development depends on valid knowledge about the domain of intervention (the object system). This suggests the need also to include discursive action. Important articulation work is carried out within system development which is not systematically supported in current methodologies. We do note, however, that the concern for integrity and completeness checks (e.g. Tabourier & Nanci, 1981; Teichroew et al., 1981) is a step in this direction. Automated tools which can perform certain logical checks and make the implications of systems specification more intelligible, can support a discourse between all stakeholders to test the claim of any system specification that it is 'valid'. Only those system descriptions which survive 'maximal criticism' of all concerned are 'warranted'.

It seems to us, therefore, that a complete and well corroborated framework of social action could provide a sounder underpinning for the next generation of OIS methodologies. As office work is much more than merely instrumental action, so too is the development of OIS (see the analysis of an actual case reported in Ngwenyama & Klein (1986)). Current methodologies with their focus on instrumental action provide an insufficient base for OIS development as they ignore the other social action types. We contend that the concepts proposed here provide a first step towards correcting this error of omission.
