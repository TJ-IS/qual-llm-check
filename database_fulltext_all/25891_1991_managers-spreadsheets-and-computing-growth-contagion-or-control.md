# Managers, spreadsheets and computing growth: contagion or control?

D Mason and L Willcocks

Information Systems Group, Victoria University of Wellington, New Zealand and City University Business School, UK

Abstract. It is widely accepted that managers do not make the most of the opportunities offered by information technology (IT). This study set out to investigate why by establishing what computing facilities managers actually use, and how they use them. The original hypothesis was that adoption of technology was limited by poor training and lack of interest in IT. We set out to establish empirical findings by interviewing managers face to face at their place of work. An exploratory investigation of this nature must adopt a non-directive approach which is not amenable to questionnaire-type survey techniques, and which produces 'soft' rather than quantitative data. As the 'soft' nature of the data ruled out strict statistical methods we used a 'search and refine' technique, to build a structure from all the information we received during the interviews until a clear body of results emerged and could be clarified and confirmed. The research broadly supports the lack of training hypothesis but finds that far from being uninterested, managers are very interested in using IT, but only where it is directly relevant to their own work. The study suggests that training is not enough and must be supplemented by control. The findings support but also refute aspects of Nolan's stage theory of computing growth. Further research to confirm these findings would be useful, as would comparative studies in organizations where controls on spreadsheets are in place.

Keywords: Computers, managerial work, organizational development, spreadsheets, stage theories.

## INTRODUCTION

It is widely accepted that organizations do not benefit fully from the opportunities offered by IT. A range of reports, [Willcocks & Mason, (1987); Brown et al. (1988); Martin, (1988); Rajan, (1988); Gibson & Ball, (1989); PA Consulting Group, (1989)], has suggested that managers are wary of IT, are undertrained in its use, and frequently fail to build on its potential for the delivery of managerial work.

Furthermore there are a priori reasons, and such reports throw light on some circumstantial evidence for there being a correlation between how well managers use IT for their personal decision support, and the effectiveness of IT acquisition within the organization as a whole. It therefore becomes important to discover how managers currently use information systems as personal tools, and whether and how such usage can be improved. The focus of this paper will be mainly on these two questions, but, as will emerge, the findings and recommendations flow into, and have considerable implications for, more effective use of IT throughout the organization.

## RESEARCH BASE

This paper's results are the interim outcome of a larger empirical investigation into how managers employ IT in personal support systems. The sample organizations were drawn from financial services, Government departments, statutory agencies, headquarters of primary producers, management and professional consultancies. This provided a useful cross-section of public and private, office-based, large, small and medium-sized enterprises. All the organizations are located in Wellington, New Zealand. The sample is opportunistic, largely reflects personal introductions and makes no claim to be a rigorous selection.

The basis of this research was to ignore preconceptions of how managers should use IT and to record simply what working managers actually did. From there we would try to establish general principles and test our starting hypotheses. We initially set up meetings with managers personally known to us and asked them to demonstrate how they used IT. We recorded the results of these meetings and looked for common threads in the working patterns of very diverse management tasks. From these connections we established tentative hypotheses and looked for evidence to support or refute them in the course of the following interviews. As the body of data from the interviews built up we quite rapidly established that the core technology was in fact spreadsheets on personal computers, and that a number of problems emerged from their use. We decided to leave the larger objectives of our main research until later and follow up the use of spreadsheets by managers as a separate study. This paper is the result of that study.

From the interviews it rapidly emerged that managerial use of software was common rather than atypical throughout these organizations. This was unrelated to the size of organization or the budget available for IT. In every case managers had access to spreadsheet packages. All had access to word processing, less than 10% had access to database software. In the larger organizations all managers had mechanisms available to retrieve data from the central data processing section, but reported that access was ‘unsatisfactory’ or ‘difficult’.

It became clear that what we needed to focus our attention on was not whether, but how, these managers used software in their daily work and how this related to their personal and organizational effectiveness. We discovered that spreadsheet packages were by far the most common software in use amongst the managers in the sample, and decided to limit the study to a detailed analysis of how this software was employed and how its use could be improved.

A further consideration presented itself. If one can posit stages in the evolution of computing within an organization, as suggested (Gibson & Nolan, (1974); Nolan, (1979); Kraemer & King, (1981); Drury, (1983)), then, whatever those stages may be, it becomes clear that the sample organizations are at a further stage than at first assumed. This offered us the further opportunity to apply and test the usefulness of aspects of the dominant stage model of computing growth as put forward by Nolan (1979), and criticized by Benbasat et al. (1984) and King & Kraemer (1984).

## RESEARCH FINDINGS

The managers in the sample all used spreadsheet packages. A range of reasons emerged for such widespread use. Firstly, as first-time users, all the respondents were impressed at the speed at which spreadsheets could be incorporated into their normal work patterns and produce tangible benefits. The major factors cited here related to the much abused concept of user-friendliness, namely: ease of application; little instruction is needed to start; spreadsheets proved eminently flexible; natural approach (the spreadsheet is laid out in rows and columns which coincides with the way managers have been trained to tackle business problems); no programming (spreadsheet packages are so well designed that even using the advanced facilities does not resemble programming).

Secondly, as end users, the respondents relished their freedom from the data-processing department, while at the same time reporting that their in-house DP professionals welcomed spreadsheets as a product which lets users tackle their own problems, and freed them from endless small programming requests. Further reasons emerged for the popularity of spreadsheet packages. For 90% of respondents the spreadsheet was their only decision-support tool. Sometimes this was because spreadsheets had formed the manager's introduction to computing (Davis & Olson, 1985). Often a knowledge of spreadsheets represented the only formal training ever received in computing techniques. In addition, 75% of respondents had received only informal training and were virtually self taught. For this type of manager, using a spreadsheet was a quick and easy way to gain benefits from available software. The use of a spreadsheet became part of their everyday work pattern and was carried out without much further thought.

However, the research threw light upon some important dysfunctional aspects of this widespread adoption and use of spreadsheet software for managerial work. The very strengths and attractiveness of these software packages led respondents to look no further for a tool to solve their computing needs. During our research it became apparent that spreadsheets had taken a firm hold in corporate computing, to such a degree that users actively resisted any suggestion that they should change. Five reasons emerged for such resistance.

1 'Why should I?'. Over half the users questioned would not consider looking beyond the spreadsheet. The spreadsheet seemed flexible enough to deal with almost any problem coming their way. The respondents had little interest in computing technology itself and wanted to spend as little time as possible learning new software packages.

2 Time invested. After spending the initial time learning the commands and concepts, users looked to obtain as much value as possible from the time invested.

3 No alternatives. Spreadsheet users were not DP professionals and had no obvious reason or occasion to examine alternative software packages. Although in all the sampled organizations the information centre or data processing departments regularly carried out software evaluations, they did so only for their own purposes. They only advised users if specifically requested.

4 'Leave well alone'. About 95% of spreadsheet applications were very simple models, designed by the user for the user. The design generally involved just a few rows and columns. The respondents were comfortable with this layout. The attitude could be summarized as: 'If it ain't broke, don't fix it'.

5 'Too busy'. In fact, many of the respondents, on being questioned, did recognize that their spreadsheet had reached the critical point where it should be replaced. But even where users were aware of shortcomings they were often just too busy to take appropriate action. Business pressures meant that few users were willing (or had the time) to scrap their over-stretched spreadsheet and start again.

## SPREADSHEETS: EMERGING PROBLEMS

These arguments for continuing with the current spreadsheets arose because users were not fully aware of the potential problems. Problems generally arise when spreadsheets grow too large. The pattern we encountered was for a user to build a small model and use it for a while. Then the user is tempted to tag bits on here and there. The flexibility of a spreadsheet makes this easy to do, at least in the beginning. As the solution being modelled became more complex, however, changes and enhancements became increasingly difficult to implement. Finally, what was formerly a simple management aid tends to evolve into something altogether less manageable. This pattern seems to derive from the user-friendly, forgiving spreadsheet interface that insulates users from the realization that they are actually using a powerful programming tool. Inexperienced users are not only unaware of the penalties of breaking design rules, but often do not realize that there are rules. In practice, and in line with the findings of Leigh & Doherty (1986), the research revealed an amazing variety of spreadsheets. They were used as a records manager, as a word processor, to budget and forecast, as a desk calculator, as a management account tool, as an account entry package, as a programming language, and even advocated as an expert system vehicle. However, the real problems arose where the spreadsheet was turned into a database. Every model has to hold some data, and to that extent a spreadsheet must incorporate a database function (Ariav & Ginsberg, 1985; Date, 1986). However, many respondents tended to expand their spreadsheets indefinitely. This research project uncovered PC spreadsheets with 50 or more columns and thousands of rows. By any definition that is a database. This uncontrolled growth caused serious problems, which for the purposes of analysis can be classified into six types.

## 1 Data redundancy

One fundamental problem with spreadsheets is that they are limited to a single table. Each piece of data to use in the spreadsheet is held in one data structure. Effectively this means that all processing is limited to a single file. This is exactly the situation which prevailed before the introduction of disk storage. Thus spreadsheets are actually a step backwards. The problem is that business applications involve multiple files (Howe, 1983). Combining several logically distinct files into one inevitably brings update, deletion and amendment difficulties (Vasta, 1989). As an example, one respondent wanted, as part of a sales application, to hold details of all the orders received, using the spreadsheet as a database. This might be structured in columns as Sale Number, Customer details, Products Ordered, Supplier Details. Printing out details of any particular order would pose no problem. Finding which sales related to a given customer would be a simple query. Adding a potential new customer meant, however, creating a dummy order. Changing any of the customer details meant a search of the whole spreadsheet because the respondent did not know which sales were for a given customer. Eventually the spreadsheet grows over large and some entries have to be deleted. Where a deleted order is the only one from a particular customer then all the details of that customer disappeared. Similarly, where that deleted order contains the last remaining reference to a product then all knowledge of that product is also lost. Where one customer has many orders then the customer details are stored many times, or blank cells allocated and storage space wasted. Relational data theory has been developed to eliminate precisely this type of problem and the solution to data redundancy, namely data normalization, lies at the heart of today's relational databases (Martin, 1984).

## 2 Slow access

Overstretching a spreadsheet emerged as bad practice because the model becomes cumbersome to use. With large models, moving from left to right and back again, repeatedly, was slow and clumsy. Moving vertically from top to bottom to see a result, and then moving back up to change the critical variable, often meant waiting through multiple disk accesses. Respondents also reported a loss of intuitive 'feel' for the model. Spreadsheets support 'What if?' investigations by showing the effect of changing a single variable directly and dynamically. This encourages managers to explore relationships and to investigate more complex associations (Thierauf, 1982, 1988). The advantage is clearest where all the elements affected can be seen on the screen simultaneously. Direct interactive feedback makes spreadsheets an invaluable learning tool for decision makers. However where a model is wider than the screen only a few variables and the outcome can be viewed simultaneously so this advantage is greatly diminished.

## 3 Inconsistency

Respondents readily recognized the clumsiness of overlarge spreadsheets and often followed the common response noted by Sprague & Carlson (1982), that is of finding some way to split it into submodels, perhaps dividing it by sales divisions, or storing current and previous year data separately. This, however, shifts rather than eliminates the problem. Updating multiple models with the same data item is tedious and error prone, and there is no simple foolproof way to ensure that all the submodels reflect the current position at all times.

## 4 Uncertainty

Increased size usually brings increased complexity. It is easy to build a large model. But in a large model with many variables there is the constant danger than the syntax is correct but that the logic is not. There is, in principle, no reason why a model of the entire firm cannot be built into a spreadsheet, or of an entire nation's economy for that matter. However, whereas one would have reservations about accepting the economy model's predictions for inflation or unemployment, there are examples from our sample where respondents operate on the basis of data from a spreadsheet incorporating the same degree of underlying uncertainty, without recognizing the dangers of hidden assumptions or untested relationships. There is unfortunately no simple reliable way to ensure the integrity and accuracy of a model. The more complex the model the harder it is to test. Professional modellers recognize this but less skilled managers are more likely to accept the model as long as the results support their expectations.

## 5 Vulnerability: security problems

Few spreadsheets investigated in the sample offered more than rudimentary security. Data integrity was usually poorly served and a cell's contents could often easily be accidentally altered. To combat this some spreadsheets offered data typing and would not, for example, allow a decimal point in an invoice number cell if that was declared as integer only. Some allowed columns or cells to be locked so they could not be updated. None offered extensive validation checks, however, or could verify the existence of an entered value by checking data held in another file. Some had password protection, but none restricted the access rights of authorized users. Neither did any of the spreadsheet packages examined restrict the view of a legitimate user to a partial view of some columns of the model, but not others. The emerging problem here is the one identified by Young (1984), namely that the unco-ordinated use of spreadsheets may increase accessibility of information for unauthorized users.

## 6 Data interchange problems

An overlarge spreadsheet brings problems when it serves only a single user. In our sample the situation became even worse where data had multiple users. The sheer size of some spreadsheets made sharing open information awkward. Using the actual terminal of another manager is usually an impractical way of sharing data, so file copying is inevitable. But if files are to be copied, preserving the security of the model then becomes a problem. In applications where a manager wanted to share some columns but not others, the only solution was to copy out the permitted columns and issue copies of those sub-sets only. But once again there is the problem of ensuring that updates to the master sheet are reflected in all the subsidiary sheets. This meant extra work for the 'data owner'. A clear finding of this research is that users of 'personal' databases are very lax when it comes to supplying data to downstream users. Such work has low priority in their eyes, and with no particular incentive to supply updates on time the common result is time-shifted data values in use throughout the organization.

The situation was worse where several users were trying to use the same data in common. As an example, one respondent reported occasions where a second user wished to update some of the cells and transfer them back to the original person, but leave other cells untouched. In the meantime the respondent had updated other cells on his own machine. The result could not be described as satisfactory.

## CONTROL: TOWARDS DATA MODEL ADMINISTRATION

The problems identified by this research stem basically from attempts to make a personal productivity tool into an all purpose programming environment. Remedial action would include, firstly, ensuring that the right tool is used, and secondly, imposing company-wide controls on the creation and use of spreadsheets. Thus it is clear from the research that only in cases where relatively large spending on their personal computing were made by managers, and/or during periods of financial exigency for the organization, can existing patterns and practices be questioned. Other factors keep these patterns and practices in place. In fact all our respondents used spreadsheets on personal microcomputers. The end users were managers and executives, not DP professionals. The managers tended to adopt a personal and limited view of information system requirements, and seldom considered the wider aspects. Those who did recognize the implications often preferred to ignore them. Individual justifications for this ranged from apathy, through 'not my responsibility', to ideas concerning the need for managerial freedom. Central IT departments allowed spreadsheets to proceed uncontrolled and uncoordinated, often regarding the phenomenon as just 'users playing around'—thus ignoring the potential problems and costs to the organization.

What has emerged from the research is the need for these organizations to formulate forms of effective control over their managers use of software. The rest of this section will discuss the directions that organizations that find themselves in this position can usefully take, in particular detailing the solution represented by the introduction of data model administration.

Firstly an organization needs to have available for its members some guidelines on spreadsheet usage. These guidelines need to be widely published and their observation, or otherwise, monitored by a person responsible for this activity. It is difficult to lay down hard and fast rules about when to use a spreadsheet and when not. However, drawing upon the findings and experience of this research project the following questions would seem to assist this process:

1 Do you have more than five models which are closely linked, or which share the same data, or have to be updated simultaneously?

2 Does the spreadsheet hold large volumes of data? Are you really running a database in disguise?

3 How many dimensions are involved in the problem? If you have a large and complex application consider using specialist modelling software.

4 Does the application involve several distinct entities? Does the model reflect the true entity relationships or does it model only one aspect of a multifaceted situation?

5 Is the data regularly needed by more than one user? Many users implies the need for many different access paths to the data not just the single view used by the original builder. Do you need to see the same data in many different report formats?

6 Are the systems purely for personal decision support or are there third parties involved? Do your results or reports go to other departments? Maybe they could use the raw data themselves. Do others have to re-key the figures from your reports?

7 Who is the end user? Would it be better just to supply summary information and omit details? Is there a need for 'Black boxing' the solution?

8 Does the data have to conform to legal requirements? Not just the data protection legislation, but also the various requirements of taxation authorities, licensing bodies and the organization's own auditors?

9 Does the application have security needs?

10 Do you require a larger screen?

In medium-sized and large organizations an uncoordinated increase in spreadsheet use can only lead to more problems, as experienced by those in our research sample. One approach to dealing with these difficulties is to create the equivalent of a database administrator to oversee the use of spreadsheets. The initial tasks of the Data Model Administrator (DMA) are listed below.

1 Registration. Record the current users and uses of spreadsheets. This ensures that corporate effort is not duplicated where different departments build essentially identical models.
2 Standardization. Establish a data dictionary-type environment for the whole organization. Create a register of data types, column names, etc. to avoid needless duplication or incompatibility across user models.

3 Definition. Centralize control of the formulae held in individual spreadsheet models in the same way that all data elements are incorporated into a data dictionary to create a central repository of unique and tightly defined data. For example, a formula for amortization, developed in one part of the organization, can be tested, named and made available to all users to ensure consistency and prevent wasted effort.

4 Validation. Any model with the potential to be used by many others should be 'walked through' with a domain expert. Thus, a manager who has developed a stock valuation model needs to talk it through with the accountant before it can be released for general use.

5 Storage and distribution. Most models are of the 'quick and dirty' variety (Cragg et al., 1989), thrown away after use and therefore constantly reinvented. Data Model Administration offers the option to make copies of all in-house models available for use as templates for new users or as shortcuts for ad hoc applications.

6 Report Generation. Central administration can overcome one of the poor features common to spreadsheets: their limited reporting formats. Users who develop large models or amass large databases will need to display their data in many different ways. Few spreadsheets have an adequate report formatter. If spreadsheet administration is done centrally then a good report designer can be purchased.

7 Coordination. Before a developer is allowed to start on a model it must be registered with the DMA to ensure that the data is not available on the system already.

8 Documentation. Every model must be adequately documented. Application programmers are not allowed to write programs without documenting them, there is no reason why modellers should be exempt.

9 Prioritization. The DMA is responsible for commissioning models, establishing priorities for modelling requests, coordinating with the overall organization data management strategy, steering users towards upwardly compatible software packages and distributing externally purchased data.

The research suggests that the proliferation of the use of spreadsheet software by managers needs to be counteracted by implementing forms of control to keep computing capacity and effort aligned with corporate objectives. While the approach outlined in this paper may not represent an ideal solution to the problems identified, it would seem, at least, to address the problems, and represent a feasible and practical development from the unsatisfactory situation pertaining. In this respect the publication of guidelines on spreadsheet usage, and the creation of a specialist data model administrator with tasks, as detailed, would seem to be an important step in the evolution of computing growth for organizations that find themselves at the same stage as the organizations in our sample.

## DISCUSSION

The research findings have some bearing on the well known and influential stage model of computing growth, as presented by Gibson & Nolan (1974) and modified in Nolan (1979). The 1974 paper suggested four growth stages of Initiation, Contagion, Control and Maturity. The 1979 paper took into account developments in computing, particularly in database management, and presented a revised six-stage model. The stages are now Initiation, Contagion, Control, Integration, Data Administration and Maturity. As both a descriptive and prescriptive model these stages have received considerable attention in the literature, and most of the criticisms need not be rehearsed here. However, a few important points emerge from our research findings.

In our 26 organizations studied, managerial use of software could usefully be seen as at the Contagion stage of computing growth, with the need to pass to the Control or a higher stage fairly quickly. However, our research supports McFarlan & McKenney (1983) in their study of 28 organizations, against Nolan (1979), because at least the first four stages of the 1979 model can exist simultaneously in any one organization. What the Nolan models could not embrace was the 1980s proliferation of relatively cheap microcomputer use throughout the modern office. From this point of view, even as a history of the evolution of computing, the 1979 Nolan model is in need of updating. Thus in our sample organizations, 'micro-proliferation', a phrase coined by Jackson (1986), represents a major control problem from the point of view of managing technological diffusion. To endorse further McFarlan & McKenney's findings, it is clear that the control problems encountered with micros had actually already been solved on the larger mainframes operated within these organizations, but that no transfer of information had taken place to confront the problem generated by micro-proliferation. Although the 26 organizations had considerable in-house experience and expertise, they had largely not built on their EDP technology experience when moving into microprocessing and office automation.

Gibson & Nolan (1974) and Nolan (1977) have been variously criticized for positing that a change in budgets would act as a surrogate for technological change within organizations and for suggesting that the amount spent on technology forms an S-shaped curve over the stages in their model. In fact, Nolan (1979) explicitly recognizes the failure of this latter prediction, and indeed in our research sample all organizations reported a continuous rise in computing costs, despite the stage of computing growth that they could be deemed to be in different parts of the organization.

While Benbasat et al. (1984), King & Kraemer (1984) and Jackson (1986) all play down the importance of computing budgets as a surrogate for technological developments, it is clear from our sample that increases in expenditure are a prime influence on how technological diffusion is managed in relation to office automation. The rationale seems to be that computing costs can be monitored fairly easily while the effectiveness of computing use is much more difficult to establish. Much less planning on technological diffusion is evident than many of the organizations like to believe. Managerial attention is focused on areas where expenditure has become large and subsequently its benefits are difficult to establish. Thus, technological diffusion is largely managed by cost considerations and often exhibits patterns similar to those established by Gibson & Nolan (1974) and Nolan (1979). A further point is that because of the relatively powerful organizational position that respondent managers occupy, their expenditure on the proliferation of microcomputers and software for personal use tends to increase beyond organizational control. Thus, as far as the managerial use of computers is concerned, the Contagion or proliferation stage is elongated, and largely due to the political circumstances that prevail. The rationale behind large expenditure by managers is that it assists their learning and adaptation with regard to IT. While there is substance to this claim, our evidence suggests that frequently our respondent managers had ceased to learn, and were mainly involved in attempts to maximize the returns from a limited understanding of, and skill in, IT.

From this point of view it may well be that Gibson & Nolan (1974) are right to see the proliferation phase in a somewhat negative light, leaving the impression that it involves misapplied effort and frequently irresponsible expenditure on computing. There is, however, an essential tension between these characteristics of the phase and their more positive description in the schema of McFarlan & McKenney (1983) as one of Experimentation and Learning. The problem is that if too much control is exerted too soon, organizational learning will not take place; in addition, important new uses of the technology may be killed off. The former development is particularly crucial as Willcocks & Mason (1987) and Eason (1988) point out, for the continued learning by the organization about IT, this, they point out, will assist in building an information-systems culture, that is, people able and willing to exploit IT. Without this it is difficult for IT to reach its full potential, however large the expenditure on hardware and software.

These considerations suggest that the concepts of 'control' and 'slack', imported by Nolan (1977 and 1979) into the six stage model of computing growth, are valuable. Here a control environment involves management systems, for example planning, chargeout, performance monitoring, to ensure the efficiency of computer use. The effect would be to increase efficiency but constrain innovation. A slack environment has top management willing to do without such controls and spend more on computing than is strictly needed, the outcome being to nurture innovation while allowing inefficiencies. Clearly the problem is to maintain a balance between these types of environment. From our evidence the predictions about the Contagion phase, as put forward by Nolan (1979), would seem to be fairly accurate with the managerial use of software, and indeed hardware, characterized by 'greater slack', but with a lack of planning resulting in poorly designed systems. The problem is then whether the subsequent move towards the Control phase represents too great a shift and achieves 'efficiency' at the expense of innovation and organizational learning. Certainly this is one of the dangers that needs to be borne in mind where organizations take on board our recommendations concerning data model administration.

From the results and discussion emerges the importance of information systems (IS) planning in organizations. From our evidence, and for our research sample, at least, Nolan (1979) would seem to be correct in the prediction of low levels of IS planning in the early stages of IT growth. From an IS management viewpoint, the usefulness of the Nolan stage model is to indicate one scenario if IS management is not strongly present at the first or any other subsequent stage in any part of an organization. However, if IS management is active, there would seem to be no reason why the stages elaborated by Nolan should be followed in strict sequence, why some stages could be omitted and higher stages entered more quickly, or why the transfer of knowledge about IS management could not take place between different parts of an organization. The late 1980s/early 1990s have seen corporate computing expenditure rise considerably to the point where appropriate and inappropriate IT expenditure and effort operate at levels and with results that can be termed 'strategic' in their consequences for the organization. In the organizations under consideration, it emerged that the Nolan phase of Contagion did precede and require, a later phase of Control, but, as Jackson (1986) suggests against Hirschheim (1983), an IS planning approach suggests the appropriateness of an initial Control stage. One could add that such a Control phase needs to have different characteristics from those advanced by Nolan. This includes the careful management of the requirement for characteristics of the 'control' and 'slack' environments to be simultaneously present in the form of a healthy equilibrium; the latter is assumed to exist only in the later Maturity stage of the Nolan model.

## CONCLUSION

The data held in spreadsheets are a major component of corporate information systems. The evidence from our study suggests that to achieve the appropriate emphasis at the right time between proliferation and control is a delicate and complex process that receives too little attention from the organizational managers of IS. The research supports many of the criticisms made against the evolutionary approach (Nolan et al. 1974, 1977, 1979), but also supports several of its assertions and findings. In particular, the sample organizations seem to have reached a stage in their use of information and IT that appears to require a move towards the data-administration approach detailed by Nolan (1979). As predicted by Nolan, centralized administration offers the sample organizations the ability to ensure that the data held in 'private' models is validated and has the potential to be integrated into the main organizational database. An alternative is to allow the situation to remain unchecked and permit users to build an underground, alternative data organization outside the formal information system. While this may induce managerial understanding of IT, our evidence is that this is limited and soon saturated, and that greater gains are possible by moving to a more integrated, formalized database management system.
