# Information management in service industries: towards a strategic framework

J. R. Beaumont and D. Walters\*

School of Management, University of Bath, England and \*Institute for Retail Studies, University of Stirling, Scotland

Abstract. Investments in information and communications technologies are large-scale, and often the real potential is not being realized. It is stressed that an information systems strategy should be aligned with the business strategy, and the management of the information resource is discussed by considering the components of a strategic framework. Aspects of implementation in the service sector are illustrated through a brief discussion of different variables which could be measured.

Keywords: Business strategy, information resource management, service sector.

## INTRODUCTION

Western economies in the twentieth century, particularly in recent decades, have exhibited enormous, absolute and relative growth in their service sectors. Moreover, as we progress into the knowledge-based society and economy of the next century, the increasing significance of information and communications technologies is being recognized by many commentators — affecting not only the ways we do business but also the nature of industry. The effective management of investments in information and communications technologies to realize the potential business benefits remains an elusive objective for many organizations. There is no relationship between the scale of the investment and its benefits; the payoffs are dependent on the quality of the management of the technologies, rather than the quantity of the investment. In general, there is insufficient up-front planning of investments; they are frequently no more than ad hoc responses to today's perceived problems. Indeed, a fundamental shortcoming is often the lack of direct linkage with the corporate business strategy. Any information strategy must be aligned with and driven by the corporate business strategy.

In this paper, a strategic framework is outlined for information management in service industries. The discussion is continued in the next section where consideration is given to strategic business opportunities, particularly in response to contemporary pressures of market potential and consumer change affecting service industries. Emphasis is given to the need both to identify and exploit business opportunities and to then be able to measure performance through supporting information systems. In the section entitled Towards a Strategic Framework for Information Management, the components of the information management framework are introduced and integrated. Illustrations of current practice are provided in the section entitled A Model for Developing a Strategic Management Information System along with brief descriptions of possible changes over time and of the factors that create such dynamics. To enable the implementation of the framework, in the section entitled Strategy Decisions and Information Strategy, a range of variables are presented to indicate the type of measurements required. Some concluding comments are given in the final section to indicate the important diagnostic characteristics of the framework.

## STRATEGIC BUSINESS OPPORTUNITIES FOR SERVICE INDUSTRIES

In the future, organizations will face more and better competition, and the successful organizations will be those that provide an added value, quality service to customers. Without getting embroiled in a discussion of strategic management (see, for example, Hussey (1990) for more details), corporate business strategy comprises different orientations such as:

(a) consolidation and productivity: to increase performance from the existing resource base and to provide margin, volume and asset utilization improvements (such as Woolworth's 'focus' groups);

(b) repositioning: to meet the changing needs of existing (and/or potential new) customer segments (such as Argos's 'superstores' and Dickens and Jones);

(c) growth: to expand into related merchandise areas, customer service support, trading formats, or possibly all of these (such as Halford's 'car servicing');

(d) diversification: to expand into new retail situations (merchandise, customer service, trading format, and communications). This could include vertical integration or coordination of supply chain activities (such as Woolworth acquiring a record distributor and WH Smith acquiring Our Price and Virgin stores);

(e) conglomerate activities: to develop a group of portfolio or subsidiary companies whose activities may be quite different from each other (such as Kingfisher) or may be based on the same market but with different market positioning (such as Ratners).

Following Walters & Knee (1989), Figure 1 is an idealized diagram of these options.

As the strategic direction shifts away from the mainstream, business risk increases. It follows that the business can reduce the risk involved by developing effective support strategy expertise. Information management (incorporating, for instance, the selection of performance indicators; the collection and dissemination of information; and the application of the information to decision-making) is one such element.

It then follows that any business developing a range of expertise with information management can benefit in two ways. First it has the advantage of being able to identify and analyse strategic options in considerable depth, possibly sooner than competitors. Secondly, information management will offer more precise control over the implementation of the selected strategy.

## TOWARDS A STRATEGIC FRAMEWORK FOR INFORMATION MANAGEMENT

Once specified, a corporate business strategy must drive the information strategy and then the specification of the IT platform or enabling infrastructure. The main reason for the early (and still relevant) investments in information and communications technologies was efficiency gains, especially in operational areas. More recently, however, more strategic investments have been made — often as an 'act of faith' that 'IT can be a source of competitive advantage' (see, for example, Beaumont & Sparks' (1990) commentary with reference to retailing). IT cannot be a source of sustainable competitive advantage, although it will become increasingly a competitive necessity for service industries.

The investments to improve productivity have been characterized by the term 'automate'. In a significant contribution to management thinking, Zuboff (1988) extends the traditional 'automate' perspective by proposing 'informate', with an explicit emphasis on the information content of administrative and productive processes in an organization.

Electronic Point of Sales (EPoS) systems, for instance, have been introduced by retails to automate their checkout and their stock control processes; this data could also be transformed into actionable information for branch-level market analysis. Similarly, systems for Direct Product Profitability could be extended usefully to determine customer profitability.

Wiseman (1985) refers to the 'hybrid' nature of many investments in large information systems. For instance, retailers' EPos systems contain a large 'automating' component through data processing (data capture, verification, collation, storage, analysis and distribution), as well as potentially 'informating' about purchase patterns and stock replenishment needs. Similarly, airline Computer Reservations Systems (CRSs) provided detailed competitor data allowing local market/route analyses (which have become vital in their deregulated market) and

Computer Aided Design (CAD) systems for branch layouts can also provide the base for a future asset management system.

While it can be argued that most organizations do not still make full use of their own databases, there has been an increased strategic use of customer databases (see, for instance, Rapp & Collins (1987) discussion of 'Maxi Marketing'). Credit-card operations have a powerful capability in this respect, offering the retailer the facility to 'track' and 'categorize' their customers and build communications activities around customer purchasing behaviour patterns.

This argument can be extended further, particularly to provide strategic 'teeth', to incorporate two additional capabilities:

## (a) 'empowerate'

## (b) transformate.

Traditional perspectives in organizations, hierarchies and bureaucracy, are becoming obsolete in the usage of networks. Given the advances in the speed, capacity and accuracy of information and communication technologies, many service organizations, such as banks and retailers, are using infrastructural 'empowerate' capabilities to permit local branch-level action while maintaining head-office control. The benefits of this 'enabling' infrastructure is only realizable if the organizational culture permits a sharing of information and decision-making and a focus on core competences. Technology in the form of 'teleconferencing' already permits this development.

Important issues that do cause concerns are organizational structures and cultures. Typically, in retailing companies, information technology has been used to reinforce centralized decision-making. This in turn has developed a culture within which 'head office' is seen as the focal point of decision-making, to the extent that for some companies any move towards decentralizing the decision-making delegation, would prove to be an uncomfortable organizational innovation.

Moreover, 'empowerate' should not be confined to an intra-organizational perspective or to inter-organizational networking. Electronic Data Interchange is of growing significance in supply-chain management. In the United Kingdom for example TRADANET has been available and used by the retail and distribution industry for many years; ODETTE is used in the motor car industry, etc.

The potential competitive benefits of such linkages should not be underestimated, especially for organizations which secure network control, such as American Airlines and United Airlines with their SABRE and APOLLO CRS. Moreover, collaboration can be a successful part of a competitive strategy to enhance customer service, as illustrated by shared Automatic Teller Machine (ATM) networks (such as Bank of Scotland/Lloyds/Royal Bank of Scotland and many others).

'Transformate' is used to indicate the ways in which information and communications technologies can support new ways of doing business and even new businesses. Many commentators, such as Peters (1987) and Handy (1989) have stressed the importance of the increased need for substantial changes, 'up-side-down', and the need for managers to be agents of change (rather than merely managers of change). For geographical expansion, home shopping is used by retailers, e.g. the Kay Catalogue is now on Prestel and soon to be available through Europe. The original airline crss are now linked with other travel and leisure organizations, such as car rentals, hotels, and entertainment. Substitute information based services, such as Comp-U-Card, the Electronic Mall and 24-hour Cable Shopping Networks, have begun to exert a considerable influence on existing retail formats and organizations.

Indeed, Drucker (1989) has described the likely nature of some future organizations as 'information-based'. Benetton, for example, has reduced its risk level by the information-based organizational methods of linking production to retailing, all controlled by itself. By subcontracting production and retailing, Benetton concentrate on data and information processing and transmission and on their design and flair skills.

By bringing together the four components — 'automate', 'informate', 'empowerate', and 'transformate' — it is important to view them as complementary, rather than as some kind of natural progression to enhanced success. The balance of the components should be developed from the corporate business strategy. (Figure 2 illustrates an idealized representation of this integration.) The Information Systems strategy is then the outcome of this integration, and it provides the basis to determine the required IT platform.

## A MODEL FOR DEVELOPING A STRATEGIC MANAGEMENT INFORMATION SYSTEM

It follows from Figure 1 that all businesses have quite different information requirements depending upon their strategy and decision-making needs. In fact Figure 1 describes how a company may achieve its growth requirements. These may be determined (or profiled) by using a simple process, that of ascertaining quantified values for its critical success factors. Critical success factors are common to all businesses. They identify, in quantitative terms, what is required of corporate performance if specific marketing and financial objectives are to be achieved. They go beyond this. By also considering the components of the critical success factors — often referred to as key variables the 'ways and means' (or corporate strategies) are also identified. It follows that this framework is likely to be some combination of each of the four elements. They should be initially supportive of each other, overlapping where there is a requirement for interactive support. Figure 2 illustrates this principle. The four elements are 'balanced' to produce a corporate information strategy.

Such a balanced approach would seem unlikely within the context of the strategy model described by Figure 1. More likely is the situation whereby one (or more) of the elements is more prominent. For example a company focusing on consolidation and productivity would require an information strategy that is 'automate' led. It would be using its information outputs to evaluate product range and branch performance. Together with an evaluation of optimal stock holding at both branches and in distribution centres. Such a strategy would take the form of Figure 4a. Here the information strategy is led by the requirement to use information to evaluate performance of the existing business with a view to improving the return on existing assets.

As the consolidation and productivity strategy is seen to become effective a shift of emphasis towards a more managerial orientation is likely to emerge. In essence, the move will be to expand the use of the data so far generated. 'Informate' is this next stage. The managerial emphasis is focused upon planning and control, for example, using EPoS data to provide an input into labour scheduling at branch level. In addition, there may be space management and store design planning. The shift from 'automate' to 'informate' is one whereby the business moves from the use of information to evaluate and rationalize its activities towards using information to manage the business. An essential feature of this stage of the process is that the focal point of control remains at a central point. Thus, labour scheduling and space allocation are management activities that automate from centrally controlled facilities; at branch levels, they are implemented with little or no modification. This emphasis is described in Figure 4b.

Consumer change is an ongoing process. The implication for the business is that it may require a more local view of its activities to be taken or, possibly, for an overall review of the positioning of the business in the market place. Both implications will require the business to consider differentiation issues. The former will require some devolution of decision-making to field and branch management if local opportunities are to be maximized. At this juncture, the information management emphasis shifts towards the 'empowerate' emphasis (although 'automate' and 'informate' capabilities remain important). Repositioning strategies also require a similar approach, however, the devolution that occurs is within a central level of head office activity. Examples of the 'empowerate' can be seen in the moves by food multiples towards 'local ranging', whereby local consumer demand is reflected and local management is 'empowered' with sufficient authority to decide upon the composition of the ranges. The creation of niche retailing activities within mass-merchandiser companies is similar. Here, however, autonomy is given to a group of managers. The creation of Top Shop in the early 1970s is possibly one of the first examples. Figure 4c describes the 'empowerate' emphasis.

Continued success and growth are usually achieved by growth through replication and development of the existing business by the addition of adjacent product and service offers. There is a point at which these can no longer offer the rate of growth required and consequently diversification and then conglomerate activities are considered. It is at this point that the information management emphasis moves once more. The shift towards 'transformate' requires the ability to use information and communications systems to support new methods of conducting business. It is likely that the business will first examine the existing business in an attempt at improving its cost-effectiveness. For example, supply-chain management systems may be developed to improve merchandise availability at distribution centres and at the point-of-sale, but at the same time stockholding throughout the chain is optimized. Customer contact and handling may be improved by developing customer databases and market penetration be increased by using the expertise in information.

These issues are also important for mergers and acquisitions. For example, Tesco's acquisition of Hillards was completed successfully and separate information systems integrated in a very short space of time, and similarly, the acquisition and reporting of Argyle/Safeway should be noted. An earlier illustration is Conran's combining of Habitat and Mothercare, founded on the creativity of the former and the strong systems of the latter.

Information and communications systems enable service organizations to communicate with and service customer-groups who may live in low density areas (where the existing retail format could not be operated economically or for those customers whose lifestyles do not permit ready and early access to existing format offers). Clearly, the developments in home shopping are of interest here. Other examples, perhaps not so obvious but nevertheless important are developments 'in-store' whereby it can expand the merchandise offer (and add service) across items that are difficult to handle for both the retailer and the customer. Perishable products such as expensive food/produce items have been dealt with in this way by US department stores. Price catalogues are yet another example of the in-store use of IT, whereby customers can compare competitive price offers by interrogating a database (see Figure 4d).

The ‘transformate’ emphasis also considers the managerial decisions and information set required for diversification and conglomerate strategies. Typically these are the issues identified in Table 1 — critical success factors and key variables. An evaluation of revenues and margins, productivity profiles and added-value cost and benefits linked together with information on market structures and market shares, (a) provide a basis for deciding whether a market entry should be attempted and if so, how. Following a decision to make an entry, the information system should be used for detailed planning and control of the strategy implementation. Figure 5 suggests how the information strategy might develop.

A 'dynamic' perspective can be obtained from Figure 6. Clearly strategy can be both dynamic and incremental. Thus we might expect a company's strategy to develop through a range of options in order to achieve its growth objectives. Figure 6 demonstrates how the corporate information-strategy emphasis might change over time. Initially a consolidation and productivity strategy can achieve a maximum rate of growth. As this option becomes less effective and repositioning becomes a more attractive option, so the emphasis shifts from 'automate' to 'informate'. Similarly, as the growth requirements are increased the information strategy emphasis moves towards an 'empowerate' emphasis and eventually towards a 'transformate' structure if continued growth is required.

## STRATEGY DECISIONS AND INFORMATION STRATEGY

As strategy alternatives are expanded, the associated risk can be expected to increase (see Figure 1). The purpose of the information strategy is to reduce risk by providing information to relevant management decisions.

Within the context of the model proposed in Figure 2 the information strategy is structured to meet both the decision type and information needs of the decision-maker. Figure 7 illustrates how objectives, considered here to be critical success factors, can be met (key variables), and these are then considered in terms of the type of strategy and the associated risk. From this follows the decisions to be made (the decision set) and the information needs to reach the decisions, and hence the nature of the information strategy is derived.

A number of examples are provided in Figure 8. For instance, to achieve a sales increase of 20% a range of options exist; the strategy options are predominantly consolidation and productivity based and the risk is low. The decisions required to be made require central monitoring and evaluation of product-range performance and pricing. These are essentially 'automate' in their nature. A more adventurous option is to identify alternative marketing-opportunities, addressing them with an appropriate offer. This suggests a re-positioning strategy with a shift in the emphasis of the information strategy.

Other examples demonstrate how the information strategy emphasis must shift to meet the corporate strategy requirements.

In Table 3, the nature of the information needs required to service the decisions detailed in Figure 7 are suggested. The information availability varies from frequently required data detailing performance on such factors as product range participation, productivity, profitability and availability; all of which is required frequently and consequently should be available on-line and with optimum currency. Primarily this data service requires an 'automate' information-strategy. Some of the data may well be used both for 'informate' and 'empowerate' purposes.

As the nature of the strategic options changes, so too does the perspective of the decision-making and moves away from the core business. The information required becomes external to the company and is required less frequently. Furthermore, the nature of the decision-making ('transformate') governs the distribution of the information. This situation is particularly the case for growth and diversification strategies where often the decision is a 'one-off' and consequently the information is sourced only when required.

## CONCLUDING COMMENTS

For long-term success, vision and management are necessary. Information is a strategic resource which must be managed effectively. Information management must be aligned with and driven by the corporate business-strategy, and it must encompass the information systems to support not only management's decision-making but also relationships with suppliers, customers and competitors. In this paper, a practical, strategic framework for information management has been outlined.

Important diagnosis options indicated by this framework should be developed empirically in organizations to determine the alignment of their corporate strategy and structure and their information strategy and the potential future developments. In addition, comparative analyses across organizations should provide much more useful and relevant management insights than crude comparisons of annual IT expenditure.

## Biographies

John R. Beaumont is a Professor of Management and Head of the School of Management at the University of Bath. Formerly ICL Professor of Applied Management Information Systems at the University of Stirling and a management consultant with Coopers and Lybrand in London and New York.

David Walters is now a consultant with David Walters Associates. Formerly Professor of Retailing and Director of the Institute for Retail Studies at the University of Stirling, and Co-Director of the Oxford Institute for Retail Management at Templeton College, Oxford.
