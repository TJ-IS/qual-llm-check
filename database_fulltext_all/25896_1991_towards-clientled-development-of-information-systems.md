# Towards client-led development of information systems

F. A. Stowell

Paisley College, Paisley DA1 2BE, Renfrewshire, UK

Abstract. With the incorporation of Information Technology into most areas of modern life, the methods used by the Computer System Analyst (CSA) needs to be reconsidered. To suppose Systems Analysis to be concerned solely with computing is to minimize the task as an information system is greater than a computer system. As such, the information system designer needs to be able to 'appreciate' the wider implications of a clients information needs. An argument is put forward that Information System Design should be undertaken by the client with the CSA acting as facilitator. This paper attempts to provide a re-appraisal of the CSA and arising from this re-appraisal, suggest that ideas originating from organizational analysis could be usefully embodied in the design process for Information Systems.

Keywords: systems analysis, information systems, organizational analysis, systems.

## INTRODUCTION

There would appear to be at least two interpretations of the role of a Systems Analyst. One interpretation, which has become a 'popular' understanding, considers it to be exclusively related to computer systems (Vitalori & Dickson, 1983). The second interpretation relates the role to problem-solving in a broader context, less quantitative in method and more oriented to the analysis of broad strategic policy questions (Smith, 1966). A Computer Systems Analyst (CSA) seems to be concerned with fitting a technological solution to a clearly defined problem-situation. The Systems Analyst on the other hand is concerned with a wider appreciation of a problem situation and with the context in which the problem resides. In the late 1970s, designers began to recognize that the introduction of computer systems produced significant changes to organizational behaviour and that the methods used for their development were inadequate in coping with both the social and the technical complexities of the task. This problem has become more acute as the 'user' gets to be computer literate. With the development of relatively cheap, large capacity and fast response personal computers in the 1980s, the way in which computers are used has become the concern of all those who work in a business rather than solely the concern of a limited number of 'specialists'.

During the past decade writers have advocated the need for the development of methodologies which make the designer more aware of the social implications of introducing computing power into organizations (Stowell, 1985; Avison et al., 1988; Rzevski, 1988). Epistemologically, the attitude exhibited by the advocates of 'socially aware' computer design symbolized a paradigm shift towards antipositism (Hirschhiem, 1985). In practical terms this shift resulted in a number of attempts to produce methodologies that focused upon the social implications of computer system design (e.g. Mumford & Henshall, 1979; Wood-Harper et al., 1985). At the same time developments were being made in organizational problem analysis (see Checkland, 1981; Hirschhiem, 1985; Miles, 1985; Avison & Fitzgerald, 1988). The purpose of this paper is not to repeat the arguments expressed by these authors but to question whether they satisfy the 'social' objectives of the design process that is advocated in the literature (Lyytinen & Klien, 1985).

## HUMAN FACTORS AND INFORMATION SYSTEM DESIGN — THE PARADOX

The availability and use of information technology has been described by some commentators as constituting an information revolution (Friedrichs & Schaffer, 1982; Forrester, 1985; Winograd & Flores, 1988) but is this true? It is important to put into context what is meant by information technology and its relationship to information systems. Although the application of technological devices make a fundamental contribution to the dissemination of information, these devices in themselves are not information systems (Scarrott, 1989). The technical process is concerned only with the translation, storage and transmission of signals but it is the interpretation (literal and contextual) of the resulting code by a human that makes it information. The information system can be considered essentially as a form of human-activity system whose actors use, amongst other things, a computer as one source of business intelligence. Thus, an information system can be viewed as an assortment of intelligence gathering devices used in a way specific to an individual so as to enable him to manage his environment. A computer, as Winograd & Flores remind us, is fundamentally a '... tool for human action...' (Winograd & Flores, 1988, p. 78).

Information systems existed long before the invention of the microchip (Buckingham et al., 1987), and yet 'information systems' which have at their heart a computer, are often considered by both the lay person and the computer scientist alike, to be the exclusive province of technology. Non-computer scientists appear, until recently, to have paid little regard to the actual development procedures of computer-based data-processing systems. It is perhaps because of this that there are so few design approaches capable of taking into account the wider considerations involved in the generation and dissemination of information.

Table 1 shows some computer-systems design methods which have been (and are being) used by computer-systems analysts over the past two decades. The dominant feature of each of the methods identified in Table 1 is the requirements generated by the computer. None of the approaches seems to differentiate between the computer and the information system. Instead the assumption seems to be that the computer is the information system and consequently the definition of data is prime. Where a methodology explicitly encourages client involvement, the client's role seems to be constrained to the identification of data or to discussions about the way in which the computer could be used. In none of the methodologies discussed is there a stage where the definition of the problem situation is other than that directly related to the needs of the computer. Despite attempts to embrace the social aspects of an information system, these methods all seem to be based upon the positivist tradition. (A similar view is expressed about socio-technical system approaches by Lyytinen & Klien, 1985.)

The analytical and design tools that are most commonly used by computer-systems analysts concentrate upon the technical aspects of the perceived situation (Miles, 1985). Why should this be so? The prime reason appears to be that the methods employed for the design and development of computer systems relate to what Winograd & Flores call the rationalistic tradition and to what Hirschheim describes as the positivist view (Hirschheim, 1985; Winograd & Flores, 1988). Such a view, when applied to information systems, assumes that it is possible to dismantle and then quantify an information system to enable it to be re-constructed in computer form. Such a proselytized view might enable us to build a computer data-processing system but it will not produce an information system.

We can recognize one problem with computer-systems design in similar terms to what Churchman refers to as 'generality' (Churchman, 1971). The preference to resurrect former ideas as a quick route forward (created in part by the design procedure adopted by CSA's) is often precipitated by the pressure to save on pre-design time. To avoid unnecessary analysis and design effort, the CSA, when faced with what is perceived to be a similar problem to that experienced in a past project, re-uses ideas and solutions. The result is a 'design' process which has more in common with mass production of motor cars than to problem solving. Such an approach can be viewed as a regurgitation of 'off-the-shelf solutions' with scant regard to the culture of the enterprise itself.

The speed at which commercially available business computer-systems have developed has meant that the design task has become specialist and increasingly more technically biased (Nelson, 1986). The development of applications generators has increased the pressure upon the analyst to provide a specification compatible with the needs of the development tool (e.g. Computer-Aided Software Engineering; Information Engineering Workstations). This reaction has often been at the expense of problem ‘appreciation’. Technological developments, particularly design tools, are seen by many practising computer-system designers as replacing the ‘time consuming’ analytical methods (Benyon & Skidmore, 1987). The net result is a reduction in the time spent gathering information, currently estimated to be around 20%, with the bulk of the time, 80%, spent structuring the problem and the requirements for a computer solution (Vitalari, 1985). This increasing bias towards technological solution may be one reason why computer systems often fail to fulfill client expectations (Looney, 1986; Herschheim & Newman, 1988; Rzevski, 1988). Despite this apparent poor record toward customer satisfaction, there is every indication that computer-systems design is becoming less ‘people’ considerate and the philosophy seems to be that difficulties related to poor problem definition can be overcome through the increased use of computer packages, such as applications generators (Grant, 1990).

The arguments to be found in the literature for a 'softer' approach to information-system design seem to have been ignored or addressed only partially to date. We can agree with Boland who argues that ' . . . organizations are constituted by acts of communication and are fundamentally an interpretive problem' (Boland, 1985). This leaves us with the difficulty in attempting both a subjective understanding of a problem in order to appreciate what the information systems are in the social and cultural terms of the organization and at the same time provide a functional translation of the problem which satisfies the practical how-to-do-it requirements of the technology. Our problem seems to relate to the apparent need for a method appropriate to a de facto 'messy' problem.

## COMPUTER SYSTEMS ANALYSTS, EDUCATION AND REDUCTIONISM

Most approaches to computer-system development apply techniques which can be described as quasi-scientific. The notion upon which these methods seem to be based is that the problem can be quantified (or clearly specified) through the application of effective techniques to achieve specified ends. The techniques themselves all appear to start from the assumption that the most important objective is to provide a detailed technical specification for the purchase of hardware and software. Any organizational action is interpreted in a strictly instrumental fashion (Lyytinen & Klien, 1985). Whilst such an objective can be accepted as being of cardinal importance to computer scientists, it can be argued that the technical specification is dependent upon the definition of the information system itself. Understanding about organizational behaviour and cultural aspects are substantive considerations in the information-development process which require a more appropriate method of appreciation than those techniques suited to technical specification (Jayaratna, 1988). A computer is a tool which facilitates improvements to information systems intrinsically through the speed and accuracy in which data is processed. In a similar way, a telephone is a system of communication which provides a means of voice-communication between remotely located individuals or groups. Neither computer nor telephone can be accurately defined as an information system but each makes an important contribution.

The underlying concept of most computer-system design methodologies is essentially reductionist and many of the methods that have been developed were created to satisfy a perceived operational need with no necessity for consideration for matters conceptual. The result of this tradition has been a stream of tools/techniques/methods which are geared to translating existing organizational operating procedures into a data specification (Connor, 1985). This practice seems to be based upon the assumption that the company information needs can be satisfied by improving the method of data processing. In 1979, Methlie highlighted this apparent confusion by differentiating between what he called the 'datalogical' and 'infological' approaches to computer-based information-system design (Methlie, 1980). Ideas about information design do not appear to have progressed over the past decade as the 1970s mainframe payroll-system thinking still seems to persist. As suggested in Table 1, most design approaches can be described in terms of Methlie's datalogical definition but it is argued, in the 1990s we require thinking which is more concerned about information systems.

The whole process of developing and designing a computer-aided information-system necessitates an intervention into the working practices and customs of an organizations culture. The analyst, and those associated, will almost certainly alter the way in which the enterprise operates and the way in which the social networks interact. Their actions are effectively the catalysts of organizational change which go beyond the narrow confines of computer hardware and software.

The methods employed in the design and development of a computer system can be argued to subscribe to a particular notion of 'organization' (Winograd & Flores, 1988; Checkland, 1989). But what are these organizational models and where does the thinking behind them originate? One way in which we can attempt to establish the answer to this question is by examining the academic loci of the subject.

## Education and training

The problems related to teaching methods for developing computer-aided information-systems is complicated by the environment in which we train our Computer-Systems Analysts. By and large, the design and development of computer systems has evolved within the traditions of nomothetic thinking. In the early years of computer-system development, computers were used to address a specific problem, e.g. payroll. This kind of thinking implies that all problems can be translated into a technical specification and by applying the right techniques a solution will be found. Such approaches when applied to the development of a computer-aided information-system rarely examine the overall problem situation or its interactions; they simply take a 'sub-system' and convert it into a computer form. As computing has become increasingly commonplace in business, this datalogical type of approach has become inadequate. The emphasis of the modern analyst's task is upon the identification, analysis and definition of an information system in which a computer might play a significant role rather than fit a problem to favoured technology.

It seems to be the case that one of the difficulties is the way in which we educate the analyst. Systems Analysis seems to have no clear intellectual 'home'. A perfunctory examination of educational directories only adds to this confusion. The general subject of computing is taught in departments of Computer Science or Information Science which can be found predominantly in the faculty of Science and to a lesser degree in faculties of Business. Computing is taught as part of a portfolio of courses offered by departments of Electronic Engineering, Mathematics Departments, Business and Management and in Information Science. Attempting to identify a suitable academic 'location' for the subject is frustrated as it is part technical and part social science. The satisfactory definition of a discipline of systems analysis or 'information science' is difficult.

It is reasonable to suppose that the way in which the subject of ‘computing’ is presented will reflect the general ethos of the faculty in which it is taught and consequently we can suppose that computing will be taught within the traditions of reductionism. Some commentators suggest that ISD has failed to become a discipline because those who are its practitioners recognize it only as the delivery of a technical system (Lyytinen & Klien, 1985).

The problem of establishing the domain of information systems as a discipline is exacerbated by the range and depth of training/education required for an individual to become an IT specialist. There is little space in the curriculum of most computer-science undergraduate courses for consideration of the wider implications and needs of information systems (Stowell, 1990a). The tendency in most educational establishments is for training to concentrate upon technological developments and upon technical design methods (often obsolete) with little or no time to contemplate the epistemological basis upon which these tools/methods themselves are based. This, it is argued, is a serious omission as without a foundation of intellectual ideas attributable to the study of Information Systems, how can Information Science be any more than a set of craft skills with a vague acknowledgement to reductionist science?

## IDENTIFYING THE NEED FOR A SUITABLE IS DESIGN APPROACH

Questions relating to the intellectual roots of methods and techniques used by Information Science in general and CSA in particular are not new. The literature provides a number of examples. For example, Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald (1982) attempted to clarify the confusion of methodologies and approaches by producing a classification of what they considered to be six major approaches to systems analysis. But in some respects this taxonomy adds to the confusion. For example, they muddle 'tools' and theoretical constructs, e.g. both data analysis and General Systems Theory are classified as an approach.

Benyon & Skidmore, (1987), to an even greater degree than Wood-Harper & Fitzgerald, seem to be concerned with systems analysis entirely within the context of computing. Consequently, they appear to be more at home treating the methods employed by CSA as tools rather than discussing them as problem-solving approaches. This thinking manifests as a practical problem when they suggest a way of combining the different methods in relation to a hierarchy of approaches. Whilst their suggestion seems to provide a convenient means of utilizing several approaches, it avoids the issue of conceptual frameworks through the implication in their paper that each method is complimentary.

Benyon & Skidmore (Ibid) suggest that the analyst should select an appropriate tool for the job from a tool-kit of methodologies. In principle this seems a good idea but it is not clear from their paper how the tool is selected aside from a general guide relating to the model of the problem. It is not as simple as they make out; they are not so much recommending a selection of a tool as the adoption of a particular epistemology. They seem to ignore the problem that each approach that they have considered was originally developed to serve a specific purpose and, furthermore, subscribes to a particular theoretical paradigm. For example, using Burrell & Morgan's framework, SSM can be located within the subjective dimension whilst the Jackson method seems to be embedded within the objective dimension (see Burrell & Morgan, 1979).

Davies & Wood-Harper attempt an examination of approaches to Information System Design by utilizing a framework comprising three dominant views of Information Systems. These three views they argue, are predominant in the IT literature (Davies & Wood-Harper, 1989). Their idea of applying a framework of this kind to IS methodologies does, with reservation, serve a useful purpose. It provides a framework to assist in highlighting the conceptual basis of the design method to be employed. An example of the way in which the idea can be used is provided by the authors through an examination of Multiview (Wood-Harper et al., 1985; Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990). Their examination suggests Multiview to be a patchwork of conceptual approaches and suggests it to be a method primarily concerned with the translation of 'information' into a data form suited to computer manipulation.

Hirschheim & Klien propose that information systems developers approach the task of designing information systems from a set of assumptions about the nature of the task. These assumptions relate to a particular epistemology about information system and will determine the way in which the system is developed. They suggest that most ISD research focuses upon the functionalist paradigm and that alternative conceptions are warranted (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989).

One difficulty with understanding information-system design through the examination of existing methods, is that this exercise may concentrate the examination upon 'how' it is done now rather than upon 'what' should be done: the methods studied may in themselves constrain thinking about the problem. If we are to rethink the role of Systems Analysis there would seem to be an advantage to be gained in disregarding the methodologies and techniques currently used for the design of ‘information’ systems and concentrate instead upon finding a way to help the analyst to express what Vickers refers to as the ‘form of the problem form’ (Vickers, 1981).

An obvious question which appears to be fundamental to the starting point of this discussion is 'What is an Information System?' to which computers might be a particular 'How'. But this question is too ambitious since it is the uniqueness of each enterprise which makes a satisfactory answer to the question problematic. Each enterprise is unique and its IS requirement will also reflect this uniqueness. How then can we address this question? An error made by those who develop design methods within computer science seems to be the assumption of organizational uniformity. Organizations appear to be assumed to be immutable and all behave in a similar way. From this reductionist assumption emanates all kinds of software development. If we reject, however, a rationalistic perspective of organization, where does such criticism lead us? It is argued here that non-positivism might offer some way forward.

## SUBJECTIVISM AND ISD

Organizational behaviour is not fixed but can be said to be determined by the values and beliefs of those who are its members (Stowell, 1989). Consequently, 'organization' as such cannot be replicated in a laboratory and hence neither can the development of a computer-based information system (ibid). To separate, however, organizational activities into 'formal' and 'informal' in order to develop computer systems for the 'formal' activities, does not provide a satisfactory answer either (Weeks, 1973). As the interaction of social relations emergent from the formal networks within the enterprise will be influenced by, and in turn influence, the conduct of individuals.

Organizations are in a constant state of change and it is reasonable to suggest that their information systems too will reflect this situation (Stowell, 1990c). The adaptive nature of organizations, which is partly the result of a reaction to operational and social exigencies, suggest that an understanding of organizational change would be best achieved by the involvement of the analyst, the problem-solver, in the problem situation. This proposition could be addressed through the adoption of the interpretive paradigm as the intellectual theory underpining information-design methodology. The interpretive paradigm is concerned with the recognition of the 'real-world' as it is at a level of subjective experience. My observation of some attempts to incorporate this thinking into information-systems design has occasionally resulted in the misapplication of the concept in practice.

Over the past decade research such as the work at the University of Lancaster and the development of Soft Systems Methodology provide lessons which may be profitably translated into the information system development process. Information System Design needs a view of the network of technologies and activities into which it fits rather than the technological devices in isolation. One difficulty facing the analyst is a practical one in that technology requires a functional explanation but information needs understanding. Despite advances in technology, the human-being is still the essential element in an information system. An Information System is a mixture of technical and social events causally explained by an observer through the perception of actions. The information-systems designer attempts to unpack and then re-pack 'reality' as the process of design takes its course. The more superficial an analysts appreciation of a situation, the more likely the client is to be dissatisfied. To gain a better understanding of the problem-situation, the analyst needs to become involved in the problem. It has been argued that Soft Systems Methodology (West, 1990) may provide the means to achieving this but often when used in this way, the essential phenomenological element is list when the practical stage of Information System design is reached.

The adoption of the subjective paradigm is not without difficulty for the analyst. Subjective involvement in the problem situation solicits criticism of analyst bias and lack of professionalism. Care should be taken to avoid this criticism through the rigorous manner in which the concept is applied. Such criticism is not the exclusive province of subjectivism as Hoyer records when writing of traditional computer-system design methodologies ‘... the system designer may adjust their development practice to prevent the consequence they consider undesirable ... the end users have no formal influence or power.’ (Hoyer, 1980, p130).

## Information systems design as action research

In order to understand the full requirements of an information-system, the analyst should not be constrained in his investigations by the analytical method that he uses. The method itself should be agnostic to the problem situation and flexible enough to meet different situations. Clearly the approach should not have the rigidity of a technique. To provide the analyst with a means of guidance that will facilitate both learning about the situation and about the method used implies that the project identification and analysis stage can beneficially be treated as a piece of action research (Stowell, 1990b).

In recent times a resurgence of interest in action research has taken place, notably with the development of Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland, 1981, 1986; Wilson, 1982; Wood-Harper et al., 1985; Wood & Doyle, 1989; Stowell, 1985, 1989). The application of 'soft' systems thinking, coupled with an appropriate means of producing a technical specification might provide one means of satisfying the requirements of ISD discussed above.

The whole method of investigation, analysis and development through to the design of an information system could be viewed as an Inquiring System (West, 1990). If the foregoing arguments are accepted the analysts first task is to gain what Vickers calls an 'appreciation' of the problem situation ('... the inner history of an individual an organization and a society ...' Vickers, 1983, p 69). The adoption of this notion should be extended throughout the study as a whole, including the stage at which the Information System is developed. It is at this stage that the epistemological difference between Information Systems as understood by the 'Systems' practitioner and Information Systems as understood by the technologist is most pronounced.

In many instances in the recent past where a subjective inquiring method has been used as a front end to information-system design, the practitioner, when s/he reaches the technical development stage seems to be engulfed by technical requirement. The subjectivism, which underpinned the inquiry right up until the latter stages of the infomation-system development, seems to be substituted by a functionalist appraisal of the problem. For example, where Soft

Systems methodology has been used as the method of inquiry (see Wood-Harper, 1985; Benyon & Skidmore, 1987), the translation of the information-system as described by the Conceptual Model is taken over by the technical specialist and used independently to develop the computer-supported information system. At the same time, this approach seems to lose many of the benefits that were gained in the development process. This approach often becomes an exercise in generality and the benefits gained from the development of the information system through the use of SSM are essentially lost. This apparent functionalist attitude to subjective information-system design is suggested to be an error and defeats the whole point of adopting a subjective approach to design. Instead, it is argued that the design of the information system must continue to be developed by the client and the analyst in the same fashion as the problem was addressed. In other words, the project as a whole should be treated as a piece of action research: the appreciation gained about the situation by the 'team' will help to guide the information-system development process. The result of this proposition is that the development of the information system can then be considered by both analysts and the group in its widest context rather than considered as a set of discrete steps in which the information system is predominated by the technology.

The IT practitioner should seek to become ‘facilitator’ and consider himself as one member of the team who has some knowledge about IT. The role of the analyst should be no different to another member of the team who may have knowledge about Accountancy or Production Control and his input should reflect that position and reduce the tendency for the IT specialist to predominate the information-system design process. If the development process is conducted in this way, the possibility for real change increases as the recognition and subsequent acceptance (or rejection) for change will come from the group rather than being imposed from outside (Stowell, 1990b).

Using a soft approach to IT design, such as SSM, is not new but using it to enable clients to design their own information system is rare and there are critics who point to the incompatibility between the Conceptual Model and a Structured Data Flow Diagram (SDFD) (Wood & Doyle, 1989; Mingers, 1990). There is, however, only an incompatibility if the development of the SDFD is viewed as a separate exercise to be undertaken by a technical expert. For example, the use of SSM for information-system design can facilitate the transfer of responsibility of design from the IT practitioner to the development group as a whole. If the development of SDFD is continued in the same spirit as the development of Conceptual Models, the difficulty of translating CM to SDFD diminishes and client-led IT design becomes a real possibility (Prior, 1990; Stowell, 1990c). It is important, however, that the iterative, continual learning process which underpins SSM is maintained throughout the IT specification stage.

## PRACTICAL EXPERIENCE OF 'CLIENT-LED DESIGN'

How Action Research can be utilized as the basis of Client-Led design is now described. An information system was developed by the staff working in the Commercial Department of a manufacturing enterprise. The enterprise is the manufacture of liquid handling equipment (e.g.

Petrol Pumps) and is based in the South of England (Stowell, 1985). The account for the project is an attempt to show the potential for creating an environment for client participation in ISD.

In this project Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) was used to create a learning environment both for the clients and the consultant. The important point which marks this study out from other SSM/IT projects is that the process of client participation was maintained throughout the project, from problem definition to the specification of the information system. The project resulted in some significant changes including the re-definition of all of the operational activities of the department.

The manufacture of liquid handling equipment is a high-precision and a competitive manufacturing market. The company held, at the time of the study, an important share of the market but the application of Information Technology was threatening their position both in terms of its use as a management aid and in product development (e.g. computer-based planning systems and LCD display rather than mechanical). Competitors were known to be incorporating IT to improve their company's market position. The dilemma for the Board of Directors of this company was to continue the rate of production and at the same time introduce new methods of working without precipitating industrial unrest.

The Board of Directors decided that the initial task was to re-examine the way the department operated and then develop an information system which would help the Commercial Department to operate more efficiently. To carry out this task, the Managing Director decided to call upon the services of a consultant. This decision created problems for the staff. As far as the staff were concerned the Managing Director's action in calling in an outsider meant that they were going to be subjected to changes in their working practices by an individual who was unlikely to have any knowledge about their problems. Several members of the Commercial Department had worked for the company for 20 years or more and had experience of several previous attempts by consultants to improve working practices. A further complication was that the company were experiencing industrial action because of a dispute over pay from the production workforce (Stowell, 1989). The first problem that the practitioner needed to address, therefore, was how to gain credibility amongst the members of staff.

The situation was a messy one in that there were clearly defined project objectives but there were also difficult social problems to address. Both problems were interrelated and neither could be dealt with in isolation. To facilitate an appreciation of the problem-situation SSM was adopted. Previous experience of its use suggested that SSM provided a quick and effective means of problem appreciation (Stowell, 1985). The first stage of SSM, Rich Picture building, quickly increased the practitioner's awareness of the problem-situation and enabled him to talk knowledgeably to members of staff within a short period of time. This had the effect of building the confidence of the practitioner and those involved as well as providing the basis for an informed discussion about problem areas.

Soon after the initial research phase, a group was formed to identify the problems and consider how these problems might be addressed. The group comprised a core group of eight members which extended to a maximum of 15 to embrace any member of the Commercial Department who wished to attend development meetings. The size of the Commercial Department at that time was 50 including both staff and managers. The group met weekly over a period of 26 weeks usually at the end of a working day and sometimes during a lunchbreak. The meetings were fully supported by the Managing Director and were always well attended.

From the outset the consultant made a conscious effort to act as a facilitator rather than group leader. For example, encouraging different members of the group to take on the responsibilities of chairperson at meetings; encouraging clerical staff as well as the managers to take on the role of team leader during fact-finding or development work. The group developed Root Definitions and Conceptual Models and then a feasible Conceptual Model. Once a model for the Commercial Department had been developed this was made available to all members of the department who were asked to consider it in terms of work related problems — real or imaginary. This exercise enabled the model to be refined into one which the department considered to be culturally acceptable (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Scholes, 1990).

The resultant proposal, which included some significant changes to existing working practices, was made to the Board of Directors by representatives from the group (not the consultant). This fact is important as it was a practical demonstration to the Board of the commitment of members of the department to the proposal. The presentations of the group to the Board was a departure from traditional consultancy practice as the consultant attended as a member of the team and not the person responsible for the proposals.

Following the acceptance of the outline proposal by the Board, the group continued with the development of an information system for the operation of the newly defined Commercial Department. The method chosen to represent the information system was that described by Gane & Sarson (1979). The group were not concerned with computer hardware nor software at this stage of the project.

The process of developing the information system was undertaken in the same participatory way that the group had used SSM. It is worth commenting here that those critics who consider this to be an impossible task may have found this so precisely because they have not allowed the staff to produce their own information-system diagram. It is argued to be an error if the consultant takes over the project at this stage. Despite the apparent technical knowledge needed to produce this type of diagram, experience shows that the client is able to continue to participate in the production of data-flow type diagrams. By enabling the client(s) to develop their own diagram, the result is more likely to represent the information needs of the tasks at hand rather than the needs of the computer.

The appreciation of the problem situation that the group as a whole had gained from the use of SSM was of benefit in the development of the Structured Data Flow Diagrams. The completed diagrams enabled the group to identify the tasks that were needed to manage the data requirements of the department and from this define the jobs related to those tasks. The Structured Data Flow Diagrams also allowed the group to identify (with the expert assistance of the practitioner) areas where Information Technology could assist in the processing of the data. The end result was a set of activities and responsibilities required for the operation of the Commercial Department, both human and IT related within the cultural context of the company.

In this study it was the members of staff within the Commercial Department who were responsible for implementing the new departmental system and not the practitioner. The practitioner achieved the role of facilitator, providing 'expert' advice when appropriate. This enabled the clients to identify their problems, develop a means of addressing those problems, and lead in the specification of an information-system which they felt would be useful to them. The final stage was for the practitioner to identify possible hardware and software to the group specification. The experience gained by the group from their roles in the development process meant that they were better placed to consider the computer assistance that might be available to them and more inclined to use the computer itself.

SSM was used to help those involved appreciate the problem-situation as a whole and to help the practitioner gain credibility in the eyes of the staff. The knowledge gained was used by the group to identify the most appropriate information-system for their needs. The appreciation gained by all those involved was then used as the basis for the development of a Structured Data Flow Diagram. From the Structured Data Flow Diagram the computing element of the information-system was identified within the context of what had been learnt about the Commercial Department information system as a whole.

## SUMMARY

The previous section provided a summary of a project in which an attempt was made to initiate client-led design. The study itself took no longer than the time a traditional study would have taken for a project of this magnitude. The study did, however, involve a large number of company employees but this was considered by the Managing Director to be a worthwhile investment since the end-result was one which achieved the desired improvements and was acceptable to the staff (Stowell, 1990c).

The project illustrates how client-led design can be fulfilled through the use of a suitable methodology, such as SSM. It is intended to show that client-led design can be realized even when the end result involves the need for a technical specification. The essence of this paper is to accentuate the importance of the ethos of participation. Client involvement is not about finding ways to bolt the client(s) to the technology (as is the tendency with CASE tools) but to provide them with a means by which they themselves can determine their information needs and to assist them in defining a technical specification. The argument expressed here is for the further development of methods truly suitable for client-led design.

Client-led design is not to be considered as de-skilling the role of the Systems Analyst but to help this role to evolve into that of an Information Systems Designer. Indeed, the skills required for client-led design are argued to be greater than those for the more traditional methods of design. It is suggested that the Information Systems Designer's technical expertise is used only at the end of the study to indicate to the client where IT may be usefully employed in an information system which s/he has aided the client to design themselves. The prime task of the designer should be to ensure a result which produces a description of an information system which includes the use of IT rather than one dedicated to an IT system.

The purpose of the project example is to emphasize the argument for client-led design rather than provide a definitive guide on how the task should be done. The role of the practitioner is emphasized as being, first of all, that of facilitator and then adviser on matters relevant to his/her expertise (just as others in the group would advise on matters relevant to their expertise). The study emphasizes that the degree of change can be regulated by the group itself and that the change may be quite radical. Consequently, the problems of individual and of management power are capable of being addressed within the development process itself (Stowell & Allen, 1988; Checkland & Scholes, 1990; Stowell, 1990b). The process suggested here proposes that radical change, of which some commentators suggest SSM to be deficient (Jackson, 1982), rests with those who are involved in the problem situation. It may be that by facilitating the control of the development process to those who are most affected by the inherent changing situation, a greater magnitude of change can be achieved than would be possible by an outsider, no matter how expert.

The methods employed in the project are, admittedly, not new but the way in which they were used may be. What this paper is proposing is the development of methods more appropriate to client-led design. It is hoped that the arguments expressed here are a small step towards that objective. Arising out of the foregoing discussion and from the experiences gained from the project the following elements are considered to be fundamental to a client-led information-systems design methodology. The methodology should be capable of providing the practitioner with a framework for:

(a) A 'subjective' appreciation of the problem situation;

(b) Client-led design;

(c) Appreciation by the client and practitioner of an information system in the widest sense;

(d) Evaluation Information Technology for its potential aid within the defined information system;

(e) Radical change.

In the reported study many of these elements, it is argued, were addressed. The use of SSM by all concerned enabled a subjective appreciation of the problem situation to be accomplished. Once appreciation had been gained, and, the basic processes of information-systems development explained, the clients were able to identify their information-system and its relationship to the operation of their department. The changes which took place were to some extent radical, in as much as there was a redefinition of all staff tasks within the department. More importantly, the changes were developed and agreed by the group as a whole rather than by someone outside the group. The changes were not seen by staff as management-inspired but more as the identification of the need for change.

This project has been reported in this study to emphasize the way in which client-led design can be achieved. The study involved managers and staff throughout the project. There was no aspect in which the participants were denied an opportunity to contribute to the development of the information-system. This feature is worth emphasizing since it seems to mark the study out from other, so called, client-centred design approaches. Traditionally these approaches have two distinct phases. The first phase relates to client participation and the second to the technical phase. The latter phase of the study often negates the former because the client's needs may be made to fit the technological requirements by the Computer Systems Analyst (Hoyer, 1980). Alternatively, there are cases where the technology has already been chosen and the participatory aspect relates to staff being given the opportunity to decide where the technology will be used. In this study the clients exercised control throughout the project.

## CONCLUSION

In this paper an attempt has been made to consider the position of the Computer Systems Analyst within the context of Information Systems Design. The contention has been made that the development of an Information System is better undertaken by those who are to use it, however, most design methodologies are developed from positivist ideas which preclude such a possibility. Those methods which attempt to include client participation are usually more concerned with 'improving' the way in which existing practices operate through the use of computing and rarely attempt to examine if the practices are appropriate to the environment in which the company presently exists. Despite the weight of literature that advocates the need for the Computer Systems Analyst to take into account the 'people needs' of IT, there is little practical advice about how this might be achieved. A brief investigation of the intellectual loci of education and training of Computer Scientists suggests that the consideration of the wider issues of organizational behaviour is a minor part of many computer science syllabuses. If 'Information Systems' is to be recognized as a discipline then attention should be given to the appropriate education of its practitioners.

Finally, an illustration was given of one attempt at client-led design through a brief account of a study in which an attempt was made to encourage the clients to develop the outline for an information system most suited to their needs. The example offered some ideas about the process of ISD in which the IT expert can act as a facilitator rather than dominate the development process.

## Biography

Frank Stowell has recently been appointed Professor of Information Systems in the Department of Computing Science at Paisley College, Scotland. He gained his PhD from the University of Lancaster in the Department of Systems and Information Management and was previously a Principal Lecturer at Portsmouth Polytechnic. Professor Stowell has a number of years consultancy experience from a wide variety of businesses and has been engaged in industry sponsored research over the past few years. His previous publications have appeared in journals such as the Journal of Applied Systems Analysis, Systems Practice, Systems Analysis, Modelling and Simulation, and Information Technology Training, as well as in a number of conference proceedings. His interests and publications are concerned with the social and philosophical issues surrounding organizational change and in particular the changes brought about by technologically-based Information Systems.
